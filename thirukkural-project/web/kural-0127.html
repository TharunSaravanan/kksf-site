Kural 127

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

யாகாவா ராயினும் நாகாக்க காவாக்கால் சோகாப்பர் சொல்லிழுக்குப் பட்டு.
Yaakaavaa raayinum naakaakka kaavaakkaal Sokaappar sollizhukkup pattu.

Meaning

Whatever else you fail to guard, guard your tongue. If you do not, you will suffer because of the mistakes in your own words.

Elementary

If you can only remember one rule, remember this: watch what you say. A friend who blurts out a secret or says something mean in anger can lose friends in a second. Even if you mess up other things, keeping your words kind protects you.

University

The couplet performs its own lesson in sound: the syllable kaa (‘guard’) recurs in yaa-kaavaar, naa-kaakka, kaavaak-kaal and so-kaappar, which makes the verse itself an exercise in controlled, measured speech. Parimelazhagar treats sokaappar (‘they will grieve’) as a single verb, like allaappar, and reads yaa (‘whatever’) as meaning ‘all things without exception.’ This couplet opens the three-verse unit on restraint of speech (mozhi adakkam, 127–129). Its claim that the tongue ranks above every other thing needing guard is a striking priority that other traditions also make: the Hadith pairs the tongue with chastity as the key to Paradise, and the Epistle of James calls the untamed tongue a fire.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 [4]
The Gita counts disciplined speech as one of the three forms of austerity (tapas), alongside those of body and mind.
Both treat control of the tongue as a spiritual discipline in its own right, not a mere nicety.
Christianity
“If any man among you seem to be religious, and bridleth not his tongue, but deceiveth his own heart, this man’s religion is vain.”
— James 1:26 (KJV) [5]
James says that all outward religion is empty if a person does not bridle his tongue.
Like the Kural, James makes guarding the tongue the one indispensable restraint, the test of all the rest.
Islam
“Whoever can guarantee (the chastity of) what is between his two jaw-bones and what is between his two legs (i.e. his tongue and his private parts), I guarantee Paradise for him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6474 [6]
The Prophet singles out guarding the tongue (with chastity) as the key to Paradise.
Both texts pick out the tongue as the one thing a person above all must guard.
Judaism
“Whoso keepeth his mouth and his tongue keepeth his soul from troubles.”
— Proverbs 21:23 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs teaches that guarding one’s speech protects one from trouble.
Almost exactly the Kural’s point: guard the tongue, or suffer the troubles that loose speech brings.
Buddhism
“Let a man guard himself against irritability in speech; let him be controlled in speech. Abandoning verbal misconduct, let him practice good conduct in speech.”
— Dhammapada 232 [8]
The Buddha tells his followers to guard and control their speech and give up verbal misconduct.
Both use the language of ‘guarding’ speech and warn against faults of the tongue.
Jainism
“By watchfulness of speech he keeps free from prevarication; thereby he enables his mind to act properly.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.54 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
Vāg-gupti, the guarding of speech, is one of the three Jain guptis (guards of mind, speech and body); the Uttarādhyayana says that guarding the tongue keeps one from falsehood and steadies the mind.
Valluvar ranks the tongue above every other thing a person must guard; Jain discipline likewise makes guarding speech one of the three basic protections of the soul.
Sikhism
“and who speaks insipid words with his tongue, is ruined, again and again.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 594, Raag Vadhans, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das warns that the person whose tongue speaks harsh, insipid words brings ruin on himself over and over.
The Kural tells us to guard the tongue above all, since those who do not come to grief through their own words; Gurbani says the unguarded tongue leads to repeated ruin.
Greek Mythology
“The best treasure a man can have is a sparing tongue, and the greatest pleasure, one that moves orderly; for if you speak evil, you yourself will soon be worse spoken of.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 719–721 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod, the poet of the Theogony, advises that a sparing, orderly tongue is a person’s greatest treasure.
Hesiod, like Valluvar, ranks the guarded tongue above other goods and warns that careless speech comes back on the speaker.
Philosophy
“And let silence be the general rule, or let only what is necessary be said, and in few words.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 [12]
Epictetus makes restraint of speech a basic rule of the Stoic life.
Both philosophers see a guarded tongue as the base of a disciplined life.

Real Life Example

Kaiser Wilhelm II and the Daily Telegraph interview
28 October 1908 · London, United Kingdom, and Berlin, Germany
On 28 October 1908 the London Daily Telegraph published an account of conversations in which Kaiser Wilhelm II spoke with what the paper called impulsive and unusual frankness, telling the English they were mad as March hares and saying that much of his own people was unfriendly to England. The remarks angered the British public, were seen in France and Russia as a diplomatic blunder, and set off a political crisis in Germany: in the Reichstag debate of 10 and 11 November all parties criticised the Kaiser, who considered abdicating, and Chancellor Bernhard von Bulow demanded that he change his behaviour. German History in Documents notes that the affair discredited him in the eyes of many Germans and ultimately weakened his position.
— Sources: [13] [14]
An emperor's unguarded talk, meant to improve relations, instead offended three foreign nations and his own parliament at once.
The kural says that whatever else you fail to guard, guard your tongue, for those who do not will grieve; the Kaiser's careless words brought him exactly that grief.
