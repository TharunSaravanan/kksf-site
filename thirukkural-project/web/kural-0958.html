Kural 958

Lineage · Kudimai · குடிமை

நலத்தின்கண் நாரின்மை தோன்றின் அவனைக் குலத்தின்கண் ஐயப் படும்.
Nalaththinkan naarinmai thondrin avanaik kulaththinkan aiyap padum.

Meaning

If someone who seems good shows a lack of love and kindness, people will start to doubt which family they really come from.

Elementary

If a boy from a famously kind family is mean to a puppy, people say, 'Is he really from that kind family?' Valluvar says that being unkind makes others wonder where you came from.

University

Naar (நார்) is literally 'fibre', the tough strand that binds, and by extension affection or love; naarinmai (நாரின்மை) is the want of it, which Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan gloss as anbu-inmai or arul-inmai, lack of love or compassion. The opening word nalam (நலம்) is read in several ways: Mu. Varadarasanar takes it as one's good qualities, Puliyur Kesikan as the family's distinction, and Kalaignar as beauty and renown. Aiyap padum (ஐயப் படும், 'will be doubted') makes the striking claim that conduct can call ancestry itself into question, which is the hinge between this chapter's praise of lineage and the ethical reading that says character, not birth, is the real proof. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the serpent Nahusha asks Yudhishthira who is a true Brahmin, Yudhishthira answers that it is the one in whom truth, charity, forgiveness, good conduct, gentleness, austerity and compassion are seen, and that a Shudra with these marks is no Shudra and a Brahmin without them is no Brahmin.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (Ajagara Parva, Nahusha's questions), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
In the Mahabharata Yudhishthira argues that conduct, not birth, is what proves a person's standing.
Like the Kural, he says that a lack of compassion casts doubt on any claim of high birth.
Christianity
“They answered and said unto him, Abraham is our father. Jesus saith unto them, If ye were Abraham's children, ye would do the works of Abraham.”
— John 8:39 (KJV) [4]
When his opponents boast of descent from Abraham, Jesus answers that their actions call that descent into question.
This is the Kural's logic exactly: unworthy conduct makes people doubt the lineage claimed.
Islam
“O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you.”
— Qur'an 49:13 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says tribes and lineages exist only so people can know one another, and that true nobility is righteousness.
Like the Kural, it makes character, not pedigree, the real test of a person's worth.
Judaism
“Anyone who has compassion for God's creatures, it is known that he is of the descendants of Abraham, our father, and anyone who does not have compassion for God's creatures, it is known that he is not of the descendants of Abraham, our father.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Beitzah 32b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
When rich Babylonian Jews refused to help a visitor, a sage declared that their lack of compassion proved they did not truly descend from Abraham.
This is almost word for word the Kural: lack of love makes one's lineage doubtful.
Buddhism
“Whosoever in this world kills living beings, once born or twice born, in whom there is no sympathy for living beings — know him as an outcast.”
— Vasala Sutta, Sn 1.7, v. 2 (tr. Piyadassi Thera) [7]
The Buddha tells a proud Brahmin that a lack of sympathy for living things, not birth, is what makes someone an outcast.
Like the Kural, it says that the absence of love reveals what a person really is, whatever their birth.
Jainism
“Those who are full of anger and pride, who kill, lie, steal, and own property, are Brâhmanas without pure birth and knowledge; they are very bad fields.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
When Brahmins proud of their pure birth mock and beat the outcast-born monk Harikesa, the protecting Yaksha declares that their cruelty shows they lack the very birth they boast of.
Their lack of kindness, as in the Kural, makes their claimed lineage doubtful, and the lecture ends by saying birth appears of no value beside virtue.
Sikhism
“His sons did not obey His Word; they turned their backs on Him as Guru.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 967, Raag Ramkali, Ramkali ki Vaar of Satta and Balwand (tr. BaniDB English) [9]
In their Vaar in Raag Ramkali, the bards Satta and Balwand recall that Guru Nanak's own sons would not accept his choice, so he installed his disciple Lehna as Guru Angad instead.
The Kural says that a lack of love in someone who seems good makes people doubt his lineage; the Granth records that Guru Nanak's sons, lacking that devotion, lost the inheritance to a disciple who had it.
Greek Mythology
Athena, disguised as Mentor, tells Telemachus that few sons are as good as their fathers, most are worse and only a few better, but that he has shown he has his father's spirit.
— Homer, Odyssey 2 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
The goddess treats a son's conduct as the test of whether he truly carries his father's line.
Like the Kural, it implies that a son who lacks his family's qualities makes people doubt the connection.
Philosophy
Seneca writes that a hall full of smoke-darkened ancestral portraits does not make a man noble, that the soul alone makes one well-born, and that the well-born man is the one fitted by nature for virtue.
— Seneca, Moral Letters 44 (tr. R. M. Gummere) [11]
Seneca moves nobility from family records to character.
Like the Kural, he implies that a man who lacks virtue has no real claim to his pedigree.

Real Life Example

Lord Ashley carries the Mines Act of 1842
1842 · London, United Kingdom
In 1842 a Royal Commission report on women and children working in British coal mines caused public dismay, and Lord Ashley, heir to the earldom of Shaftesbury, took up their cause in the House of Commons. He went down a coal mine himself to see the conditions, and pressed a bill through Parliament the same year. The Mines Act of 1842 banned women and girls, and boys under ten, from working underground.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ashley used the standing of an aristocratic family on behalf of people who had no voice in Parliament, and became known as one of the great reformers of the century.
The kural treats compassion as the mark of good birth whose absence makes people doubt a person's lineage, and Ashley's compassion was what made his nobility respected.
