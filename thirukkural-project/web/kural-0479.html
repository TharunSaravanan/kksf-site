Kural 479

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

அளவறிந்து வாழாதான் வாழ்க்கை உளபோல இல்லாகித் தோன்றாக் கெடும்.
Alavarindhu vaazhaadhaan vaazhkkai ulapola Illaagith thondraak kedum.

Meaning

A person who does not live within what they can afford may seem to be doing well, but their life will vanish and leave nothing behind.

Elementary

Someone who spends as if they have more than they do might look rich for a while. But soon it all disappears, as if it had never been there.

University

The couplet diagnoses an illusion. Ula pola means ‘as if existing’: the spendthrift’s life looks abundant, but it illaagi (becomes nothing) and thondraak kedum (perishes without a trace, not even appearing afterwards). The phrase alavu arindhu vaazhaadhaan ties personal finance back to the chapter’s thesis: living well requires knowledge of one’s measure just as war does. The warning is less about spending itself than about the gap between appearance and substance. Wealth that is not measured is only apparent.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the dice game, Yudhiṣṭhira keeps staking beyond what he can afford to lose: his wealth, his kingdom, his brothers, himself and finally Draupadī. An emperor who has just performed the Rājasūya sacrifice ends the day with nothing.
— Mahābhārata, Sabhā Parva (Dyūta sub-parva; tr. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Yudhiṣṭhira’s vast apparent prosperity vanishes in one sitting because he does not stop within the measure of what he can afford.
It dramatises the Kural’s ula pola illaagi: a life that seemed full becomes empty.
Christianity
“And not many days after the younger son gathered all together, and took his journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living.”
— Luke 15:13 (KJV) [5]
The prodigal son takes his inheritance, lives as if it were endless, and is soon reduced to feeding pigs.
It shows the Kural’s point that a life not lived within its measure looks rich and then vanishes.
Islam
“…and do not spend wastefully. Indeed, the wasteful are brothers of the devils, and ever has Satan been to his Lord ungrateful.”
— Qur’an 17:26–27 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an forbids squandering (tabdhīr) and ranks it as a serious moral failing.
It parallels the Kural’s condemnation of living beyond one’s measure. The Qur’an stresses the moral gravity, while the Kural stresses the practical collapse.
Judaism
“There is treasure to be desired and oil in the dwelling of the wise; but a foolish man spendeth it up.”
— Proverbs 21:20 (KJV) [5]
The wise man’s house keeps its stores, while the fool consumes everything.
It gives the Kural’s contrast between living by measure and wasting until nothing remains.
Buddhism
The Buddha lists six channels through which a householder’s wealth drains away: intoxicants, roaming the streets at unseemly hours, frequenting fairs and shows, gambling, bad companions and idleness. Each is described as dissipating what has been earned.
— Dīgha Nikāya 31, Sigālovāda Sutta (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
Buddhist lay ethics shows how unmeasured habits quietly empty a household.
It parallels the Kural’s picture of a life that seems prosperous while its substance is draining away.
Jainism
“After having enjoyed pleasant seats, beds, carriages, riches, and pleasures, after having squandered his wealth which he had so much trouble in gaining, and after having committed many sins, he will, under the burden of his Karman, and believing only in the visible world, be grieved in the hour of death like the ram at the arrival of a guest.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.8–9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The parable compares the self-indulgent man to a ram fattened for a guest: it looks well fed and thriving until the day it is slaughtered.
The Kural's spendthrift seems to prosper and then vanishes without a trace; the Jain fattened ram gives the same picture of apparent plenty ending suddenly.
Sikhism
“As the bubbles in the water well up and disappear again, so is the universe created; says Nanak, listen, O my friend!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 25), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur compares worldly existence to a bubble that rises and vanishes on the water.
The Kural says the life of one who lives beyond his means seems to prosper, then vanishes without a trace; Guru Tegh Bahadur's bubble has the same brief show and quick end.
Greek Mythology
Cursed by Ceres with insatiable Hunger, King Erysichthon eats through his whole fortune. He sells his daughter Mestra again and again to buy more food, and finally, with nothing left, devours his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738–878 [10]
Consumption without any measure destroys first a rich king’s wealth and then the king himself.
It is an extreme image of the Kural’s life that becomes nothing through consumption without measure.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes the prodigal as someone ruined through his own fault, since wasting one’s substance is a kind of ruining oneself, because life depends on property. He adds that prodigals soon exhaust their means and so are driven to take from wrong sources.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1, 1120a–1121b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle treats prodigality as a vice of excess that destroys its possessor’s livelihood.
It matches the Kural’s claim that one who does not live within his measure is ruined, and by his own doing.

Real Life Example

Rembrandt loses his house and collection
1656-1658 · Amsterdam, Netherlands
In 1639 Rembrandt bought a large house on the Jodenbreestraat in Amsterdam, and he spent heavily on paintings, prints, antiquities and studio props. Mounting debts left him unable to keep up the payments, and in 1656 an official inventory was made of his possessions, nearly all of which were then sold at auction. The house was sold in 1658, and Rembrandt moved to a rented home on the Rozengracht, where he died in 1669.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The most celebrated painter of his city lived in apparent prosperity for years before his household and collection were dispersed.
The kural says the life of one who does not live within his means seems to flourish and then vanishes, as Rembrandt's grand house and collection did.
