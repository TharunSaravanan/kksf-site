Kural 727

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

பகையகத்துப் பேடிகை ஒள்வாள் அவையகத்து அஞ்சு மவன்கற்ற நூல்.
Pakaiyakaththup pedikai olvaal avaiyakaththu Anju mavankatra nool.

Meaning

The learning of someone who is afraid to speak in front of a group is like a shiny sword in the hand of a coward facing the enemy.

Elementary

Picture a very sharp, shiny sword held by someone who is shaking too hard to lift it. It looks impressive but does nothing. Learning you’re too scared to share is just like that sword.

University

This couplet turns 726 into a single simile: pēṭi kai oḷ vāḷ, “the bright sword in the hand of a pēṭi.” In classical Tamil, pēṭi means a person who is neither man nor woman, traditionally glossed as a eunuch or hermaphrodite. Pope and Drew translate it “hermaphrodite,” and Pope’s verse has “sexless being.” The martial culture of the time used such a person as a stock figure for someone unfit for battle; the image reflects that period’s assumptions, not a judgement the modern reader needs to share. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all stress uselessness: the sword is fine, but the hand fails it. The couplet also repeats 723’s pakai-y-akattu / avai-y-akattu frame. The sandhi text reads “பேடிகை யொள்வா ளவையகத் / தஞ்சு மவன்கற்ற நூல்.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Pṛthā, do not yield to this degrading impotence. It does not become you. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise, O chastiser of the enemy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.3 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna rebukes Arjuna’s collapse with the word klaibya, the state of a klība, a man unfit for manly action, and tells him to stand up.
The Gita uses the same kind of image as the Kural’s pēṭi, a warrior disarmed by fear, to shame a skilled man into using his skill.
Christianity
In the garden of Gethsemane, Peter draws his sword and cuts off the ear of the high priest’s servant. Hours later, in the high priest’s courtyard, he denies knowing Jesus when a servant girl questions him.
— John 18:10, 18:15-17 (KJV) [4]
Peter’s sword is quick, but his courage fails him the moment he has to speak before those around him.
This is the Kural’s pairing of sword and assembly, showing that a drawn blade is no proof of courage to speak.
Islam
“And whoever turns his back to them on such a day, unless swerving [as a strategy] for war or joining [another] company, has certainly returned with anger [upon him] from Allah”
— Qur’an 8:16 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an condemns the fighter who flees the battle line out of fear.
An armed man who flees is the first half of the Kural’s simile.
Judaism
“What man is there that is fearful and faint-hearted? let him go and return unto his house, lest his brethren’s heart melt as his heart.”
— Deuteronomy 20:8 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Before battle, the Torah sends fearful soldiers home, because a frightened fighter is of no use and weakens everyone else.
An armed but fearful man is useless, which is the core of the Kural’s simile.
Buddhism
“Firstly, one warrior falters and founders at the mere sight of a cloud of dust. He doesn’t stay firm, and fails to plunge into battle.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.75, Yodhājīva Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha describes five kinds of warrior, the first of whom loses his nerve before the fighting even starts, and compares them to monks who give up.
An armed man who falters before battle is exactly the pēṭi’s useless sword.
Jainism
“As at the time of the battle the coward looks behind him for a ditch, thicket, or other hiding-place, (thinking that) nobody knows who will win; 'A moment's moment will bring the decision; when we lose, we shall fly.' Thus thinks the coward.”
— Sutrakritanga I.3.3.1-2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga uses the coward who plans his escape in battle as a picture of weak monks who, when tested, fall back on worldly skills instead of standing firm.
The Kural's learned man who fears the assembly is like a sharp sword in a coward's hand before the enemy; the Jain text uses the same coward-in-battle figure for someone whose equipment is wasted by lack of nerve.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maru, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir defines the true hero as one who actually stands and fights for the right and never quits the field.
The Kural pictures learning held by the fearful as a bright sword in the hand of one unfit to fight, never used; Kabir's hero is the opposite, one who uses his strength and stays on the field.
Greek Mythology
When Hector challenges any Achaean champion to single combat, the Greek chiefs sit silent, ashamed to refuse and afraid to accept, until old Nestor shames them and nine warriors finally stand up.
— Homer, Iliad 7.92-169 [10]
Fully armed heroes are frozen by fear until they are reproached.
Weapons in the hands of men gripped by fear are worth nothing until courage returns.
Philosophy
Epictetus notes that a lyre player who sings and plays well on his own becomes anxious when he walks into the theatre. The cause is not a lack of skill but his wish for applause, which is not in his control.
— Epictetus, Discourses 2.13 (“On Anxiety”) [11]
Fear of the audience, not lack of ability, is what makes a skilled performer falter.
The skill is sharp but unusable once the performer is afraid, like the gleaming sword in the Kural.

Real Life Example

Henry Cavendish's electrical discoveries stay unpublished for a century
1770s to 1879 · London, England, and Cambridge, England
Henry Cavendish published an early version of his theory of electricity in 1771, but although he continued to work on the subject he published nothing more on it. His manuscripts, recording many careful experiments, remained with his family for about a hundred years. James Clerk Maxwell acquired them in 1874 and published an edition in 1879, showing that Cavendish had anticipated results later credited to others.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Results that could have advanced the science of electricity in Cavendish's lifetime lay hidden, so that others received the credit for rediscovering them.
The kural compares the learning of one who will not use it before the assembly to a gleaming sword in a hand that never strikes, and Cavendish's unpublished electrical work is a vivid case of fine learning left unused.
