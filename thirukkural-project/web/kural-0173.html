Kural 173

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

சிற்றின்பம் வெஃகி அறனல்ல செய்யாரே மற்றின்பம் வேண்டு பவர்.
Sitrinbam vehki aranalla seyyaare Matrinbam vendu pavar.

Meaning

People who want the other, lasting happiness will never do wrong just to grab a small pleasure that soon passes.

Elementary

If you are saving your pocket money for something you really love, you won’t spend it on a cheap sweet that’s gone in a minute. This kural says people who want the big, lasting kind of happiness don’t do bad things for tiny, quick treats.

University

The kural sets up a contrast between சிற்றின்பம் (sitrinbam, “petty pleasure”) and மற்றின்பம் (matrinbam, “the other joy”). Parimelazhagar glosses the latter as the bliss of heaven or liberation, while modern commentators such as Mu. Varadarasan read it as the enduring happiness that flows from aram (virtue); either way, the argument is a rational trade-off, not a prohibition. Covetousness is foolish because it sacrifices a greater, lasting good for a trivial, fleeting one. This is among the most widely shared structures in world ethics: the Buddha’s ‘renounce the lesser happiness for the greater’, Nachiketas refusing Death’s riches, and Socrates rebuking Athens for preferring money to the soul all have the same shape.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses.” … “No man can be made happy by wealth.”
— Katha Upanishad I.1.26-27 (tr. Max Müller) [3]
When Death offers the boy Nachiketas cattle, gold, long life, and a kingdom to stop him asking about the Self, Nachiketas refuses: such pleasures do not last and cannot truly satisfy.
Nachiketas is the Kural’s model: he wants the ‘other joy’ and so will not trade it for small, passing pleasures.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt.”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts earthly treasure, which decays and can be stolen, with heavenly treasure, which lasts.
Same contrast of a perishable lesser good against an imperishable greater one.
Islam
“And do not extend your eyes toward that by which We have given enjoyment to [some] categories of them, [its being but] the splendor of worldly life by which We test them. And the provision of your Lord is better and more enduring.”
— Qur’an 20:131 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an tells the believer not to look longingly at the worldly enjoyments of others, because God’s provision is better and lasts longer.
An almost exact match: don’t covet others’ passing pleasures, because the other joy is better and more enduring.
Judaism
“More precious is one hour in repentance and good deeds in this world, than all the life of the world to come; And more precious is one hour of the tranquility of the world to come, than all the life of this world.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 4:17 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah weighs this world’s pleasures against the world to come, and says one hour of that tranquility outweighs a whole lifetime here.
Like the Kural, it measures worldly pleasure against a far greater joy that makes the smaller one not worth sinning for.
Buddhism
“If by renouncing a lesser happiness one may realize a greater happiness, let the wise man renounce the lesser, having regard for the greater.”
— Dhammapada 290 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha tells the wise to give up small pleasures when doing so leads to a far greater happiness.
This is the Kural’s sitrinbam/matrinbam argument almost word for word.
Jainism
“Pleasures bring only a moment's happiness, but suffering for a very long time, intense suffering, but slight happiness; they are an obstacle to the liberation from existence, and are a very mine of evils.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The two sons of the priest Bhṛgu refuse their father's offer of wealth and pleasures, explaining that momentary enjoyment costs long suffering and blocks the lasting goal of liberation.
Valluvar says those who seek the other, lasting joy will not do wrong for a petty, passing pleasure, and the Jain verse sets the same contrast between a moment's pleasure and the enduring good of liberation.
Sikhism
“O Baba, the pleasures of other foods are false.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 16, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that sensual enjoyments pursued for their own sake lead to ruin, while the true joy lies in the Lord's Name.
The Kural says those who seek the lasting joy will not do wrong for a small passing pleasure; Guru Nanak likewise calls such pleasures false compared with lasting delight in God.
Greek Mythology
Stranded on Thrinacia, Odysseus’ starving crew ignored his warning and slaughtered the sacred cattle of Helios for a feast. Zeus destroyed their ship and every one of them lost the homecoming they had struggled ten years to reach.
— Homer, Odyssey 12 [10]
The sailors took something that was not theirs for a few days of feasting and lost their lives and their return home.
They traded the ‘other joy’ (homecoming) for a petty, unlawful pleasure, exactly what the Kural says the wise never do.
Philosophy
“O my friend, why do you who are a citizen of the great and mighty and wise city of Athens, care so much about laying up the greatest amount of money and honor and reputation, and so little about wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul?”
— Plato, Apology 29d-e (tr. Jowett) [11]
Socrates tells his jury that he spends his life challenging people who chase money while neglecting the soul.
Socrates, like Valluvar, calls it a bad trade to chase lesser goods at the expense of the greater one.

Real Life Example

Bobby Jones calls a penalty on himself at the U.S. Open
June 1925 · Worcester Country Club, Worcester, Massachusetts, United States
During the first round of the 1925 U.S. Open, the amateur golfer Bobby Jones's club moved his ball slightly as he addressed it on the 11th hole. No official, playing partner or spectator saw it, but Jones reported it and took a one-stroke penalty. He finished the 72 holes tied with Willie Macfarlane and lost the title in a playoff.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Jones gave up a national championship rather than take an advantage no one else would have known about, and his integrity became part of golf's tradition.
The kural says those who seek lasting joy will not do wrong for a small passing gain, and Jones preferred his honour to a title won unfairly.
