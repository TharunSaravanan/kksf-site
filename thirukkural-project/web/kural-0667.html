Kural 667

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

உருவுகண்டு எள்ளாமை வேண்டும் உருள்பெருந்தேர்க்கு அச்சாணி அன்னார் உடைத்து.
UruvukaNdu eLLaamai vaeNdum uruLperundhaerkku AchchaaNi annaar udaiththu.

Meaning

Never look down on someone because of how small or plain they look. Some people are like the tiny pin that holds the wheel on a huge rolling chariot.

Elementary

A giant temple chariot has huge wheels, but a tiny metal pin keeps each wheel from falling off. Without that little pin, the whole chariot stops! Some people are small or quiet, but they hold everything together, so never laugh at someone for being small.

University

Uru (form, size, appearance) and eḷḷāmai (not despising) open the couplet, and its image is the accāṇi, the small pin driven through the end of the axle (accu) that keeps the wheel of a great rolling temple chariot (uruḷ perun tēr) in place. Parimelazhagar, and Solomon Pappaiah after him, apply it within the chapter’s theme: men of firm mind may be small in body yet great in achievement, so they must not be judged by appearance; Kalaignar and Mu. Varadarasanar give it the wider moral that no one should be belittled for their size. The couplet reverses the usual link between bulk and strength, placing strength once again in firmness rather than outward form, as in 661. The three sources agree on the text; in sandhi form the lines read uruvukaṇ ṭeḷḷāmai vēṇṭu muruḷperuntērk / kaccāṇi yaṉṉā ruṭaittu.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Viṣṇu comes to King Bali’s sacrifice as Vāmana, a dwarf brahmin boy, and asks for just three steps of land; granted it, he grows to cosmic size and covers the earth and heavens in two strides.
— Śrīmad Bhāgavatam 8.18-8.20 (Bhaktivedanta Vedabase) [4]
The Vāmana avatāra is India’s classic warning that small size can hide immeasurable power.
Bali’s court, seeing only a dwarf, learns what the Kural teaches: never judge strength by size.
Christianity
“Behold also the ships, which though they be so great, and are driven of fierce winds, yet are they turned about with a very small helm, whithersoever the governor listeth.”
— James 3:4 (KJV) [5]
James points out that a huge ship is steered by a tiny rudder, to show how a small thing can control a great one.
The helm and the linchpin make the same point: a small part can govern a great vessel.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth but He looks to your heart and to your deeds.”
— Sahih Muslim 2564c (Sunnah.com) [6]
The Prophet teaches that worth is measured by inner disposition and deeds, not by outward appearance or means.
Both texts forbid judging people by their outward form.
Judaism
“But GOD said to Samuel, “Pay no attention to his appearance or his stature, for I have rejected him. For [GOD sees] not as humans see; humans see only what is visible, but GOD sees into the heart.”
— 1 Samuel 16:7 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, via Sefaria) [7]
Sent to anoint a king, Samuel is warned not to be impressed by Eliab’s height; the chosen one is the youngest, the overlooked shepherd David.
‘Pay no attention to his appearance or his stature’ is almost the Kural’s uruvu kaṇṭu eḷḷāmai.
Buddhism
Venerable Bhaddiya the Dwarf (Lakuṇṭaka Bhaddiya), whose small and unattractive body drew mockery, is taken by Sāriputta for a mere learner; the Buddha declares that Bhaddiya has already cut the cycle and reached freedom.
— Udāna 7.2, Bhaddiya Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Udāna records the Buddha correcting a senior disciple who misjudged a small monk’s attainment.
The dwarf-monk is the Kural’s linchpin: small in form, complete in achievement.
Jainism
“Who is that dandy coming there? he is swarthy, dreadful, with a turned-up nose, miserably clad, a very devil of a dirty man”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.6 and 12.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Brahmin priests mock the outcaste monk Harikesa for his looks and poverty, until his spiritual power is revealed and they cry that the value of penance has become visible and birth appears of no value.
The story enacts the kural's warning not to despise anyone for small size or plain appearance, for the scorned figure proves to be the one on whom everything turns.
Sikhism
“Nanak seeks the company of the lowest of the low class, the very lowest of the low.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak deliberately sides with those society despises, rejecting caste and outward status as measures of worth.
The Kural warns against scorning anyone for small size or humble looks, since such people can be the linchpin of a great chariot; Guru Nanak likewise refuses to look down on the lowly.
Greek Mythology
“...now you know that it is possible for even a Mouse to confer benefits on a Lion.”
— Aesop’s Fables, The Lion and the Mouse (trans. G. F. Townsend) [11]
The lion laughs at a mouse’s promise to repay him, but when the lion is caught in hunters’ ropes, the mouse gnaws him free.
The despised small creature proves indispensable, just as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
“I say, that he is exactly like the busts of Silenus, which are set up in the statuaries’ shops, holding pipes and flutes in their mouths; and they are made to open in the middle, and have images of gods inside them.”
— Plato, Symposium 215a-b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Alcibiades compares Socrates, notoriously ugly, to cheap satyr figurines that open to reveal golden images of gods inside.
Socrates is the philosopher’s linchpin: unimpressive outside, indispensable within.

Real Life Example

FC Barcelona signs the small thirteen-year-old Lionel Messi
2000 · Barcelona, Spain
As a boy in Argentina Lionel Messi was diagnosed with a growth hormone deficiency, and when his club there stopped paying for his treatment his family sought a club abroad. FC Barcelona's officials hesitated at first over his small size and the cost of the hormone therapy, but the club signed him and paid for his treatment when he arrived at the age of thirteen. He went on to become one of the most successful players in the club's history.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A player some thought too small became the centre of one of the greatest teams of his era, showing how misleading physical size can be as a measure of ability.
The kural warns that no one should be scorned for small size, since the world has people like the little linchpin that holds a great chariot wheel, and Barcelona's decision not to judge the young Messi by his height gave the club its linchpin.
