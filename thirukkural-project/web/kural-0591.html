Kural 591

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

உடையர் எனப்படுவது ஊக்கம் அஃதில்லார் உடையது உடையரோ மற்று.
Udaiyar enappaduvadhu ookkam aqdhillaar Udaiyadhu udaiyaro matru.

Meaning

Inner drive is the only thing a person can truly be said to own. People without it do not really own anything, whatever else they have.

Elementary

Imagine two children who are each given a box of paints. One gets excited and paints every day; the other leaves the box closed on a shelf. Only the first one really ‘has’ paints. The Kural says the eager, energetic spirit is the thing that makes anything you own actually yours.

University

The chapter opens by redefining possession itself: “udaiyar enappaduvadhu” (what it is to be called a possessor) is ookkam, which Parimelazhagar glosses as mental elation or upsurge of the will toward action (ullaezhuchi), the drive that animates effort rather than effort itself. The rhetorical question “udaiyadhu udaiyaro” (do they own what they own?) denies that the unenergetic possess even their existing wealth, since without ookkam it will neither be defended nor increased; “matru” is treated by the commentators as an expletive particle (asai). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all paraphrase the line as ‘only the energetic are truly possessors’, and the text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net. The adhikaram sits in Arasiyal (royalty), after Espionage and before Unsluggishness (Madiyinmai), Manly Effort (Aalvinaiyudaimai) and Not Despairing in Trouble, forming a cluster on the psychology of exertion that parallels the Sanskrit utsāha-śakti, the ‘power of energy’ that Kautilya lists among a king’s three powers. Thirukkural.net titles the chapter ‘Magnanimity’, while Pope and ytamizh.com use ‘Energy’, reflecting the double sense of ookkam as both drive and largeness of spirit.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The performer is said to be in the mode of goodness, when he or she is free from egotism and attachment, endowed with enthusiasm and determination, and equipoised in success and failure.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.26 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna describes the best kind of worker as one filled with utsāha (zeal) and dhṛti (resolve), who keeps working steadily whatever the outcome.
Utsāha is the Sanskrit counterpart of ookkam, and the Gita, like the Kural, makes it a defining trait of the worthy person.
Christianity
“And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men;”
— Colossians 3:23 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to put their whole heart and energy into every task, treating even ordinary work as service to God.
Both texts treat wholehearted inner energy as what gives a person’s life and work its real value.
Islam
“A strong believer is better and is more lovable to Allah than a weak believer, and there is good in everyone, (but) cherish that which gives you benefit (in the Hereafter) and seek help from Allah and do not lose heart…”
— Sahih Muslim 2664 [5]
The Prophet praises the strong, active believer and tells people to pursue what benefits them eagerly and never give in to helplessness.
The Kural’s ookkam and the hadith’s strength and refusal of ‘ajz (helplessness) both mark the energetic person as the one of real worth.
Judaism
“Whatsoever thy hand attaineth to do by thy strength, that do; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.”
— Ecclesiastes 9:10 (JPS 1917) [6]
Koheleth urges people to act with all their strength now, because this life is the only arena for effort.
Like the Kural, it holds that the energy you bring to action is what counts, more than anything you merely hold.
Buddhism
In the Kasibharadvaja Sutta the Buddha answers a ploughing brahmin by describing his own ‘farming’, in which faith is the seed and energy (viriya) is the draught-ox that carries him forward to freedom from bondage.
— Sutta Nipata 1.4 (Kasibharadvaja Sutta) [7]
The Buddha tells a farmer that his spiritual work has its own plough and oxen, and energy is the beast that pulls everything forward.
As in the Kural, energy is the working asset that makes every other endowment productive.
Jainism
“Four things of paramount value are difficult to obtain here by a living being: human birth, instruction in the Law, belief in it, and energy in self-control.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 3.1 (cf. 3.10) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira names energy or exertion (vīrya) in self-control as one of the four rarest and most valuable possessions; verse 10 adds that many who approve of the religion never fulfil it strenuously.
The Kural says energy is the only thing that truly makes one an owner; the Uttaradhyayana likewise ranks energy among the few possessions of real worth.
Sikhism
“Make the effort, and chant the Lord's Name. O very fortunate ones, earn this wealth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 48, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan calls for udam, active effort, and names the Lord's Name as the wealth that such effort earns.
The Kural says only those with inner energy truly possess anything; Guru Arjan likewise ties real wealth to effort, though the wealth he means is the Name, not property.
Greek Mythology
Heracles, born with divine strength, wins his lasting fame only through the Twelve Labours, a lifetime of relentless exertion undertaken at Eurystheus’ command.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5 (the Labours of Heracles) [10]
Heracles’ gifts meant little until he threw himself into one exhausting task after another, and his tireless striving is what made him a hero.
The myth illustrates the Kural’s point that endowment without energetic use is not true possession.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that happiness lies in virtuous activity, not in merely having a good disposition: just as at the Olympic Games it is not the finest and strongest who are crowned but those who actually compete, so in life those who act rightly win the prize.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.8 (1099a) [11]
For Aristotle a capacity you never use is like a strong athlete who never enters the race; only active exercise (energeia) earns the good.
The Kural likewise says that what you hold counts only if energy puts it to work.

Real Life Example

Babur loses his homeland and goes on to found the Mughal Empire
1494-1526 · Fergana and Samarkand (Central Asia), Kabul, and Panipat, India
Babur inherited the kingdom of Fergana as a boy in 1494 and spent years trying to hold Samarkand, but in the end lost both Samarkand and his native Fergana. He took Kabul in 1504 and made it his base, and after several raids into India he defeated Sultan Ibrahim Lodi of Delhi at the First Battle of Panipat in April 1526. By his death in 1530 he had laid the foundation of the Mughal Empire.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Having lost every possession he was born to, Babur still had the drive that let him build something far larger than what he lost.
The kural says the only real possession is inner energy and that those without it do not truly own even what they hold, and Babur's life shows energy outlasting the loss of a whole kingdom.
