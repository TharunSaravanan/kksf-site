Kural 787

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

அழிவி னவைநீக்கி ஆறுய்த்து அழிவின்கண் அல்லல் உழப்பதாம் நட்பு.
Azhivi navainikki aaruyththu azhivinkan allal uzhappadhaam natpu.

Meaning

A friend stops you from doing what would ruin you, guides you onto the right path, and shares your pain when trouble comes anyway.

Elementary

A real friend does three things. First, if you’re about to do something that will get you into big trouble, they stop you. Second, they help you find a better thing to do. Third, if something bad happens anyway, they don’t run away; they stay and feel sad with you. That’s what a friend is for.

University

The verse lists three duties in order: azhivinavai nīkki (அழிவினவை நீக்கி, ‘removing the things that lead to ruin’), āRu uytthu (ஆறுய்த்து, ‘sending him along the right way’, from āRu, path), and, if ruin still comes (azhivinkaN, அழிவின்கண்), allal uzhappathu (அல்லல் உழப்பது, ‘toiling in his distress alongside him’). Parimelazhagar takes the first two as prevention and correction, carrying on from the rebuke of kural 784, and the third as the friend’s readiness to share in misfortune he could not prevent. The printed line அழிவி னவைநீக்கி is a metrical split of அழிவினவை நீக்கி. Both web sources give the same text, and Pope’s ‘it weeps with him who weeps’ echoes Romans 12:15.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, when Arjuna throws down his bow in despair, Krishna, serving as his charioteer, talks him out of his paralysis, shows him the path of duty, and stays at his side through the whole war.
— Bhagavad Gita, chapters 1-2 and 18 [3]
Krishna turns Arjuna away from collapse, guides him with the teaching of the Gita, and shares the dangers of the battle with him.
Krishna carries out all three of the Kural’s duties: steering his friend from ruin, setting him on the right path, and standing with him in hardship.
Christianity
“Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep.”
— Romans 12:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks believers to share both the joy and the sorrow of others.
This matches the Kural’s last duty, to share a friend’s grief, and Pope’s English couplet echoes it directly.
Islam
“While they both were in the cave, he reassured his companion, ‘Do not worry; Allah is certainly with us.’”
— Qur’an 9:40 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
During the flight from Mecca, the Prophet and Abu Bakr hid together in the cave of Thawr while their pursuers searched for them.
Abu Bakr chose to share the Prophet’s danger in the cave, just as the Kural says a friend shares distress.
Judaism
When Job’s three friends hear of his disasters, they travel to him, weep, tear their robes, and sit with him on the ground for seven days and nights without a word, seeing how great his suffering is.
— Job 2:11-13 [6]
Before their later arguments, Job’s friends simply come and share his grief in silence.
Their silent week is the Kural’s third duty: suffering alongside a friend whose ruin could not be prevented.
Buddhism
“(i) he restrains one from doing evil, (ii) he encourages one to do good” … “in misfortune he does not forsake one”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha lists among the marks of true friends holding one back from evil, urging one toward good, and not deserting one in misfortune.
The Kural’s three duties appear almost point for point in this list.
Jainism
When the newly ordained prince Meghakumāra, crushed by the burden of self-control, wavered and wanted to give up the monk's life, Mahāvīra stopped him by reminding him of his past birth as an elephant that held its foot in the air to spare a hare. Megha became steady in his vows again, repented and went on with his austerities.
— Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Parva 10 (Mahāvīracaritra), 'Part 7: Story of Meghakumāra' (tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org); the episode is first told in Jñātādharmakathā 1 [8]
Jain tradition presents the Jina as a guide who heads off a follower's fall at the moment of weakness and sets him back on the path.
The Kural's friend turns a companion away from ruin and guides him onto the right way, and Mahāvīra does the same for Megha, though the story has no counterpart to the Kural's third duty of sharing in a misfortune that could not be prevented.
Sikhism
“They alone are my friends, who travel along with me; and in that place, where the accounts are called for, they appear standing with me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's hymn (traditionally sung to Sajjan) defines true friends as those who stay with us right up to the moment of reckoning.
The Kural says a friend shares our suffering when ruin comes; Guru Nanak's true friends still stand beside us when the account is called and judgement falls.
Greek Mythology
When Orestes is captured in the land of the Taurians and only one of the two friends may go free, Pylades refuses to save himself and leave Orestes to die alone, saying he shared his voyage and will share his fate.
— Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris 674-715 [10]
Pylades has followed Orestes into exile and danger, and now insists on sharing even death with him.
This is the Kural’s friend who, when ruin comes, stays to suffer it together.
Philosophy
“Such friendship enhances prosperity, and relieves adversity of its burden by halving and sharing it.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 6 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Laelius says friendship doubles our good fortune and halves our misfortune by sharing it.
Cicero’s ‘halving and sharing’ adversity is the Kural’s allal uzhappathu, carrying a friend’s pain with him.

Real Life Example

Friedrich Engels supports Karl Marx for decades
1850s-1895 · Manchester and London, England, United Kingdom
Friedrich Engels, the son of a cotton manufacturer, first met Karl Marx in 1842 and renewed the contact in 1844. While Marx lived in London and wrote, Engels worked for many years in his family's firm in Manchester and sent him money, support that Smithsonian magazine describes as lasting nearly forty years and beginning even before Engels himself was well off. After Marx died in 1883, Engels devoted much of his remaining time to publishing his friend's unfinished work.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Engels kept his friend and his family afloat through long years of poverty and then preserved his friend's writings after his death.
The kural says friendship keeps a friend from ruin, helps him along his path and shares his hardship when it comes, and Engels did all of this for Marx over four decades.
