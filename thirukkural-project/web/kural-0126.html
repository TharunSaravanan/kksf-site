Kural 126

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

ஒருமையுள் ஆமைபோல் ஐந்தடக்கல் ஆற்றின் எழுமையும் ஏமாப் புடைத்து.
Orumaiyul aamaipol aindhadakkal aatrin Ezhumaiyum emaap pudaiththu.

Meaning

If, in one life, a person can pull in their five senses the way a tortoise pulls in its legs, that power will protect them for seven births (all their lives to come).

Elementary

A turtle pulls its head and four legs into its shell when danger comes. We have five senses: eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin. If we can ‘pull them in’ when they want something harmful, we are protected just like the turtle in its shell.

University

The tortoise image (aamai) also appears in Bhagavad Gita 2.58 and in the Pali Kumma Sutta, which points to a shared South Asian stock of contemplative imagery rather than direct borrowing. Parimelazhagar explains the simile exactly: the tortoise withdraws its five members (four legs and head) so that harm cannot get in, and a person should restrain the five senses so that sin cannot get in. He classes this couplet as teaching bodily restraint (meyyadakkam). The contrast of orumai (‘one birth’) and ezhumai (‘seven births’) draws on karma doctrine: effort in one life gives a safeguard (emaappu, ‘protection, fortress’) across many lives, though Karunanidhi secularizes it as ‘for all time.’ The tortoise does not destroy its limbs; it withdraws and extends them at will, so the ideal is mastery of the senses, not mutilation of them.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is able to withdraw his senses from sense objects, as the tortoise draws its limbs within the shell, is firmly fixed in perfect consciousness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.58 [4]
Krishna uses the very same tortoise image for the sage who can pull his senses back from their objects.
An almost exact parallel: the same animal, the same withdrawal of the senses, the same idea of protection through self-mastery.
Christianity
“But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:27 (KJV) [5]
Paul describes disciplining his body like an athlete so that he is not himself disqualified.
Both texts treat mastering the body as a safeguard that protects a person’s ultimate destiny.
Islam
“Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them. Indeed, Allah is Acquainted with what they do.”
— Qur’an 24:30 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an commands believers to guard their eyes and bodies as a means of purity.
Guarding the senses, like the tortoise guarding its limbs, keeps a person pure and protected.
Judaism
“that ye go not about after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go astray;”
— Numbers 15:39 (JPS 1917) [7]
The commandment of the tzitzit (fringes) is given so that Israel will not be led astray by roaming heart and eyes.
Both texts see the unrestrained senses as what lead a person astray, and reining them in as what protects.
Buddhism
“Like a tortoise with its limbs withdrawn in its shell, so the monk, the thoughts of the heart.”
— Kumma Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 35.199 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
In the Kumma Sutta, a tortoise keeps its four legs and neck withdrawn until a hungry jackal gives up; likewise Mara finds no opening in a monk whose sense-doors are guarded.
The same tortoise and the same five withdrawn members (four legs and neck) as in the Kural, with the same lesson: guard the senses and harm finds no way in.
Jainism
“As a tortoise withdraws his limbs within his own body, even so does the valiant withdraw his mind within himself from all sins. He also withdraws his hands, legs, mind, sense-organs, sinful moods, evil words, pride, and deceitfulness. This indeed is the valor of the valiant.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.8.16–18, English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [9]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga uses the tortoise drawing in its limbs as the model for the heroic ascetic who withdraws the senses, speech and passions from sin.
This is the Kural's own image: drawing in the five senses like a tortoise its limbs, which in Jain terms stops the inflow (āsrava) of karma that would bind the soul over many births.
Sikhism
“I have brought the ten sensory organs under my control, and erased every trace of the five thieves.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 693, Raag Dhanaasree, Bhagat Namdev (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Namdev describes bringing all the senses under control and wiping out the passions that ride on them.
The Kural's tortoise drawing in its limbs is the image of the senses withdrawn and guarded; Namdev's verse states the same mastery of the senses directly.
Greek Mythology
To pass the Sirens, whose song lures sailors to their death, Odysseus stops his crew’s ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast so he cannot act on what he hears.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.39–54, 12.154–200 (paraphrase) [11]
Odysseus survives by deliberately shutting off, or binding, his senses’ power to pull him toward destruction.
Like the tortoise, Odysseus protects himself by pulling his senses back from a deadly attraction.
Philosophy
“Philosophy, Socrates says, frees the soul by “persuading her to retire from them, and abstain from all but the necessary use of them, and be gathered up and collected into herself”
— Plato, Phaedo 83a (trans. Jowett) [12]
In the Phaedo, the philosopher draws the soul back from the deceptive senses into itself.
Plato’s soul ‘gathered into herself’ is almost the tortoise drawing into its shell: withdrawal from the senses as the path to safety and truth.

Real Life Example

Gandhi's three vows before sailing to England
1888 · Rajkot, India
When the 18-year-old Mohandas Gandhi prepared to leave Rajkot to study law in England in 1888, his mother feared that young men there took to meat and liquor and hesitated to let him go. Becharji Swami, a Jain monk and family adviser, administered a solemn oath in which Gandhi vowed to abstain from wine, women and meat, and only then did his mother give her permission. Gandhi recounted the episode in his autobiography, The Story of My Experiments with Truth.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Gandhi later treated keeping these vows as an early discipline of his life, and self-restraint of the senses became central to his ideas.
The kural praises drawing in the five senses as a tortoise draws in its limbs; Gandhi's three vows were a deliberate withdrawal from the pleasures his mother feared would capture him abroad.
