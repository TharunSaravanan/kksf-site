Kural 1021

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

கருமம் செயஒருவன் கைதூவேன் என்னும் பெருமையின் பீடுடையது இல்.
Karumam seya oruvan kaithooven ennum perumaiyin peedudaiyadhu il.

Meaning

There is no greater honour than a person's promise, 'I will not stop working to help my family grow.'

Elementary

Imagine a girl who promises, ‘I will keep helping my family every day, and I won’t give up when it gets boring.’ Valluvar says that promise, kept day after day, is the proudest thing a person can wear, prouder than any medal.

University

The chapter opens by naming its ideal: the person who says kai thooven (கைதூவேன், ‘I will not let my hand rest’) in karumam (கருமம், ‘the work’), understood by Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan as the duty of raising one's kudi (குடி, ‘family, lineage, community’). Peedu (பீடு) means dignity or eminence, so the couplet ranks this self-imposed vow above every other form of perumai (பெருமை, ‘greatness’), the subject of chapter 98. Solomon Pappaiah widens kudi to ‘house and country’, a reading he keeps through the whole chapter, while Kesikan, in the commentarial line of Parimelazhagar, keeps it to one's own family line. Pope renders it ‘Who says “I’ll do my work, nor slack my hand”, / His greatness, clothed with dignity supreme, shall stand.’ The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For if I ever failed to engage in carefully performing prescribed duties, O Pārtha, certainly all men would follow My path.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.23 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna says that although He needs nothing, He never stops working, because others take their cue from Him.
Like the Kural's ‘I will not rest my hand’, the greatest one refuses to slacken in his work.
Christianity
“Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might”
— Ecclesiastes 9:10 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher urges full-strength effort in whatever work lies at hand, since the chance to act does not last.
Both texts honour the hand that does not hold back from its task.
Islam
“Nobody has ever eaten a better meal than that which one has earned by working with one's own hands. The Prophet (ﷺ) of Allah, David used to eat from the earnings of his manual labor.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2072 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet praises food earned by one's own labour and holds up the prophet-king David as one who lived by his hands.
Like the Kural, it treats tireless work with one's own hands as a mark of honour.
Judaism
“It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you at liberty to neglect it”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:16 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon teaches that no one person completes the great work, but no one may walk away from it either.
The Kural's vow ‘I will not slack my hand’ is the same refusal to neglect the work.
Buddhism
“Ever grows the glory of him who is energetic, mindful and pure in conduct, discerning and self-controlled, righteous and heedful.”
— Dhammapada 24 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha says that renown keeps growing for the person who is energetic and disciplined.
Both texts say the highest honour belongs to the person of unflagging effort.
Jainism
“You have crossed the great ocean; why do you halt so near the shore? make haste to get on the other side; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira urges his disciple Gautama not to slacken now that most of the journey is done, but to press on to the end.
The Jain refrain shares the Kural's refusal to stop before the work is finished, though its goal is liberation, not a family's rise.
Sikhism
“With your hands and feet, do all your work, but let your consciousness remain with the Immaculate Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1376, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 213, Namdev's reply to Trilochan) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok among those of Bhagat Kabir, given as Namdev's reply to Trilochan, the devotee is told to keep doing all his work with his own hands and feet while his mind stays on God. It is a classic statement of the Sikh householder ideal of honest work joined to remembrance.
The Kural honours the person who vows never to rest his hands from the work of raising his family; the salok likewise tells the devotee never to stop working with his hands.
Greek Mythology
“Work is no disgrace: it is idleness which is a disgrace.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days, passage at ll. 293-319 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, sacred-texts.com) [10]
Hesiod tells his brother Perses that honest toil is honourable and only idleness brings shame.
Both poets place honour in the person who keeps working.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius tells himself, when he is reluctant to leave his bed at dawn, that he is rising to do the work of a human being and was not made merely to lie warm under the blankets.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1 [11]
The Stoic emperor treats unflagging effort at one's proper work as the very purpose of a human life.
His self-command matches the Kural's ‘I will not rest my hand.’

Real Life Example

Mary McLeod Bethune opens her school with $1.50
3 October 1904 · Daytona, Florida, United States
Mary McLeod Bethune, the daughter of formerly enslaved parents, opened the Daytona Literary and Industrial Training School for Negro Girls in a rented house with $1.50, five girls and her own son. She raised money tirelessly to keep it going, and within a few years it had grown to some 250 students; it later merged with the Cookman Institute to become Bethune-Cookman.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bethune built a lasting institution for her community from almost nothing through unceasing personal labour.
The kural ranks above all other greatness the vow never to rest one's hands from raising one's people, and Bethune's school embodies that vow.
