Kural 2

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

கற்றதனால் ஆய பயனென்கொல் வாலறிவன் நற்றாள் தொழாஅர் எனின்.
Katradhanaal aaya payan en kol vaal-arivan, natraal thozhaaar enin.

Meaning

What good is all your learning if you do not bow at the feet of God, who knows everything perfectly?

Elementary

Imagine acing every test but never once saying thank you to your teacher. This kural says learning without being humble and thankful toward God doesn’t really count for much.

University

This verse critiques a purely instrumental or accumulative view of education. “Vaal-arivan” (“the one of spotless/pure knowledge”) refers to a transcendent, perfect intelligence — the ground of all true knowing, not a sectarian god. Valluvar’s claim is that erudition divorced from reverence toward that ultimate source is functionally sterile: it produces information without transformation. This is a recurring cross-cultural theme — the distinction between accumulated data and lived, humbling wisdom.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Distinguishes ‘aparā vidyā’ (lower knowledge: the Vedas, grammar, ritual, etc.) from ‘parā vidyā’ (higher knowledge: that by which the imperishable Self is realized).
— Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.12–13 [3]
The text says all ordinary book-learning is ‘lower knowledge’ — genuinely useful, but incomplete — unless it’s joined to the ‘higher knowledge’ that leads to realizing ultimate reality.
Same two-tier idea as the Kural: study by itself is the ‘lower’ half of learning; reverence for the ultimate is what completes it.
Christianity
“Though I... understand all mysteries, and all knowledge... and have not charity, I am nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that even total intellectual mastery is worth nothing without love — love is what gives knowledge its value.
Paul makes the same trade here — knowledge vs. love — as the Kural makes between learning and reverence: one without the other is empty.
Islam
“...it is only those who have knowledge among His servants that fear/revere Allah.”
— Qur'an 35:28, Fatir [5]
This verse ties real knowledge directly to reverence — the more truly someone understands, the more awe it produces in them.
Implies the reverse of the Kural’s claim too: knowledge that produces no reverence hasn’t really arrived yet.
Judaism
“The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding.”
— Proverbs 9:10 (Tanakh, Mishlei / KJV) [4]
This proverb makes reverence for God the starting point of wisdom itself, not an optional add-on to it.
Nearly the Kural’s exact claim in a single line: without reverence, there’s no real wisdom to begin with.
Buddhism
Describes reciting scripture without living by it as being “like a cowherd who counts the cattle of others” — he tends them all day but owns none.
— Dhammapada, verses 19–20 [6]
The Buddha compares someone who has memorized scripture but doesn’t practice it to a hired hand who never actually benefits from the cattle he tends.
Same warning as the Kural: knowledge you haven’t lived out or humbled yourself to isn’t really yours.
Jainism
“Without (right) faith there is no (right) knowledge, without (right) knowledge there is no virtuous conduct, without virtues there is no deliverance, and without deliverance there is no perfection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 28.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
In the Jain 'road to final deliverance', mere learning is not yet right knowledge; it becomes so only when grounded in right faith in the truths the Jinas taught.
Like the Kural, it says learning that is not anchored in reverence for the perfectly-knowing one (for Jains, the omniscient Jina) fails to bear its true fruit.
Sikhism
“You may read and read loads of books; you may read and study vast multitudes of books... O Nanak! Only one thing is of any account: everything else is useless babbling and idle talk in ego.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 467, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak piles up images of endless study (cartloads, boatloads, a lifetime of reading) and then says that only one thing counts, the Name of God. Without that, learning is just ego talking.
Same verdict as the Kural: learning that never bows to the source of wisdom is worth nothing.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus gives his son Icarus the knowledge and means to fly (wax-and-feather wings) along with a clear warning not to fly too high — Icarus ignores it, the wax melts, and he falls into the sea.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book VIII (the myth of Daedalus and Icarus) [9]
Icarus has the same skill his father does, but not the humility to heed the wisdom that was supposed to come with it, and the skill alone destroys him.
A cautionary version of the Kural’s point: ability or knowledge used without humility toward the wisdom that should guide it ends in ruin.
Philosophy
Socrates claims his only advantage over men reputed wise is that he, unlike them, knows that he does not really know.
— Plato, Apology (Socrates' defense) [10]
Socrates’ wisdom isn’t more facts — it’s the humility to recognize the real limits of what he knows.
Matches the Kural’s deeper point: real wisdom starts with humility, not with accumulating more facts.

Real Life Example

Friar Gregor Mendel's pea experiments at St Thomas's Abbey
1856-1863 · Brno (Brunn), Moravia, now Czech Republic
Gregor Mendel, an Augustinian friar at the Abbey of St Thomas in Brno, spent eight growing seasons from 1856 to 1863 breeding and counting pea plants in the abbey garden. His results laid the mathematical foundation of genetics, though their importance was recognised only after his death. The abbey followed the Augustinian motto per scientiam ad sapientiam, from knowledge to wisdom, and its abbot supported Mendel's research.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Mendel's work shows rigorous learning carried out inside a life of religious vows, in a community that treated knowledge as a road to wisdom rather than an end in itself.
The kural asks what learning is worth without reverence for the One of pure wisdom; Mendel's abbey framed scholarship itself as a path from knowledge toward that wisdom.
