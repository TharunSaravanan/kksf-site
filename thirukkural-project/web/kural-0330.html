Kural 330

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

உயிருடம்பின் நீக்கியா ரென்ப செயிருடம்பின் செல்லாத்தீ வாழ்க்கை யவர்.
Uyirudambin neekkiyaa renba seyirudambin Sellaaththee vaazhkkai yavar.

Meaning

People say that those who now live poor, miserable lives in sick bodies are the ones who once took the lives of other beings.

Elementary

It’s like planting seeds: if you plant thorns, you get thorns later. Valluvar says that hurting and killing are like planting thorn seeds — sooner or later, sickness and hard times grow back toward the one who planted them.

University

The final couplet adopts the voice of received wisdom (eṉpa, “they say”): those with diseased bodies (ceyir uṭampu) living a miserable, impoverished life (cellāt tī vāḻkkai) are those who “removed life from bodies” (uyir uṭampiṉ nīkkiyār). All the major commentators read this karmically, as the fruit of killing in a former birth. The couplet closes the chapter by grounding the ethics of non-killing in moral causation; it has parallels in Manu and in the Buddha’s teaching that harming beings leads to sickliness. Modern readers will note that such teaching can be misused to blame the sick and poor — a danger also recognised within other traditions, as when Jesus rejects the idea that a man’s blindness was caused by sin (John 9:3).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“injury (to sentient beings) is punished by general sickliness”
— Manusmriti 11.52 (with 11.48) (G. Bühler trans.) [4]
In its catalogue of the bodily marks of past sins, Manu assigns chronic illness as the consequence of harming living beings, stating earlier that some suffer for crimes “committed in a former (existence).”
Almost identical to the Kural: harming life in a former existence yields a diseased body now.
Christianity
“Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword.”
— Matthew 26:52 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells Peter to sheathe his sword at the arrest, warning that violence returns upon the violent.
Shares the Kural’s principle that those who take life reap suffering in return — though within one lifetime, not across births.
Islam
“And whatever strikes you of disaster - it is for what your hands have earned; but He pardons much.”
— Qur’an 42:30 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an teaches that misfortunes are linked to one’s own deeds, though God forgives much.
Like the Kural, it traces present suffering to past wrongdoing.
Judaism
“because you drowned others, they drowned you. And in the end, they that drowned you will be drowned.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:6 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Hillel, seeing a skull floating on the water, reflects that those who kill are killed in turn.
Expresses the Kural’s logic of measure-for-measure consequence for taking life.
Buddhism
“He who inflicts violence on those who are unarmed, and offends those who are inoffensive, will soon come upon one of these ten states: Sharp pain, or disaster, bodily injury, serious illness, or derangement of mind, … or loss of wealth…”
— Dhammapada 137–140 (Buddharakkhita trans.) [8]
The Dhammapada lists the painful consequences — including illness and poverty — that befall those who harm the harmless.
Nearly the Kural: illness and a ruined life follow from violence against living beings (cf. MN 135 on sickliness).
Jainism
“Carefully conducting himself, he should mind this: blindness, deafness, dumbness, one-eyedness, hunchbackedness, blackness, variety of colour (he will always experience); because of his carelessness he is born in many births, he experiences various feelings.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.2.3.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The Ācārāṅga teaches that bodily afflictions in later births follow from careless conduct, which in Jain terms (pramāda) is the root of injury to living beings.
The Kural traces diseased bodies and wretched lives to past killing; the Ācārāṅga likewise attributes bodily defects in rebirth to careless, injurious conduct.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, they oppress living beings and kill them, and call it proper. When the Lord calls for their account, what will their condition be?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1375, Salok Bhagat Kabir, Salok 199 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir warns that those who kill living beings by force and justify it as lawful must still answer for it when their deeds are reckoned.
The Kural says the wise see wretched, diseased lives as the fruit of having taken life from bodies; Kabir likewise warns that killing is entered in the account and its consequence will come.
Greek Mythology
Tantalus, who in later tradition killed his son Pelops and served him to the gods, is shown by Odysseus in the underworld standing in a pool under fruit trees, forever hungry and thirsty as water and fruit recede from him.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.582–592; cf. Pindar, Olympian 1 [11]
His endless deprivation is the archetypal Greek image of punishment for an abominable killing.
Mirrors the Kural’s “wretched life” of want as the fruit of having taken life.
Philosophy
Plato cites an ancient teaching that the murderer is punished in Hades and, when born again on earth, must suffer the very thing he did, dying at the hands of another.
— Plato, Laws 9.870d–e, 872e–873a [12]
In the Laws, Plato uses this tale of reincarnational justice to deter killing.
Very close to the Kural: killing in one life brings suffering in the next.

Real Life Example

Charles Myers brings shell shock into the medical literature
13 February 1915 · Le Touquet, France, and London, England (published in The Lancet)
Charles Myers, a British psychologist, published in The Lancet an account of three soldiers admitted to a war hospital at Le Touquet who had lost memory, vision, smell and taste. His paper introduced the term shell shock into medical literature; soldiers had already been using it, as large numbers of men were being sent home with nervous and mental breakdown. Shell shock went on to affect very large numbers of soldiers during the First World War.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Myers's work began the medical recognition that war leaves lasting injuries of the mind and body on those who take part in it.
The kural says those who live wretched lives in afflicted bodies are those who once took life, and shell shock showed how the work of killing in war could leave its participants broken.
