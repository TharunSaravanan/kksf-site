Kural 708

The Knowledge of Signals · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

முகம்நோக்கி நிற்க அமையும் அகம்நோக்கி உற்ற துணர்வார்ப் பெறின்.
Mukamnokki nirka amaiyum akamnokki Utradhu unarvaarp perin.

Meaning

If you have people who can look into your heart and understand what has happened to you, it is enough to stand where they can see your face.

Elementary

When you come home after a bad day, a good parent can often tell just by looking at you. You don’t have to explain; you just stand there, and they already know you need a hug. Valluvar says that is what great helpers are like.

University

The couplet balances two ‘lookings’: mukam nokki (looking at the face) and akam nokki (looking into the heart). Nirka amaiyum means ‘it suffices to stand’, and utradhu is ‘what has befallen’ (a trouble or need). Parimelazhagar makes the king the one who stands: if he has ministers able to look into his mind and perceive what has occurred, he need not voice his trouble; standing and looking at their faces is enough, and they will understand and remedy it. Some modern readers (e.g., Kalaignar) generalise it to anyone who can read hearts. The couplet is the practical payoff of 701 and 703: the value of the perceptive minister is that the ruler is spared the need to speak, which in court may itself be dangerous. Variant spelling: ytamizh prints முகம்நோக்கி, the sandhi edition முகநோக்கி நிற்க வமையு மகநோக்கி.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The poor Brahmin Sudama visits his boyhood friend Krishna but is too ashamed to ask for help; Krishna understands his need unspoken and sends him home to find his hut turned into a mansion.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.80-81 [3]
Sudama asks for nothing; his presence is enough for Krishna, who reads his heart.
This is the Kural’s scene exactly: standing before one who reads hearts is enough.
Christianity
“for your Father knoweth what things ye have need of, before ye ask him.”
— Matthew 6:8 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his followers not to pile up words in prayer, since God already knows their needs.
As in the Kural, one who knows the heart makes spoken requests unnecessary.
Islam
“Finally Abu-l-Qasim (the Prophet) passed by me and he smiled when he saw me, for he knew what was in my heart and on my face.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6452 [5]
Abu Hurayrah, starving, hints at his hunger to others who miss it; the Prophet reads it on his face and feeds him.
Abu Hurayrah only had to stand before the right person; his face was enough, as the Kural says.
Judaism
“And it was so, when the king saw Esther the queen standing in the court, that she obtained favour in his sight ... Then said the king unto her: ‘What wilt thou, queen Esther? for whatever thy request, even to the half of the kingdom, it shall be given thee.’”
— Esther 5:2-3 (JPS 1917) [6]
Esther risks her life simply by standing uninvited before the king; seeing her, he understands she has an urgent request before she speaks.
Esther only has to stand before the king’s gaze, and her unspoken purpose is recognised.
Buddhism
After his awakening the Buddha inclines not to teach; Brahma Sahampati, knowing the Buddha’s thought with his own mind, appears before him and begs him to teach.
— Majjhima Nikaya 26, Ariyapariyesana Sutta [7]
The Buddha says nothing aloud; the deity perceives his unspoken inclination and responds.
An unspoken inner state is perceived and answered by one who looks into the mind, as the Kural describes.
Jainism
“Guessing the teacher's thoughts and the purport of his words, one should express one's assent, and execute (what he desires to be done).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.43 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The ideal disciple reads his teacher's thoughts and acts on them, so the teacher does not have to explain what he needs.
The Kural says it is enough to stand before those who read the heart; the Jain verse describes exactly that kind of attendant from the other side.
Sikhism
“You know completely the condition of my mind; who else could I go to tell of it?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 382, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan tells God that the whole condition (birtha) of his mind is already known to Him, so there is no need to explain it to anyone.
The Kural says that before those who look into the heart it is enough simply to stand and be seen; Guru Arjan says the Lord already knows the mind's trouble without being told.
Greek Mythology
“You know it; why tell you what you know well already?”
— Homer, Iliad 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
When Thetis rises from the sea to her weeping son, Achilles says she already knows his grief without being told.
Achilles only has to be seen by one who knows his heart; explanation is unnecessary.
Philosophy
Adam Smith argues that we come to feel what another feels by imaginatively placing ourselves in their situation, so that a sympathetic observer grasps a sufferer’s state from their circumstances and looks.
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments I.i.1 [11]
Sympathy is the faculty that lets one person understand another’s condition without full explanation.
The Kural’s perceptive helper is Smith’s sympathetic spectator at work.

Real Life Example

Jean-Dominique Bauby dictates a memoir by blinking one eye
1995-1997 · A naval hospital near Calais, France
On 8 December 1995, Jean-Dominique Bauby, editor of the French edition of Elle, suffered a stroke and awoke from a coma about 20 days later unable to speak and able to move only his left eyelid. A therapist devised a system in which the alphabet was read aloud and he blinked at the letter he wanted, and with an assistant writing down each letter he dictated his memoir, The Diving Bell and the Butterfly. The book was published just days before he died of pneumonia at the age of 44.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bauby could no longer speak, but the people around him learned to read his eye so well that he could share a whole book of his inner life.
The kural says that if you have people who can look into your heart, it is enough simply to stand before them, and Bauby's helpers understood him through his face and eye alone.
