Kural 256

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

தினற்பொருட்டால் கொல்லாது உலகெனின் யாரும் விலைப்பொருட்டால் ஊன்றருவா ரில்.
Thinarporuttaal kollaathu ulagenin yaarum, Vilaipporuttaal oondraruvaar il.

Meaning

If the world did not kill animals to eat them, no one would kill animals and sell their meat for money.

Elementary

Why does a shop sell something? Because people buy it. If nobody bought meat, nobody would kill animals to sell it. So the person who buys is part of the reason the animal was killed.

University

This is the most analytical couplet of the chapter, and it answers a specific excuse: “I did not kill; I only bought meat already killed.” Valluvar identifies the causal chain — தினற்பொருட்டு (for the sake of eating) generates விலைப்பொருட்டு (for the sake of price) — so that the consumer’s demand is the root cause of the butcher’s act. G. U. Pope’s translation makes the rebuttal explicit (“Who’d kill and sell, I pray, if none came there the flesh to buy?”). The argument is strikingly modern, anticipating demand-side ethics, and directly contests the Theravada allowance of meat not seen, heard or suspected to be killed for oneself; it closely matches Manu 5.51, which counts the buyer among the slayers.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who permits (the slaughter of an animal), he who cuts it up, he who kills it, he who buys or sells (meat), he who cooks it, he who serves it up, and he who eats it, (must all be considered as) the slayers (of the animal).”
— Manusmriti 5.51 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu spreads the guilt of the killing across the whole chain, including buyer, seller and eater.
Exactly the Kural’s point: buying and eating make one a participant in the slaughter.
Christianity
“It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor any thing whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak.”
— Romans 14:21 (KJV) [4]
Paul says it is better to give up meat if eating it harms others.
Like the Kural, Paul holds the eater responsible for the wider effects of his eating — though his concern is fellow believers, not animals.
Islam
“There is no person who kills a small bird or anything larger, for no just reason, but Allah will ask him about it.”
— Sunan an-Nasa'i 4445 (graded Hasan) [5]
Every killing of an animal must have a legitimate purpose, and each will be accounted for before God.
Shares the Kural’s insistence that the purpose behind killing matters morally; Islam accepts eating as a just purpose, the Kural does not.
Judaism
“When the LORD thy God shall enlarge thy border… and thou shalt say, I will eat flesh, because thy soul longeth to eat flesh; thou mayest eat flesh, whatsoever thy soul lusteth after.”
— Deuteronomy 12:20 (KJV) [4]
The Torah frames ordinary meat-eating as a permission granted to human craving, which many rabbinic and later thinkers (e.g., Rav Kook) read as a concession rather than an ideal.
The text itself locates the origin of killing in the eater’s desire — the same causal root the Kural identifies.
Buddhism
“Monks, a lay follower should not engage in five types of business. Which five? Business in weapons, business in human beings, business in meat, business in intoxicants, and business in poison.”
— Vanijja Sutta, AN 5.177 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha forbids lay followers from trading in meat as a livelihood.
Targets the same “killing for price” the Kural names; but the Jivaka Sutta permits monks to eat meat not killed specifically for them, which the Kural’s demand argument implicitly rejects.
Jainism
“A true monk should not accept such food and drink as has been especially prepared for him along with slaughter of living beings.” … “Those who praise the gift, are accessory to the killing of beings.”
— Sutrakritanga I.11.14, I.11.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The Sutrakritanga holds that whoever consumes food or approves of gifts that involve killing shares in the guilt of the killing.
Valluvar answers the buyer who says 'I did not kill' by showing that demand creates the killing; Jain teaching likewise makes the one who accepts or praises slaughtered food an accessory to the slaughter.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, it is tyranny to use force; the Lord shall call you to account.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1375, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir says that any use of force against another living being is oppression, and God will demand an answer for it.
Valluvar refuses to let the buyer of meat shift the blame to the butcher; the nearest Sikh teaching is that everyone who takes part in violence answers for it.
Greek Mythology
At Mecone, Prometheus divided a slaughtered ox so that humans kept the meat and the gods received only bones wrapped in fat; from then on sacrifice fed human appetite while appearing to honour the gods.
— Hesiod, Theogony 535-557 [9]
The myth explains why sacrificial meat ends up on human tables.
It exposes how human appetite drives the killing behind a respectable pretext — as the Kural exposes the buyer’s excuse.
Philosophy
“What a terrible thing it is to look on when the tables of the rich are spread… And it is even more terrible to look on when they are taken away, for more is left than has been eaten. So the beasts died for nothing!”
— Plutarch, Moralia, On the Eating of Flesh I.4 (994F; Loeb trans.) [10]
Plutarch points out that the killing is driven by the appetite and display of diners, who waste much of what was slaughtered.
Like the Kural, he places responsibility for the slaughter on the consumers whose demand causes it.

Real Life Example

CITES bans the international ivory trade
October 1989 · Lausanne, Switzerland
After the 1970s and 1980s, when demand for ivory drove the poaching of hundreds of thousands of African elephants, the parties to the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species met in Lausanne in 1989 and moved the African elephant to Appendix I. This effectively banned international commercial trade in ivory, with the listing taking effect in January 1990. In the years that followed, ivory markets shrank and prices fell, and poaching declined in many areas, though later one-off sales and continuing illegal trade weakened the ban's effect.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The ban showed in practice that cutting off the buyers' market is one of the most effective ways to stop the killing.
The kural argues that no one would kill for money if no one wanted to buy, and the ivory ban put that reasoning into international law.
