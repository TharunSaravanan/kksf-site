Kural 1246

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

கலந்துணர்த்தும் காதலர்க் கண்டாற் புலந்துணராய் பொய்க்காய்வு காய்திஎன் நெஞ்சு.
Kalandhunarththum kaadhalark kandaar pulandhunaraai poikkaaivu kaaidhi-en nenju.

Meaning

My heart, when you see the one you love, who ends our sulking by holding us close, you cannot even stay upset. So the anger you show him now is fake.

Elementary

Imagine pretending to be cross with your best friend, but the moment they come and give you a hug, you start smiling. So you tell yourself, “I’m not really angry, I’m just pretending!”

University

The couplet turns on the Tamil love-poetry concept of ūṭal / pulavi (a lover’s feigned sulking that is meant to be dissolved): the husband is one who kalantu uṇarttum (‘removes the sulk by union’), and when the heart sees him it does not pulantu uṇar (‘sulk and then make up’) at all. Hence its present anger is poykkāyvu (‘false heat, sham anger’, from kāy ‘to burn, be angry’) that it kāyti (‘burns with’). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan follow this reading closely, while Solomon Pappaiah expands it into an exhortation: ‘give up this pretended hatred and go to him’. The couplet anticipates the later chapters on pulavi (131-133). Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (both read kaṇṭāṟ with the sandhi ṟ).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda, Radha, hurt by Krishna’s wandering, holds herself aloof in proud anger (mana), and her friend urges her not to be harsh with Krishna, since her resentment hurts only herself, and to go to him.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Sargas 8-9 (paraphrase) [3]
The poem portrays a lover’s anger as a pose that melts away when faced with the beloved’s return.
The Kural’s heroine admits the same: the anger her heart shows her husband is false and would vanish at the sight of him.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath:”
— Ephesians 4:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul allows that anger arises but insists it must not be held on to.
The Kural’s heroine sees that her anger toward her husband cannot and should not last, for it is not real.
Islam
“Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good;”
— Qur’an 3:134 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who hold back their anger and forgive.
The Kural’s heroine recognises that her anger toward her husband is hollow and gives way at once to love.
Judaism
In the Song of Songs, the woman at first makes excuses not to open the door to her beloved, but her heart is moved for him and she rises to open, only to find he has gone.
— Song of Songs 5:2-6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) (paraphrase) [6]
Her show of reluctance gives way immediately to longing once she senses him near.
The Kural’s heroine likewise knows her reluctance and anger are a pose that her heart cannot keep up when the beloved is near.
Buddhism
“He who checks rising anger as a charioteer checks a rolling chariot, him I call a true charioteer. Others only hold the reins.”
— Dhammapada 222 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha praises the person who can stop anger the moment it arises.
The Kural’s heroine sees that her anger toward her husband is not even worth reining in, for it is false and gives way at the sight of him.
Jainism
“Pleasant things (by themselves) do not cause indifference nor emotions (as anger, &c.); but by either hating or loving them, a man undergoes such a change through delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.101 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching traces both love and anger to the same root of delusion, so that one easily turns into the other.
The Kural’s heroine sees that her anger at her husband is only the other face of her love, and so is not real anger at all.
Sikhism
“The happy soul-bride knows the worth of her Husband Lord. Renouncing pride, she enjoys peace and pleasure.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 793, Raag Soohee, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas says the bride who truly values her Husband lets go of her pride, and so enjoys union with Him.
The Kural's wife tells her heart its anger is false, since it melts the moment the beloved embraces them; Ravidas likewise teaches that the bride who knows her Husband's worth drops her pride and enjoys His company.
Greek Mythology
When Troy fell, Menelaus came upon Helen intending to kill her for her betrayal, but at the sight of her beauty he let his sword fall and took her back.
— Euripides, Andromache c. 627-631; cf. Aristophanes, Lysistrata 155-156 (paraphrase) [10]
In this well-known tradition, the husband’s anger cannot survive the sight of the woman he loves.
The Kural’s heroine knows her anger is equally hollow and would collapse the moment she saw her husband.
Philosophy
Seneca argues that anger is not a natural necessity but a choice of the mind, which can be checked at its first stirring by reason.
— Seneca, De Ira, Book 2 (paraphrase) [11]
For Seneca, anger exists only because the mind assents to it, and so it can be dissolved.
The Kural’s heroine sees through her own anger in just this way: it is a pose her heart has adopted, not something real.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton remarry in Botswana
10 October 1975 · Kasane, near Chobe Game Park, Botswana
Actors Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton married in Montreal in 1964 and divorced in 1974 after stormy years together. About sixteen months later, on 10 October 1975, they secretly remarried in a ceremony performed by a district commissioner at Kasane, near Chobe Game Park in Botswana. The second marriage lasted about nine months.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Their remarriage became the best-known modern example of a couple whose quarrels could not keep them apart.
The kural says the heart cannot even keep up a sulk when it sees the beloved, so its anger is false, and Taylor and Burton's divorce gave way to remarriage as soon as they were together again.
