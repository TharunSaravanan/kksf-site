Kural 1013

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

ஊனைக் குறித்த உயிரெல்லாம் நாண்என்னும் நன்மை குறித்தது சால்பு.
Oonaik kuriththa uyirellaam naanennum nanmai kuriththadhu saalpu.

Meaning

Every living being lives in a body. In the same way, perfect character lives in the good quality called shame.

Elementary

A snail lives in its shell and a turtle lives in its shell; they can't live without them. Valluvar says goodness lives inside shame the same way. If you take away a person's shame, their goodness has no home left.

University

The kural builds an exact parallel. Uyir ellaam oonaik kuriththa (உயிரெல்லாம் ஊனைக் குறித்த): all lives have the body of flesh (ஊன்) as their seat. Saalpu (சால்பு, 'fullness of noble character', the virtue treated in ch. 99, Saanraanmai) has as its seat the nanmai (நன்மை, 'goodness') called naan. The verb kuriththal ('to aim at, to take as one's place') is repeated in both lines. Kesikan presses the image further: as lives never willingly leave their body, saalpu never leaves shame. Kalaignar renders saalpu as maanbu ('dignity'). Pope's ‘perfect virtue ever dwells with shame’ keeps the doubled structure. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…gentleness; modesty; steady determination; vigor; forgiveness; fortitude; cleanliness; and freedom from envy and from the passion for honor – these transcendental qualities, O son of Bharata, belong to godly men endowed with divine nature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna lists hrīḥ, modesty, among the qualities that make up the ‘divine endowment’ (daivī sampad).
For the Gita as for the Kural, modesty is part of the seat of a perfected character.
Christianity
“What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?”
— 1 Corinthians 6:19 (KJV) [4]
Paul pictures the body as the dwelling of the Spirit, and calls believers to purity because of who lives in them.
The verse matches only the Kural's dwelling-place image (a body housing what is highest), not its claim about shame.
Islam
“Faith (Belief) consists of more than sixty branches (i.e. parts). And Haya … is a part of faith.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 9 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet counts haya', the sense of shame and modesty, as an inseparable branch of faith.
As the Kural seats saalpu in shame, the hadith makes shame part of the very body of faith.
Judaism
“When pride cometh, then cometh shame; But with the lowly is wisdom.”
— Proverbs 11:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs holds that wisdom dwells with the tzenu'im, the modest or lowly, while pride ends in disgrace.
Like the Kural, it locates the highest human excellence in modesty.
Buddhism
“Difficult is life for the modest one who always seeks purity, is detached and unassuming, clean in life, and discerning.”
— Dhammapada 245 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Dhammapada lists modesty first among the traits of the one who pursues purity and discernment.
The verse ties modesty to the full set of noble qualities, much as the Kural seats saalpu in shame.
Jainism
“The body is the boat, life is the sailor, and the Circle of Births is the ocean which is crossed by the great sages.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.73 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the dialogue between Kesi and Gautama, the body is the vessel in which the jiva (life) dwells and travels across samsara.
It shares the Kural's first-line image of life housed in flesh, though not the claim that nobility is housed in shame.
Sikhism
“Modesty and righteousness both, O Nanak, are qualities of those who are blessed with true wealth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1287, Raag Malaar, Malaar ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak pairs saram (modesty) with dharam (righteousness), as two qualities that dwell together in those who are truly rich.
The Kural says noble character dwells in shame as life dwells in the body; Guru Nanak likewise treats modesty as the companion and seat of righteousness.
Greek Mythology
Pindar calls Aidos (Shame or Reverence) the daughter of Prometheus (Forethought). She is a goddess who gives men excellence and joy, and when forgetfulness clouds the mind she slips away.
— Pindar, Olympian Ode 7.43-47 [10]
In Greek religious imagination, Aidos is a divine power with whom excellence (arete) comes and goes.
Like the Kural, it treats shame as the place where excellence lives, so that losing it means losing excellence.
Philosophy
Spinoza grants that shame is not a virtue, since it is a kind of pain, but says it is good insofar as it shows that the person feeling it wants to live honourably. In the same way, pain is good insofar as it shows the injured part is not yet putrefied.
— Spinoza, Ethics IV, Proposition 58, Scholium (tr. R.H.M. Elwes) [11]
For Spinoza, shame is a sign that the desire for an honourable life is still present.
Like the Kural, he treats shame as evidence that goodness is still at home in a person.

Real Life Example

JAL's president takes responsibility after the Flight 123 crash
August 1985 · Tokyo, Japan
On 12 August 1985 a Japan Air Lines Boeing 747 crashed into a mountain in Gunma Prefecture, the worst single-aircraft accident in history up to that time. JAL president Yasumoto Takagi apologised in person to the victims' relatives, publicly accepted responsibility for the accident and told Prime Minister Yasuhiro Nakasone that he would resign; a successor was named later that year.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Time noted that Japanese leaders considered it ethically required to leave office when something went disastrously wrong on their watch, even without direct personal fault.
The kural makes shame the very seat of nobility, as the body is the seat of life, and Takagi's response showed a leader's standing resting on his readiness to feel and answer for a disgrace.
