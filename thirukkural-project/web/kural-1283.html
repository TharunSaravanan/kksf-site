Kural 1283

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

பேணாது பெட்பவே செய்யினும் கொண்கனைக் காணா தமையல கண்.
Paenaadhu petpavae seyyinum konkanaik kaanaa thamaiyala kan.

Meaning

Even if my husband ignores me and does only what he likes, my eyes cannot rest without seeing him.

Elementary

Sometimes a friend ignores you, and you feel hurt, but you still keep looking out for them at the window. The wife in this poem feels the same: even when her husband seems careless, her eyes keep searching for him.

University

Pēṇātu means ‘without caring for (me)’, ‘neglecting’, and peṭpavē ceyyiṉum ‘even if he does only what he desires’; koṇkaṉ (‘husband, the one who took me’) is the object of kāṇātu (‘without seeing’), and amaiyala kaṇ ‘the eyes do not rest/settle’. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses ‘my eyes do not settle without seeing my lover’, Puliyur Kesikan ‘my eyes find no peace’, and Kalaignar ‘though he does not embrace me and does what pleases him, my eyes are not at peace without seeing him’. The couplet shows the heroine’s will to resent overruled by her eyes’ longing, a motif that links to chapter 118 (kaṇvitumpaḻital, the eyes’ eager suffering). Pope’s prose explanation (‘Though my eyes disregard me and do what is pleasing to my husband’) takes the eyes as the subject of the first clause, a construal not followed by the modern commentators cited here. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the ‘song to the bumblebee’, a gopi rebukes Krishna’s messenger, calling Krishna fickle and ungrateful for leaving them, yet confesses that the women of Vraja cannot stop speaking and thinking of him.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47 (Bhramara Gita) (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The gopis feel slighted by Krishna’s departure to Mathura but are unable to turn their minds away from him.
Like the wife in the Kural, they are neglected by the beloved yet cannot rest without him.
Christianity
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Solomon 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song cannot rest at night for longing to find her absent beloved.
As in the Kural, the lover’s restlessness ends only when the beloved is seen.
Islam
“And women in the city said, ‘The wife of al-'Azeez is seeking to seduce her slave boy; he has impassioned her with love. Indeed, we see her [to be] in clear error.’”
— Qur’an 12:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the wife of al-Aziz consumed by love for Joseph even though he refuses her.
Her love persists despite being spurned, as the Kural’s wife longs for a husband who slights her, though the Qur’anic context is an unlawful passion it criticises.
Judaism
“And if I say: ‘I will not make mention of Him, nor speak any more in His name’, then there is in my heart as it were a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I weary myself to hold it in, but cannot.”
— Jeremiah 20:9 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Jeremiah, mistreated for his calling, resolves to give it up but finds the fire in his heart too strong to contain.
The Kural’s wife likewise cannot hold back her longing for one who has slighted her.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada names not seeing the beloved as a specific form of suffering.
The Kural’s eyes that cannot rest without seeing the husband are exactly this pain, though the Buddha’s remedy is detachment rather than reunion.
Jainism
“How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana describes the eye’s attachment to what it sees as a craving that is never satisfied.
The Kural’s eyes that cannot rest without seeing the husband show the same restless craving of sight, though the Jain text diagnoses it as bondage.
Sikhism
“Even if You cut my body apart, I shall not pull my limbs away from You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 484, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir declares that whatever the Lord does to him, even cutting his body apart, he will not turn away.
The wife in the Kural says that even if her husband neglects her and does as he pleases, her eyes cannot rest without seeing him; Kabir likewise vows a love that does not turn away whatever the beloved does.
Greek Mythology
The nymph Echo, spurned by Narcissus, still follows him and pines for him, wasting away until only her voice remains.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.359-401 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Echo keeps loving a youth who scorns her and cannot tear herself away from him.
Like the Kural’s wife, Echo is slighted by the one she loves yet cannot rest away from him.
Philosophy
Aristophanes, in Plato’s Symposium, tells how lovers are halves of a once-whole being; when they find each other they do not wish to be parted even for a moment, and cannot say what they want except to be together.
— Plato, Symposium 192b-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato’s myth explains love as an irresistible longing for one’s missing half.
The Kural’s eyes that will not rest until they see the husband reflect that longing for the missing half.

Real Life Example

Sofya Tolstaya at the window of the stationmaster’s house at Astapovo
November 1910 · Astapovo railway station, Ryazan province, Russia
In late October 1910 the 82-year-old Leo Tolstoy secretly left his estate at Yasnaya Polyana, leaving behind his wife Sofya, to whom he had been married for 48 years. He fell ill with pneumonia on the journey and lay dying in the stationmaster’s house at the small railway station of Astapovo. Sofya rushed there, but in keeping with his wishes his followers kept her out; she paced the platform and tried to look in through the windows, watched by photographers and film crews, and was allowed to his side only at the very end.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Though her husband had walked out on her without a word of farewell, Sofya could not stay away and could not stop trying to see him.
The kural’s wife says that even if her husband slights her and does as he pleases, her eyes cannot rest without seeing him, which is almost exactly what the world watched Sofya Tolstaya do at Astapovo.
