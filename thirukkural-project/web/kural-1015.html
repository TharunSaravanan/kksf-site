Kural 1015

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

பிறர்பழியும் தம்பழியும் நாணுவார் நாணுக்கு உறைபதி என்னும் உலகு.
Pirarpazhiyum thampazhiyum naanuvaar naanukku uraipadhi ennum ulagu.

Meaning

People who feel ashamed of other people's wrongs as much as their own are the ones the world calls the true home of shame.

Elementary

When your friend gets caught being unkind, do you just laugh? Or do you feel a little hot in the face, as if it were you? Valluvar says the people who feel ashamed even when someone else does wrong are the ones whose hearts are truly good.

University

Pirar pazhi (பிறர்பழி, 'the blame or disgrace of others') and tham pazhi (தம்பழி, 'one's own disgrace') are set side by side. The one who naanuvaar (நாணுவார், 'feels shame') at both is called by the world naanukku uraipadhi (நாணுக்கு உறைபதி, 'the dwelling-place of shame itself'). Kesikan stresses that such a person weighs the two kinds of blame equally. Pappaiah reads ‘not only one's own but also others' blame’. Pope's couplet, ‘Who feel ashamed for others' guilt as for their own’, follows the same line. The kural extends shame from a private brake to a shared moral sensitivity, which fits the Kudiyiyal's concern with the honour of family and community. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the dice-hall of the Mahabharata, while elders like Bhishma and Drona stay silent as Draupadi is humiliated, the young Kaurava prince Vikarna rises alone. He is stung by the shame the assembly is bringing on itself, and he protests that the wrong being done must be named.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Dyuta sub-parva), Vikarna's protest [3]
Vikarna feels the disgrace of his own kin and elders as if it were his own, and cannot keep silent.
He is the Kural's naanukku uraipadhi: ashamed of others' wrongdoing as much as of his own.
Christianity
“Who is weak, and I am not weak? who is offended, and I burn not?”
— 2 Corinthians 11:29 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that when any believer is made to stumble, he himself burns with distress.
Like the Kural's ideal, Paul feels another's moral fall as his own.
Islam
“You see the believers as regards their being merciful among themselves and showing love among themselves and being kind, resembling one body, so that, if any part of the body is not well then the whole body shares the sleeplessness (insomnia) and fever with it.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6011 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet describes the community as one body, so that one member's affliction is felt by all.
This solidarity underlies the Kural's ideal of feeling another's disgrace as one's own.
Judaism
“O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift up my face to Thee, my God; for our iniquities are increased over our head, and our guiltiness is grown up unto the heavens.”
— Ezra 9:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Ezra, who had not himself transgressed, tears his garments and confesses the people's sin as ‘our iniquities’, blushing before God.
This is the Kural's ideal exactly: an innocent man ashamed of others' guilt as if it were his own.
Buddhism
“Easily seen is the fault of others, but one's own fault is difficult to see. Like chaff one winnows another's faults, but hides one's own, even as a crafty fowler hides behind sham branches.”
— Dhammapada 252 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha criticises the habit of airing others' faults while hiding one's own.
The Kural's noble person does the opposite: he weighs others' blame and his own alike and feels both.
Jainism
Umasvati teaches four attitudes to cultivate: friendliness (maitri) toward all living beings, joy (pramoda) in the virtuous, compassion (karunya) for those in distress, and equanimity (madhyasthya) toward the ill-behaved.
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.6 (Svetambara numbering; 7.11 in Digambara numbering), tr. Nathmal Tatia [8]
These four reflections widen the Jain practitioner's moral feeling to include the condition of others.
They share the Kural's outward-turned sensitivity, though Jainism asks for compassion and equanimity toward others' faults, not shame at them.
Sikhism
“No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes the state reached in the company of the holy, in which no one is felt to be other than oneself.
The Kural praises those who feel another's disgrace as keenly as their own; Guru Arjan's refusal to count anyone a stranger is the outlook from which such shared shame grows.
Greek Mythology
When Paris shrinks back from facing Menelaus, his brother Hector rebukes him bitterly. Paris's cowardice is a disgrace to their father, their city and all their people, and Hector feels that disgrace keenly as his own family's.
— Homer, Iliad 3.38-57 [10]
Hector cannot treat his brother's shame as someone else's business; it stains the whole house.
Like the Kural's ideal, Hector feels his kinsman's disgrace as his own.
Philosophy
Adam Smith observes that we blush for the impudence and rudeness of another person even when he himself seems to have no sense of how improperly he is behaving.
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments I.i.1.10 (1759) [11]
Smith's account of sympathy explains how we come to feel a shame that properly belongs to someone else.
This is the Kural's ‘ashamed of others' blame as of one's own’, explained through the psychology of sympathy.

Real Life Example

Gandhi calls off his campaign after the violence at Chauri Chaura
February 1922 · Bardoli, Gujarat, India
On 4 February 1922 a crowd of protesters in Chauri Chaura, in the United Provinces, set fire to a police station and 22 policemen died. Although Gandhi was far away and had not ordered it, he called the episode a sign that the country was not ready, suspended the mass civil disobedience he had planned to launch from Bardoli, and fasted as a penance; Britannica records that he described his own launch of the campaign as a "Himalayan blunder."
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gandhi treated a wrong done by others in the name of his movement as his own fault, to the dismay of many colleagues who wanted the campaign to continue.
The kural praises those who feel ashamed of others' wrongs as of their own, and Gandhi's penance for the deed of a distant crowd is a near-literal example.
