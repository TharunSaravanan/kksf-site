Kural 1049

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

நெருப்பினுள் துஞ்சலும் ஆகும் நிரப்பினுள் யாதொன்றும் கண்பாடு அரிது.
NeruppinuL thunjalum aagum nirappinuL yaadhondrum kaNpaadu aridhu.

Meaning

A person might somehow sleep in the middle of a fire, but in the middle of poverty, they cannot close their eyes at all.

Elementary

Have you ever been so worried that you could not fall asleep? Valluvar says that worrying about having no food or money is so strong that it is even harder to sleep than if you were lying next to a fire.

University

The hyperbole sets two ‘insides’ side by side: neruppinuL (‘within fire’) and nirappinuL (‘within want’). Thunjal (துஞ்சல், ‘sleeping’) is said to be possible (aagum) in the first, while kaNpaadu (கண்பாடு, ‘the closing of the eyes, sleep’) is aridhu (‘rare, hard’) in the second, yaadhondrum (‘by any means at all’). Puliyur Kesikan explains how sleeping in fire might be possible, through the help of mantra and medicine, and Solomon Pappaiah through yogic power; Pappaiah also narrows nirappu to the torment of hunger. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar keep the image general. The couplet follows naturally from 1048: the dread of tomorrow’s want is what keeps the poor awake. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not disturbed in mind even amidst the threefold miseries or elated when there is happiness, and who is free from attachment, fear and anger, is called a sage of steady mind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna describes the sthita-prajna, whose mind remains calm in every kind of misery.
The Kural observes that want makes rest impossible; the Gita holds up the sage whose peace no misery can disturb.
Christianity
“The sleep of a labouring man is sweet, whether he eat little or much: but the abundance of the rich will not suffer him to sleep.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:12 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet says the labourer sleeps well whatever he eats, while the rich man’s wealth keeps him awake.
Both texts tie sleep to one’s material state, but Ecclesiastes reverses the Kural, giving the sleepless nights to the rich.
Islam
“They were touched by poverty and hardship and were shaken until [even their] messenger and those who believed with him said, ‘When is the help of Allah?’ Unquestionably, the help of Allah is near.”
— Qur’an 2:214 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an recalls earlier believers so shaken by poverty and hardship that even their prophet cried out for help.
The deep agitation that poverty produces, the Kural’s sleeplessness, is acknowledged here before relief is promised.
Judaism
“Master of the universe, I accept upon myself all the suffering in the world, but not poverty, for when I go out to the marketplace and I do not have in my possession a peruta with which to purchase, what will I eat?”
— Shemot Rabbah 31:12 (The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah, 2022) [6]
In the Midrash, Job, offered a choice, prefers every other suffering to poverty because of the gnawing question of what he will eat.
Job’s preference for any suffering over the anxiety of want parallels the Kural’s preference for sleeping in fire over lying awake in poverty.
Buddhism
“Yes, young man. I have slept in ease. Of those in the world who sleep in ease, I am one.”
— Hatthaka Sutta, AN 3.34 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
On a freezing winter night on a thin bed of leaves, the Buddha says he slept well, because passion, aversion and delusion, the real fevers that rob sleep, are gone.
The Kural says want makes sleep impossible; the Buddha shows that the one who has abandoned craving sleeps in ease even in extreme hardship.
Jainism
“Happy are we, happy live we who call nothing our own; when Mithilâ is on fire, nothing is burned that belongs to me.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Told by Indra that his palace is burning, the renouncing king Nami replies that nothing of his can burn, since he owns nothing.
The Kural sets fire against want; Nami shows that for one who has given up possession, neither fire nor want can disturb his peace.
Sikhism
“Fareed, I thought that I was in trouble; the whole world is in trouble! When I climbed the hill and looked around, I saw this fire in each and every home.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 81) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid describes suffering as a fire that he first thought burned only in his own house, until a wider view showed it burning in every home.
The Kural says a man might sleep in the middle of fire but never in the middle of want; Farid likewise pictures human distress as a fire burning inside each household.
Greek Mythology
Tantalus, punished in the underworld, stands in water beneath laden fruit trees, yet the water recedes and the branches lift whenever he reaches for them, so he is never fed and never at rest.
— Homer, Odyssey 11 (Tantalus in the underworld) [10]
His torment is endless want in sight of plenty, a restlessness that never ends.
Tantalus embodies the Kural’s claim that want allows no rest, more unbearable than physical torment.
Philosophy
“For what sleep is possible in a lodging? Who but the wealthy get sleep in Rome?”
— Juvenal, Satire 3 (tr. G.G. Ramsay, 1918) [11]
Juvenal complains that in Rome only the rich can afford quiet enough to sleep.
The satirist arrives at the Kural’s conclusion by a different road: poverty means no sleep.

Real Life Example

George Orwell publishes Down and Out in Paris and London
1933 · Paris, France, and London, England
George Orwell's first book, Down and Out in Paris and London, published in 1933, recounts in lightly fictionalised form his own time living among the poor. In Paris he lived in a slum and worked as a hotel dishwasher, and in London and on the roads of England he lived as a tramp, queuing for a bed in the casual wards and lodging houses where a mug of tea, a piece of bread and a blanket were all that was offered.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The book showed readers from the inside how destitution governs every hour of a poor person's day and night, and it helped change how homelessness was discussed in Britain.
The kural says a person may somehow sleep in fire but never in the midst of want, and Orwell's account shows the destitute spending their nights in anxious search of a place to lie down.
