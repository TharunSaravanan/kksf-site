Kural 439

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

வியவற்க எஞ்ஞான்றும் தன்னை நயவற்க நன்றி பயவா வினை.
Viyavarka enjnaandrum thannai nayavarka Nandri payavaa vinai.

Meaning

Never, at any time, think too highly of yourself, and never want to do things that bring no good.

Elementary

Being impressed with yourself makes you stop noticing your mistakes. This kural gives two simple rules: never be too proud of yourself, and don’t want to do things that don’t do any good.

University

Two prohibitions run in parallel: வியவற்க (do not wonder at, marvel at) with தன்னை (oneself), and நயவற்க (do not desire) with நன்றி பயவா வினை (deeds that yield no good). எஞ்ஞான்றும் (‘at any time’) makes the first absolute: even at the height of success, self-admiration is forbidden. The two are causally linked. Self-admiration blinds a person to their own faults, which undoes the self-scrutiny of Kural 436, and a self-admiring ruler is the one most easily drawn into vain, unprofitable projects. Solomon Pappaiah reads the second clause politically: a ruler should not want to do what does not benefit the country and its governance.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Humility; pridelessness; nonviolence; tolerance; simplicity; approaching a bona fide spiritual master...”
— Bhagavad Gita 13.8-12 (opening) [3]
Krishna lists humility and freedom from pride first among the qualities that make up true knowledge.
The Kural’s ‘never admire yourself’ is the Gita’s humility and pridelessness, placed at the head of the virtues.
Christianity
“Let another man praise thee, and not thine own mouth; a stranger, and not thine own lips.”
— Proverbs 27:2 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that praise should come from others, never from yourself.
Same prohibition as the Kural: never admire or praise yourself.
Islam
“So do not claim yourselves to be pure; He is most knowing of who fears Him.”
— Quran 53:32 (Sahih International, final clause) [5]
The Quran forbids people to declare themselves pure or righteous, since only God truly knows who is God-fearing.
Both forbid self-admiration; the Quran grounds it in the fact that only God sees the heart.
Judaism
“If you have learned much Torah, do not claim credit for yourself, because for such a purpose were you created.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:8 [6]
Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai teaches that even great learning is no reason to admire yourself; you have only done what you were made for.
Like the Kural’s ‘never, at any time’, it forbids self-admiration even when there is real achievement.
Buddhism
“'Let both laymen and monks think that it was done by me. In every work, great and small, let them follow me' — such is the ambition of the fool; thus his desire and pride increase.”
— Dhammapada 74 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes the fool who wants credit and admiration for everything, so his pride and desire keep growing.
Both texts warn that self-admiration feeds itself and spoils one’s aims; the fool’s wish for credit is the opposite of the Kural’s counsel.
Jainism
“'Frequently (I have been born) in a high family, frequently in a low one; I am not mean, nor noble, nor do I desire (social preferment).' Thus reflecting, who would brag about his family or about his glory, or for what should he long?”
— Acaranga Sutra I.2.3.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
The Acaranga undercuts self-admiration by reminding the aspirant that across countless rebirths each soul has been both high and low, so pride in birth or glory has no ground.
Valluvar forbids admiring oneself at any time; the Acaranga gives a distinctly Jain reason for the same rule: through rebirth, every status one might boast of has already been held and lost many times.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself good — goodness shall not draw near him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani Guru Arjan teaches that self-praise is itself a mark of haumai, and that the person who proclaims his own goodness drives goodness away.
The Kural forbids admiring oneself at any time; Guru Arjan states that self-congratulation empties a person of the very virtue he claims.
Greek Mythology
Narcissus, who has scorned all who love him, falls in love with his own reflection in a pool and wastes away gazing at it, unable to turn from himself.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3 (Narcissus and Echo) [10]
A young man who admires only himself is destroyed by that self-admiration.
Narcissus is the extreme image of the Kural’s first warning: self-admiration leads to deeds that yield nothing and finally to ruin.
Philosophy
“The marks of a proficient are, that he censures no one, praises no one, blames no one, accuses no one, says nothing concerning himself as being anybody, or knowing anything: when he is, in any instance, hindered or restrained, he accuses himself...”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 48 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus says a person making real progress never talks about himself as important, and looks for fault in himself first.
Epictetus joins the Kural’s ban on self-admiration with the chapter’s central idea of self-accusation (Kural 436).

Real Life Example

Bokassa crowns himself emperor
December 1977 · Bangui, Central African Republic
Jean-Bedel Bokassa, who had ruled the Central African Republic since 1966, renamed it the Central African Empire and in December 1977 crowned himself emperor in a lavish ceremony. The event cost more than 20 million dollars, financed largely by France, in one of the world's poorest countries. Bokassa was overthrown in September 1979 in a coup backed by France.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The coronation became a byword for a ruler's self-glorification at his people's expense, and it brought him ridicule rather than respect.
The kural forbids a ruler to admire himself or to desire deeds that bring no good, and Bokassa's coronation was both at once.
