Kural 1172

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

தெரிந்துணரா நோக்கிய உண்கண் பரிந்துணராப் பைதல் உழப்பது எவன்.
Therindhunaraa noakkiya un-kan parindhunaraap paidhal uzhappadhu evan.

Meaning

My dark-lined eyes looked at him back then without thinking about what would follow. Why do they suffer now, not seeing that they caused this sadness themselves?

Elementary

Think of someone who eats a whole bag of sweets without thinking, and then complains of a tummy ache as if it came from nowhere. The lady says her eyes did the same: they gazed at him without thinking ahead, and now they cry as if they don’t know why it hurts.

University

The couplet balances two negated verbs of knowing: terintu uṇarā nōkkiya, ‘looked without discerning and understanding’ (then), and parintu uṇarā, ‘not perceiving with sympathy’ or ‘not grasping’ (now). Uṇkaṇ (உண்கண்) is the conventional ‘eye fit to be eaten’, i.e. kohl-lined and lovely, and paital (பைதல்) is grief or affliction; uḻappatu evaṉ asks ‘why do they toil in it?’ Mu. Varadarasanar reads the second clause as the eyes failing to feel affection now; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar read it as the eyes failing to recognise that they brought this on themselves and should bear it. Pope gives: ‘How glancing eyes, that rash unweeting looked that day, / With sorrow measureless are wasting now away!’ No textual variants were found between ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna traces a chain that begins with merely dwelling on sense objects: from that contemplation arises attachment, from attachment desire, from desire anger, and so on down to the loss of discernment and ruin.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62-63 (paraphrase) [3]
The Gita teaches that unreflective attention to an object is the first link in a chain that ends in suffering.
The heroine’s eyes ‘looked without discerning’, and the whole chain of longing and pain followed from that first unweighed glance.
Christianity
“And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat…”
— Genesis 3:6 (KJV) [4]
In Eden, what is ‘pleasant to the eyes’ is taken without weighing the consequence, and sorrow follows.
The Kural’s eyes likewise acted on delight without foresight, and are now left with the grief that followed.
Islam
“Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them.”
— Qur’an 24:30 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an asks believers to restrain their gaze, recognising that unguarded looking leads the heart where it should not go.
The heroine wishes, in effect, that her eyes had been more guarded and discerning when they first looked.
Judaism
“…that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them; and that ye go not about after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go astray.”
— Numbers 15:39 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The fringes (tzitzit) are given so that Israel will not follow wherever the heart and eyes wander.
The Kural’s eyes did just that, going ‘after’ what they saw without considering where it led.
Buddhism
“Seeing your eyes, my sensual delight grows all the more.” — to which the nun Subha replies: “you run blind after what is unreal.”
— Therigatha 14.1, Subha Jivakambavanika (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
A libertine stops the nun Subha and confesses he is captivated by her eyes; she answers that his vision is distorted and he chases a mirage.
The libertine’s undiscerning gaze is the Kural’s ‘looking without understanding’; the Kural’s heroine is now living with what such looking leads to.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of ‘colours,’ will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana compares the person ruled by what the eye sees to a moth that flies into the flame it is drawn to.
The heroine’s eyes flew toward their beloved like the moth, without discernment, and are now burnt by the absence that followed.
Sikhism
“In a dream, He came, and went away again; I cried so many tears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 558, Raag Vadhans, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's soul-bride glimpses the Husband Lord only in a dream, and when He is gone again she weeps.
The Kural's heroine says her eyes once looked at her lover without a thought for what would follow, and now they suffer; Guru Nanak's bride likewise has a fleeting sight of the Beloved and is left in tears.
Greek Mythology
Narcissus falls in love with a reflection in a pool without understanding what he is seeing; Ovid’s narrator tells him that what he seeks is nowhere and that he is deceived by his own eyes.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.415-510 (paraphrase) [10]
Narcissus’s eyes fasten on an image before his mind grasps what it is, and he wastes away from that undiscerning gaze.
Like Narcissus, the Kural’s eyes ‘looked without understanding’ and brought on a pining they cannot escape.
Philosophy
Hume argues that reason alone can never oppose a passion in directing the will, and that reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions.
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 2.3.3 (paraphrase) [11]
On Hume’s view, desires are not first weighed by reason; they move us, and reason only follows.
The heroine’s eyes illustrate Hume’s point: they acted on delight, not on reflection, and reason arrives only afterwards to complain.

Real Life Example

Robert Burns writes Ae Fond Kiss on parting from Agnes McLehose
December 1791 · Scotland, United Kingdom
Robert Burns and Agnes McLehose of Glasgow carried on a passionate but platonic correspondence under the pen names Sylvander and Clarinda, since she was married. In December 1791, as she prepared to sail to Jamaica to try to reconcile with her husband, the two met for the last time, and Burns wrote the song Ae Fond Kiss for her on their parting. Decades later Agnes wrote in her journal that she had parted with Burns in 1791, never to meet again in this world.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The song's famous lament is that the lovers would never have been broken-hearted if they had never met, a reflection on how a love so gladly begun can lead to grief.
The kural blames the eyes that once gazed at the beloved without weighing what would follow and now suffer for it, which is the same thought Burns put into his farewell song.
