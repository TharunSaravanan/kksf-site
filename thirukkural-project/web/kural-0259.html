Kural 259

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

அவிசொரிந் தாயிரம் வேட்டலின் ஒன்றன் உயிர்செகுத் துண்ணாமை நன்று.
Avisorindh thaayiram vettalin ondran, Uyirsekuth thunnaamai nandru.

Meaning

Not killing a single creature to eat it is better than making a thousand offerings by pouring ghee (melted butter) into a holy fire.

Elementary

Some people think doing big, fancy religious ceremonies makes them very good. The Kural says: sparing just one animal’s life, and not eating it, is better than a thousand of those ceremonies.

University

This couplet makes a pointed comparison with Vedic ritual: அவி (havis, oblations such as ghee) poured into the fire in a thousand வேள்வி (yajñas, sacrifices). By ranking the simple restraint of not killing one creature above a thousand sacrifices, Valluvar subordinates ritual merit to ethical conduct — a critique shared with the Jain and Buddhist movements and with the later Upanishadic interiorisation of sacrifice. The numerical contrast (ஆயிரம் versus ஒன்றன் — “a thousand” versus “one”) is characteristic of Valluvar’s rhetoric of disproportion. Manu 5.53 offers a strikingly similar valuation, equating lifelong abstention from meat with a century of horse-sacrifices, while the Hebrew prophets and Jesus prefer “mercy, and not sacrifice.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who during a hundred years annually offers a horse-sacrifice, and he who entirely abstains from meat, obtain the same reward for their meritorious (conduct).”
— Manusmriti 5.53 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu equates lifelong abstinence from meat with a hundred years of the grandest Vedic sacrifice.
Almost the same comparison as the Kural: abstention from flesh outweighs vast ritual offerings.
Christianity
“But go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice”
— Matthew 9:13 (KJV) [4]
Jesus quotes Hosea to say God values mercy over ritual offerings.
Same ranking as the Kural: compassion outweighs sacrifice.
Islam
“Their meat will not reach Allah, nor will their blood, but what reaches Him is piety from you.”
— Quran 22:37 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran says God receives none of the sacrificial animal’s meat or blood — only the worshipper’s inner devotion.
Like the Kural, it declares that the outward offering is worthless compared with the inner moral state.
Judaism
“For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings.”
— Hosea 6:6 (KJV) [4]
Hosea says God prefers loving-kindness (ḥesed) to animal offerings.
The same prophetic ranking of compassion above ritual that the Kural expresses.
Buddhism
In the Kutadanta Sutta, the Buddha tells a Brahmin of a great ancient sacrifice in which no oxen, goats, fowl or pigs were slain and no trees cut; only ghee, oil, butter, curds and honey were offered — and then teaches that giving, virtue and the precepts are still greater sacrifices.
— Kutadanta Sutta, Digha Nikaya 5 [6]
The Buddha redefines sacrifice as bloodless and ultimately ethical.
Like the Kural, it ranks not killing above ritual offerings.
Jainism
“You (use) Kusa-grass, sacrificial poles, straw and wood, you touch water in the evening and in the morning; thereby you injure living beings, and in your ignorance you commit sins again and again.” … “Penance is my fire; life my fireplace; right exertion is my sacrificial ladle; the body the dried cowdung; Karman is my fuel; self-control, right exertion, and tranquillity are the oblations, praised by the sages, which I offer.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.39, 12.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The outcaste-born monk Harikesa tells Brahmin sacrificers that their fire rites injure living beings, and redefines true sacrifice as inner discipline whose first rule is 'doing no injury to living beings' (12.41).
Valluvar ranks not killing a single creature above a thousand fire sacrifices; Harikesa likewise sets non-injury and inner penance above, and against, the Vedic fire offering.
Sikhism
“You kill living beings, and call it a righteous action. Tell me, brother, what would you call an unrighteous action?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1103, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Raag Maaroo Kabir asks a priest who kills animals in sacrificial rites how he can call that dharma, and what then he would call sin.
Valluvar ranks not killing a single creature above a thousand fire-sacrifices; Kabir likewise rejects killing done in the name of sacred rites.
Greek Mythology
“It is not enough to have committed such wickedness: they involve the gods in crime, and believe that the gods above delight in the slaughter of suffering oxen!”
— Ovid, Metamorphoses XV (trans. A. S. Kline) [9]
Ovid’s Pythagoras attacks animal sacrifice as dragging the gods into human cruelty.
Shares the Kural’s rejection of the idea that killing for ritual can be meritorious.
Philosophy
“And the altar did not reek with pure bull's blood, but this was held in the greatest abomination among men, to eat the goodly limbs after tearing out the life.”
— Empedocles, fr. 128 DK (trans. J. Burnet) [10]
Empedocles describes a golden age when worship was bloodless and killing to eat was the worst abomination.
Joins sacrifice and meat-eating in one condemnation, as the Kural does.

Real Life Example

Ashoka's First Rock Edict forbids animal sacrifice
3rd century BCE · Mauryan Empire (Girnar and other edict sites), India
In the first of his Major Rock Edicts, the Mauryan emperor Ashoka proclaimed that in his domain no living beings were to be slaughtered or offered in sacrifice. The same edict recorded that his royal kitchen, where many thousands of animals had once been killed for food, now killed only two peacocks and a deer, and the deer not always, and that in time even these would not be killed.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A great king publicly placed sparing animal life above the sacrificial rites that were then a mark of royal piety.
The kural ranks not killing one creature above a thousand fire sacrifices, and Ashoka's edict set aside sacrifice itself in favour of not killing.
