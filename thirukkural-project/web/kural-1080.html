Kural 1080

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

எற்றிற் குரியர் கயவரொன்று உற்றக்கால் விற்றற்கு உரியர் விரைந்து.
Etrir kuriyar kayavarondru utrakkaal vitrarku uriyar viraindhu.

Meaning

What are mean people good for? When trouble comes, they rush to sell themselves, and that is all they are fit for.

Elementary

Imagine a team captain who, the moment the game gets hard, switches to the other team because they promise him a prize. He cannot be trusted. The Kural says base people are like that: at the first sign of trouble, they will sell out anyone, even themselves.

University

The chapter, and the whole Porutpal, ends with a rhetorical question: etrirku uriyar (எற்றிற்கு உரியர், 'for what are they fit?'). The answer: ondru utrakkaal (ஒன்று உற்றக்கால், 'when some calamity comes') they are fit vitrarku (விற்றற்கு, 'for selling', i.e. selling themselves) viraindhu (விரைந்து, 'quickly'). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read it as the base hastening to sell themselves to others to escape a misfortune; Puliyur Kesikan adds that they are fit for no other work; Solomon Pappaiah extends it to profit or loss, saying they make themselves others' slaves. The final sarcasm returns to the first couplet: the base have the human form but none of the steadfastness that gives a person worth. Pope: 'For what is base man fit, if griefs assail? / Himself to offer, there and then, for sale!' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, to keep his promised word to the sage Vishvamitra, sold his wife, his son and finally himself into servitude, enduring every hardship rather than break faith.
— Markandeya Purana, Harishchandra episode (tr. F. E. Pargiter) [3]
Harishchandra's self-sale is the noble opposite of the Kural's: he sells himself to keep his truth, not to escape trouble.
The contrast sharpens the Kural's point: the base sell themselves to save their skins, while the noble suffer anything to keep faith.
Christianity
“And said unto them, What will ye give me, and I will deliver him unto you? And they covenanted with him for thirty pieces of silver.”
— Matthew 26:15 (KJV) [4]
Judas goes to the chief priests and offers to betray Jesus for money.
Judas is the Kural's base man, ready to sell himself and his loyalty for a price.
Islam
“Indeed, those who exchange the covenant of Allāh and their [own] oaths for a small price will have no share in the Hereafter”
— Qur'an 3:77 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an condemns those who sell their solemn word for a little worldly gain.
Like the Kural's base, they are quick to sell what should never be for sale.
Judaism
“And Esau said: ‘Behold, I am at the point to die; and what profit shall the birthright do to me?’ And Jacob said: ‘Swear to me first’; and he swore unto him; and he sold his birthright unto Jacob.”
— Genesis 25:32-33 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Faint with hunger, Esau sells his birthright on the spot for a bowl of lentils.
Esau is the Kural's base man exactly: at the first hardship he hastens to sell what is most precious.
Buddhism
“The good renounce (attachment for) everything. The virtuous do not prattle with a yearning for pleasures. The wise show no elation or depression when touched by happiness or sorrow.”
— Dhammapada 83 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says the wise stay steady whether fortune or misfortune comes.
The Kural's base man is the opposite: the first trouble knocks him off balance and he sells himself.
Jainism
“If any misfortune happens and he suffers pain, he should cheerfully steady his mind, and bear the ills that attack him.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the lecture on the twenty-two hardships (parishaha), Mahavira teaches the monk to meet misfortune with a steady mind and not be defeated by it.
This is the opposite of the Kural's base man, who gives himself away the moment trouble comes.
Sikhism
“If you desire to play this game of love with Me, then step onto My Path with your head in hand.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaran te Vadheek, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok, Guru Nanak says the path of love for God is open only to someone ready to give his life. Sikhs read this as the root of the Gurus' readiness for martyrdom.
The Kural says the base are good only for selling themselves the moment trouble comes; Guru Nanak's ideal is the opposite, a person who comes with head in hand and will not sell out at any price.
Greek Mythology
At Thermopylae, the Malian Ephialtes went to Xerxes hoping for a large reward and showed the Persians the mountain path around the pass, betraying the Greeks who held it.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.213 (tr. A. D. Godley) [10]
Ephialtes sold out his own side for gain at the decisive moment, and his name became a byword for traitor.
Ephialtes is the Kural's base man, ready to sell himself and others when the crisis comes.
Philosophy
“it is you who know yourself, how much you are worth to yourself, and at what price you sell yourself; for men sell themselves at various prices.”
— Epictetus, Discourses 1.2 (tr. George Long) [11]
Epictetus says each person decides how cheaply he will sell his integrity, and the Stoic refuses to sell it at all.
The Kural's base man is the one whose price is lowest: he sells himself at the first trouble.

Real Life Example

Alcibiades flees to Sparta
415 BCE · Athens and Sparta, Greece
The Athenian general Alcibiades, a commander of the expedition against Syracuse, was recalled to face charges of sacrilege that carried the death penalty. Instead of returning, he escaped and went over to Athens' enemy Sparta. There he advised the Spartans to send help to Syracuse and to fortify Decelea in Athenian territory, which did serious harm to Athens.
— Sources: [12] [13]
His switch of sides became one of the most famous betrayals in ancient history and contributed to Athens' defeat in the Peloponnesian War.
The kural says that when trouble comes, the base hurry to sell themselves; facing a death sentence, Alcibiades quickly offered his skills to his city's enemy.
