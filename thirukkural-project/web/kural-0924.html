Kural 924

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

நாண்என்னும் நல்லாள் புறங்கொடுக்கும் கள்ளென்னும் பேணாப் பெருங்குற்றத் தார்க்கு.
Naanennum nallaal purangodukkum kallennum penaap perungutrath thaarkku.

Meaning

The good lady called Shame (the feeling that stops us from doing wrong) turns her back on those who commit the great wrong of drinking, a wrong no one should welcome.

Elementary

Picture Modesty as a kind lady who walks beside you and whispers ‘don't do that, it's embarrassing.’ The Kural says that when someone gets drunk she turns around and walks away, so there's no one left to stop him doing shameful things.

University

Naan (நாண், modesty, the sense of shame) is personified as a nallaal (நல்லாள், a good woman). Purangodukkum (புறங்கொடுக்கும்), literally 'gives the back', is a battlefield term for retreat, so Modesty flees from the drinker as a beaten warrior flees. The parallel compounds naanennum nallaal / kallennum penaap perungutram (கள்ளென்னும் பேணாப் பெருங்குற்றம், 'the great fault called toddy, which none would cherish') set a personified virtue against a personified vice. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses that she will not stand before the drinker; Solomon Pappaiah renders it as Modesty showing her back and going away. The idea looks ahead to Naanudaimai (chapter 102). The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Manusmriti counts drinking sura among the mahapatakas, the great sins, together with killing a Brahmin, stealing gold and violating the teacher's bed, and prescribes severe penances for it.
— Manusmriti, Chapter 11 (Bühler trans.) [3]
Dharma law ranks drinking among the gravest offences a twice-born person can commit.
The Kural's perungutram, ‘great fault’, has a formal counterpart in Manu's ‘great sin’.
Christianity
“Let us walk honestly, as in the day; not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying.”
— Romans 13:13 (KJV) [4]
Paul sets decent, honest conduct against drunkenness and the shameless behaviour that comes with it.
In both texts drunkenness is where decency and modesty give way.
Islam
“An adulterer, at the time he is committing illegal sexual intercourse is not a believer; and a person, at the time of drinking an alcoholic drink is not a believer; …”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5578 [5]
At the moment of drinking, faith is said to leave the drinker.
As the Kural's Modesty turns her back, faith (which other hadith pair with modesty) steps away from the drinker.
Judaism
“You shall be sated with shame Rather than glory: Drink in your turn and stagger! The cup in GOD's right hand Shall come around to you, And disgrace to your glory.”
— Habakkuk 2:16 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Habakkuk pronounces that the one who makes others drunk will himself drink and stagger into disgrace.
Drink brings disgrace in place of dignity, the same exchange the Kural describes.
Buddhism
“There are, young householder, these six evil consequences in indulging in intoxicants which cause infatuation and heedlessness: (i) loss of wealth, (ii) increase of quarrels, (iii) susceptibility to disease, (iv) earning an evil reputation, (v) shameless exposure of body, (vi) weakening of intellect.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (Narada Thera trans.) [7]
The Buddha lists shamelessness among the direct results of intoxicants.
‘Shameless exposure’ is the Buddhist way of saying Modesty has left the drinker.
Jainism
Hemacandra's list of the thirty-five qualities of an ideal Jain householder includes salajja, being moved by a sense of shame, alongside such virtues as fearing sin and mastering the senses, while abstinence from wine is counted among the householder's eight root virtues.
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of thirty-five śrāvaka-guṇas (no. 30, salajja) and the eight mūlaguṇas, as summarised in V.A. Sangave, 'Prescription of Ethical Code' (jainworld.com) [8]
Jain lay ethics treats a living sense of shame (lajjā) as a mark of the good householder, and renouncing wine as one of his basic vows.
Valluvar pictures Modesty as a noble woman who turns her back on the drinker; in the Jain householder's code, shame and abstinence from wine likewise go together as signs of the same upright character.
Sikhism
“Make virtue your bread, good conduct the ghee, and modesty the meat to eat.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 553, Raag Bihaagraa, Bihaagre ki Vaar, Salok (Mardana 1), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Having pictured desire as wine and the mind as a drunkard, Guru Nanak offers a different meal: virtue, good conduct and modesty (saram). The Gurmukh who lives on these is cleansed of sin.
The Kural says the lady Modesty turns her back on the drinker, and Guru Nanak puts modesty at the very place where the drinker puts his wine.
Greek Mythology
“When the wine had got into his head he went mad and did ill deeds about the house of Peirithous; this angered the heroes who were there assembled, …”
— Homer, Odyssey 21 (Butler trans.) [10]
The centaur Eurytion, drunk at a wedding feast, behaves disgracefully in his host's house and is punished.
Once wine took hold, all restraint and sense of shame left Eurytion, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“Drunkenness kindles and discloses every kind of vice, and removes the sense of shame that veils our evil undertakings. For more men abstain from forbidden actions because they are ashamed of sinning than because their inclinations are good.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 83.19 (Gummere trans.) [11]
Seneca says drink takes away the shame that usually keeps people from wrongdoing.
This is almost exactly the Kural's picture of Modesty leaving the drinker.

Real Life Example

Andrew Johnson's intoxicated vice-presidential inaugural speech
4 March 1865 · Senate Chamber, United States Capitol, Washington, D.C., United States
Before being sworn in as vice president, Andrew Johnson, who had spent the previous evening drinking and reportedly took three glasses of whiskey that morning against fatigue and a cold, rose to address a Senate Chamber filled with senators, justices, diplomats and President Lincoln. He rambled for about twenty minutes about his humble origins, ignored outgoing vice president Hannibal Hamlin's tugs on his coat, and was too disoriented to swear in the new senators, a task a clerk completed. Senators were mortified, reporters wired the story across the country, and both Massachusetts senators introduced a resolution calling for his resignation.
— Sources: [12] [13]
In one morning of drink a man about to stand next in line for the presidency publicly shamed himself before the nation's leaders.
The kural says the noble lady Modesty turns her back on those who drink, and on that morning Johnson's sense of shame deserted him in front of the whole Senate.
