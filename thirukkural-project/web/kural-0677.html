Kural 677

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

செய்வினை செய்வான் செயன்முறை அவ்வினை உள்ளறிவான் உள்ளம் கொளல்.
Seyvinai seyvaan seyanmurai avvinai Ullarivaan ullam kolal.

Meaning

The right way to do a task is to find out what someone who knows that task well thinks about it.

Elementary

If you want to learn to bake bread, the best first step is to ask a baker who has done it a thousand times. They know the little secrets no recipe tells you.

University

Ullarivaan means 'one who knows the inside' of the work: its hidden difficulties, tricks and secrets. Ullam kolal means 'to take (on) his mind or view'. Parimelazhagar glosses the knower as someone who has already carried out such an undertaking and knows its secret (marmam), and Solomon Pappaiah likewise reads 'one who has done this work before'. The sound pattern (seyvinai seyvaan seyanmurai; ullarivaan ullam) is deliberate and makes the couplet easy to remember. The older sandhi text reads செயன்முறை யவ்வினை / யுள்ளறிவா னுள்ளங் கொளல். The kural extends the earlier chapter on seeking the help of the great (periyaaraith thunaikkodal, ch. 45) to the practical level of expertise.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Learn the Truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him with reverence and render service unto him. Such an enlightened Saint can impart knowledge unto you because he has seen the Truth.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that real knowledge comes from someone who has seen the truth directly.
The Gita's tattva-darshin, one who has seen the reality, is the spiritual counterpart of the Kural's ullarivaan, one who knows a task from within.
Christianity
“And Philip ran thither to him, and heard him read the prophet Esaias, and said, Understandest thou what thou readest? And he said, How can I, except some man should guide me?”
— Acts 8:30-31 (KJV) [4]
The Ethiopian official admits he cannot understand the scripture alone and invites Philip to explain it.
Like the Kural, the story holds that a task is best approached by seeking out someone who understands it from inside.
Islam
“So ask the people of the message if you do not know.”
— Quran 16:43 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran tells those who lack knowledge to ask those who have it.
This is the Kural's method in one line: whoever does not know, should ask the one who does.
Judaism
Jethro watches Moses judging the people alone from morning to evening, tells him the method is not good and will wear him out, and advises him to appoint capable officers over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens; Moses follows the advice.
— Exodus 18:13-24 [4]
An experienced outsider who understands the work of governing shows Moses a better method.
Moses does what the Kural recommends: he adopts the view of someone who understands how the work is best done.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure.”
— Dhammapada 76 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha compares a wise adviser to a guide who knows where treasure is buried.
The guide to hidden treasure is the Kural's ullarivaan, the one who knows what is hidden inside a task.
Jainism
“Guessing the teacher's thoughts and the purport of his words, one should express one's assent, and execute (what he desires to be done).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.43 (cf. 1.46) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The lecture on discipline teaches the pupil to grasp the mind of the experienced teacher and act upon it, and promises that satisfied teachers will hand on their weighty knowledge.
The kural's method of learning the mind of one who knows the work from inside is closely paralleled by this Jain rule of taking on the teacher's intention before acting.
Sikhism
“Go and ask the happy, pure soul-brides, how did they obtain their Husband Lord?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 722, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak tells the seeker to learn the way from those who have already reached the goal, rather than relying on cleverness of one's own.
The Kural says the right method is to take the mind of one who knows the task from the inside, having done it before; Guru Nanak sends the seeker to those who have already succeeded.
Greek Mythology
Circe tells Odysseus that before he can find his way home he must go to the house of Hades and consult the seer Tiresias, who alone can tell him the route and the measure of his journey.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.488-495 [9]
Odysseus makes the terrible journey because only Tiresias knows the path from within.
Even the resourceful Odysseus follows the Kural's method, seeking out the one who truly knows the task before attempting it.
Philosophy
Socrates asks Crito whether a man in athletic training should heed the praise and blame of everyone, or only of the one man who is his doctor or trainer; Crito agrees it is the expert alone.
— Plato, Crito 47a-c [10]
Socrates uses this to argue that in any matter one should follow the person who has real knowledge of it, not the crowd.
Socrates and the Kural agree that the proper way to act is to take the view of the one who knows the work.

Real Life Example

The Iwakura Mission studies Western institutions at first hand
1871-1873 · United States and Europe
In 1871 Japan's new Meiji government sent a large delegation led by Iwakura Tomomi, with senior leaders including Kido Takayoshi, Okubo Toshimichi and Ito Hirobumi, on a journey of about two years through the United States and Europe. Although it also hoped to prepare for revising Japan's unequal treaties, the mission divided into teams to study education, administration, finance, law and industry in the countries that had already built modern states. Its members returned with ideas that shaped Japan's later reforms, including the conviction that the country needed a constitution.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Rather than guessing how to build a modern state, Japan's leaders went to observe those who had already done it and learned from them directly.
The kural says the right way for anyone undertaking a task is to learn the mind of someone who knows that task from the inside, and Japan's leaders crossed the world to study nations that had already done what Japan set out to do.
