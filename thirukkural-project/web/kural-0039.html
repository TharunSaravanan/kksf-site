Kural 39

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

அறத்தான் வருவதே இன்பம் மற்றெல்லாம் புறத்த புகழும் இல.
Araththaan varuvadhe inpam matrellaam, Puraththa pukazhum ila.

Meaning

Only happiness that comes from doing right is real happiness. Everything else is not happiness at all, and it brings no respect either.

Elementary

Winning a game by cheating does not really feel like winning, and nobody claps for it. This kural says the only fun that is truly fun is the kind you got the right way.

University

The key word is புறத்த — 'they are outside', i.e. outside the category of இன்பம் (inbam, pleasure) altogether. Valluvar is not saying illicit pleasure is a lesser pleasure; he is saying it does not belong to the class at all, and Parimelazhagar completes the logic by noting that what lies outside pleasure is துன்பம், pain. The second clause adds a social criterion: such gains are also புகழும் இல, devoid of honour. Reading the verse against the third book of the Kural, the காமத்துப்பால் devoted entirely to erotic love, shows that Valluvar is not renouncing pleasure but defining its legitimate channel; Parimelazhagar accordingly glosses அறத்தான் as இல்லறத்தோடு பொருந்தி, 'in accordance with domestic virtue'. The verse thus quietly integrates the three aims of life rather than ranking them.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.38 (Bhaktivedanta, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
In his taxonomy of the three kinds of happiness Krishna distinguishes rājasic pleasure, sweet at the outset and toxic in its outcome, from sāttvic happiness that begins hard and ends in clarity.
Both texts deny the name of real pleasure to enjoyment that is not grounded in right conduct.
Christianity
“Choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season;”
— Hebrews 11:25 (KJV) [4]
Moses is praised for rejecting Egypt's enjoyments as temporary — 'for a season' — in favour of a harder path with a lasting outcome.
It draws the Kural's line between pleasure that comes through righteousness and pleasure that does not, and denies the latter any standing.
Islam
“Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds.”
— Qur'an 16:97, An-Nahl (Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The promise of ḥayātan ṭayyiba — a good or wholesome life — attaches a present-tense quality of living, not merely a future reward, to righteous action.
It affirms the Kural's positive claim that the pleasure worth having is the pleasure that arrives by way of virtue.
Judaism
“Her ways are pleasant ways, And all her paths, peaceful.”
— Proverbs 3:17 (JPS/Sefaria Tanakh) [6]
Proverbs personifies Wisdom and claims that the experience of walking her road is itself pleasant and peaceful, not merely instrumentally rewarding.
Like the Kural, it locates genuine pleasantness inside the righteous path rather than as a prize at its end.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells the householder Anathapindika that there are four kinds of bliss available to a layman — the bliss of having wealth, of using it, of being free from debt, and of blamelessness — and concludes with a verse saying that the first three together are not worth one sixteenth of a sixteenth of the bliss of blamelessness, which consists in blameless action of body, speech and mind.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.62, Anana Sutta (Debtless), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
The sutta grants that material enjoyments are real pleasures for a householder, then ranks the pleasure of an unreproachable life incomparably above them.
It matches both halves of the Kural — virtue-derived pleasure is the real thing, and the criterion involves blame and praise.
Jainism
“All singing is but prattle, all dancing is but mocking, all ornaments are but a burden, all pleasures produce but pains. O king, pleasures which the ignorant like, but which produce pains, do not delight pious monks who care not for pleasure, but are intent on the virtues of right conduct.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.16-17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The monk Citta tells King Brahmadatta that pleasures pursued for their own sake only end in pain, and that the joy of the wise lies in the virtues of right conduct.
The Kural says that only what comes through virtue is true pleasure, and that everything else falls outside pleasure altogether. Citta likewise reclassifies sense-pleasure as pain and places real delight in right conduct.
Sikhism
“O Baba, the pleasures of other foods are false. Eating them, the body is ruined, and wickedness and corruption enter into the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 16, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In this Sri Raag hymn Guru Nanak sets the 'thirty-six flavours' found in love of the One against the pleasures of food, dress and riding, repeating in a refrain that all such pleasures are false and breed corruption.
Like Valluvar, it denies the name of true pleasure to any enjoyment cut off from the right way, treating it as a source of harm.
Greek Mythology
In Prodicus' allegory, reported by Xenophon, the young Heracles is met at a crossroads by two women. Vice promises him the shortest and pleasantest road, with every enjoyment taken without effort. Virtue answers that Vice does not even wait for genuine desire before gorging, so that she eats without hunger and drinks without thirst — and that of all things truly good and noble, the gods give nothing to men without toil.
— Prodicus, The Choice of Heracles, in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 [10]
Virtue's rebuttal is not that pleasure is bad but that Vice's shortcuts destroy the very conditions under which pleasure can be felt, and earn no honour from gods or men.
It argues the Kural's exact case: pleasure taken outside virtue is not really pleasure, and brings no praise with it.
Philosophy
“It is impossible to live a pleasant life without living wisely and well and justly, and it is impossible to live wisely and well and justly without living pleasantly.”
— Epicurus, Principal Doctrines (Kyriai Doxai) 5 [11]
Epicurus — a hedonist by definition — makes justice and prudence not constraints on pleasure but its necessary conditions, so that the unjust life cannot be a pleasant one.
This is Kural 39 as a philosophical identity: pleasure and virtue are not two goods to be traded off but one.

Real Life Example

Ben Johnson is stripped of his Olympic gold medal
24-27 September 1988 · Seoul, South Korea
On 24 September 1988 the Canadian sprinter Ben Johnson won the 100 metres at the Seoul Olympics in a world-record 9.79 seconds. Three days later he tested positive for the steroid stanozolol, and the International Olympic Committee stripped him of the gold medal and awarded it to the runner-up, Carl Lewis. Johnson, celebrated as the world's fastest man one day, became a byword for cheating in sport.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode is one of the most famous reversals in sporting history and a lasting lesson that victory gained by breaking the rules brings disgrace rather than glory.
The kural says only what comes by way of virtue is true joy, while everything else is outside joy and brings no honour, and Johnson's triumph, won by forbidden means, was taken away within days and left only dishonour.
