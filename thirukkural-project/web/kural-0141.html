Kural 141

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

பிறன்பொருளாள் பெட்டொழுகும் பேதைமை ஞாலத்து அறம்பொருள் கண்டார்கண் இல்.
Piranporulaal pettozhugum pedhaimai gnaalaththu, Aramporul kandaarkan il.

Meaning

People in this world who truly understand goodness and wealth are never so foolish as to desire another man’s wife.

Elementary

Imagine someone who has studied all the rules of a game very carefully. That person would never try to grab another player's pieces, because they understand how the game works and why the rules matter. This kural says people who really understand what is right and good would never want someone else's husband or wife.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter by framing adultery first as a failure of intelligence (பேதைமை, 'folly') rather than simply as a sin. The phrase அறம்பொருள் கண்டார் — 'those who have seen aram and porul' — deliberately invokes the first two aims of life that structure the Kural itself (virtue and worldly prosperity), implying that a person who grasps either domain properly would recognise such desire as self-defeating. The word பொருளாள் ('she who is another's porul') reflects the social vocabulary of its age, in which a wife was spoken of as belonging within another's household; modern readers often recast it as a violation of another's committed bond. The kural thus locates the root of the wrong in ignorance, a move that parallels Socratic and Buddhist views that wrongdoing flows from failed understanding.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him not show particular attention to an enemy, to the friend of an enemy, to a wicked man, to a thief, or to the wife of another man.”
— Manusmriti 4.133 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
The Laws of Manu, a classical Hindu code of dharma, lists another man's wife among the people a householder should not seek out or single out for attention.
Those who have studied dharma (aram) are taught this rule explicitly; the Kural says those who truly understand it never fall into the folly.
Christianity
“For this is the will of God, even your sanctification, that ye should abstain from fornication: That every one of you should know how to possess his vessel in sanctification and honour; Not in the lust of concupiscence, even as the Gentiles which know not God.”
— 1 Thessalonians 4:3-5 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers that sexual self-control is part of knowing God, and contrasts it with the behaviour of those who do not know God.
Both texts tie sexual restraint to knowledge: those who 'know' (God, or aram) do not act on such lust.
Islam
“And [also prohibited to you are all] married women except those your right hands possess. [This is] the decree of Allah upon you.”
— Qur'an 4:24 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an lists married women among those whom a man is forbidden to seek, calling this a decree of God.
Anyone who knows the divine law knows a married woman is off-limits, just as the Kural says those who know aram never pursue another's wife.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's.”
— Exodus 20:17 (KJV) [4]
The tenth commandment forbids even desiring a neighbour's wife, listing her among the things that belong within the neighbour's household.
Like the Kural, the Torah forbids the desire itself (not only the act), and frames the wife as part of another's household.
Buddhism
“Knowing well these causes of downfall in the world, the noble sage endowed with insight shares a happy realm.”
— Parabhava Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.6 (trans. Narada Thera) [6]
At the end of a list of the causes of a person's downfall (which includes going to the wives of others), the Buddha says the wise person who understands them avoids ruin.
The same logic as the Kural: true understanding of what ruins a life keeps the wise from this folly.
Jainism
“Being infatuated, he is unable to discriminate between the good and the evil. And he is not able to do anything right or proper.”
— Tattvārthasūtra 7.9, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi on unchastity (abrahma) (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In explaining why the vows must be guarded, Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi says that unchastity first destroys a person's judgment, and from that come all the other harms.
Valluvar calls coveting another's wife a folly (பேதைமை) that people who know dharma never fall into, and the Jain commentary likewise calls it a loss of the very power to tell good from evil.
Sikhism
“You have abandoned your own wife, but now you sneak glances at other women. God is not found by wearing religious robes; you are utterly miserable!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1348, Raag Prabhati, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan exposes the man who makes a show of religion while his eye strays to other men's wives, and says such a life brings only misery.
The Kural says those who have truly grasped virtue never fall into this folly; Guru Arjan shows that outward religiosity without that understanding does not prevent it.
Greek Mythology
Paris, a Trojan prince, was welcomed as a guest by King Menelaus of Sparta and then carried off Menelaus's wife Helen. In the Iliad (Book 3) his own brother Hector rebukes him as a disgrace, and his act brings war and ruin on all of Troy.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 3 (narrative summary) [9]
Paris had every advantage of rank and upbringing, yet his desire for another man's wife showed a complete lack of good judgement.
Paris is the classic example of the 'folly' the Kural names: a failure to see what virtue and plain self-interest both demand.
Philosophy
“But most people do not do these, but take refuge in theory and think they are being philosophers and will become good in this way.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.4 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle warns that knowing moral theory is not enough; people become good only by actually practising good acts.
Valluvar's 'those who have seen aram' are people whose understanding shows in conduct; Aristotle agrees that real ethical knowledge is proven by action.

Real Life Example

Edward VIII abdicates to marry Wallis Simpson
10-11 December 1936 · London, United Kingdom
King Edward VIII wished to marry Wallis Simpson, an American who was still married to her second husband, Ernest Simpson, when the king's attachment to her became a matter of state; she obtained a preliminary divorce decree only in October 1936. As head of the Church of England, the king faced firm opposition from Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin's government and the Church to his marrying a divorced woman whose former husbands were living, and Baldwin rejected a proposed morganatic marriage. Edward abdicated in December 1936, telling the nation by radio that he could not carry out his duties without the help and support of the woman he loved, and his brother became King George VI.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Edward had been raised from birth to understand the duties and public role of the crown, yet he gave up the throne rather than give up a woman who had been another man's wife.
The kural says the folly of desiring another man's wife is not found in those who truly understand virtue and worldly affairs; Edward's choice cost him the very position his whole training had prepared him to fill.
