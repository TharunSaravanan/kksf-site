Kural 14

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

ஏரின் உழாஅர் உழவர் புயல்என்னும் வாரி வளங்குன்றிக் கால்.
Erin uzhaaar uzhavar puyal ennum, Vaari valam kundrik kaal.

Meaning

When rain, the world’s great source of riches, does not fall enough, even farmers will stop ploughing their fields.

Elementary

A bicycle is useless if the tyres have no air in them; you can own it, but you cannot ride it. This kural says a farmer's plough is like that. However strong the farmer is, if the rain does not come, the plough just sits there.

University

The economic vocabulary here is deliberate: வாரி (vaari) is a revenue term, the yield or income of a realm, so Valluvar literally calls rain the kingdom's principal income stream — a framing that anticipates the Poruṭpāl's treatment of statecraft and chapter 104 on உழவு (agriculture). உழாஅர் (uzhaaar, "they do not plough") carries an அளபெடை, a metrical elongation that lends the line a note of weary finality. புயல் (puyal) denotes rain in its abundant, gusting monsoon form rather than a drizzle. The couplet's force lies in its choice of the farmer as subject: of all people the ploughman is the most industrious, and if even he downs tools, no amount of human effort can substitute for the sky.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya's Arthaśāstra instructs the state's superintendent of agriculture to measure annual rainfall in droṇas for each type of terrain — dry, wet and marshy country — and to decide which crops may be sown on the basis of how much rain has actually fallen.
— Kautilya, Arthaśāstra, Book II, Chapter 24 (Shamasastry translation) [4]
The treatise makes rainfall data an explicit input to agricultural planning and revenue forecasting for the realm.
It is the administrative version of this Kural: below a certain rainfall the ploughing simply does not happen.
Christianity
“Behold, the husbandman waiteth for the precious fruit of the earth, and hath long patience for it, until he receive the early and latter rain.”
— James 5:7 (KJV) [5]
James takes the farmer as the model of patience precisely because the farmer's work is hostage to rain he cannot command.
Both verses make the farmer's dependence on rainfall the definitive example of human effort waiting on the sky.
Islam
“And have you seen that [seed] which you sow? Is it you who makes it grow, or are We the grower?”
— Qur'an 56:63–64, Al-Waqi'ah (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an confronts the farmer with the limits of his own agency: he can sow, but the growth is not in his hands.
It makes the Kural's point from the other direction — ploughing and sowing are worthless unless a power beyond the farmer supplies the water.
Judaism
The Mishnah prescribes an escalating public response when the winter rains fail: after successive rounds of communal fasts pass unanswered, Jews are to cut back on business, on building and planting, on betrothals and weddings, and on greetings between neighbours.
— Mishnah Ta'anit 1:4–7 [7]
Rabbinic law treats a failed rainy season as grounds for suspending ordinary economic activity, including planting itself.
It legislates exactly what the Kural observes — when the rain does not come, the planting stops.
Buddhism
“Conviction is my seed, / austerity my rain, / discernment my yoke & plow, / conscience my pole, / mind my yoke-tie, / mindfulness my plowshare & goad.”
— Kasi Bhāradvāja Sutta, Sutta Nipāta 1.4 (Thanissaro Bhikkhu translation) [8]
Challenged by the brahmin farmer Kasi Bharadvaja to justify eating without ploughing, the Buddha answers by mapping each part of the plough team onto a quality of spiritual practice.
The Buddha's metaphor concedes the Kural's literal premise — no rain, no ploughing — which is exactly why rain can stand for the indispensable element in any cultivation.
Jainism
“The husbandmen throw the corn on high ground and on low ground, hoping (for a return). For the like motive give unto me; I may be the field which may produce merit (as the return for your benevolence).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The Jain monk Harikesha, refused alms at a Brahmin sacrifice, appeals to the farmer's hope of a harvest to show that giving to a self-controlled ascetic is a field that yields merit.
It draws on the same farming life the Kural describes, in which ploughing and sowing are acts of hope in a return, though the Jain verse uses farming as a metaphor rather than speaking of drought.
Sikhism
“Make your mind the farmer, good deeds the farm, modesty the water, and your body the field. Let the Lord’s Name be the seed, contentment the plow, and your humble dress the fence.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 595, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak, who spent his last years farming at Kartarpur, uses the farmer's whole round of work as a picture of the spiritual life. Water is one of its essential inputs.
The Kural pictures the ploughman as the most hard-working of people, whose labour stops without rain. Guru Nanak builds a spiritual farm in which water is a necessary part.
Greek Mythology
“Mark, when you hear the voice of the crane who cries year by year from the clouds above, for she gives the signal for ploughing and shows the season of rainy winter; but she vexes the heart of the man who has no oxen.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 448–451 (Evelyn-White translation) [11]
Hesiod's farming calendar ties the start of ploughing to the arrival of the rainy season, announced by migrating cranes.
Both poets treat ploughing as an activity that begins only when the rains arrive and is meaningless without them.
Philosophy
“A prayer of the Athenians: Rain, rain, O dear Zeus, down on the ploughed fields of the Athenians and on the plains.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.7 (Long translation) [12]
Marcus Aurelius cites this old Athenian prayer as his model of how one should pray at all — simply, nobly, and for the one thing the fields actually need.
It puts the Kural's insight in the mouth of a whole city: the ploughed field is finished work until rain falls on it.

Real Life Example

The Dust Bowl drives farmers off the Great Plains
1930s · Southern Great Plains, United States
Severe drought struck the southern Great Plains in the 1930s, and crops began failing from 1931. Without deep-rooted prairie grasses to hold the over-ploughed soil, it blew away in great dust storms. With no way to make a living, many farm families abandoned their land, and hundreds of thousands of people left the region.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The Dust Bowl showed how quickly a farming society collapses when the rain on which its ploughing depends fails.
The kural says that when rain, the great source of a land's yield, fails, even farmers stop driving the plough, and the Dust Bowl saw farm families abandon their fields entirely.
