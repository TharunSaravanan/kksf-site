Kural 453

Avoiding Mean Associations · Sitrinanjeraamai · சிற்றினஞ்சேராமை

மனத்தானாம் மாந்தர்க் குணர்ச்சி இனத்தானாம் இன்னான் எனப்படுஞ் சொல்.
Manaththaanaam maandhark kunarchchi inaththaanaam Innaan enappadunj chol.

Meaning

Our power to understand things comes from our own mind. But how others judge us, saying “that is the kind of person they are,” comes from the friends we keep.

Elementary

What you think and feel comes from inside you. But what other people say about you, like ‘he’s a good guy’ or ‘he’s trouble’, mostly depends on who your friends are.

University

The couplet sets inner and outer against each other: உணர்ச்சி (perception, awareness) arises from மனம் (mind), while reputation, இன்னான் எனப்படுஞ் சொல் (‘the word by which one is called such a man’), arises from இனம் (one’s group). This is a sociological observation: others cannot see into a person’s mind, so they judge by association. Valluvar does not call this judgement unfair. He treats it as a fact the prudent must reckon with, which is why the chapter sits in the Porul (polity) book rather than Aram. Together with 454 it forms a two-step argument: first reputation, then even inner wisdom, turns out to depend on one’s company.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Karna is generous, brave and bound by his word, yet because he stands with Duryodhana’s faction he is remembered as part of the Kaurava wrong and dies on its side.
— Mahabharata (Adi, Sabha and Karna Parvas) [4]
His personal nobility could not override the name that came from the company he chose.
Karna’s உணர்ச்சி came from his own mind, but his இன்னான் (‘what kind of man he is’) was settled by his association with Duryodhana.
Christianity
“Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners.”
— Matthew 11:19 (KJV) [5]
Jesus’s critics judge him by the people he eats with. Jesus quotes the accusation to show how such people think, and does not accept it.
This confirms the Kural’s observation that society labels a person by their company. The Gospel, however, rejects the verdict when the purpose of the company is redemptive.
Islam
“A man follows the religion of his friend; so each one should consider whom he makes his friend.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4833 [6]
The Prophet says a person’s way of life can be read from their close friends, so friends should be chosen with care.
Like the Kural, it says a person’s identity is judged by, and actually shaped by, their companions.
Judaism
The Mishnah’s saying “woe to the wicked, woe to his neighbour” is applied by Rashi to the tribe of Reuben, who camped beside Korah and were drawn into his rebellion.
— Mishnah Negaim 12:6; Rashi on Numbers 3:29 [7]
Being near the wicked brings both ruin and a shared name.
Reuben is remembered alongside Korah because of whom they camped beside, which illustrates the Kural’s point about reputation from company.
Buddhism
The Buddha warns Sigala that one who associates with evil companions finds that gamblers, libertines, drunkards, cheats, swindlers and ruffians become his friends and companions.
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 [8]
Among the dangers of bad company, the Buddha lists that one’s whole circle, and so one’s standing, is defined by such people.
This matches the Kural’s point that one comes to be counted as ‘such a man’ by the circle one keeps.
Jainism
“Though innocent in intention, persons will be despised on account of the (bad) company they have joined. In the forest both the scent-giving sandal and the teak tree will be burned when the brushwood, which has been cut, has caught fire.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 18 'Good Society', stanza 10 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar warns that even a person with a pure inner intention is judged, and suffers, by the company they keep, just as fine sandalwood burns along with the brushwood around it.
Valluvar sets inner perception, which comes from the mind, against the verdict 'he is such a man', which comes from one's company. The Naladiyar makes the same split between innocent intention and reputation earned through company.
Sikhism
“Of all plants, the sandalwood tree is the most sublime. Everything near the sandalwood tree becomes fragrant like sandalwood.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 834, Raag Bilaaval, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Ram Das uses the sandalwood tree, which perfumes everything growing near it, as an image of the saints whose company transforms those around them.
The Kural says the verdict 'he is such-and-such a man' comes from one's company; Guru Ram Das likewise shows plants taking on the very name and nature of what they stand beside.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus returns, the goatherd Melanthius and the disloyal maidservants, who had sided with the suitors, share the suitors’ punishment.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 22 [11]
They are judged not by their rank but by the camp they joined.
This fits the Kural’s view that the world pronounces its verdict on a person according to their company.
Philosophy
The orator Aeschines reminds the Athenian jury that they put Socrates to death because he was shown to have educated Critias, one of the Thirty Tyrants.
— Aeschines, Against Timarchus 173 [12]
Socrates’s public name was shaped by his association with notorious pupils, whatever his own inner philosophy.
This is a historical case of the Kural’s claim: people read one’s mind from one’s company.

Real Life Example

Mary, Queen of Scots, marries the Earl of Bothwell
1567 · Edinburgh, Scotland
In February 1567 Lord Darnley, husband of Mary, Queen of Scots, was killed when the house where he was staying was blown up, and James Hepburn, Earl of Bothwell, was widely held to be the chief suspect. Bothwell was acquitted at a trial with no prosecution case, and in May 1567 Mary married him. The Scottish nobles turned against the couple, Mary surrendered to them at Carberry Hill in June, was held at Lochleven Castle and was forced to abdicate in favour of her infant son in July 1567.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Whatever Mary herself knew, a question historians still debate, her marriage to the suspected man was enough for her subjects to judge her by him.
The kural says that others cannot see into the mind and so name a person by the company they keep, and Mary lost her crown largely on the strength of whom she joined herself to.
