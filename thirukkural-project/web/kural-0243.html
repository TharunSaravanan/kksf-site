Kural 243

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

அருள்சேர்ந்த நெஞ்சினார்க் கில்லை இருள்சேர்ந்த இன்னா உலகம் புகல்.
Arulserndha nenjinaark killai irulserndha, Innaa ulagam pugal.

Meaning

People whose hearts are full of kindness will never enter the dark world of suffering.

Elementary

Think of kindness as a lamp you carry inside you. Wherever you go, it keeps the dark away. Valluvar says people with kind hearts will never end up in the dark, sad place.

University

The couplet is built on a sound-and-image rhyme: arul serndha (joined with grace) against irul serndha (joined with darkness). Traditional commentators such as Parimelazhagar and Solomon Pappaiah read the ‘dark world of pain’ as naragam (hell); modern commentators such as Mu. Varadarasan and Kalaignar take it as the darkness of ignorance and misery in this life. The two readings are compatible: on either view a compassionate heart is inherently protected from darkness, whether that darkness is a place one goes after death or a state one lives in now. The line compresses a moral cosmology found widely, in which a being’s inner quality decides its destination, as in the karmic ‘happy and unhappy destinations’ of the Pali canon or the Isles of the Blessed and Tartarus in Plato.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The transcendental qualities are conducive to liberation, whereas the demonic qualities make for bondage.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.5 [3]
Having listed compassion among the divine qualities (16.2), Krishna says those qualities lead to freedom while their opposites lead to bondage.
Same moral cosmology: a compassionate nature leads away from darkness and bondage.
Christianity
“Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy.”
— Matthew 5:7 (KJV) [4]
The Beatitude promises that those who show mercy will themselves receive mercy.
Both texts promise the merciful a protected destiny; the Kural phrases it negatively, as exemption from the dark world.
Islam
“The merciful are shown mercy by Ar-Rahman. Be merciful on the earth, and you will be shown mercy from Who is above the heavens.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1924 (graded hasan) [5]
The Prophet links human mercy directly to receiving the mercy of God, whose name Ar-Rahman is built on the same root.
The merciful are shielded by divine mercy, just as the compassionate heart is kept from the world of pain.
Judaism
“anyone who has compassion for God’s creatures will receive compassion from Heaven, and anyone who does not have compassion for God’s creatures will not receive compassion from Heaven.”
— Talmud, Shabbat 151b [6]
Rabbi Gamliel, son of Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, derives this from Deuteronomy 13:18.
Heaven’s treatment of a person mirrors their compassion toward creatures, echoing the Kural’s promise to the compassionate heart.
Buddhism
“some woman or man is a killer of living beings, murderous, bloody-handed, given to blows and violence, merciless to living beings... he reappears in a state of deprivation, in an unhappy destination, in perdition, in hell.”
— Cula-kammavibhanga Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 135 (trans. Nanamoli) [7]
The Buddha contrasts this with one who ‘dwells compassionate for the welfare of all living beings’, who reappears ‘in a happy destination, in the heavenly world’.
Almost a commentary on the Kural: mercilessness leads to hell, compassion keeps one out of it.
Jainism
“He who despises the Law that enjoins compassion, and praises the Law that permits slaughter, and who feeds but a single unprincipled man, even if he be a king, will go to darkness, and not to the gods.”
— Sutrakritanga II.6.45 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In his debate with a Vedic priest, the Jain monk Ardraka says that whoever rejects the law of compassion goes to darkness (Jacobi notes the original word literally means 'night').
Valluvar says those whose hearts hold compassion never enter the dark world of suffering; Ardraka states the same truth from the other side, that those who scorn compassion go to darkness.
Sikhism
“Where there is awesome and terrible heat and blazing sunshine, there, the Name of the Lord will give you shade.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 264, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 2, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani Guru Arjan promises that in the terrifying passages after death the Name of God will shelter the soul.
The Kural says those filled with compassion never enter the dark world of suffering; the nearest Sikh assurance is that the Name shields the soul in that fearful realm.
Greek Mythology
Socrates’ closing myth in the Gorgias: under Zeus’s law, souls who lived justly and piously go to the Isles of the Blessed, while the unjust are sent to Tartarus, the prison of punishment.
— Plato, Gorgias 523a-526d [10]
Judges of the dead examine the naked soul, and its moral character decides where it goes.
Shares the Kural’s idea that inner character decides whether one enters the dark world, though the Greek criterion is justice rather than compassion.
Philosophy
“Until he extends the circle of his compassion to all living things, man will not himself find peace.”
— Albert Schweitzer, The Philosophy of Civilization (trans. C. T. Campion) [11]
Schweitzer argues that an ethic limited to human relations leaves people inwardly unsettled.
Read with Varadarasan’s this-worldly interpretation of the kural, the compassionate heart escapes the darkness of inner misery.

Real Life Example

Corrie ten Boom survives Ravensbruck and turns to healing work
1944-1945 · Haarlem, Netherlands, and Ravensbruck, Germany
Corrie ten Boom and her family hid Jews and resistance workers in their home in Haarlem until it was raided on 28 February 1944; the six people in the hiding place were not found. Corrie was imprisoned and deported with her sister Betsie to the Ravensbruck concentration camp, where Betsie died in December 1944, and Corrie was released at the end of that month. After the war she helped run a home for camp survivors and worked at rehabilitation with former Dutch collaborators.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her compassion took her into one of the darkest places of the century, yet she came out of it devoted to healing rather than to bitterness.
On the modern commentators' reading of the kural, the darkness the compassionate never enter is inner misery, and ten Boom passed through a camp without being swallowed by hatred.
