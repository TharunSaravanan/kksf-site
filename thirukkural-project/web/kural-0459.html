Kural 459

Avoiding Mean Associations · Sitrinanjeraamai · சிற்றினஞ்சேராமை

மனநலத்தின் ஆகும் மறுமைமற் றஃதும் இனநலத்தின் ஏமாப் புடைத்து.
Mananalaththin aakum marumaimat rahdhum Inanalaththin emaap pudaiththu.

Meaning

A good mind earns a happy life in the next world. Even that is made safe by keeping good company.

Elementary

A good heart earns you happiness after this life. But good friends make that happiness even more certain by helping you stay good all the way through.

University

The previous couplet made good company a fortress for virtue in this life; this one extends the claim to மறுமை, the next world. Even the reward that seems most purely individual, earned by one’s own inner goodness (மனநலம்), is secured (ஏமாப்பு, repeated from 458) by one’s company. The மற்று… அஃதும் (‘and even that’) is emphatic. The pairing of 458 and 459 moves from this world to the next and leaves no domain where company does not matter.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“satsaṅgatve nissaṅgatvaṃ nissaṅgatve nirmohatvam | nirmohatve niścalatattvaṃ niścalatattve jīvanmuktiḥ”
— Adi Shankaracharya, Bhaja Govindam, verse 9 [4]
Shankara’s verse traces a chain: from good company comes detachment, from detachment freedom from delusion, from that steadiness in truth, and from that liberation.
This matches the Kural’s claim that the highest spiritual attainment is secured through good company.
Christianity
“And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.”
— Luke 23:43 (KJV) [5]
The repentant thief, crucified next to Jesus, turns to him and receives the promise of paradise in his company.
Salvation here comes through a change of heart and is sealed by the company he is with, which parallels the Kural.
Islam
When a man asked the Prophet when the Hour would come, the Prophet asked what he had prepared for it. The man said only his love of Allah and His Messenger, and the Prophet replied that he would be with those he loved.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3688 [6]
The company one loves in this life decides one’s company in the next.
This is close to the Kural’s claim that good company secures the afterlife.
Judaism
“Then they that feared the LORD spake often one to another: and the LORD hearkened, and heard it, and a book of remembrance was written before him for them that feared the LORD.”
— Malachi 3:16 (KJV) [5]
The fellowship of the God-fearing is itself recorded for their future reward.
This links good company to one’s standing in the world to come, as the Kural does.
Buddhism
“Not to associate with fools, to associate with the wise, and to honor those who are worthy of honor — this is the highest blessing.”
— Mangala Sutta, Khuddakapatha 5 / Sutta Nipata 2.4 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Mangala Sutta puts right association first in its ladder of blessings, which ends in the realisation of Nibbana.
As in the Kural, the path to the ultimate good begins with and is secured by good company.
Jainism
“His knowledge will be honoured, his doubts will be removed, he will gladden the heart of his teacher by his good acts; kept in safety by the performance of austerities and by meditation, being as it were a great light, he will keep the five vows.” … “Honoured by gods, Gandharvas, and men, he will, on leaving this body which consists of dirt and impurities, become either an eternal Siddha, or a god of great power and small imperfections.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.47–48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on discipline ends by following the disciple who has served his teachers beyond death, to rebirth as a powerful god or to final liberation as a Siddha.
Valluvar says bliss in the world to come is earned by goodness of mind and made secure by good company. The Jain disciple's reward after death likewise comes through his own vows and his bond with his teachers.
Sikhism
“In the Company of the Holy, one meets the Incomprehensible Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 271, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan teaches that union with God, the highest spiritual goal, is reached within the Saadh Sangat.
The Kural says even bliss in the world to come is made secure by goodness of company; Guru Arjan makes holy company the place where the soul meets God.
Greek Mythology
Proteus tells Menelaus that he will not die in Argos but be taken to the Elysian plain, because he is Helen’s husband and so son-in-law of Zeus.
— Homer, Odyssey 4.561–569 [10]
A blessed afterlife granted because of whom one is joined to.
This is a near-literal case of the next world secured by association, though it is kinship, not moral company.
Philosophy
Socrates, facing death, says that if death leads to a place where he can talk with Orpheus, Musaeus, Hesiod and Homer, he would be willing to die again and again.
— Plato, Apology 41a [11]
For Socrates, the good of the next world is largely the company of the good and wise.
This echoes the Kural’s link between the afterlife and good company.

Real Life Example

Geert Groote and the Brethren of the Common Life
late 14th century · Deventer, Netherlands
After a religious conversion in 1374, the Dutch preacher Geert Groote gave up his worldly goods and began the movement later called the Devotio Moderna, which stressed meditation and the inner life. With his friend Florentius Radewyns he gathered followers who lived together, copied manuscripts for their living and prayed in common, forming the Brethren of the Common Life. The community shaped later figures such as Thomas a Kempis, the probable author of The Imitation of Christ.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The movement was built on the idea that a devout life is best sustained by living and working alongside others who share it.
The kural says that even blessedness in the world to come, earned by goodness of mind, is made secure by good company, which is the very principle on which the Brethren lived.
