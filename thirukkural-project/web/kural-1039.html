Kural 1039

Agriculture · Uzhavu · உழவு

செல்லான் கிழவன் இருப்பின் நிலம்புலந்து இல்லாளின் ஊடி விடும்.
Sellaan kizhavan iruppin nilampulandhu illaaLin ooti vidum.

Meaning

If the owner does not go to his land and look after it, the land will sulk and turn away from him, like an upset wife.

Elementary

If you plant a garden and then forget about it, the plants droop and the weeds take over, as if the garden were upset with you. Valluvar says land is like a family member: it needs you to show up and care for it every day.

University

kizhavan (கிழவன்) here means 'owner' (of the land), not 'old man'; sellaan iruppin (செல்லான் இருப்பின்) means 'if he stays without going (to the field)'. The land then pulandhu (புலந்து, 'feeling aggrieved') and oodi vidum (ஊடி விடும், 'turns away in sulking') like an illaaL (இல்லாள், 'wife'). The verbs pulavi and oodal are technical terms of the Kamaththuppal for a lover's quarrel (compare chapters 131-133), so Valluvar borrows the vocabulary of love to describe neglected land. Puliyur Kesikan, following Parimelazhagar, glosses that the owner must go daily and do what is needed; Solomon Pappaiah spells out that the land, like a wife, first withers inwardly and then withholds its yield. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Auspicious Sītā, come thou near: we venerate and worship thee That thou mayst bless and prosper us and bring us fruits abundantly.”
— Rig Veda 4.57.6 (tr. Ralph T.H. Griffith, 1896, sacred-texts.com) [3]
In the Vedic field hymn the furrow is personified as the goddess Sita, who must be approached and honoured before she bears fruit.
The Veda, like the Kural, treats the field as a feminine presence whose yield depends on the attention it receives.
Christianity
“I went by the field of the slothful, and by the vineyard of the man void of understanding; And, lo, it was all grown over with thorns, and nettles had covered the face thereof, and the stone wall thereof was broken down.”
— Proverbs 24:30-31 (KJV) [4]
The wise man walks past the neglected field of a lazy owner and finds it overgrown and ruined.
A field abandoned by its owner turns against him, which is the Kural's warning.
Islam
“Whoever has land should cultivate it himself or give it to his (Muslim) brother gratis; otherwise keep it uncultivated.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2340 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet tells landholders to work their land themselves, or give it to someone who will, rather than profit from it at a distance.
Like the Kural, the hadith expects a landholder to attend to his own land in person.
Judaism
“Mind well the looks of your flock; Pay attention to your herds; For property does not last forever, Or a crown for all generations.”
— Proverbs 27:23-24 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs tells the owner to keep a personal eye on his livestock, since wealth that is neglected does not last.
Proverbs, like the Kural, says an owner's personal attention is what keeps land and livestock productive.
Buddhism
“There is the case where a farming householder has three fields: one excellent field, one middling, and one poor—sandy, salty, with bad soil. … he would sow the seed first in the excellent field.”
— Desana Sutta, SN 42.7 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, dhammatalks.org) [7]
The Buddha uses a farmer who knows and attends to each of his fields as an image of a teacher who knows his listeners.
The farmer's close knowledge of his own fields reflects the owner's personal attention that the Kural requires.
Jainism
“Three merchants set out on their travels, each with his capital; one of them gained there much, the second returned with his capital, and the third merchant came home after having lost his capital. This parable is taken from common life; learn (to apply it) to the Law.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.14-15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's parable warns that capital that is neglected is lost, and applies this to human life and spiritual gain.
Neglected resources turn into loss, the warning the Kural gives the absent landowner.
Sikhism
“You work the well and irrigate the fields, but the oxen have already left to graze in the jungle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 368, Raag Asa, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das uses the image of a farmer whose oxen have wandered off while he imagines his fields are being watered, as a picture of outward religious effort while the untended mind roams away.
The Kural warns that land whose owner stays at home sulks like a neglected wife; Guru Ram Das likewise shows a field that fails because no one is actually tending it.
Greek Mythology
“Do not put your work off till to-morrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 410-412 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, 1914, sacred-texts.com) [10]
Hesiod warns that a farmer who keeps postponing his field work will have an empty barn.
The farmer who stays away from his work loses the harvest, the Kural's land that withholds itself.
Philosophy
“So he asked one who was reputed clever with horses what is the quickest way of fattening a horse. ‘The master's eye,’ replied the man. I think we may apply the answer generally, Socrates, and say that the master's eye in the main does the good and worthy work.”
— Xenophon, Oeconomicus 12.20 (tr. E.C. Marchant, Loeb 1923, Perseus Digital Library) [11]
Ischomachus cites the Persian proverb that the owner's own eye is what makes his property thrive.
'The master's eye' is almost exactly the Kural's demand that the owner go to his land himself.

Real Life Example

George Washington's daily ride around his Mount Vernon farms
1780s-1799 · Mount Vernon, Virginia, United States
When in residence at Mount Vernon, George Washington made a daily inspection tour of his farms on horseback, a ride of around twenty miles, and his managers reported to him on the week's work every Saturday. When he was away, as during his presidency, he demanded detailed weekly written reports from his farm manager.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Washington regarded the owner's constant personal attention as essential to a productive farm; the work itself was done largely by enslaved people.
The kural warns that land sulks like a neglected wife when its owner stays at home, and Washington made a point of going to his fields every day.
