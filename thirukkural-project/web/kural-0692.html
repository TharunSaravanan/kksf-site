Kural 692

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

மன்னர் விழைப விழையாமை மன்னரால் மன்னிய ஆக்கந் தரும்.
Mannar vizhaipa vizhaiyaamai mannaraal Manniya aakkam tharum.

Meaning

If you do not want the things the king himself wants, the king will give you success that lasts.

Elementary

Picture a class helper who never tries to grab the teacher’s special chair or the teacher’s favourite pen. Because the teacher never has to worry about that helper, the teacher trusts them more and more. Not wanting what belongs to the boss is how you earn lasting trust.

University

The couplet turns on the repeated root vizhai (to desire): the servant must not desire (vizhaiyaamai) what the kings desire (mannar vizhaipa). Parimelazhagar takes the objects to be the king’s own enjoyments, such as his ornaments, pleasures and women, which a courtier must never share or rival. Mu. Varadarasanar instead reads “those whom the king favours”, meaning one should not compete for the king’s favourites. Either way, the reward is manniya aakkam, prosperity that lasts (manniya = enduring), and it comes through the king himself (mannaraal). The play on mannar/manniya is typical of Valluvar’s sound patterning. The older text in thirukkural-thamizh.org reads மன்னரான் for மன்னரால், a dialectal instrumental ending with the same meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bharata refuses the throne his mother schemed to win for him. He goes to the forest to bring back his brother Rama, and when Rama will not return he rules only as regent, with Rama’s sandals placed on the throne.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (Bharata and the padukas) [3]
Bharata will not claim anything that belongs to the rightful king, even when it is handed to him, and he is remembered with lasting honour for it.
Bharata shows the Kural’s rule in its purest form: he refuses to desire what is the king’s, and that refusal secures his enduring standing.
Christianity
“When thou sittest to eat with a ruler, consider diligently what is before thee: And put a knife to thy throat, if thou be a man given to appetite. Be not desirous of his dainties: for they are deceitful meat.”
— Proverbs 23:1-3 (KJV) [4]
The proverb tells someone eating at a ruler’s table to hold back and not crave the ruler’s luxuries, because they are a trap.
Both texts tell the courtier not to desire the ruler’s own delights.
Islam
“And do not extend your eyes toward that by which We have given enjoyment to [some] categories of them, [its being but] the splendor of worldly life by which We test them. And the provision of your Lord is better and more enduring.”
— Quran 20:131 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran tells believers not to gaze longingly at the luxuries of the powerful, because those are a test. What God provides is better and lasts longer.
Like the Kural, it sets not coveting the splendour of the great against a more enduring reward.
Judaism
“Love work, hate acting the superior, and do not attempt to draw near to the ruling authority.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:10 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Shemaiah teaches that one should work honestly, dislike playing the lord, and not court the government.
‘Hate acting the superior’ is close to the Kural’s point: do not want the powers and pleasures that belong to the ruler.
Buddhism
“Let him on no account ever enjoy the same pleasures as the king, let him follow behind in everything, such a one may dwell in a king’s court.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 147 [7]
Vidhura’s rules for courtiers forbid enjoying what the king enjoys. The courtier is always to stay a step behind the king.
This is the Kural’s rule almost exactly: do not desire the king’s own pleasures.
Jainism
“Not resentful, having few wants, begging from strangers, and not being dainty, a wise man should not long for pleasant things, nor be sorry afterwards (for not having got them).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the trouble of 'respectful treatment', the monk is told to want little and not to long for pleasant things.
The Kural advises the courtier not to covet what the king desires; the Jain verse teaches a more general freedom from craving pleasant things, which would keep one from coveting a ruler's enjoyments.
Sikhism
“To take what rightfully belongs to another, is like a Muslim eating pork, or a Hindu eating beef.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh, Maajh ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak makes coveting and taking what is another's due as abhorrent as the gravest taboo of one's own faith.
The Kural tells the servant not to covet what the king himself desires; Guru Nanak's rule against taking what belongs to another covers that case as a general principle.
Greek Mythology
The courtier Damocles praised the wealth of the tyrant Dionysius of Syracuse, so Dionysius let him enjoy it for a day. Damocles sat among golden dishes and perfumes, but a sword hung above his head by a single horsehair, and he soon begged to leave.
— Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 5.61-62 (Damocles) [10]
The courtier envied his ruler’s luxury, was given a taste of it, and found out what danger came with it.
The Kural tells the servant not to desire the king’s things. Damocles is the classic courtier who did, and learned why.
Philosophy
Epictetus says that an invitation to a great man’s dinner is sold at a price, namely flattery and attendance. If you are unwilling to pay it, you should not complain about going without the dinner, because you still have not had to flatter.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 25 [11]
The Stoic teaches that the privileges of the powerful always come with a price, and that a free person can simply decline to want them.
Like the Kural, Epictetus asks us to stop wanting the great man’s goods, although for him the reward is inner freedom rather than royal favour.

Real Life Example

Belisarius refuses the crown the Goths offered him at Ravenna
540 · Ravenna, Italy (Byzantine Empire)
By 540 the hard-pressed Ostrogoths in Italy offered to surrender to the Byzantine general Belisarius if he would rule over them as emperor. Belisarius, who served the emperor Justinian, dissembled: he accepted the Goths' capitulation at Ravenna and then refused the title, claiming the kingdom and its treasury in Justinian's name. Justinian, who had already come to fear that so popular a commander might aim at his throne, recalled him from Italy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The one prize a ruler most jealously guards is his own crown, and Belisarius pointedly declined to reach for it even when it was offered to him.
The kural advises the servant not to covet what the king himself desires, and Belisarius turned down the very throne that Justinian held dear.
