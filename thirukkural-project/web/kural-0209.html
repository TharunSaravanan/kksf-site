Kural 209

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

தன்னைத்தான் காதல னாயின் எனைத்தொன்றும் துன்னற்க தீவினைப் பால்.
Thannaiththaan kaadhala naayin enaiththondrum, Thunnarka theevinaip paal.

Meaning

If you truly love yourself, do not go near any bad deed at all, even a small one.

Elementary

If you love yourself, you take care of yourself — you brush your teeth and wear a helmet on your bike. This kural says that taking care of yourself also means staying away from bad deeds, even tiny ones, because they end up hurting you.

University

Valluvar here turns self-love (தன்னைத்தான் காதலன்) — often treated as a vice — into the very motive for virtue: anyone who genuinely wishes himself well will keep away from evil. The key intensifier is எனைத்தொன்றும் (‘any whatsoever, however slight’), and the verb துன்னற்க means ‘do not approach or come near’, implying avoidance of the whole territory of evil (தீவினைப் பால், ‘the side/portion of evil’) rather than only its grave acts. This is a form of ‘enlightened self-interest’: properly understood, self-love and morality coincide. Aristotle makes the same argument in Nicomachean Ethics IX.8, and the Dhammapada puts it in almost the same words: ‘If one holds oneself dear, one should diligently watch oneself.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One must deliver himself with the help of his mind, and not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [4]
Krishna teaches that a person can be his own friend or his own enemy; true self-friendship means raising oneself rather than sinking into what degrades.
Same idea that genuine self-regard requires restraining oneself from what harms.
Christianity
“Abstain from all appearance of evil.”
— 1 Thessalonians 5:22 (KJV) [5]
Paul urges believers to keep away from every form of evil, not only its gross expressions.
Matches the Kural’s ‘however small’ and ‘do not go near’ — avoiding the whole range of evil.
Islam
“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it, And whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it.”
— Qur’an 99:7–8 (Sahih International) [6]
Nothing is too small to count: even an atom’s weight of evil will be shown to its doer.
Supports the Kural’s warning against evil ‘however small’ — every speck returns to oneself.
Judaism
“And be careful with a light commandment as with a grave one… reckon… the gain [that may be obtained through the committing] of a transgression against the loss [entailed] thereby.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Rabbi Judah the Prince advises weighing any apparent gain from a sin against the loss it brings, and treating light matters as seriously as grave ones.
Same prudential self-interest and refusal to treat any wrong as trivial.
Buddhism
“If one holds oneself dear, one should diligently watch oneself.”
— Dhammapada 157 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha teaches that genuine self-love expresses itself as vigilant self-guarding against wrong.
Almost a verbatim counterpart of ‘if one loves oneself’ in the Kural.
Jainism
“Man! Thou art thy own friend; why wishest thou for a friend beyond thyself? … Man! restraining thyself (from the outward world) ‘thou wilt get free from pain.’”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.3.3.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The Ācārāṅga tells each person that he is his own friend, and that restraint, not outside help, frees one from pain.
Valluvar says anyone who truly loves himself will keep away from every evil; the Ācārāṅga likewise makes the self its own friend, and self-restraint the way to escape suffering.
Sikhism
“Why do you do such evil deeds, that you shall have to suffer so? Do not do any evil at all; look ahead to the future with foresight.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa ki Vaar, Pauri 21), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a pauri of Asa ki Vaar, the Guru argues from plain self-interest: since one must suffer for one's deeds, one should do no evil at all and look ahead.
The Kural says that anyone who truly loves himself should not go near evil of any kind, however small; the pauri makes the same appeal to self-interest and demands no evil at all.
Greek Mythology
In Prodicus’s fable, the young Heracles at a crossroads is approached by Vice, who offers an easy road of pleasure, and Virtue, who offers a steep road; he chooses Virtue as the path that truly benefits him.
— Prodicus, ‘The Choice of Heracles’ in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21–34 (paraphrase) [11]
Heracles’ choice treats virtue as what is genuinely good for oneself, and vice as a flattering path to self-harm.
Next-best: choosing virtue out of true regard for one’s own good.
Philosophy
“Therefore the good man should be a lover of self (for he will both himself profit by doing noble acts, and will benefit his fellows), but the wicked man should not; for he will hurt both himself and his neighbours, following as he does evil passions.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.8, 1169a (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle distinguishes true self-love, which seeks the noble, from the vulgar kind that grasps at pleasures and harms the self.
Same insight: rightly understood, loving oneself means keeping away from evil.

Real Life Example

Hansie Cronje is banned from cricket for life
April - October 2000 · South Africa
Hansie Cronje, the widely admired captain of South Africa's cricket team, initially denied allegations made by New Delhi police in April 2000 that he had taken money from a bookmaker, but then admitted wrongdoing and lost the captaincy. In June 2000 he told the government-appointed King Commission that he had received thousands of dollars from gamblers and bookmakers on five separate occasions between 1996 and 2000, and that he had offered teammates money to underperform. In October 2000 the United Cricket Board of South Africa banned him for life from all its cricket activities.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Payments that may have seemed small and private ended a celebrated career and turned a national hero into a byword for corruption in sport.
The kural says that anyone who truly loves himself will not go near even the smallest evil, and Cronje's acceptance of bookmakers' money destroyed everything he had built for himself.
