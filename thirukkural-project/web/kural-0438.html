Kural 438

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

பற்றுள்ளம் என்னும் இவறன்மை எற்றுள்ளும் எண்ணப் படுவதொன் றன்று.
Patrullam ennum ivaranmai etrullum Ennap paduvathon randru.

Meaning

Stinginess, the greedy grip that holds on to everything, is not counted along with other faults. It stands apart as worse than all of them.

Elementary

This kural says that stinginess, a heart that grabs and won’t let go, is not just one bad habit on a list. It is in a class of its own, worse than all the others.

University

பற்றுள்ளம் (‘a clinging heart’) defines இவறன்மை (miserliness) as a disposition of attachment, not merely an act of not giving. The phrase எற்றுள்ளும் எண்ணப்படுவது ஒன்று அன்று (‘it is not a thing to be counted among any [faults]’) is deliberately ambiguous: Mu. Varadarasan reads it as ‘a separate fault that cannot be counted along with others’, and Solomon Pappaiah and Karunanidhi read it as ‘the most terrible of all faults’. Either way it is the chapter’s climax on avarice, which has now appeared three times (432, 437, 438). The ruler-centred reading matters: a stingy king starves the gifts, rewards and public works on which loyalty and prosperity depend, so his avarice spreads into every other failure.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 [3]
Krishna names greed as one of the three gateways to hell that must be abandoned.
Both rank greed among the most destructive faults; the Kural goes further and sets it above all others.
Christianity
“For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”
— 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul calls the love of money the root from which all kinds of evil grow.
Like the Kural, Paul sets the grasping attachment to wealth apart from, and beneath, other evils.
Islam
“...be on your guard against stinginess for stinginess destroyed those who were before you, as it incited them to shed blood and make lawful what was unlawful for them.”
— Sahih Muslim 2578 [5]
The Prophet warns that stinginess (shuḥḥ) destroyed earlier peoples by driving them to bloodshed and lawbreaking.
Both treat miserliness not as a small failing but as a master-fault that leads to every other wrong.
Judaism
“There are four types of character in human beings: One that says: ‘mine is mine, and yours is yours’: this is a commonplace type; and some say this is a sodom-type of character.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:10 [6]
The Mishnah notes that some sages see the attitude ‘what’s mine is mine’ as the character of Sodom, whose sin (Ezekiel 16:49) was not helping the poor.
Both texts see the refusal to share as worse than it looks: the Kural sets it apart from all faults, the Mishnah links it to Sodom.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says misers cannot reach heaven, while the wise find joy in giving.
Both single out miserliness as a fault with uniquely serious results.
Jainism
“If there were numberless mountains of gold and silver, as big as Kailâsa, they would not satisfy a greedy man; for his avidity is boundless like space.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Refusing Indra's advice to fill his treasury, Nami answers that greed has no limit: no amount of wealth can ever satisfy a grasping heart.
Valluvar sets the clinging, miserly heart apart from every other fault; Nami likewise describes avarice as boundless, unlike any fault that can be measured or satisfied.
Sikhism
“O mortal, why have you attached yourself to greed, and lost the jewel of life?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 481, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir warns that clinging greed (lobh) squanders the priceless human birth.
The Kural sets the grasping, clinging heart of avarice apart as a fault beyond all others; Kabir likewise treats attachment to greed as the loss of life's single jewel.
Greek Mythology
Erysichthon cuts down Demeter’s sacred oak for his own use; she curses him with insatiable hunger, so he consumes all his wealth, sells his daughter, and finally devours his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Erysichthon) [10]
A man who grabs for himself is cursed with a craving that can never be satisfied and destroys him completely.
Erysichthon dramatises the Kural’s ‘grasping heart’ as a fault that swallows everything else.
Philosophy
“But meanness is both incurable (for old age and every disability is thought to make men mean) and more innate in men than prodigality... for not only is it a greater evil than prodigality, but men err more often in this direction...”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says stinginess is worse than wastefulness: it is incurable, more natural to people, and more common.
Aristotle, like the Kural, gives miserliness a special rank as the worse and more stubborn fault.

Real Life Example

Emperor Galba refuses the soldiers' promised reward
68-69 CE · Rome, Roman Empire (now Italy)
Servius Sulpicius Galba became Roman emperor in 68 CE after the fall of Nero, and Suetonius describes his reputation for stinginess. He refused to pay the Praetorian Guard the donative, a cash reward, that had been promised in his name, and he passed over his ally Otho to name another man as his heir. Otho won the guard over with a promise of payment, and on 15 January 69 the praetorians killed Galba in the Roman Forum after a reign of about seven months.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Galba's tight grip on money turned the very soldiers who should have protected him into the instrument of his fall.
The kural calls a grasping heart a fault beyond all others, and Galba's refusal to part with what he owed undid every other strength he had as emperor.
