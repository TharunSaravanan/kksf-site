Kural 1237

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

பாடு பெறுதியோ நெஞ்சே கொடியார்க்கென் வாடுதோட் பூசல் உரைத்து.
Paadu perudhiyo nenje kotiyaarkken vaatudhot poosal uraiththu.

Meaning

My heart, why not go to that hard-hearted man and tell him how loudly my thin shoulders cry out? Wouldn't that bring you praise?

Elementary

Sometimes when we miss someone, we talk to ourselves, like “Come on, brave heart, go tell them how we feel!” The girl in this verse talks to her own heart and asks it to go to the person she loves and tell him how sad and thin she has become.

University

The heroine now addresses her own nenjam (heart, mind) as a possible messenger, a device developed at length in the next chapter, 125 (neñcoṭu kiḷattal, ‘speaking to the heart’). Pāṭu peṟutiyō means ‘will you gain greatness/glory?’; the question can be read as an exhortation (Kalaignar: ‘will you not gain glory?’) or as a rueful challenge (Solomon Pappaiah: ‘will you gain glory by telling him these empty rumours?’). Vāṭu tōḷ pūcal is ‘the clamour of my withering shoulders’, pūcal being a noisy outcry or public uproar, so the wasting body is imagined as shouting. Koṭiyārkku (‘to the cruel one’) resumes the word the heroine resisted in 1236, which Mu. Varadarasanar softens to ‘the one who is called cruel’. Pope: ‘My heart! say ought of glory wilt thou gain, / If to that cruel one thou of thy wasted arms complain?’ Ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree on the text, including the sandhi form வாடுதோட் பூசல்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Meghaduta, the exiled Yaksha, unable to reach his wife, asks a passing rain-cloud to carry his message to her across India and tell her of his suffering and enduring love.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [3]
Kalidasa’s poem makes an improvised messenger carry a separated lover’s complaint to the beloved.
The Kural’s heroine also searches for a go-between and settles on her own heart to carry her complaint.
Christianity
“I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love.”
— Song of Solomon 5:8 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song begs others to carry word of her love-sickness to her absent beloved.
The Kural’s heroine sends her own heart on the same errand: to tell him how she wastes away.
Islam
“He said, ‘I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know.’”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob refuses to air his grief before people and takes his complaint only to God.
The heroine’s complaint likewise has one proper recipient: she would carry it only to the one who can end it.
Judaism
“I pour out my complaint before Him, I declare before Him my trouble.”
— Psalms 142:3 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The psalmist, alone in a cave, sets out his distress before the one who can help.
The Kural’s heroine wants her heart to pour out the ‘clamour’ of her wasting body before her husband in the same way.
Buddhism
In his long verses in the Theragatha, the monk Talaputa speaks directly to his own mind, reasoning with it, pleading with it and reproaching it, asking when it will at last do what he wishes.
— Theragatha 19, Talaputa (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Theragatha preserves a monk’s dramatic dialogue with his own heart.
The Kural’s heroine also turns and addresses her heart as a separate person who could act for her.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra defines one form of arta-dhyana, ‘sorrowful brooding’, as the mind’s repeated dwelling on regaining a pleasing object from which one has been separated.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.31 (arta-dhyana on separation from the desired) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain psychology names exactly the anxious, circling thought that separation from the beloved produces, and classes it as karmically binding.
The heroine’s urging of her heart to run to the absent one is such brooding, which Jain teaching would counsel her to still.
Sikhism
“My mind longs for the Blessed Vision of the Guru's Darshan.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 96, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
These are the opening words of the Shabad Hazare, which Sikh tradition says the young Guru Arjan sent as verse-letters to his father, Guru Ram Das, while he was kept away in Lahore.
The Kural's wife urges her heart to go to the hard-hearted one and tell him how her shoulders cry out; Guru Arjan's longing mind likewise speaks across the distance to the one he is parted from.
Greek Mythology
Ovid’s Penelope opens her letter to Ulysses by telling him not to write back but to come himself, and then pours out the sleepless nights and fears she has endured while Troy has fallen and he still has not returned.
— Ovid, Heroides 1 (Penelope to Ulysses) (paraphrase) [10]
The Heroides are letters in which waiting women send their complaints to absent men.
The Kural’s heroine has no letter, so she would send her heart to carry the complaint of her wasting body.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius repeatedly speaks to his own soul as to another person, chiding it that it is doing itself wrong and that time to honour itself is running short.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.6 (paraphrase; cf. tr. George Long) [11]
The Stoic emperor’s private notebook uses self-address to argue with his own mind.
The Kural’s heroine also speaks to her heart as a separate agent, though to spur it toward love, not toward Stoic self-command.

Real Life Example

Kalidasa writes the Meghaduta, the Cloud Messenger
c. 5th century CE · India
Around the 5th century CE the Sanskrit poet Kalidasa wrote the Meghaduta (Cloud Messenger), a lyric love poem of about 115 verses. In it an exiled yaksha, a nature spirit grieving for his beloved on a remote mountain, asks a passing cloud at the start of the monsoon to carry his message to her in the Himalayan city of Alaka, and much of the poem describes the landscapes the cloud will cross.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem became the model for a whole genre of messenger poems in Indian literature.
The kural's wife asks her own heart to go to her husband and tell him of her suffering, just as Kalidasa's grieving lover sends a cloud to carry his message to his distant beloved.
