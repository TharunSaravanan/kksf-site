Kural 219

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

நயனுடையான் நல்கூர்ந்தா னாதல் செயும்நீர செய்யாது அமைகலா வாறு.
Nayanudaiyaan nalkoorndhaa naadhal seyumneera Seyyaadhu amaigalaa vaaru.

Meaning

For a kind person, being poor means only one thing: the pain of not being able to do the good things they always do.

Elementary

Imagine a kid who loves sharing snacks with everyone. If one day there are no snacks to share, the kid feels sad — not because they are hungry, but because they cannot give. That sadness is what ‘being poor’ means to a kind person.

University

Valluvar redefines poverty (nalkooral) from the benefactor's point of view: it is not the loss of personal comforts but the anguish of being unable to perform the benefactions that are his nature (seyum neera). Parimelazhagar underlines this: poverty for such a person is ‘not the inability to enjoy what he would enjoy.’ The verb amaigalaa (‘cannot rest content’) captures a moral restlessness, turning the absence of means into a spiritual rather than material deprivation. Paired with 218, the couplet completes Parimelazhagar's point that oppuravu is not to be abandoned because of want.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I do not pray to the Supreme Personality of Godhead for the eight perfections of mystic yoga, nor for salvation from repeated birth and death. I want only to stay among all the living entities and suffer all distresses on their behalf, so that they may be freed from suffering.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 9.21.12 [4]
Rantideva, having given away his last food and water, prays only to share and relieve the sufferings of all beings.
Like the Kural's nayanudaiyaan, his only real want is the ability to relieve others.
Christianity
“For if there be first a willing mind, it is accepted according to that a man hath, and not according to that he hath not.”
— 2 Corinthians 8:12 (KJV) [5]
Paul says what counts is the willing heart; gifts are judged by what a person has, not by what they lack.
Both locate generosity in the inner disposition that persists when means run out.
Islam
“Every Muslim has to give in charity.” The people asked, “O Allah's Prophet! If someone has nothing to give, what will he do?” He said, “He should work with his hands and benefit himself and also give in charity (from what he earns).”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1445 [6]
The Prophet teaches that even someone with nothing must find a way to give — by working, helping the needy, or doing good deeds.
Like the Kural, it treats the inability to give as a problem the good person must still address.
Judaism
“Anyone who gives a peruta to a poor person receives six blessings, and whoever consoles him with words of comfort and encouragement receives eleven blessings.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 9b (Sefaria, William Davidson ed.) [7]
Rabbi Yitzhak teaches that comforting the poor with words is even greater than giving money.
Offers the person without means a way to still do good, answering the Kural's anguish of inability.
Buddhism
In the Sasa Jataka, the Bodhisatta born as a hare eats only grass and reflects that he has no oil, rice, or beans to give any beggar who might come; when Sakka arrives disguised as a hungry brahmin, the hare offers his own body by leaping into the fire.
— Jataka No. 316, Sasa Jataka (trans. H. T. Francis and R. A. Neil, ed. E. B. Cowell) [8]
The hare's one concern is that he lacks anything fit to give a hungry guest, so he gives himself.
Dramatises the Kural's idea that a kind being's true poverty is the inability to give.
Jainism
“Let a man who regards both this world and the next, give what he can as he gets it; and if, through poverty, giving be impossible, to abstain from begging will be to give twice.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 5 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Nālaṭiyār tells the generous to give whatever they can, and counts it as a double gift when someone too poor to give at least does not burden others by begging.
Valluvar says that for the kind, poverty means only being unable to give; the Nālaṭiyār also treats poverty as the point where giving becomes impossible, and finds a form of giving even there.
Sikhism
“The God-conscious being delights in doing good to others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 273, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani Sahib), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan describes the brahm giani, the one who knows God, as someone whose joy lies in doing good to others.
The Kural says that for the kind-hearted, poverty means only the pain of being unable to do good; Guru Arjan likewise makes doing good to others the very delight of the awakened person.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor old couple, welcome disguised Zeus and Hermes with the little they have and even try to kill their only goose to feed them.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611–724 [11]
The couple's poverty does not stop them; they strain to give more than they have.
Shows the ache of the kind-hearted to give despite want, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“It is not, therefore, the thing which is done or given, but the spirit in which it is done or given, that must be considered, because a benefit exists, not in that which is done or given, but in the mind of the doer or giver.”
— Seneca, De Beneficiis 1.6 (trans. Aubrey Stewart) [12]
Seneca argues that a benefit lies in the giver's intention, not in the material gift.
Supports the Kural's view that the benefactor's identity lies in the will to give, which persists even without means.

Real Life Example

Oseola McCarty gives her life savings for scholarships
26 July 1995 · Hattiesburg, Mississippi, United States
Oseola McCarty, who left school in the sixth grade and spent most of her life washing and ironing other people's clothes by hand, gave 150,000 dollars, most of her life savings, to the University of Southern Mississippi in 1995 to fund scholarships for students in financial need. Asked about the gift, she said she did not regret any of it and only wished she had more to give. Local businesses and donors around the world added to the fund, and she received the Presidential Citizens Medal from President Bill Clinton that year.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A woman of very small means measured her life not by what she owned but by how much good she could do with it.
The kural says that for a kind-hearted person the only real poverty is being unable to do the good deeds that are his nature, and McCarty's one regret was that she did not have more to give.
