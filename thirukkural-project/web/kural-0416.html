Kural 416

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

எனைத்தானும் நல்லவை கேட்க அனைத்தானும் ஆன்ற பெருமை தரும்.
Enaiththaanum nallavai ketka anaiththaanum Aandra perumai tharum.

Meaning

Listen to good things, even if only a little. Even that little will bring you great honor.

Elementary

You don’t have to listen to a huge lecture. Even hearing one small good idea is worth it, because even a little bit of goodness can make you a much better person.

University

The kural balances எனைத்தானும் (‘however little’) against அனைத்தானும் (‘to that very extent’), so that the benefit is proportionate to what is heard, yet always leads to ஆன்ற பெருமை — full, well-rounded greatness. Two conditions are embedded: the content must be நல்லவை (good things), and the act must be real listening. The couplet thus both encourages even small, occasional listening and implicitly warns against hearing what is not good — a selectivity that parallels many traditions’ counsel about what one lets into the ear.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Shanti mantra “bhadraṃ karṇebhiḥ śṛṇuyāma devāḥ” — “O gods, may we hear with our ears what is auspicious; may we see with our eyes what is auspicious.
— Rig Veda 1.89.8; peace invocation of the Mundaka and Prashna Upanishads [4]
Recited at the opening of the Mundaka and Prashna Upanishads, this Rig Vedic prayer asks that the ear receive only what is good.
Both texts direct the ear specifically toward ‘good things’ (நல்லவை / bhadram).
Christianity
“Take heed what ye hear: with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you: and unto you that hear shall more be given.”
— Mark 4:24 (KJV) [5]
Jesus warns his hearers to be careful what they listen to, and promises increase to those who hear rightly.
Selective hearing of what is good leads to growth — the Kural’s ‘however little, it brings greatness’.
Islam
“Who listen to speech and follow the best of it. Those are the ones Allah has guided, and those are people of understanding.”
— Qur’an 39:18 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an praises those who listen discerningly and adopt the best of what they hear.
Both texts value hearing selectively for the good, and connect it with honour/understanding.
Judaism
Ben Zoma asks, “Who is wise?” and answers, “One who learns from every person,” citing the Psalm: “From all my teachers I have gained understanding.
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 [7]
Wisdom comes from gathering good teaching from every source, however humble.
Like the Kural, it urges taking in good wherever and in whatever measure it is found.
Buddhism
“Better than a thousand useless words is one useful word, hearing which one attains peace.”
— Dhammapada 100 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [8]
A single beneficial word heard is worth more than any quantity of empty speech.
Near-identical point: even a small amount of good hearing yields great benefit.
Jainism
“After listening to scriptures, a person comes to know what is good and what is sinful, having thus known through listening one ought to perform what leads to welfare.”
— Saman Suttam v. 245 (Samyagjnanasutra); the Prakrit is from the Dasavaikalika Sutra 4 (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [9]
This Jain verse (Prakrit 'socca janai kallanam') makes hearing the source of moral knowledge. From listening one learns good and evil and then acts for one's welfare.
Valluvar urges us to listen to what is good, however little, because it leads to greatness. The Jain verse likewise traces right action and welfare back to hearing.
Sikhism
“Within the mind are gems, jewels and rubies, if you listen to the Guru's Teachings, even once.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Japji Sahib (pauri 6), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the sixth pauri of Japji Sahib Guru Nanak says that hearing even one teaching of the Guru fills the mind with treasures.
The Kural says to listen to good things, however little, since even a little brings full greatness; Guru Nanak says even a single teaching heard yields jewels.
Greek Mythology
Warned by Circe, Odysseus stops his crew’s ears with wax so they cannot hear the Sirens, whose beautiful song lures sailors to death; only he listens, bound to the mast.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.39–54, 165–200 [11]
The Sirens are the classic Greek warning that not everything pleasant to hear is good.
The negative counterpart of the Kural: hear what is good (நல்லவை), and guard the ear against what destroys.
Philosophy
Seneca advises Lucilius to acquire each day something that will fortify him against poverty, death and other misfortunes, and to select one thought daily to digest thoroughly.
— Seneca, Epistulae Morales 2.4 [12]
Small daily portions of good teaching, well absorbed, build lasting strength of character.
Even a little good taken in regularly yields greatness — the Kural’s ‘however little’.

Real Life Example

Booker T. Washington overhears talk of Hampton Institute in a coal mine
c. 1872 · Malden, West Virginia, United States
As a teenager working in a coal mine near Malden, West Virginia, Booker T. Washington, who had been born into slavery, overheard two miners talking in the dark about a school in Virginia for Black students where poor students could work to pay for their board while learning a trade. After some months more in the mine and in domestic service, he set out for the Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute in the fall of 1872, arriving with only fifty cents. He graduated and went on to lead Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, becoming one of the most prominent educators in American history.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
A few words overheard in a mine changed the course of a life and, through Tuskegee, the education of thousands.
The kural says that listening to good things, however little, brings full greatness, and a single overheard conversation set Washington on the road to national prominence.
