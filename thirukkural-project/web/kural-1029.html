Kural 1029

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

இடும்பைக்கே கொள்கலம் கொல்லோ குடும்பத்தைக் குற்ற மறைப்பான் உடம்பு.
Idumbaikke kolkalam kollo kudumbaththaik kutra maraippaan udambu.

Meaning

Is the body of a person who protects their family from harm made only to hold suffering?

Elementary

Think of a parent who works late, carries heavy bags, and gets up at night when the baby cries. Their body is tired and sore. Valluvar asks: is that body only made for hardship? His answer is that their toil is also full of love and worth.

University

The couplet is a rhetorical question: is the udambu (உடம்பு, ‘body’) of the one who kutram maraippaan (குற்றம் மறைப்பான், ‘wards off harm’ from his kudumbam, குடும்பம், ‘family’) merely an idumbaikke kolkalam (இடும்பைக்கே கொள்கலம், ‘a vessel for suffering alone’)? The particle kollo (கொல்லோ) marks wonder or doubt. Varadarasanar reads it plainly as ‘is his body only a home for suffering?’, conceding the hardship; Kesikan and Pappaiah draw the implied answer, that it is also a vessel of joy; Karunanidhi says such a person is honoured as one born to bear suffering. Pope gives ‘Is not his body vase that various sorrows fill, / Who would his household screen from every ill?’ The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons. They arise from sense perception, O scion of Bharata, and one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that bodily pain and pleasure come and go like seasons and must be borne with steadiness.
It matches the Kural's picture of a body that must bear hardship for the sake of duty.
Christianity
Jesus teaches that no one has greater love than the person who lays down his life for his friends.
— John 15:13 (KJV) [4]
Self-sacrifice for others is presented as the highest form of love.
The Kural's body given over to hardship to protect the family is this kind of love.
Islam
“O you who have believed, protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones”
— Qur'an 66:6 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an commands believers to shield their families from ruin.
Like the Kural's family-protector, the believer is charged with guarding his household from harm.
Judaism
Nehemiah, rebuilding Jerusalem's walls under threat, tells the people not to fear but to remember the great and awesome Lord and fight for their brothers, sons, daughters, wives and homes.
— Nehemiah 4:8 (Hebrew numbering; 4:14 in English Bibles), Tanakh via Sefaria [6]
The builders work with one hand and keep a weapon in the other, to protect their families while they build.
It is the Kural's picture of a body spent in shielding one's family.
Buddhism
In the Mahakapi Jataka the Bodhisatta, born as a monkey king, stretches his own body as a bridge across a river so his troop can escape the king's archers, and is fatally hurt doing so.
— Mahakapi Jataka (Jataka No. 407, tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The monkey king turns his own body into the means of his people's safety.
It vividly answers the Kural's question about the body of the one who shields his family.
Jainism
“Mother, father, daughter-in-law, brother, wife, and sons will not be able to help me, when I suffer for my own deeds.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that no family member can share the suffering that one's own karma brings.
Jainism sees bodily suffering as one's own karma and the family as no refuge, so it offers a contrast to the Kural's ideal of suffering for one's family.
Sikhism
In 1675 the ninth Guru, Guru Tegh Bahadur, went to Delhi on behalf of Kashmiri Brahmins who were being forced to convert, refused to give up his faith himself, and was publicly executed on the emperor Aurangzeb's orders.
— M. A. Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), vol. 4, Life of Guru Tegh Bahadur [9]
Sikhs remember his martyrdom as giving his life to shield others' freedom of faith, people who were not even of his own religion.
The Kural asks whether the body of one who shields his people from harm is only a vessel for suffering; Guru Tegh Bahadur took suffering and death on his own body to shield those who came to him for protection.
Greek Mythology
Prometheus, chained to a rock as punishment for giving fire and the arts to humankind, endures endless bodily torment rather than betray those he protected.
— Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound [10]
His body becomes a vessel of suffering because he chose to shield and raise up humanity.
He embodies the Kural's question about the body of the one who protects his own.
Philosophy
“It is true of the good man too that he does many acts for the sake of his friends and his country, and if necessary dies for them; for he will throw away both wealth and honours and in general the goods that are objects of competition, gaining for himself nobility …”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.8, 1169a (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that the good man's sacrifices for others, even his life, are not losses, because he wins for himself the greater good of nobility.
This gives the answer the Kural's question implies: the protector's suffering body is not a mere vessel of pain, since his sacrifice is what makes him noble.

Real Life Example

Dostoevsky takes on his late brother's family and debts
1864-1865 · St Petersburg, Russia
When Fyodor Dostoevsky's brother and co-editor Mikhail died in 1864, Fyodor assumed financial responsibility for Mikhail's family and the debts of their journal Epoch, which collapsed in 1865. The years that followed were a time of acute financial hardship in which he wrote under heavy pressure and spent long periods abroad to escape creditors.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dostoevsky shouldered burdens that were not strictly his own, and they shaped years of his life and work.
The kural asks whether the body of one who shields his family from harm is only a vessel for suffering, and Dostoevsky's hard years carrying his brother's family are a vivid case.
