Kural 7

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

தனக்குவமை இல்லாதான் தாள்சேர்ந்தார்க் கல்லால் மனக்கவலை மாற்றல் அரிது.
Thanakku-uvamai illaadhaan thaal serndhaark kallaal, mana-kavalai maatral aridhu.

Meaning

Except for those who cling to the feet of the One beyond all comparison, it is very hard for anyone to be free of the mind’s anxieties.

Elementary

Everybody worries sometimes. This kural says the only way to truly stop worrying deep down is to hold onto something so great that nothing else can compare to it.

University

“Thanakku uvamai illaadhaan” (“the One with no likeness to Himself”) situates the removal of mental anxiety not in technique or willpower but in relationship to a reality that relativizes every other object of comparison. If nothing else is truly comparable, ordinary anxieties (always about something comparable — status, loss, gain) lose their grip. This mirrors a recurring move across theistic and philosophical traditions: relocating the anxious self’s center of gravity onto something that cannot be lost or reduced by comparison.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“To the seer, all things have verily become the Self: what delusion, what sorrow, can there be for him who beholds that oneness?”
— Isha Upanishad, verse 7 [3]
The text says that once someone truly sees all things as one with the Self, there’s no separate ‘other’ thing left to feel anxious about.
Matches the Kural’s logic — remove the sense of separateness (or find something incomparable) and anxiety has nothing left to attach to.
Christianity
“Be careful for nothing... and the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds.”
— Philippians 4:6-7 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells his readers not to be anxious about anything, but to bring every worry to God in prayer; in exchange, God's peace guards their hearts and minds.
A direct match for the Kural’s promise: real, lasting relief from anxiety, achieved specifically by turning to God.
Islam
“Verily, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest.”
— Qur'an 13:28, Ar-Ra'd [5]
This verse states as a general rule that hearts only truly settle down through remembering God — nothing else does the job as completely.
Almost a direct restatement of the Kural’s claim that only the incomparable One can truly end anxiety.
Judaism
“Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee.”
— Psalm 55:22 (Tanakh, Ketuvim / KJV) [4]
The psalmist’s advice is strikingly physical — literally hand your burden over to God, who will carry it and keep you steady.
The same transfer-of-burden logic the Kural implies: anxiety lifts when you have somewhere incomparable to put it.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that if fear arises while alone in the forest, a monk should recall the qualities of the Buddha, Dhamma, and Sangha — doing so makes the fear pass away.
— Dhajagga Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 11.3) [6]
Rather than fighting fear directly, the practice is to redirect attention toward something recognized as supremely trustworthy, which dissolves the fear.
A genuine Buddhist parallel to the Kural’s logic — anxiety dissolves by turning attention toward something beyond ordinary comparison, though Buddhism's 'object' is the Triple Gem rather than a singular incomparable God.
Greek Mythology
Orestes, tormented endlessly by the Furies for killing his mother, finds relief only when the goddess Athena convenes a formal trial and resolves his case.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides [7]
His anxiety isn’t something Orestes can think his way out of — it only ends when a higher authority intervenes and settles the matter for good.
Dramatizes the Kural’s claim in narrative form: ordinary human effort can’t end this kind of torment; only appeal to something greater finally can.
Philosophy
Awaiting execution on false charges, Boethius is consoled by Lady Philosophy, who argues that true happiness and peace of mind can only be found in the one highest Good, which she identifies with God.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy [8]
Boethius finds that fixing his attention on the highest, unconditional good is what finally settles a mind that fortune's ups and downs couldn't.
A near-perfect philosophical dramatization of the Kural’s exact claim — anxiety only truly ends by anchoring to something beyond all comparison.
