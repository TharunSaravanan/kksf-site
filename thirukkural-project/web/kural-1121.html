Kural 1121

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

பாலொடு தேன்கலந் தற்றே பணிமொழி வாலெயிறு ஊறிய நீர்.
Paalodu thaenkalandh thatrae panimozhi vaaleyiru ooriya neer.

Meaning

The wetness on the white teeth of this softly speaking girl is like milk and honey mixed together.

Elementary

Think of the sweetest drink you can imagine, warm milk with a spoon of honey stirred in. The poet says that being close to the person he loves feels that sweet. It is his way of saying that love makes everything about the beloved seem delightful.

University

Chapter 113 belongs to the kalaviyal (the section on secret, premarital love) and opens with the hero speaking after union, declaring the special excellence (sirappu) of the love itself rather than of the beloved's looks, which was the topic of ch. 112. Pani-mozhi (பணிமொழி, 'gentle, deferential speech') names the heroine by her soft voice, and vaal-eyiru (வாலெயிறு, 'white teeth') is a stock feature of the akam heroine; ooriya neer ('the water that wells up') is a decorous image for the sweetness of a kiss. Milk-and-honey (paalodu thaen) is the classical Tamil figure for perfect sweetness; Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss the line this way, Pope renders 'The dew on her white teeth, whose voice is soft and low, / Is as when milk and honey mingled flow', and the commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar reads the chapter's first five couplets as the hero's words. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In an Atharvaveda love charm the speaker declares that honey is on the tip and at the root of his tongue, asking that his speech and presence become as sweet as honey to the one he loves.
— Atharvaveda 1.34 (charm with licorice to secure a woman's love; cf. tr. Maurice Bloomfield, SBE 42) (paraphrase) [3]
The hymn uses honey (madhu) as the measure of sweetness that love should carry between two people.
Like the Kural, it reaches for honey on the tongue to express the sweetness of lovers' closeness.
Christianity
“Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth: for thy love is better than wine.”
— Song of Solomon 1:2 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs opens with the beloved longing for a kiss and ranking love above the best drink she knows.
The Kural likewise ranks the sweetness of the beloved's mouth above the finest drink, milk mixed with honey.
Islam
“rivers of milk the taste of which never changes, rivers of wine delicious to those who drink, and rivers of purified honey”
— Qur'an 47:15 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an describes Paradise with rivers of unspoiled milk and purified honey as images of perfect, lasting delight.
Milk and honey stand for supreme sweetness in both texts, though the Qur'an applies it to Paradise, not to a lover.
Judaism
“Thy lips, O my bride, drop honey— Honey and milk are under thy tongue”
— Song of Songs 4:11 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover of the Song of Songs says that honey and milk lie under his bride's tongue.
This is almost the Kural's own image: the beloved's mouth holds milk and honey.
Buddhism
In the opening suttas of the Anguttara Nikaya the Buddha says he knows of no other taste that so overpowers a man's mind as the taste of a woman, and likewise for a woman and the taste of a man.
— Anguttara Nikaya 1.1-10, Rupadi Vagga (paraphrase) [7]
The point is a warning: the sweetness lovers find in each other is the strongest of sensual bonds.
The Kural celebrates exactly this overpowering sweetness; Buddhism names it in order to loosen its grip.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain sermon on carelessness compares sensual pleasure to a fruit that tastes sweet but proves poisonous.
Where the Kural savours a sweetness like milk and honey, the Jain text warns that such sweetness is the bait of karmic bondage.
Sikhism
“My Dear Lord and Master, my Friend, speaks so sweetly.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 784, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of the bride-soul, Guru Arjan calls the Lord a friend (sajan) whose every word is sweet, using the Punjabi diminutive 'mith bolarha', the sweet-speaking one.
As the Kural's lover tastes milk and honey in the mouth of his soft-spoken beloved, the Guru delights in the sweetness of the Beloved's speech.
Greek Mythology
Homer introduces old Nestor, the clear-voiced speaker of Pylos, as a man from whose tongue speech flowed sweeter than honey.
— Homer, Iliad 1.247-249 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Greek epic, like Tamil poetry, uses honey as the measure of sweet and gentle speech.
The Kural's heroine is pani-mozhi, 'soft of speech', and her mouth is likened to honey in the same way.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that self-indulgence and temperance concern the pleasures of touch and taste, the pleasures humans share with other animals, and that the self-indulgent delight above all in touch.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.10 (1117b-1118b) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle these are the pleasures most in need of moderation because they are the most immediate.
The Kural lingers over exactly such a pleasure of taste and touch, treating it as the high point of love rather than as a test of temperance.

Real Life Example

Auguste Rodin's The Kiss
c. 1882–1898 · Paris, France
Around 1882 the French sculptor Auguste Rodin designed a pair of embracing lovers for his great bronze doorway The Gates of Hell, based on Paolo and Francesca from Dante's Divine Comedy. In 1886 he removed the group from the door because its mood of happiness did not suit the project, exhibited it on its own in 1887, and the public named it The Kiss. The French state commissioned a large marble version in 1888, which Rodin carved over nearly ten years and exhibited in 1898; it is now in the Musée Rodin in Paris.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Kiss became one of the most famous sculptures in the world and a lasting symbol of tender love.
The kural says the sweetness of the beloved's kiss is like milk mixed with honey, and Rodin's The Kiss is the best-known work of art devoted to that very moment of sweetness.
