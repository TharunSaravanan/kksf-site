Kural 307

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

சினத்தைப் பொருளென்று கொண்டவன் கேடு நிலத்தறைந்தான் கைபிழையா தற்று.
Sinaththaip porulendru kondavan kedu Nilaththaraindhaan kai pizhaiyaadhatru.

Meaning

A person who treats anger as something precious will surely be ruined, just as someone who hits the ground with their hand will surely hurt that hand.

Elementary

If you slap the floor as hard as you can, the floor doesn't feel anything, but your hand really hurts. Using anger as your 'power' is the same: the person who uses it gets hurt the most.

University

The target here is a specific attitude: treating anger as porul, a thing of worth or a useful tool, whether as a mark of strength, a way to command respect, or a means of getting results. The simile of striking the ground (nilaththu araindhaan) is precise: the ground is unharmed while the striker's hand is certain to be injured, so anger's force rebounds on its user. Kai pizhaiyaadhu ('the hand does not escape') stresses certainty, not mere probability. The couplet enters a long-running debate, found in Aristotle and Seneca, about whether anger is a useful servant of virtue; Valluvar sides firmly with those who deny it any value.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The sage Durvasa, enraged that King Ambarisha broke his fast before Durvasa returned, creates a fiery demon to kill the king; Vishnu's discus destroys the demon and then pursues Durvasa through all the worlds until he humbly seeks the forgiveness of the very king he attacked.
— Bhagavata Purana, Canto 9, chapters 4-5 [3]
Durvasa, famous for wielding anger as power, finds his own wrath turned back upon himself.
A narrative version of the struck ground: the anger he valued as a weapon rebounds on its user.
Christianity
“Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein: and he that rolleth a stone, it will return upon him.”
— Proverbs 26:27 (KJV) [4]
The proverb states that harm aimed at others returns to its author.
The same rebound logic as the kural's hand striking the ground.
Islam
“...but the evil plot does not encompass except its own people.”
— Quran 35:43 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran states that malicious scheming ultimately surrounds and ruins those who devise it.
Like the kural, harm that springs from hostility returns upon its originator.
Judaism
“Be not hasty in thy spirit to be angry; For anger resteth in the bosom of fools.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:9 (JPS 1917) [6]
Kohelet warns against quick anger, saying it 'rests' or is cherished in the bosom of fools.
The fool who keeps anger in his bosom is the one who 'takes anger as a treasure' in the kural, and both texts expect his undoing.
Buddhism
“Like fine dust thrown against the wind, evil falls back upon that fool who offends an inoffensive, pure and guiltless man.”
— Dhammapada 125 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares harming the innocent to throwing dust into the wind, where it blows back into the thrower's face.
A near-identical physical image to the hand striking the ground: the force returns to its source.
Jainism
“The ascetic jumped up angrily, thinking, ‘I’ll kill the young disciple,’ and began to run. Blind with anger, he ran against a pillar and was killed.” … “Flaming with anger like a fire with an oblation, he seized an axe with a sharp edge and ran forward … As he fell, the sharp axe fell on him and split his head in two.”
— Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X (Mahāvīracaritra), ch. 3, part 10, 'Episode of Caṇḍakauśika' (tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org) [8]
This is the back-story of the serpent Caṇḍakauśika. In two successive lives a man who treated his rage as a weapon was killed by it: first he ran into a pillar, then his own axe split his head. Hemacandra comments that 'anger which has a sharp continuity goes along into another birth.'
Valluvar compares the man who prizes anger to someone who strikes the ground and only hurts his own hand. In Hemacandra's story the angry man wields his anger, and in the end his own axe, and the blow comes back on him.
Sikhism
“Fareed, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks Sheikh Farid advises against answering blows with blows. He counsels humility instead of retaliation.
The Kural says one who treats anger as useful will be hurt as surely as a hand that strikes the ground. Farid's advice not to strike back rejects anger as a tool of response.
Greek Mythology
Achilles nurses his wrath against Agamemnon as a matter of honour and refuses to fight; the result is the death of his dearest friend Patroclus, after which he bitterly curses strife and anger.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 9, 16, and 18 [10]
Achilles treats his anger as something precious, a defence of his honour, and it destroys what he loved most.
The anger he valued rebounds on him, as the hand striking the earth cannot escape pain.
Philosophy
“...and very like a falling rock which breaks itself to pieces upon the very thing which it crushes.”
— Seneca, On Anger (De Ira), Book I.1 (trans. Aubrey Stewart, 1900) [11]
Seneca's opening description of anger compares it to a falling rock that shatters on what it hits; later he rejects Aristotle's view that anger is a useful 'weapon' for virtue.
Both the image (force that shatters its source) and the argument (anger has no real value) match the kural.

Real Life Example

General Patton's slapping incidents in Sicily
August 1943 · Army evacuation hospitals, Sicily, Italy
During the Sicily campaign, Lieutenant General George S. Patton visited army hospitals and struck two soldiers suffering from what would today be diagnosed as combat stress, accusing them of cowardice. When the incidents became known, General Eisenhower reprimanded him and he was ordered to apologise personally to the two soldiers and to his entire Seventh Army. He lost his Mediterranean command and was passed over for command of American ground forces in the Normandy invasion, which went instead to Omar Bradley.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Patton treated harsh anger as a tool of leadership, and the episode badly damaged the career of one of the most gifted commanders of the war.
The kural says ruin surely comes to one who treats anger as something valuable, just as the hand that strikes the ground cannot escape injury, and Patton's blows rebounded on his own career.
