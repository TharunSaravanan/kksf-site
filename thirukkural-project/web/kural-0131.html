Kural 131

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

ஒழுக்கம் விழுப்பந் தரலான் ஒழுக்கம் உயிரினும் ஓம்பப் படும்.
Ozhukkam vizhuppam tharalaan ozhukkam Uyirinum oampap padum.

Meaning

Good behaviour is what makes a person truly great. So guard it even more carefully than your own life.

Elementary

Think of your good behavior as the most precious treasure you own, even more precious than your own body. It is what makes people trust and respect you, so you protect it more than anything else.

University

The chapter opens with a deliberately extreme ranking: ozhukkam (conduct, from ozhuku, ‘to proceed along a course’) is to be guarded (ompa) above uyir, life itself. The reason clause, vizhuppam tharalaan (‘because it confers eminence’), grounds the claim in what conduct produces rather than in divine command; bare life, as Pope's rendering notes, is something ‘all men share’, while conduct is what distinguishes. The word ozhukkam frames the first line at both ends, so the verse itself performs the act of guarding. Parimelazhagar reads ozhukkam as the conduct proper to one's station, whereas modern commentators (Varadarasanar, Karunanidhi) make it universal. The idea that some values outrank survival recurs widely, from Socrates' refusal to weigh life against right action to the rabbinic rule that certain sins must be refused even on pain of death.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The rule of conduct is transcendent law, whether it be taught in the revealed texts or in the sacred tradition; hence a twice-born man who possesses regard for himself, should be always careful to (follow) it.”
— Manusmriti 1.108 (tr. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Manu calls ācāra, the rule of conduct, the highest law, and tells anyone with self-respect to follow it with constant care.
Like the Kural, it puts conduct at the top of all values and asks that it be guarded vigilantly.
Christianity
“be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life.”
— Revelation 2:10 (KJV) [4]
The risen Christ tells the church at Smyrna to stay faithful even if it costs them their lives, and promises a crown in return.
Both place faithful conduct above survival and attach a reward of eminence (a ‘crown’ in one, vizhuppam in the other) to it.
Islam
The Prophet is reported to have said that he was sent to perfect (complete) good character.
— Muwatta Malik, Book 47 (Good Character), Hadith 8 [5]
In this saying, the Prophet describes the whole purpose of his mission as bringing good character to completion.
Both treat conduct and character as the central human good, though the hadith does not rank it above life.
Judaism
The rabbis ruled that for three transgressions (idolatry, forbidden sexual relations and murder) a person must accept death rather than commit them: yehareg ve'al ya'avor, ‘let him be killed and not transgress.’
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 74a [6]
Jewish law normally lets almost any commandment be set aside to save life, but for these three core wrongs it requires a person to give up life instead.
This puts into law the Kural's claim that some conduct must be guarded even above life.
Buddhism
“Better it is to live one day virtuous and meditative than to live a hundred years immoral and uncontrolled.”
— Dhammapada 110 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha measures a life by its virtue, not its length. One good day is worth more than a century of bad conduct.
Both value conduct over the mere continuation of life.
Jainism
“Rājīmatī to the monk Rathanemi, who tried to seduce her: “Fie upon you, famous knight, who want to quaff the vomited drink for the sake of this life; it would be better for you to die.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 22.40–46, quoting 22.42 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Rājīmatī, true to her vows, tells a lapsing monk that death is preferable to breaking his conduct, and her words turn him back to the Law; the verse also recurs in the Daśavaikālika (2.7).
Valluvar says conduct must be guarded above life itself, and Rājīmatī states that rule in its starkest form: better to die than fall from one's vows.
Sikhism
In 1675 the ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, was executed in Delhi by the Mughal authorities. He is remembered as the second Sikh martyr and for his stand for religious freedom; Sikh tradition holds that he accepted death rather than yield.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Tegh Bahadur' (Tamanna Nangia, fact-checked by Britannica Editors) [9]
Sikh memory treats Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom as the model of a principle held more dearly than one's own life.
The Kural ranks right conduct above life itself; the Guru's martyrdom is Sikhism's defining example of exactly that ranking.
Greek Mythology
Antigone buries her brother Polyneices against King Creon's decree, knowing the penalty is death, because she holds the unwritten laws of the gods above her own survival.
— Sophocles, Antigone (c. 441 BCE) [10]
Antigone would rather die doing what is right than live by breaking a sacred duty.
She guards right conduct above her life, just as the Kural asks.
Philosophy
“a man who is good for anything ought not to calculate the chance of living or dying; he ought only to consider whether in doing anything he is doing right or wrong”
— Plato, Apology 28b (tr. B. Jowett) [11]
At his trial, Socrates says a good person does not weigh survival at all. The only question is whether an action is right.
This is almost exactly the Kural's ranking: right conduct first, life second.

Real Life Example

Jan Hus refuses to recant at the Council of Constance
6 July 1415 · Constance (Konstanz), Germany
The Czech priest and reformer Jan Hus travelled to the Council of Constance in 1414 expecting to explain his views, but he was imprisoned soon after he arrived. At his hearings in June 1415 he was presented with a list of teachings attributed to him and urged to recant in order to save his life; he refused, and maintained that he did not hold all of the teachings as charged. On 6 July 1415 the council condemned him as a heretic and handed him over to the secular authorities, who executed him by burning that same day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Hus chose death over disowning what he believed, and his execution made him a lasting national and religious symbol in Bohemia and an inspiration to later reformers such as Martin Luther.
The kural ranks right conduct above life itself, and Hus, offered his life in exchange for a recantation he considered false, guarded his integrity at the cost of his life.
