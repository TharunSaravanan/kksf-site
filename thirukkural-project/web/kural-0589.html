Kural 589

Detectives · Otraadal · ஒற்றாடல்

ஒற்றெற் றுணராமை ஆள்க உடன்மூவர் சொற்றொக்க தேறப் படும்.
Otret runaraamai aalga udanmoovar Sotrokka thaerap padum.

Meaning

Use spies so that none of them knows about the others. When three such spies report the same thing, you can believe it.

Elementary

If three students are each asked separately what happened at recess, and they can’t talk to each other first, but all three tell the same story, the teacher can be pretty sure it is true. This kural says a wise leader checks the truth the same way.

University

The first line says to employ (ஆள்க) spies in a way that one does not become aware (உணராமை) of another. The second says that when the words of three (மூவர் சொல்) agree together (தொக்க), the thing may be trusted (தேறப்படும்). There is a real textual variant in the first word. ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print ஒற்றெற் றுணராமை, while thirukkural-thamizh.org prints ஒற்றொற் றுணராமை, i.e. ஒற்று ஒற்று உணராமை, ‘one spy not knowing another spy’. That is the sense every commentator, including Parimelazhagar, gives, and the -எற்று form is best explained as a sandhi or scribal variant. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read the second line as ‘if the three agree, take it as true’. Pappaiah is more cautious: even when three agree, examine it before accepting. The rule is strikingly close to Arthashastra 1.12, which requires agreement from three independent sources and says the spy-units must not know one another.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Neither the institutes of espionage nor they (the wandering spies) shall know each other … When the information thus received from these three different sources is exactly of the same version, it shall be held reliable. If they (the three sources) frequently differ, the spies concerned shall either be punished in secret or dismissed.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.12 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [4]
Kautilya requires spy networks that do not know one another and accepts a report only when three independent sources agree.
Spies unaware of one another and three agreeing sources: both halves of the Kural, point for point.
Christianity
In the story of Susanna, the young Daniel separates the two elders who have falsely accused her and questions each alone about which tree the alleged act happened under. One says a mastic tree and the other a holm oak, their lie is exposed, and Susanna is saved.
— Susanna (Daniel 13 in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles; KJV Apocrypha), vv. 51-59 (paraphrase) [5]
By keeping the witnesses apart, Daniel makes sure their stories are truly independent, so their disagreement shows the truth.
Keeping informants from knowing one another and then comparing their words is the Kural’s method exactly.
Islam
“And bring to witness two witnesses from among your men … That is more just in the sight of Allāh and stronger as evidence and more likely to prevent doubt between you.”
— Quran 2:282 (Sahih International, excerpt) [6]
The Quran requires more than one witness for important transactions, because corroboration strengthens evidence and removes doubt.
Several witnesses to remove doubt reflects the Kural’s agreement of three.
Judaism
“And afterward they bring in the second witness and examine him in the same manner. If their statements are found to be congruent the judges then discuss the matter.”
— Mishnah Sanhedrin 3:6 (William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz translation, Sefaria) [7]
The Mishnah requires witnesses to be questioned one at a time with the others sent out of the room, and their testimony counts only if their separate accounts agree.
Separate examination so that no witness knows another’s answer, followed by a check for agreement, is the Kural’s procedure in a courtroom.
Buddhism
“Without approval and without scorn, but carefully studying the sentences word by word, one should trace them in the Discourses and verify them by the Discipline.”
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahāparinibbāna Sutta 4.8-11 (trans. Sister Vajira & Francis Story) [8]
In the ‘four great references’, the Buddha says that any claim to be his teaching must be checked against two independent standards, the Suttas and the Vinaya, before it is accepted.
Accepting a report only when independent sources agree is the principle behind the Kural’s three agreeing spies.
Jainism
“He who understands the true nature of all substances by means of all proofs (pramâna) and nayas, believes by a complete course of study.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 28.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Jain epistemology holds that a thing is properly known only when it is examined through valid means of knowledge (pramāṇa) and from several partial standpoints (naya), which are then brought together.
The Kural trusts a matter only when three independent spies' reports agree; the Jain method likewise reaches confidence by combining several separate lines of evidence and viewpoint rather than relying on one.
Sikhism
“Meeting with the council, my doubts were not dispelled. The chiefs did not give me satisfaction.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 621, Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan describes turning to the village council (the panch) and to the chiefs, and finding that neither could set his anxious mind at rest.
The Kural trusts a matter once three independent spies agree; Guru Arjan's verse is the nearest Sikh reflection on seeking certainty from several authorities, and it is partly a counterpoint, since their word did not settle his doubt.
Greek Mythology
To test the oracles, King Croesus sent separate messengers to several shrines at once, each to ask on the hundredth day what the king was doing at that moment. Only Delphi (and, by some accounts, Amphiaraus) answered correctly, that he was boiling a tortoise and lamb in a bronze pot.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.46-49 (paraphrase) [11]
Croesus set up independent, simultaneous inquiries so that the sources could not coordinate, then trusted only those that proved accurate.
Independent informants who cannot confer, judged by whether their answers match reality, is the Kural’s method in legendary form.
Philosophy
Thucydides says he did not rely on the first account he heard or on his own impressions, but tested every report as rigorously as he could, which was hard because different eyewitnesses gave differing accounts of the same events.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 1.22 (paraphrase) [12]
The founder of critical history describes his method as cross-examining several independent witnesses.
Comparing independent reports and accepting only what holds up is what the Kural asks of a king.

Real Life Example

Independent reports and air photographs confirm the German rocket site at Peenemunde
1939-1943 · Peenemunde, Germany, and RAF Medmenham, England
From 1939 British intelligence received scattered reports, including the anonymous Oslo Report, that Germany was developing long-range rockets and other secret weapons, but these were at first doubted. Reports from other sources pointed to Peenemunde on the Baltic coast, and photo-reconnaissance flights between 1942 and 1943 were examined by interpreters at RAF Medmenham, where Constance Babington Smith's section searched for secret weapons. When separate lines of intelligence and the aerial photographs agreed, Bomber Command attacked Peenemunde on the night of 17-18 August 1943 in Operation Hydra.
— Sources: [13] [14]
No single report was trusted on its own; action came only when separate, unconnected sources told the same story.
The kural says to use agents who do not know of one another and to trust a matter when their separate reports agree, which is how Britain confirmed the threat from Peenemunde before striking it.
