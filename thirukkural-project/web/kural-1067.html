Kural 1067

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

இரப்பன் இரப்பாரை எல்லாம் இரப்பின் கரப்பார் இரவன்மின் என்று.
Irappan irappaarai ellaam irappin karappaar iravanmin endru.

Meaning

I beg everyone who begs: if you must beg, never beg from people who hide what they have.

Elementary

If you ever really need help, ask someone with a kind and open heart, not someone who pretends to have nothing. The poet even says 'please' to everyone who has to ask for help, because he cares about them.

University

The couplet is a striking play on one verb, irattal ('to beg'): irappan ('I beg') irappaarai ellaam ('all beggars') irappin ('if you beg') karappaar iravanmin ('do not beg of concealers'). Valluvar thus makes himself a petitioner on behalf of petitioners, a rhetorical humility the commentators note. Karappaar (கரப்பார், 'those who conceal') recalls karappu in ch. 106 and 1061's karavaadhu; the advice, in the line following Parimelazhagar as given by Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar, is that a person forced to ask should at least avoid those who hide what they have. Pope renders it 'One thing I beg of beggars all, 'If beg ye may, / Of those who hide their wealth, beg not, I pray'.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Taittiriya Upanishad's parting instruction to students says that gifts should be given with faith and not without faith, with generosity, with modesty, with awe and with understanding.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.3 (paraphrase) [3]
The Upanishad treats a grudging or faithless gift as defective giving.
The Kural's 'concealers' are exactly the grudging givers the Upanishad rejects, and it warns askers away from them.
Christianity
“Eat thou not the bread of him that hath an evil eye, neither desire thou his dainty meats: For as he thinketh in his heart, so is he: Eat and drink, saith he to thee; but his heart is not with thee.”
— Proverbs 23:6-7 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns against taking food from a stingy host whose heart is not in the giving.
This is the Kural's advice: do not seek from those who give with a hidden, unwilling heart.
Islam
“Who are stingy and enjoin upon [other] people stinginess and conceal what Allāh has given them of His bounty - and We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment -”
— Qur'an 4:37 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an condemns those who conceal the bounty God has given them.
The Qur'an's 'concealers' match the Kural's karappaar, the very people askers are told to avoid.
Judaism
“He who desires that he himself should not give and that others too should not give: he is a wicked man.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:13 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Pirkei Avot sorts givers into four types and calls the one who neither gives nor wants others to give wicked.
The Kural's karappaar belong to this worst type, so the asker should not turn to them.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada sets the miser against the wise man who delights in giving.
The Kural tells askers to go only to the open-handed, never to the miser the Dhammapada condemns.
Jainism
“Will the excellent then go for alms to any others but to those who will embrace them and say, Come to my house and eat?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 3 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet says that the noble, if they must ask, go only to those who welcome them warmly.
This is the Kural's advice in positive form: ask only of the open-hearted, never of concealers.
Sikhism
“The Great Giver keeps on giving, while those who receive grow weary of receiving.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Japji Sahib (pauri 3), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak describes God as a Giver whose giving never stops, so that it is the receivers who tire first.
The Kural begs every beggar never to ask from people who hide what they have; Guru Nanak points to the one Giver who never holds anything back.
Greek Mythology
“Give to one who gives, but do not give to one who does not give. A man gives to the free-handed, but no one gives to the close-fisted.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 354-355 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod contrasts the free-handed and the close-fisted and advises dealing with the generous.
Like the Kural, Hesiod separates open givers from tight-fisted ones and steers people towards the former.
Philosophy
Seneca observes that a benefit given slowly and grudgingly, after the asker has been kept waiting, loses its value, since the giver who hesitates has long been unwilling.
— Seneca, On Benefits 2.1-2 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) (paraphrase) [11]
Seneca treats reluctant, hidden unwillingness as spoiling a gift.
The Kural's karappaar give, if at all, in just this spirit, so askers should avoid them.

Real Life Example

Alexandre Petion equips Simon Bolivar for a new campaign
1815-1816 · Les Cayes and Port-au-Prince, Haiti
After defeats on the mainland and a period of exile in Jamaica, Simon Bolivar went to Haiti at the end of 1815. President Alexandre Petion supplied him with arms, munitions, provisions and men for a new expedition against Spanish rule, asking in return only that Bolivar abolish slavery in the lands he freed. Bolivar sailed for Venezuela in 1816 proclaiming the abolition of slavery as one of his aims.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Historians regard Petion's open-handed help as decisive for the revival of the independence struggle in northern South America.
The kural begs those who must ask to ask only of people who do not hide what they have, and Bolivar found in Petion a giver who gave freely, asking nothing for himself.
