Kural 132

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

பரிந்தோம்பிக் காக்க ஒழுக்கம் தெரிந்தோம்பித் தேரினும் அஃதே துணை.
Parindhoambik kaakka ozhukkam therindhoambith Therinum aqdhe thunai.

Meaning

Work hard to guard your good behaviour. Think about everything else, and you will find that good behaviour is the only friend that truly stays with you.

Elementary

Imagine testing every toy, game and friend to find the one that will stay with you forever. Valluvar says that after checking everything, the one thing that never leaves you is being good, so take good care of it.

University

Two phrases of care frame the couplet: parindhu ompi kaakka (‘guard it, cherishing it even through pain’) and therindhu ompi therinum (‘even after examining and weighing all’). The second line sounds almost empirical. After surveying the alternatives (wealth, learning, kin, other virtues), one finds that conduct is the only thunai, companion or support. Parimelazhagar and Pappaiah gloss thunai as the aid that goes with a person into this life and the next, which ties the couplet to a widespread image: good conduct is the one thing that ‘goes with’ a person at death. Karunanidhi stresses bearing hardship to keep it. Aristotle offers a close philosophical parallel when he calls virtuous activity more lasting than even scientific knowledge.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For in the next world neither father, nor mother, nor wife, nor sons, nor relations stay to be his companions; spiritual merit alone remains (with him).”
— Manusmriti 4.239 (tr. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Manu says every human tie falls away at death, and only the merit a person has built by right living goes on with them.
This mirrors the Kural's ‘it alone is the companion’: after every alternative is weighed, conduct is what remains.
Christianity
“Take fast hold of instruction; let her not go: keep her; for she is thy life.”
— Proverbs 4:13 (KJV) [4]
The father in Proverbs tells his son to hold on to moral instruction and never let it go, because it is life itself.
It shares the Kural's language of guarding and holding fast to the discipline of right living.
Islam
“When carried to his grave, a dead person is followed by three, two of which return (after his burial) and one remains with him: his relative, his property, and his deeds follow him; relatives and his property go back while his deeds remain with him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6514 [5]
The Prophet says that of everything that follows a person to the grave, only their deeds stay with them.
This matches the Kural's claim that conduct is the one true companion.
Judaism
“when a man passes away there accompany him neither gold nor silver, nor precious stones nor pearls, but Torah and good deeds alone”
— Pirkei Avot 6:9 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Yose ben Kisma turns down a fortune because only Torah and good deeds go with a person when they die.
Both identify right conduct as the companion that outlasts every other possession.
Buddhism
“Good is virtue until life's end, good is faith that is steadfast, good is the acquisition of wisdom, and good is the avoidance of evil.”
— Dhammapada 333 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha praises virtue (sīla) that is kept all the way to the end of life.
Like the Kural, it treats virtue as something to be guarded for a whole lifetime.
Jainism
“Neither his kinsmen, nor his friends, nor his sons, nor his relations will share his suffering, he alone has to bear it; for the Karman follows the doer. Leaving behind bipeds and quadrupeds, his fields, his house, his wealth, his corn, and everything; against his will, and accompanied only by his Karman, he enters a new existence, either a good or a bad one.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 13.23–24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The sage Citra tells King Brahmadatta that at death everything is left behind and only one's karma, the fruit of one's conduct, goes along.
Valluvar finds after weighing everything that conduct alone is the companion that stays; Citra's sermon makes karma, the fruit of conduct, the only thing that goes with a person into the next life.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, as many as are the sins one commits, so many are the chains around his neck. If he possesses virtues, then the chains are cut away; these virtues are his brothers, his true brothers.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 595, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says faults bind a person like chains, while virtues free him and stand by him like real kin.
The Kural finds conduct to be the one true companion after weighing every alternative; Guru Nanak calls virtues a person's only true brothers.
Greek Mythology
In Prodicus' fable of the Choice of Heracles, Virtue offers the young hero a hard road but calls herself a faithful helper and ally in labor, in peace and in war, while Vice offers only easy pleasures.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21–34 (Prodicus' ‘Choice of Heracles’) [10]
Heracles is shown that Virtue is a companion who stays with you through every hardship.
After comparing the alternatives, the chooser finds virtue to be the one reliable companion, as in the Kural's second line.
Philosophy
“no function of man has so much permanence as virtuous activities (these are thought to be more durable even than knowledge of the sciences)”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.10, 1100b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that virtuous activity is the most stable and lasting thing a human being can have, more lasting even than knowledge.
Both conclude, after comparison, that conduct is the most dependable human possession.

Real Life Example

Boethius writes the Consolation of Philosophy in prison
c. 524-526 · Ostrogothic Kingdom of Italy (now Italy)
Boethius, a Roman senator who had become Master of Offices, one of the most senior officials at the court of the Ostrogothic king Theoderic, fell out with others at court, was accused of treason and of practising magic, and was imprisoned and later executed. While awaiting death he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy, an imagined dialogue in prose and verse between the prisoner and a lady who personifies Philosophy. In it Philosophy distinguishes the goods of fortune, such as riches, status and power, from the true goods of virtue and wisdom, and argues that his fall had not taken from him the things of real worth.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Written by a man who had lost wealth, office and freedom, the book became one of the most widely read works of the Middle Ages and a classic statement that virtue is the one possession fortune cannot remove.
Like the kural, Boethius weighed wealth, rank and power one by one and concluded that virtue alone remained a true companion when everything else was gone.
