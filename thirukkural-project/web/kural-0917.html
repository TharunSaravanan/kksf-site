Kural 917

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

நிறைநெஞ்சம் இல்லவர் தோய்வார் பிறநெஞ்சிற் பேணிப் புணர்பவர் தோள்.
Nirainenjam illavar thoyvaar piranenjir penip punarpavar thol.

Meaning

Only people without a steady, self-controlled heart will go to the arms of women who hug them while their own hearts are set on someone else.

Elementary

Someone who is pretending to be your friend while really thinking about your video games is not giving you their heart. Valluvar says only a person who cannot control their own wishes keeps running after friends like that.

University

Nirai-nenjam (நிறைநெஞ்சம்) is ‘a full or restrained heart’; nirai is the classical virtue of self-control, the power to hold the mind in check, which Mu. Varadarasanar glosses as ‘the strength to rule one’s heart’. The women are those who embrace while pira-nenjil peni (பிறநெஞ்சிற் பேணி), ‘cherishing something else in their heart’, which Puliyur Kesigan and Solomon Pappaiah identify as the money they want. The couplet thus contrasts two divided states: the women’s hearts are elsewhere, and the men lack a governed heart of their own. Parimelazhagar connects nirai with the self-mastery praised in chapter 13 (Adakkamudaimai). Both base sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Katha Upanishad’s chariot image, the senses are horses and the mind the reins; for one who lacks understanding and whose mind is never held firm, the senses run wild like a charioteer’s vicious horses.
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.3-6 [3]
The Upanishad teaches that an unrestrained mind lets the senses drag a person wherever they go.
Kural 917’s men without nirai-nenjam are exactly such charioteers who have lost the reins.
Christianity
“He that hath no rule over his own spirit is like a city that is broken down, and without walls.”
— Proverbs 25:28 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs compares a person without self-control to a city with no defenses.
Valluvar’s nirai-nenjam illavar are this undefended city.
Islam
“But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination,” (the next verse adds: then Paradise will be his refuge).”
— Qur’an 79:40-41 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises the one who restrains the self from its desires.
This is the nirai-nenjam that Kural 917 says the weak lack.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city’.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma defines true strength as mastery of one’s own impulses.
Kural 917 measures a man the same way: those without that mastery are the ones who fall.
Buddhism
“For a person tormented by evil thoughts, who is passion-dominated and given to the pursuit of pleasure, his craving steadily grows. He makes the fetter strong, indeed.”
— Dhammapada 349 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says an undisciplined mind makes the fetter of craving stronger.
Valluvar’s men without a governed heart tighten that fetter by seeking such company.
Jainism
“One man (women) have in their heart, another in their words, and another still in their actions. Therefore a monk should not trust women, knowing that they are full of deceit.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga warns that the one whose embrace a man receives may have her heart set on someone else.
This mirrors Kural 917's women who embrace while 'cherishing another in their hearts' (pira-nenjil peni), and the steady, self-governed heart (nirai-nenjam) needed to resist them.
Sikhism
“The Dark Age of Kali Yuga is the vessel, filled with the wine of sexual desire; the mind is the drunkard.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 553, Raag Bihaagraa, Bihaagre ki Vaar, Salok (Mardana 1), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok addressed to Mardana, Guru Nanak pictures sexual desire as wine and the unruly mind as the one who keeps drinking it. The cure he offers is to make truth, good deeds and modesty the mind’s real food and drink.
The Kural says only a heart without self-mastery seeks arms that belong elsewhere, and this salok locates the problem in a mind that cannot stop drinking desire.
Greek Mythology
Circe turns Odysseus’ companions into swine with her enchanted food, but Odysseus, protected by the herb moly from Hermes, keeps his mind and is not changed.
— Homer, Odyssey 10 [10]
The myth separates those overcome by enchantment from the one who keeps control of himself.
Kural 917’s nirai-nenjam is Odysseus’ moly: without it, men are transformed by seduction.
Philosophy
“If you see an attractive person, you will find that self-restraint is the ability you have against your desire.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 10 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus teaches that each temptation calls on a matching inner capacity.
That capacity is Valluvar’s nirai, and those who lack it are the ones Kural 917 describes.

Real Life Example

The Countess of Castiglione is sent to win Napoleon III for Italy
1855-1857 · Paris, France
Virginia Oldoini, Countess of Castiglione, a cousin of the Piedmontese statesman Count Cavour, was sent to Paris to win the French emperor Napoleon III over to the cause of Italian unification. She caused a sensation at the French court and soon, though briefly, became the emperor's mistress. The relationship ended around 1857, after she was ordered to leave Paris for a time following an attack on the emperor in which she had no part.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her attachment to the emperor was, by the account of her own sponsors, a means to a political end.
The kural speaks of women who embrace a man while their hearts are set on something else, and the Countess's mission for Italy is a documented case of exactly that.
