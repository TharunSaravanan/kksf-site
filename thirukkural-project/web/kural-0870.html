Kural 870

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

கல்லான் வெகுளும் சிறுபொருள் எஞ்ஞான்றும் ஒல்லானை ஒல்லா தொளி.
Kallaan vekulum siruporul enjnaandrum ollaanai ollaa tholi.

Meaning

A person who will not take even the small, easy win of fighting an enemy with no learning will never become famous.

Elementary

If you won’t even try an easy level of a game, how will you ever beat the hard ones and earn a trophy? Valluvar says that someone who shies away from small, easy challenges never earns a great name.

University

The syntax is compressed: kallaan vekulum siruporul (கல்லான் வெகுளும் சிறுபொருள்) is ‘the small gain of being angry with (fighting) the unlearned man’, and ollaanai ollaadhu oli (ஒல்லானை ஒல்லாது ஒளி) says that light, i.e. fame, will not unite with the one who does not obtain it. Varadarasanar on thirukkural.net reads: one who cannot do the easy thing of opposing the unlearned will never gain glory; Pappaiah adds that the gain from fighting one ignorant of the treatises is small, yet the king who disdains it earns no renown; Kalaignar has fame mock such a man for hesitating even before untrained foes. Note: ytamizh.com’s Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar glosses for 870 are misplaced (they discuss forgiving old friends, the theme of kural 810); the couplet text itself is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna tells Arjuna that if he refuses to fight this righteous battle he will abandon his duty and his good name, and people will speak of his dishonour for ever.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.33-34 [3]
For a warrior, shrinking from a fight he ought to take means the loss of glory.
The Kural likewise says that glory never comes to one who will not take up a fight within his reach.
Christianity
Jesus teaches that the one who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much, and the one unjust in the least is unjust also in much.
— Luke 16:10 (KJV) [4]
Small tasks are the test that qualifies a person for greater ones.
Valluvar’s man who will not take the small gain shows he is unfit for great glory.
Islam
“O you who have believed, what is [the matter] with you that, when you are told to go forth in the cause of Allāh, you adhere heavily to the earth?”
— Qur’an 9:38 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an rebukes those who hang back when called to act.
Like the Kural, it faults the reluctance that forfeits honour.
Judaism
Hillel teaches that in a place where there are no worthy men, one should strive to be one.
— Pirkei Avot 2:5 (Hillel; numbered 2:6 in some editions) [6]
The Mishnah urges a person to step up when the situation calls for it, rather than hold back.
Valluvar likewise expects the capable to take up what lies before them, or forfeit their standing.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that one who conquers himself is a greater victor than one who conquers a thousand times a thousand men in battle.
— Dhammapada 103 [7]
The Buddha redirects the idea of glory from outer victories to inner mastery.
It offers a counterpoint to the Kural’s worldly calculus of fame through victory.
Jainism
“Penance is of no good if performed by noble men who have turned monks (for the sake of fame); but that penance of which nobody else knows anything (is meritorious). Do not spread your own fame!”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.8.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga denies that seeking glory has any spiritual worth and says that hidden, unadvertised effort is the only kind that counts.
The Kural says glory never comes to the man who will not take the easy gain of opposing an unlearned foe, while the Jain verse tells the seeker not to pursue glory at all, an honest contrast rather than a parallel.
Sikhism
“That wretched being, whom no one knows - chanting the Naam, the Name of the Lord, he is honored in the four directions.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 386, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that even an unknown, lowly person wins honour everywhere through the Name of God.
The Kural says glory never comes to one who will not take even the easy gain of opposing an unlearned foe; Gurbani locates the source of lasting honour elsewhere, in the Naam.
Greek Mythology
Achilles tells the embassy that his mother foretold two fates: if he stays and fights at Troy he will die young but win undying glory; if he goes home he will live long without fame.
— Homer, Iliad 9.410-416 [10]
In Homer, glory (kleos) is won only by those who accept the fight.
Valluvar likewise says fame never comes to one who declines the contest.
Philosophy
Sun Tzu says that what the ancients called a clever fighter is one who not only wins but wins with ease, by defeating an enemy already defeated.
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War IV.11-13 (tr. Lionel Giles) [11]
Strategy consists in seizing victories that are within easy reach.
Valluvar likewise faults the man who will not take the easy win over an unlearned foe.

Real Life Example

Howe halts after Long Island and Washington's army escapes
27-30 August 1776 · Brooklyn, New York, United States
After defeating the inexperienced Continental Army at the Battle of Long Island on 27 August 1776, British General William Howe halted his attack and began siege works rather than pressing the assault, overruling subordinates who argued for a renewed attack. On the night of 29-30 August, George Washington ferried about 9,000 troops across the East River to Manhattan, helped at dawn by fog. Howe did not pursue, and the American army survived to fight on.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A commander who held every advantage over a raw, untrained army let the chance of a decisive victory slip away.
The kural says one who will not take even the easy gain of opposing an untrained foe will never win glory, and Howe's pause at Brooklyn is remembered as just such a missed opportunity.
