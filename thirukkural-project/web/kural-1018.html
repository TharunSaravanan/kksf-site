Kural 1018

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

பிறர்நாணத் தக்கது தான்நாணா னாயின் அறம்நாணத் தக்கது உடைத்து.
Pirarnaanath thakkadhu thaannaanaa naayin aramnaanath thakkadhu udaiththu.

Meaning

If a person feels no shame at things that would make others ashamed, then goodness itself (what is right) will be ashamed of that person.

Elementary

Imagine someone does something so rude that everyone watching feels embarrassed, but he just grins. Valluvar says that even Goodness itself would feel embarrassed to be his friend and would quietly walk away from him.

University

The kural personifies aram (அறம், 'virtue, dharma'). If one does not feel shame (thaan naanaan aayin) at what is pirar naanath thakkadhu (பிறர்நாணத் தக்கது, 'fit to make others ashamed'), then he udaiththu (உடைத்து, 'has in him') something aram naanath thakkadhu, at which virtue itself would blush. There are two lines of interpretation. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar say virtue, ashamed, abandons him. Pappaiah and Kesikan say he harbours a fault at which virtue is ashamed. Kesikan's gloss reads the first line as ‘what onlookers would blame’. Pope's ‘Virtue will shrink away ashamed of thee!’ follows the abandonment reading. The repeated naanath thakkadhu across both lines is characteristic Valluvar wordplay. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who are demoniac do not know what is to be done and what is not to be done. Neither cleanliness nor proper behavior nor truth is found in them.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.7 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
The Gita describes the āsuric person as one from whom right conduct and truth have departed.
This is the shameless person of the Kural, from whom virtue has withdrawn.
Christianity
“Whose end is destruction, whose God is their belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who mind earthly things.”
— Philippians 3:19 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes people who glory in what ought to shame them.
They are the Kural's shameless ones, unashamed of what should shame anyone.
Islam
“One of the sayings of the early Prophets which the people have got is: If you don't feel ashamed … do whatever you like.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6120 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet quotes the ancient maxim that once shame is gone, nothing holds a person back from anything.
Like the Kural, it makes shamelessness the moment moral restraint is lost.
Judaism
“They shall be put to shame because they have committed abomination; Yea, they are not at all ashamed, Neither know they how to blush; Therefore they shall fall among them that fall”
— Jeremiah 6:15 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Jeremiah condemns a people who commit abominations without the least blush.
This is the Kural's shameless man, who will not blush at what should shame him, and who falls.
Buddhism
“Easy is life for the shameless one who is impudent as a crow, is backbiting and forward, arrogant and corrupt.”
— Dhammapada 244 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Dhammapada says the shameless, brazen as a crow, find life easy precisely because nothing restrains them.
This is the Kural's person who feels no shame where others would.
Jainism
“By repenting of one's sins to oneself the soul obtains repentance, and becoming indifferent by repentance he prepares for himself an (ascending) scale of virtues, by which he destroys the Karman resulting from delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 29.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Nindā, inwardly condemning one's own faults, is what keeps a soul climbing the scale of virtues.
This is the Kural's point from the other side: without inner shame at wrongdoing, virtue has nowhere to stand.
Sikhism
“Modesty and righteousness both have vanished, and falsehood struts around like a leader, O Lalo.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 722, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Describing the sack of Saidpur in his Babarvani hymns, Guru Nanak tells his disciple Lalo that saram (modesty) and dharam (righteousness) have both gone into hiding.
The Kural warns that when a person loses shame, virtue itself is ashamed and leaves; Guru Nanak shows modesty and righteousness leaving together.
Greek Mythology
“And then Aidos and Nemesis … with their sweet forms wrapped in white robes, will go from the wide-pathed earth and forsake mankind to join the company of the deathless gods”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 197-200 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, Theoi Classical Texts Library) [10]
Hesiod foresees that when men grow utterly shameless, the goddesses Shame and Righteous Indignation will abandon the earth.
This is the Kural's image exactly: a divine personified virtue, ashamed, departs from the shameless.
Philosophy
Theophrastus sketches the Shameless Man (anaischyntos), who cheats the people he owes, borrows from the person he has just defrauded, and treats anything as acceptable as long as he gains by it, untroubled by what others think.
— Theophrastus, Characters 9 (The Shameless Man) [11]
Theophrastus' character sketch shows how one who feels no shame drifts from every decent norm.
It portrays the man the Kural warns of, who does unblushingly what would shame anyone else.

Real Life Example

Joseph Welch asks Senator McCarthy whether he has any sense of decency
9 June 1954 · Washington, D.C., United States
During the televised Army-McCarthy hearings, Senator Joseph McCarthy publicly accused Fred Fisher, a young lawyer in the firm of Army counsel Joseph Welch, of past links to a group he called a Communist front. Welch rebuked him before the cameras, ending with the question "Have you no sense of decency?", and the hearing room broke into applause. In December 1954 the Senate voted to condemn McCarthy's conduct.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The exchange is widely seen as the turning point at which public opinion turned against McCarthy.
The kural says that a man who feels no shame at what would shame others is abandoned by virtue itself, and McCarthy's standing collapsed soon after he was publicly shown to lack that shame.
