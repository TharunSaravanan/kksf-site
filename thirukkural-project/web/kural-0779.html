Kural 779

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

இழைத்தது இகவாமைச் சாவாரை யாரே பிழைத்தது ஒறுக்கிற் பவர்.
Izhaiththadhu igavaamaich chaavaarai yaare Pizhaiththadhu orukkir pavar.

Meaning

Who could blame for failure those who die keeping the promise they made?

Elementary

If a friend promised to protect you and gave everything trying, no one would say they failed. Keeping your word with all your heart is what counts, even if things don’t turn out perfectly.

University

Izhaiththadhu refers to what one has pledged, the sool or battle-vow sworn before the king; igavaamai is “not transgressing” it; saavaarai are those who die so. The rhetorical question yaare ... orukkirpavar asks “who would punish (or reproach) them” for pizhaiththadhu, the failure. Commentators divide: Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it as those who die fighting in fulfilment of their vow, whom no one can reproach; Solomon Pappaiah reads it as even if the vow went unfulfilled, death in the attempt removes all blame. Either way, death in fidelity to one’s word places the warrior beyond censure.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, either you will be killed on the battlefield and attain the heavenly planets, or you will conquer and enjoy the earthly kingdom. Therefore, get up with determination and fight.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.37 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that a warrior who fights in duty cannot truly lose: death and victory are both honourable outcomes.
The Kural also holds that one who dies keeping his vow cannot be counted a failure.
Christianity
“Fear none of those things which thou shalt suffer … be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life.”
— Revelation 2:10 (KJV) [4]
The risen Christ encourages the church at Smyrna to remain faithful even if it costs their lives.
Faithfulness to death is honoured, not reproached, just as the Kural says.
Islam
“Among the believers are men true to what they promised Allah. Among them is he who has fulfilled his vow [to the death], and among them is he who awaits [his chance]. And they did not alter [the terms of their commitment] by any alteration”
— Qur’an 33:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who kept their pledge even to death.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s praise of those who die without transgressing their vow.
Judaism
“If anyone makes a vow to GOD or takes an oath imposing an obligation on themselves, they shall not break their pledge; they must carry out all that has crossed their lips.”
— Numbers 30:3 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Torah makes keeping one’s vow a sacred obligation.
The Kural’s warrior honours exactly this principle, at the cost of his life.
Buddhism
“Better it is to live one day strenuous and resolute than to live a hundred years sluggish and dissipated.”
— Dhammapada 112 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada prizes resolute effort over a long but slack life.
The Kural honours resolve kept to the end; Buddhism shares the esteem for resolve, though it would direct it to the path rather than the battlefield.
Jainism
“These two ways of life ending with death have been declared: death with one's will, and death against one's will. Death against one's will is that of ignorant men, and it happens (to the same individual) many times. Death with one's will is that of wise men, and at best it happens but once.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.2–3 (cf. 5.31–32) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira praises the wise man's 'death with one's will', which in Jain practice is the vow of sallekhana kept to the end (5.31–32), and sets it against the fool's frightened death.
The Kural says no one can blame those who die keeping the vow they made, and Jainism likewise honours the one who keeps his vow until death as having died the best death.
Sikhism
During the siege of Anandpur some forty Sikhs signed a disclaimer (bedava) renouncing Guru Gobind Singh and went home. Shamed by Mai Bhago, they returned and died fighting the pursuing Mughal force at Khidrana on 29 December 1705. According to tradition, the Guru tore up the disclaimer at the request of the dying Mahan Singh and blessed them as the Chali Mukte, the Forty Liberated Ones; Khidrana became Muktsar.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, 'Chali Mukte' (paraphrase); the torn bedava follows Sikh tradition [9]
Their death in faithful battle wiped out their earlier failure, and they are remembered in the daily Ardas.
The Kural asks who could blame those who die keeping their vow, even if the vow was not fulfilled; the Chali Mukte had broken faith, yet by dying for the Guru they were freed of all blame.
Greek Mythology
At Thermopylae Leonidas and his Spartans hold the pass to the last; their epitaph, attributed to Simonides, tells the passer-by to report to Sparta that they lie there obedient to its laws.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.228 (paraphrase) [10]
Herodotus records the stand and its epitaph as the model of keeping faith with one’s charge to the end.
No one reproaches the Spartans for losing the pass; like the Kural’s warriors, their faithful death is their glory.
Philosophy
In the Crito, Socrates refuses his friend’s plan of escape, arguing that he must keep his agreement with the laws of Athens even though it means his death.
— Plato, Crito (paraphrase) [11]
Socrates holds that breaking a just agreement to save one’s life would be a greater wrong than dying.
Like the Kural’s warrior, Socrates dies rather than break faith, and history does not count it his failure.

Real Life Example

Leonidas and his Spartans remain at Thermopylae
480 BCE · Thermopylae, central Greece
When a Persian force found a mountain path around the Greek position at the pass of Thermopylae, most of the allied Greek army left. The Spartan king Leonidas and his 300 Spartans stayed to hold the pass, together with contingents including some 700 Thespians, and were killed. Herodotus, the main source, reports that Leonidas thought it unfitting for the Spartans to desert the post they had been sent to hold, though he presents some details as hearsay.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Thermopylae was a military defeat, yet the defenders who died holding their post became one of history's lasting symbols of honour.
The kural asks who could blame those who die keeping the vow they made, and no one has ever reproached Leonidas's men for losing a battle they died to keep faith with.
