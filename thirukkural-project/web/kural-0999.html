Kural 999

Courtesy · Panpudaimai · பண்புடைமை

நகல்வல்லர் அல்லார்க்கு மாயிரு ஞாலம் பகலும்பாற் பட்டன்று இருள்.
Nakalvallar allaarkku maayiru gnaalam pakalumpaar pattandru irul.

Meaning

For people who cannot enjoy friendly, cheerful company, this huge wide world is dark even in the middle of the day.

Elementary

Imagine a sunny day at the park, but you won't play, laugh, or talk with anyone. Even with the sun shining, it would feel gloomy. Valluvar says a heart that can't smile and join in makes the whole world feel dark.

University

Nagal vallar allaar (நகல்வல்லர் அல்லார்) are 'those not able to laugh', that is, unable to mix with others and share friendly cheer; for them maa-iru gnaalam (மாயிரு ஞாலம், 'the great, vast earth') is, even by day (pagalum), fallen into darkness (paal patta-andru irul, பாற்பட்டன்று இருள்). Mu. Varadarasanar glosses nagal as 'mingling with others and rejoicing' (பிறரோடு கலந்து பழகி மகிழ), Pappaiah ties it explicitly to lack of good character, and Kalaignar to inability to enjoy friends; Pope's prose 'To those who cannot rejoice, the wide world is buried darkness even in (broad) day light' follows this line. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads nagal as 'harmonizing with others' and iru as 'two-fold', an idiosyncratic segmentation. The transliteration of gnaalam varies across sites (Gnaalam on ytamizh, ñālam on thirukkural.net). The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not connected with the Supreme [in Kṛṣṇa consciousness] can have neither transcendental intelligence nor a steady mind, without which there is no possibility of peace. And how can there be any happiness without peace?”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.66 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that without an inner connection there is no peace, and without peace no happiness.
Both texts say that the lack of an inner quality turns life joyless, though the Gita's missing quality is spiritual connection rather than sociable cheer.
Christianity
“All the days of the afflicted are evil: but he that is of a merry heart hath a continual feast.”
— Proverbs 15:15 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs contrasts the gloom of the downcast with the cheerful heart that makes every day a feast.
Like the Kural, it says the inner capacity for cheer colours the whole world, turning days to gloom or feast.
Islam
“Your smiling in the face of your brother is charity, commanding good and forbidding evil is charity, your giving directions to a man lost in the land is charity for you.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1956 (graded hasan) [5]
The Prophet counts a smile offered to another as an act of charity.
It shares the Kural's high regard for warm, smiling sociability as a moral good.
Judaism
“They said to him: We are jesters, and we cheer up the depressed. Alternatively, when we see two people who have a quarrel between them, we strive to make peace.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Taanit 22a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
Elijah points out two jesters in the marketplace as destined for the World to Come because they bring cheer to the sad and make peace.
The Talmud prizes the very capacity for shared laughter that the Kural says keeps the world from darkness.
Buddhism
In the Dhammapada the Buddha's disciples declare that they live happily, possessing nothing, and will feed on joy like the Radiant Gods.
— Dhammapada 200 [7]
The verse celebrates a joy (piti) that does not depend on possessions.
It shares the Kural's sense that joy lights up life, though Buddhist joy is inward and ascetic rather than social.
Jainism
“Though emaciated like the joint of a crow's (leg) and covered with a network of veins, he should know the permitted measure of food and drink, and wander about with a cheerful mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Even when worn thin by fasting, the Jain monk is told to go about with a cheerful mind.
The Jain stress on cheerfulness in all conditions touches the Kural's point that a joyless mind darkens life, though without its social dimension.
Sikhism
“If a hundred moons were to rise, and a thousand suns appeared, even with such light, there would still be pitch darkness without the Guru.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 463, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 2, Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad says that no amount of outer light can lift the darkness of a life without the Guru. Outward brightness and inner darkness can exist together.
The Kural says the wide world lies in darkness even by day for those who cannot share in friendly cheer, and Guru Angad's verse uses the same paradox of darkness in the midst of light.
Greek Mythology
“A long time she sat upon the stool without speaking because of her sorrow, and greeted no one by word or by sign, but rested, never smiling, and tasting neither food nor drinks because she pined with longing for her deep-bosomed daughter, until careful Iambe -- who pleased her moods in aftertime also -- moved the holy lady with many a quip and jest to smile and laugh and cheer her heart.”
— Homeric Hymn 2, To Demeter 198–204 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White; sacred-texts.com) [10]
The grieving Demeter, who has withdrawn from all company, is drawn back into life by Iambe's jokes.
Demeter's unsmiling isolation and the light that returns with laughter dramatize the Kural's image of a world dark without shared cheer.
Philosophy
“While those who can neither make a joke themselves nor put up with those who do are thought to be boorish and unpolished.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.8 (tr. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive) [11]
Aristotle calls the person who can neither joke nor bear joking boorish, lacking a social virtue needed for good company.
This is Aristotle's name for the Kural's nagal vallar allaar, those unable to share in laughter.

Real Life Example

The Christmas Truce on the Western Front
24-25 December 1914 · Western Front, Belgium and northern France
Late on Christmas Eve 1914, British troops in parts of the Western Front heard German soldiers in the opposite trenches singing carols and saw lanterns and small trees along their parapets. On Christmas Day men from both sides walked out into no man's land, exchanged food, tobacco and souvenirs, took photographs, buried their dead and in places played impromptu football. The truces were unofficial and uneven, fighting continued elsewhere, and there was no comparable truce later in the war.
— Sources: [12] [13]
For a brief moment, sharing simple good cheer with the enemy lifted some of the darkness of the war for the men in the trenches.
The kural says that for those unable to share friendly cheer the wide world is dark even in daylight, and the truce showed the reverse: shared cheer brought light into one of history's darkest settings.
