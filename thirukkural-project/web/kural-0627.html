Kural 627

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

இலக்கம் உடம்பிடும்பைக் கென்று கலக்கத்தைக் கையாறாக் கொள்ளாதாம் மேல்.
Ilakkam utampitumpaik kendru kalakkaththaik Kaiyaaraak kollaadhaam mel.

Meaning

Great people know the body is just a target for pain. So they do not treat the worry that pain brings as a disaster.

Elementary

A football goal is made to have balls kicked at it; nobody is shocked when a ball hits it. The Kural says our bodies are a bit like that: bumps, colds and aches are bound to come, so wise people don’t panic when they do.

University

Ilakkam (from ilakku, ‘target’, cognate with Sanskrit lakṣya) makes the body the mark at which afflictions are aimed, an idea the commentators connect with the inevitability of bodily pain for every embodied being. Kalakkam is inner agitation, and kaiyāṟu is a rarer word that the traditional commentary takes as distress or helpless grief, so that the great ‘do not take agitation as (a cause of) distress’; Mu. Varadarasanar instead renders the construction as not adopting agitation as their way of conduct, showing a genuine interpretive divergence. Mēl, ‘the superior ones’, is the subject, with the verb in the honorific-epicene form koḷḷātām. The text is identical in both sources consulted, and Pope’s ‘Man’s frame is sorrow’s target, the noble mind reflects’ keeps the reasoning explicit.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kunti, the contact between the senses and the sense objects gives rise to fleeting perceptions of happiness and distress. These are non-permanent, and come and go like the winter and summer seasons. O descendent of Bharat, one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.14 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna explains that bodily pleasure and pain are passing sensations like seasons, to be endured with titikṣā (forbearance).
The Gita and the Kural both hold that because bodily pain is natural and passing, the wise do not let it disturb the mind.
Christianity
“For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day.”
— 2 Corinthians 4:16 (KJV) [4]
Paul accepts that the body is wearing away under hardship while the inner self keeps being renewed.
Like the Kural, Paul separates the body, which takes the blows, from the inner person, who need not lose heart.
Islam
“We have certainly created man into hardship.”
— Qur’an 90:4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an states plainly that toil and difficulty (kabad) are built into the human condition from creation.
This is the Kural’s premise that the body is made to be a target of affliction, so hardship should not surprise or unsettle us.
Judaism
“But man is born unto trouble, As the sparks fly upward.”
— Job 5:7 (JPS 1917) [6]
Job’s friend Eliphaz observes that trouble is as natural to human beings as rising sparks are to fire.
Eliphaz states the Kural’s first line, that human life is a natural target for sorrow.
Buddhism
In the Sallatha Sutta the Buddha compares a painful bodily feeling to being struck by an arrow; the untaught person then adds a second arrow of mental distress by worrying and lamenting, while the well-taught disciple feels only the first, bodily arrow.
— Samyutta Nikaya 36.6 (Sallatha Sutta) [7]
Bodily pain is unavoidable, but the extra layer of mental agitation about it is optional and can be dropped.
The Kural’s great ones accept the body as pain’s target yet refuse the kalakkam of the mind, exactly the Buddha’s refusal of the second arrow.
Jainism
“This body is not permanent, it is impure and of impure origin; it is but a transitory residence (of the soul) and a miserable vessel of suffering.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 19.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Prince Mrigaputra tells his parents why he wants to renounce: the body is impermanent and by its nature a vessel of suffering for the soul (jiva) that dwells in it.
The Kural says the noble know the body is a target for pain and so are not shaken by it; Mrigaputra likewise sees the body as a natural vessel of suffering.
Sikhism
“Fareed, I thought that I was in trouble; the whole world is in trouble!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Sheikh Farid included in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sufi saint realises that suffering is not his alone but the common lot of everyone in the world.
The Kural says the noble know the body is simply a target for pain and so do not treat it as a calamity; Farid likewise sees pain as the shared lot of all embodied beings.
Greek Mythology
Achilles tells grieving Priam that two jars stand on Zeus’ threshold, one of evil gifts and one of good; Zeus gives some men a mixture and others only evil, so that no mortal escapes sorrow and all must endure what comes.
— Homer, Iliad 24.525-533 [10]
Homer’s image makes suffering a built-in portion of every human life handed out by the gods.
Achilles’ jars express the Kural’s premise that the embodied person is a natural target of trouble and should bear it.
Philosophy
“Sickness is a hindrance to the body, but not to your ability to choose, unless that is your choice. Lameness is a hindrance to the leg, but not to your ability to choose.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 9 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus insists that bodily afflictions touch only the body, not the mind’s own power of choice.
Both texts locate pain in the body and deny it any right to throw the mind into turmoil.

Real Life Example

Matisse turns to paper cut-outs after major surgery
1941-1954 · Nice, France
In 1941 Henri Matisse underwent serious abdominal surgery that left him in poor health and bedridden much of the time, often working from a bed or a wheelchair. Unable to stand at an easel for long, he turned increasingly to cutting shapes with scissors from paper painted with gouache by his assistants, which were then pinned up as he directed. These cut-outs, made in what he called a second life, became some of the most celebrated works of his career.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Matisse did not let a failing body become the end of his art; he treated its limits as a condition to work within and found a new form of expression.
The kural says the noble know the body is simply a target for pain and so do not treat its distress as a true calamity, and Matisse, confined to bed and chair, went on creating as if his body's suffering were no disaster to his work.
