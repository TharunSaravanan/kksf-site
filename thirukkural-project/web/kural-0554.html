Kural 554

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

கூழுங் குடியும் ஒருங்கிழக்கும் கோல்கோடிச் சூழாது செய்யும் அரசு.
Koozhung kudiyum orungizhakkum kolkodich Choozhaathu seyyum arasu.

Meaning

A king who twists justice and acts without thinking about what will happen will lose both his riches and his people at once.

Elementary

Imagine a lemonade stand owner who cheats customers without thinking ahead. Soon the customers stop coming, and with them goes the money. Valluvar says a careless, unfair king loses his treasure and his people together, because the people are where the treasure comes from.

University

கூழ் (literally gruel or food) stands for wealth, and குடி for the subjects. Parimelazhagar makes the pairing precise: கூழ் is the wealth already amassed, and குடி is the people who are the source of wealth yet to be earned, so the tyrant loses past and future income together. சூழாது means ‘without deliberating’ on what will follow, and கோல்கோடி means ‘bending the sceptre’; he notes கோடி stands for the infinitive கோட. He adds that the next couplet gives the cause of losing wealth already amassed. Manakkudavar marks this couplet as stating the fault that comes from acting without deliberation. Kalaignar reads குடி more loosely as ‘the people’s esteem’. The text is the same across the sources consulted, and Pope’s rendering ‘who counsel doth refuse’ follows the sense of சூழாது.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That king who is illiberal, and without affection, who afflicts his subjects by undue chastisements, and who is rash in his acts, soon meets with destruction.”
— Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva, Section XCII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
The sage Vamadeva warns King Vasumanas that harsh, rash rule leads straight to ruin.
Rash action (சூழாது) and unjust punishment (கோல்கோடி) are the two faults the Kural names.
Christianity
Thomas Aquinas argues that tyrannical rule cannot last long, because it is hateful to the multitude and rests on fear, which is a weak foundation, so tyrants are soon overthrown.
— Thomas Aquinas, De Regno (On Kingship) I.10 [5]
For Aquinas, oppression undermines the very base of the ruler’s power.
It agrees with the Kural that the unjust king loses his people, and with them his hold on power.
Islam
“Of Pharaoh and his people after they pursued the Israelites, the Qur’an says: “So We removed them from gardens and springs” and from treasures and an honoured station.”
— Qur’an 26:57-58 (Saheeh International) [6]
Pharaoh’s oppression ends with the loss of all his wealth and power at a single stroke.
This is the Kural’s ‘loses both wealth and people at once’.
Judaism
“My father made your yoke heavy, but I will add to your yoke; my father chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions.”
— 1 Kings 12:14 (JPS 1917) [7]
King Rehoboam rejects the elders’ advice to lighten the people’s burden, and the ten northern tribes break away from him.
He is exactly the king who ‘does not deliberate’ and bends the rod, and so loses most of his people and revenue in one day.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells King Ajātasattu’s minister that the Vajjians will not decline as long as they meet often, act in harmony, keep their established laws, and honour and listen to their elders.
— Dīgha Nikāya 16, Mahāparinibbāna Sutta 1.4 [8]
The ‘conditions of non-decline’ tie a state’s survival to consultation and just custom.
They are the exact opposite of the Kural’s king, who acts without counsel and bends justice.
Jainism
“In vain he adopts nakedness, who errs about matters of paramount interest; neither this world nor the next will be his; he is a loser in both respects in the world.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.49 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The sage tells King Śreṇika that someone who errs about the things that matter most loses on both sides at once, in this life and the next.
The Kural's thoughtless, unjust king loses his wealth and his people together, and the Jain verse likewise describes a wrong-doer who loses both things at once.
Sikhism
“Where are the games, the stables, the horses? Where are the drums and the bugles?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Lamenting the sack of Saidpur by Babar's army, Guru Nanak asks where all the splendour of the ruling class has gone; in the same hymn he says that those who had remembered the Lord beforehand would not have been punished.
The Kural says a king who bends justice without thought loses wealth and people at once; Guru Nanak pictures a whole order's wealth and pomp vanishing in a single stroke.
Greek Mythology
Creon, king of Thebes, ignores his son Haemon and the prophet Tiresias and persists in an unjust decree. By the end he has lost his son and his wife, and the city has turned against him.
— Sophocles, Antigone [11]
Sophocles shows a ruler who refuses counsel and loses everything at once.
Creon’s fall is the Kural’s king who ‘acts without deliberation’ and loses all together.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that tyranny and oligarchy are the shortest-lived of all constitutions, and that most tyrannies have lasted only a short time.
— Aristotle, Politics V.12, 1315b (trans. B. Jowett) [12]
His survey of Greek history finds oppressive rule unstable by nature.
It gives historical support to the Kural’s claim that the bent sceptre brings quick and total loss.

Real Life Example

Louis XIV revokes the Edict of Nantes
October 1685 · Fontainebleau, France
In October 1685 Louis XIV issued the Edict of Fontainebleau, revoking the Edict of Nantes that had given French Protestants (Huguenots) religious toleration since 1598. Although emigration was forbidden, at least 200,000 Huguenots fled to the Netherlands, England, Prussia, America and elsewhere over the following years. Many were skilled craftsmen and merchants, and their departure strengthened the economies of France's rivals while weakening France's own.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A single unjust decree drove away a large and productive part of the population and with it much of the country's skill and wealth.
The kural says a king who bends justice without thinking of the consequences loses wealth and people together, and the revocation did exactly that.
