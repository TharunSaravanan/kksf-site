Kural 424

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

எண்பொருள வாகச் செலச்சொல்லித் தான்பிறர்வாய் நுண்பொருள் காண்ப தறிவு.
Enporula vaagach selachcholli thaanpirarvaai Nunporul kaanpa tharivu.

Meaning

Wisdom is speaking so that others easily understand you and remember what you said, and also catching the deeper meaning in what others say.

Elementary

A wise person does two things with words. When they talk, they explain things so clearly that others understand easily. When they listen, they catch even the small, hidden points in what others are saying.

University

The couplet describes wisdom in communication as running in two directions. In speaking, எண்பொருள ஆக means 'so that the meaning is easy' and செலச் சொல்லி means 'speaking so that it goes in', that is, lodges in the listener. In listening, நுண்பொருள் காண்பது means 'seeing the subtle or fine meaning' in another's words. The contrast between எண் (easy, simple) and நுண் (subtle, fine) is deliberate. The wise person simplifies when giving and refines when receiving. This is a sophisticated account of communicative intelligence, close to what classical rhetoric called knowing one's audience (Plato's Phaedrus) and to hermeneutic charity in interpreting others. For rulers and ministers, the Porutpal setting, it is practical advice: make your orders clear, and read the unstated intentions of envoys and courtiers.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 [4]
Krishna describes disciplined speech as speech shaped for its effect on the listener: true, agreeable, and helpful.
This covers the speaking half of the Kural, speaking so that words land well with the hearer.
Christianity
“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man.”
— Colossians 4:6 (KJV) [5]
Paul urges speech adapted to each listener: gracious, apt, and responsive to the person being answered.
Both texts see wise speech as speech tailored to land with the hearer. Knowing 'how to answer every man' requires grasping what each person means.
Islam
“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best.”
— Qur'an 16:125 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an ties hikmah (wisdom) directly to how one speaks: persuasively, kindly, and in the best manner for the audience.
Both texts connect wisdom with speaking in a way that actually reaches the hearer.
Judaism
The Mishnah lists seven marks of a wise person: he does not speak before one greater in wisdom, does not interrupt, is not hasty to answer, asks relevant questions and answers to the point, speaks on first things first, admits when he has not understood, and acknowledges the truth.
— Pirkei Avot 5:7 [7]
The sage is defined by disciplined conversation: careful listening, fitting replies, and orderly speech.
Like the Kural, Avot treats wisdom as skill in both hearing and speaking.
Buddhism
“That monk who has control over his tongue, is moderate in speech, unassuming and who explains the Teaching in both letter and spirit — whatever he says is pleasing.”
— Dhammapada 363 [8]
The Dhammapada praises the monk whose speech conveys both the letter and the inner meaning of the Dhamma, and so is received well.
Explaining 'in letter and spirit' parallels the Kural's pairing of plain meaning with subtle meaning, and speech that 'pleases' reaches the hearer.
Jainism
In the debate at Sravasti, the monk Kesi puts his questions to Gautama as riddles: a thousand foes attacking him, a poisonous plant in the heart, a raging fire, a runaway horse, a leaking boat. Each time Gautama grasps the hidden meaning and answers in plain, memorable words (the foe is the unsubdued self and the passions, the fire is the passions, the boat is the body), until Kesi and the whole assembly are satisfied.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.35-85 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The episode presents an ideal of communication on both sides: Gautama sees the subtle sense in what Kesi says and then gives that sense back in clear, simple terms that his hearers can take in.
Valluvar's wise person both speaks so that the meaning easily sinks in and sees the subtle meaning in others' words; Gautama shows both skills, decoding Kesi's riddles and answering in a way the whole assembly can follow.
Sikhism
“Those words are acceptable, which, when spoken, bring honor.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Siree Raag Guru Nanak says that only speech that earns honour, before people and before God, is true speech.
The Kural defines wisdom as speaking so that the meaning lands easily in the listener, and grasping subtle meaning in others; Guru Nanak likewise sets a standard for speech worth speaking.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Nestor of Pylos is introduced as the clear-voiced orator 'from whose tongue flowed speech sweeter than honey'. He is repeatedly sent to reconcile quarrelling chiefs because his words reach them.
— Homer, Iliad 1.247-284 [11]
Nestor is Homer's model of the counsellor whose speech persuades because it is well judged for his audience.
He exemplifies the speaking half of the Kural: words that go in and settle in the hearer's mind.
Philosophy
Socrates argues that a true art of speech requires knowing the nature of the soul. The speaker must identify what kind of soul he addresses and fit the speech to it, simple speech to a simple soul and complex speech to a complex one.
— Plato, Phaedrus 271a-272b [12]
For Plato, real rhetoric depends on understanding the listener, which in turn requires a subtle perception of other minds.
Both texts see communicative wisdom as fitting speech to the hearer and perceiving what lies within others.

Real Life Example

Kennedy answers Khrushchev's first letter in the Cuban Missile Crisis
26-28 October 1962 · Washington, D.C., United States, and Moscow, Soviet Union
At the height of the Cuban Missile Crisis, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev sent President John F. Kennedy a private letter on 26 October 1962 suggesting that the missiles in Cuba could be removed in return for a U.S. promise not to invade Cuba, followed on 27 October by a second, public message that also demanded the removal of U.S. missiles from Turkey. On the advice of his officials, Kennedy replied to the terms of the first letter, setting out clear steps for removing the missiles under United Nations supervision in exchange for a pledge not to attack Cuba, while Robert Kennedy privately told the Soviet ambassador that the missiles in Turkey would later be withdrawn. On 28 October Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be removed from Cuba.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Reading the opening in the first, more conciliatory letter and answering it plainly helped end the most dangerous confrontation of the Cold War.
The kural defines wisdom as speaking so that one's meaning goes home easily while grasping the subtle meaning in what others say, and Kennedy's team did both, reading the possibility in Khrushchev's first letter and answering it in clear, acceptable terms.
