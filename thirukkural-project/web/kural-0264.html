Kural 264

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

ஒன்னார்த் தெறலும் உவந்தாரை ஆக்கலும் எண்ணின் தவத்தான் வரும்.
Onnaar theralum uvandhaarai aakkalum Ennin thavaththaan varum.

Meaning

If a person who practices strict self-control wishes it, that power alone can defeat enemies and lift up friends.

Elementary

In old stories, people who did very long and sincere penance got special powers — like a superhero who trained for years. Valluvar says such a person could, just by wishing it, stop bad people and help good friends.

University

Here Valluvar adopts the widespread Indian belief that tapas generates real, even cosmic, power — the power to curse (சாபம்) and to bless (அருள்), as Parimelazhagar glosses ஆற்றல் in the neighbouring verse. The conditional எண்ணின் (‘if he wills’) is crucial: after 261 defined the ascetic as one who harms no one, the implication is that such power exists but is normally left unused. The verse thus holds a tension typical of Indian ascetic literature, where rishis like Vishvamitra or Durvasa possess destructive capacity that their own ethic ought to restrain. Modern commentators such as Kalaignar demythologize it as the practical power of resolve and self-control.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Vishvamitra, having amassed great tapas, uses its power to raise his devotee King Trishanku bodily toward heaven and, when the gods cast him down, creates a new constellation of heavens for him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda, sargas 57-60 [3]
The Ramayana’s famous episode shows tapas as a force that can uplift a friend in defiance even of the gods.
A textbook illustration of ‘uplifting those who love him’ through tapas; Vishvamitra elsewhere also curses his foes.
Christianity
“And Elijah answered and said to the captain of fifty, If I be a man of God, then let fire come down from heaven, and consume thee and thy fifty. And there came down fire from heaven, and consumed him and his fifty.”
— 2 Kings 1:10 (KJV) [4]
The ascetic prophet Elijah calls down fire on the soldiers sent to seize him.
A holy man’s spiritual standing directly destroys his enemies, as the Kural says tapas can.
Islam
“Allah said, ‘I will declare war against him who shows hostility to a pious worshipper of Mine… and if he asks Me, I will give him, and if he asks My protection (Refuge), I will protect him.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6502 (Hadith Qudsi) [5]
One who draws near through extra devotions gains God’s protection against enemies and has requests granted.
Devotional discipline yields both defeat of foes and fulfilment of wishes, though the agent is God, not the ascetic’s own power.
Judaism
After twelve years hiding in a cave in study and deprivation, Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and his son emerged with such spiritual intensity that whatever they gazed upon was burned, until a heavenly voice sent them back.
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 33b [6]
The Talmud portrays ascetic withdrawal as generating a dangerous, destructive spiritual force.
Close to the Kural’s claim that tapas can destroy; the story also criticizes its unrestrained use.
Buddhism
The Buddha lists supernormal powers (iddhi) — multiplying the body, passing through walls, walking on water — as fruits of the concentrated mind of the disciplined monk, while elsewhere forbidding their display.
— Samaññaphala Sutta (DN 2) [7]
Early Buddhism accepts that meditative discipline yields extraordinary powers but treats them as secondary and potentially dangerous.
Agrees that discipline yields power; differs in never endorsing its use to destroy enemies.
Jainism
“He is the man of great fame and might, of awful piety and power; do not injure him who cannot be injured, lest he consume you all by the fire (of his virtue).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
When Brahmin youths beat the outcaste-born monk Harikesa, Bhadra warns them of his ascetic power and Yakshas strike them down, yet Harikesa himself declares, 'There is not the least hatred in me' (12.32).
The Kural credits tapas with power to crush foes and raise up friends; the Harikesa story shows that power vividly, though in Jain telling the monk himself wills no harm.
Sikhism
“He Himself is the Supreme Master of all; wealth and miraculous spiritual powers, and all other external tastes and pleasures, are all like beads on a string.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib, Pauri 29, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Guru Nanak tells the yogis that God alone is Master, and that the occult powers (siddhis) they seek through austerity are only side pleasures.
The Kural credits the ascetic with power to destroy enemies and raise friends; Guru Nanak places all such power in God and treats yogic powers as distractions.
Greek Mythology
Chryses, priest of Apollo, is insulted by Agamemnon; he prays to Apollo, who sends a plague of arrows upon the Greek army.
— Homer, Iliad 1.8-52 [10]
A holy man’s standing with a god becomes a weapon against those who wrong him.
Resembles the ‘destruction of enemies’ power of the Kural’s ascetic, but grounded in priestly service, not austerity.
Philosophy
Plotinus argues that the sage who has turned inward to contemplation cannot be touched by magic spells and that only the irrational part of a person is subject to such forces.
— Plotinus, Enneads IV.4.43-44 [11]
Neoplatonism grants the contemplative a kind of invulnerability but not active power over others.
A distant parallel: the disciplined soul gains power, but only defensive, never used to harm or promote others.

Real Life Example

Gandhi's fast brings calm to Calcutta
1-4 September 1947 · Calcutta (Kolkata), West Bengal, India
When communal rioting broke out again in Calcutta soon after Independence, Gandhi began a fast on the evening of 1 September 1947, saying he would continue until the violence stopped. Within days the rioting cooled, and leaders of Hindu, Muslim, Sikh and other groups came to him pledging to prevent further violence. He broke the fast on the evening of 4 September.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single old man's voluntary self-denial achieved a peace that force had not, which is why the episode is often cited as an example of the practical power of fasting and self-discipline.
The kural says the power of penance, if the ascetic wills it, can subdue what opposes him and raise up those he loves, and Gandhi's fast subdued the riots and protected the people he cared for.
