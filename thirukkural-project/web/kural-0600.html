Kural 600

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

உரமொருவற்கு உள்ள வெறுக்கைஅஃ தில்லார் மரம்மக்க ளாதலே வேறு.
Uram oruvarku ulla verukkai aqdhillaar Maram makkal aadhale veru.

Meaning

A person’s real strength is having lots of inner drive. People without it are just trees that happen to have human shape.

Elementary

A tree stands in one place all day and never tries anything new. The Kural says a person with no energy or drive is like a tree that happens to look like a human. What makes us truly alive is our eagerness to act.

University

‘Uram’ is strength or firmness, and ‘ulla verukkai’ is read in two ways: Mu. Varadarasanar takes verukkai as abundance, hence ‘excess of ookkam is one’s strength’, while others take verukkai in its lexical sense of wealth (selvam), hence ‘energy is the mind’s wealth’, which is Pope’s ‘energy is mental wealth’. Solomon Pappaiah glosses uram as thinniya arivu (firm understanding). The second line’s ‘maram makkal aadhale veru’ is devastating: the only difference (veru) between the energy-less and trees is that they have become human in form. Parimelazhagar notes the chapter closes by returning to 591, where the energy-less were denied possession; here they are denied full humanity. The text is identical in the base sources; the sandhi form ‘verukkaiaqdhillaar’ contains the aaydham (ஃ) of ‘aqdhu’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Queen Vidula rebukes her son Sanjaya, who lies despondent after defeat, telling him it is better to blaze up for a moment than to smoulder for a long time, and urging him to rise and fight.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva 133-136 (Vidula’s counsel), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
A mother shames her listless son, saying a life without energy is not worth living.
Vidula’s contempt for smouldering inertia matches the Kural’s picture of the energy-less as mere trees.
Christianity
“Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord;”
— Romans 12:11 (KJV) [4]
Paul lists fervour of spirit and freedom from sloth as marks of Christian life.
Both texts see spiritual energy as essential and sluggishness as a failure of real life.
Islam
“And when you see them, their forms please you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech. [They are] as if they were pieces of wood propped up…”
— Qur’an 63:4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says the hypocrites look impressive but are hollow inside, like timber propped against a wall.
The Kural uses the same wood image for people with human form but no inner life of energy.
Judaism
“In a place where there are no men, strive to be a man.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:5 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Hillel urges people to step up with energy and responsibility when others do nothing.
Like the Kural, it treats being fully human as a matter of active striving, not mere form.
Buddhism
“Better it is to live one day strenuous and resolute than to live a hundred years sluggish and dissipated.”
— Dhammapada 112 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada values a single day of energetic life above a century of lazy existence.
Both texts say that life without energy is hardly life at all.
Jainism
“When the soul has once got into a vegetable-body, it remains long in that state, for an endless time, after which its lot is not much bettered; Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 10.9 (refrain from 10.1) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jain doctrine treats plants as living souls with a single sense; Mahavira warns that a soul which falls into plant-existence through carelessness may stay there for endless time, which is why human birth must not be wasted.
The Kural says people without inner energy are mere trees in human form; Jainism makes the warning literal, since slackness can lead the soul into an actual vegetable body.
Sikhism
“They belong to the human species, but they act like animals.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 267, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan says of those who live without the Name that they have human birth but not human worth.
The Kural says those without inner energy differ from trees only in having human shape; Guru Arjan likewise denies full humanity to those who have the form without the substance.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says both gods and men are angry with the man who lives idle, for he is like the stingless drones that waste the bees’ labour, eating without working.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 303-306 [10]
Hesiod compares the idle to drones in a hive, living beings that contribute nothing.
Hesiod’s drone and the Kural’s tree are both images of life without energy.
Philosophy
Aristotle, seeking the distinctive human function, sets aside mere life because it is shared even with plants, and locates the human good in the active exercise of the rational soul.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.7 (1097b-1098a) [11]
For Aristotle, a person who only lives and grows is functioning at the level of a plant, not yet as a human being.
This is almost the Kural’s own claim: without active energy, humans are trees in human shape.

Real Life Example

The deaf Beethoven completes and premieres his Ninth Symphony
7 May 1824 · Vienna, Austria
Ludwig van Beethoven's hearing had been failing since his late twenties, and by the 1820s he was profoundly deaf. He nevertheless completed his Ninth Symphony, with a choral finale setting Schiller's Ode to Joy, and it was first performed at the Theater am Karntnertor in Vienna on 7 May 1824. Beethoven stood on stage beside the conductor indicating the tempos while the performance was actually directed by others.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Deprived of the sense on which a composer most depends, Beethoven still produced one of the most celebrated works in Western music.
The kural says a person's real strength is abundance of inner energy, and Beethoven's Ninth shows that energy accomplishing what physical loss seemed to forbid.
