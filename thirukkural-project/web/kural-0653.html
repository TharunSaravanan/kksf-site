Kural 653

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

ஒஓதல் வேண்டும் ஒளிமாழ்கும் செய்வினை ஆஅதும் என்னு மவர்.
Oodhal vendum olimaazhkum seyvinai Aaadhum ennu mavar.

Meaning

People who say 'we want to rise higher' must stay away from any action that would dim their good name.

Elementary

If you want to become class captain, you wouldn’t cheat on a test, because once people see you cheat they stop trusting you. Anyone who wants to grow greater has to keep their good name bright and stay away from anything that would spoil it.

University

Two alapeṭai (metrical lengthenings) mark the couplet: ஒஓதல் for ஓதல்/ஒருவுதல் (avoiding, shunning), and ஆஅதும் for ஆதும், ‘we shall become (greater)’, which Parimelazhagar takes as the resolve of those who seek to rise ever higher. Oḷi (light, radiance) stands here for reputation and dignity, and oḷi-māḻkum seyviṉai means acts that cause that radiance to fade. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both take oḷi as pukaḻ (fame), linking this verse to the fame-and-good test of 652, while Solomon Pappaiah reads it as ‘one’s standing in one’s own lifetime’. The logic is a statesman’s: ambition itself is a reason for scruple, since a stained name blocks the rise one hopes for. ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree on the text; thirukkural-thamizh.org prints the sandhi-joined form ஒஓதல் வேண்டு மொளிமாழ்குஞ் செய்வினை / யாஅது மென்னு மவர்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for a respectable person, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [4]
Krishna warns Arjuna that deserting his duty would bring lasting disgrace, which for a man of standing is worse than dying.
Both texts treat a dimmed name as a loss too great for someone who aims high to risk.
Christianity
“Having your conversation honest among the Gentiles: that, whereas they speak against you as evildoers, they may by your good works, which they shall behold, glorify God in the day of visitation.”
— 1 Peter 2:12 (KJV) [5]
Peter tells scattered believers to live so honestly that even their critics will be won over by their good works.
Like the Kural, Peter treats blameless conduct as the guard of a good name that others are watching.
Islam
“Whoever desires honor [through power] - then to Allah belongs all honor. To Him ascends good speech, and righteous work raises it. But they who plot evil deeds will have a severe punishment, and the plotting of those - it will perish.”
— Quran 35:10 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse tells those who seek honour that only righteous work truly raises a person, while scheming comes to nothing.
This fits the Kural closely: people who want to rise must hold to deeds that lift, not ones that disgrace.
Judaism
“There are three crowns: the crown of Torah, the crown of priesthood, and the crown of royalty, but the crown of a good name supersedes them all.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:13 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Rabbi Shimon ranks a good name above even learning, priesthood and kingship.
The Kural likewise treats a bright name as the thing an ambitious person must guard above all.
Buddhism
“Not the sweet smell of flowers, not even the fragrance of sandal, tagara, or jasmine blows against the wind. But the fragrance of the virtuous blows against the wind.”
— Dhammapada 54 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada says the reputation of the virtuous spreads in every direction, which no ordinary perfume can do.
The ‘fragrance’ of virtue is the Buddhist image for the same radiance (oḷi) that the Kural says wrong deeds would dim.
Jainism
“His knowledge will be honoured, his doubts will be removed, he will gladden the heart of his teacher by his good acts; kept in safety by the performance of austerities and by meditation, being as it were a great light, he will keep the five vows.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.47 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The disciple who rises through good conduct is pictured as a great light, his standing secured by austerity, meditation and keeping the vows.
The kural tells those who mean to rise higher to avoid any deed that dims their radiance; the Jain verse shows the other side, the light that pure conduct and kept vows make shine.
Sikhism
“Those who have meditated on the Naam, the Name of the Lord, and departed after having worked by the sweat of their brows -O Nanak, their faces are radiant in the Court of the Lord, and many are saved along with them!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 8, Jap (Japji Sahib, closing salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
The closing salok of Japji promises radiant faces to those whose lives combined remembrance of God with honest toil.
The Kural tells those who would rise higher to avoid any deed that dims the light of their good name; Japji likewise links a shining face, a radiant honour, to a life of upright effort.
Greek Mythology
Hector tells Andromache he would feel dire shame before the Trojan men and women if he shrank from battle like a coward, for he has learned always to stand among the foremost and win glory for his father and himself.
— Homer, Iliad 6.441-446 [11]
Hector chooses danger over any act that would stain the honour he and his house have built.
Like the Kural’s aspirant, Hector will not do what dims his name, whatever it costs him.
Philosophy
Aristotle’s great-souled person (megalopsychos), who is concerned above all with honour, will not stoop to anything base or shameful, since honour is the greatest of external goods and is fitting only to the good.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3 (1123b-1124a) [12]
For Aristotle real honour can belong only to virtue, so one who seeks it cannot act basely.
This matches the Kural’s logic that the ambition to rise is itself a reason to avoid tarnishing deeds.

Real Life Example

Rabindranath Tagore renounces his knighthood
31 May 1919 · Calcutta (Kolkata), India
Rabindranath Tagore, winner of the 1913 Nobel Prize in Literature, had been knighted in 1915. After the Jallianwala Bagh massacre in Amritsar in April 1919 and the harsh measures that followed in the Punjab, he wrote to the Viceroy, Lord Chelmsford, on 31 May 1919, asking to be relieved of his knighthood. He wrote that badges of honour had become a mark of shame in that context and that he wished to stand, without special distinctions, beside his countrymen; the letter was published in The Statesman in June 1919.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Tagore judged that keeping an honour from a government that had acted as it had in the Punjab would tarnish his name, and he gave it up publicly.
The kural says those who wish to rise must avoid anything that dims the light of their good name, and Tagore chose to give up a title rather than let it dim his standing with his own people.
