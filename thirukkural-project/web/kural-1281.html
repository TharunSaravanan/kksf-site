Kural 1281

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

உள்ளக் களித்தலும் காண மகிழ்தலும் கள்ளுக்கில் காமத்திற் குண்டு.
Ullak kaliththalum kaana makizhdhalum kallukkil kaamaththir kundu.

Meaning

To feel joy just by thinking of someone, and to be happy just by seeing them: strong drink cannot do this, but love can.

Elementary

A treat only makes you happy once you eat it. But when you miss someone you love, just thinking of them makes you smile, and seeing them makes you jump for joy. The poet says love is stronger than any treat, because it works even before it arrives.

University

The chapter title puṇarcci vitumpal combines puṇarcci (union, coming together) and vitumpal (eager longing, impatient haste); it belongs to the kaṟpu (wedded love) section and follows kuṟippaṟivuṟuttal, describing the eagerness of husband and wife to be reunited, an eagerness strong enough to override the playful sulking (ūṭal) that convention expects. The couplet contrasts kaḷ (toddy, palm wine) with kāmam (love): uḷḷak kaḷittal is intoxication from mere thought (uḷḷal, to think) and kāṇa makiḻtal is joy from mere sight, and neither belongs to wine, which must actually be drunk. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss it as two states (nilai) that wine lacks but love possesses; Solomon Pappaiah adds that thought excites without overwhelming the senses while sight overwhelms them; Kalaignar sums it up as ‘wine gives pleasure only when drunk, love gives it at a thought or a glance.’ The comparison with wine recurs in kural 1288 of this chapter and echoes the earlier chapter on abstaining from toddy (kaḷḷuṇṇāmai, ch. 93). The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the gopis of Vraja hear Krishna’s flute on the autumn night, they drop whatever they are doing, milking, cooking, serving their families, and hurry toward the sound, already overwhelmed before they have even reached him.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29.4-8 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Bhagavata presents the gopis as so absorbed in Krishna that the mere sound and thought of him carries them away.
Like the Kural’s lover, the gopis are intoxicated by love at a hint of the beloved, before any meeting.
Christianity
“How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! how much better is thy love than wine! and the smell of thine ointments than all spices!”
— Song of Solomon 4:10 (KJV) [4]
The bridegroom of the Song declares the delight of his beloved’s love to surpass wine itself.
The Kural makes the same comparison: love outdoes wine in the joy it gives.
Islam
“Hail to thee, then, O LOVE, sweet madness! Thou who healest all our infirmities!”
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book I, Prologue (tr. E.H. Whinfield) [5]
In the prologue to his Masnavi, the Sufi poet Rumi praises love as a sweet madness that cures every ill.
The Kural calls love an intoxication greater than wine; Rumi likewise names love a ‘sweet madness’, a delight beyond reason.
Judaism
“Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth— For thy love is better than wine.”
— Song of Songs 1:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs opens with the beloved’s longing and her declaration that love surpasses wine.
This is the closest scriptural twin to the Kural’s claim that love gives a joy wine cannot.
Buddhism
“From lust springs grief, from lust springs fear. For one who is wholly free from craving there is no grief; whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 215 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada teaches that passionate desire, however sweet, is the root of grief and fear.
Where the Kural celebrates love as a delight greater than wine, the Buddhist view treats such intoxication as a source of suffering, so the parallel is one of contrast.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana warns that sensual pleasures are like a fruit that is delicious at first and poisonous afterwards.
The Kural ranks love above wine as a delight; Jain teaching treats both alike as sweet poisons, so the parallel is a sharp contrast.
Sikhism
“All the rest of the world is deluded by doubt, while my mind is intoxicated with the Sublime Essence of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 92, Sri Raag, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says his mind is drunk not on any worldly drink but on the sweet essence of the Lord.
The Kural says love, not wine, can delight a person merely by thinking of or seeing the beloved; Kabir likewise speaks of an intoxication that comes from love of the Lord rather than from drink.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod describes Eros as the fairest of the deathless gods, who loosens the limbs and overcomes the mind and wise counsel of all gods and men.
— Hesiod, Theogony 120-122 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
In the Theogony, Eros is a primal power whose effect on the mind is overwhelming, like a drug.
The Kural likewise presents love as a force that intoxicates the mind more surely than wine.
Philosophy
Socrates argues that the greatest blessings come through a divine madness, and that the madness of love is the best of these, sent by the gods for the lover’s happiness.
— Plato, Phaedrus 244a-245c (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
In the Phaedrus, Plato reverses the usual view that love’s intoxication is merely harmful and calls it a god-given madness.
The Kural’s praise of love as an elation that comes at a thought or a glance is close to Plato’s idea of love as a blessed madness.

Real Life Example

Crown Prince Akihito meets Shōda Michiko on a Karuizawa tennis court
August 1957 · Karuizawa, Nagano Prefecture, Japan
In August 1957 Japan’s Crown Prince Akihito met Shōda Michiko, the daughter of a flour-milling company president, on a tennis court in the mountain resort of Karuizawa. The two kept meeting over games of tennis, and in April 1959 they married, breaking a long tradition: she was the first commoner to marry a Japanese crown prince. The romance set off a wave of public enthusiasm in Japan, including a national craze for tennis.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A courtship that began simply with seeing each other across a tennis net became one of the most celebrated royal marriages of the twentieth century.
The kural says love, unlike wine, brings joy just by seeing and thinking of the beloved, and this romance grew out of nothing more than repeated meetings on a tennis court.
