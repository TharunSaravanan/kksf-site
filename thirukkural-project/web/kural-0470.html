Kural 470

Acting after Due Consideration · Therindhu Seyalvakai · தெரிந்து செயல்வகை

எள்ளாத எண்ணிச் செயல்வேண்டும் தம்மொடு கொள்ளாத கொள்ளாது உலகு.
Ellaadha ennich cheyalventum thammotu, Kollaadha kollaadhu ulagu.

Meaning

Think carefully and do only things the world will not look down on. People do not accept actions that do not fit who you are and your place in life.

Elementary

Before you act, ask: will good people respect this, and does it fit who I am? People won’t accept actions that don’t suit the person doing them.

University

The closing couplet adds a social test to the practical tests of the preceding kurals. எள்ளாத (‘what is not despised’) introduces public judgement, and the wordplay கொள்ளாத கொள்ளாது (‘the world will not accept what is not accepted with oneself’) sets the criterion of fit. An action must suit the agent’s station, capacity and character (தம்மொடு). Parimelazhagar takes it that the world will not approve deeds out of keeping with one’s status, even when the deeds themselves are not bad. For a ruler, legitimacy depends on acting in ways subjects and peers can recognise as fitting. The chapter thus ends by setting individual calculation inside a community of judgement.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna teaches that it is better to perform one’s own duty (svadharma), even imperfectly, than another’s duty well; and that whatever a great person does, others follow as their standard.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.35 and 3.21 [4]
Do what fits your own role and nature, and remember that people watch and copy those in high positions.
Action fitting one’s own station, judged by the watching world, echoes தம்மொடு கொள்ளாத.
Christianity
Paul speaks of “providing for honest things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men.
— 2 Corinthians 8:21 (KJV) [5]
Paul says it isn’t enough to be right before God; your conduct should also look honourable to other people.
Acting in ways the world will not scorn is the Kural’s standard too.
Islam
The Prophet said: righteousness is good character, and sin is what wavers in your soul and you would dislike people to find out about.
— Sahih Muslim 2553 [6]
One test of wrongdoing is whether you’d be embarrassed for others to learn of it.
Uses the imagined judgement of others as a check on action, like the Kural’s ‘what the world will not scorn’.
Judaism
Rabbi said: which is the right path a person should choose? Whatever is an honour to the one who does it and brings him honour from others.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:1 [7]
Pick the way of acting that you can respect in yourself and that other people will respect too.
The same two tests as the Kural: fitting to oneself, and approved by the world.
Buddhism
In the Kalama Sutta the Buddha tells the Kalamas that when they know for themselves that certain things are unskillful, blameworthy and criticised by the wise, and lead to harm, they should abandon them.
— Kalama Sutta (Kesamutti Sutta), Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 [8]
One way to check an action is to ask whether wise people would criticise it.
‘Criticised by the wise’ is the Buddhist form of the Kural’s ‘what the worthy would not scorn’.
Jainism
“Prasiddham desacharam samacharan : Following the reputable usages of the country.” … “Adsa-kalayoh charyam tyajan : Avoiding action which is inappropriate to time and place.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of the thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, 'Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code' (jainworld.com) [9]
Among the qualities Hemacandra expects of a Jain householder are keeping to the respected customs of the land and avoiding conduct out of keeping with time and place.
The Kural's final test is that an act should be fitting so the world does not scorn it, and Hemacandra's layman measures his conduct by the same standard of social fitness.
Sikhism
“Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 62, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Siree Raag Guru Nanak puts truthful conduct above even the knowledge of truth, so that how one lives is the final test.
The Kural asks for conduct that will not earn the world's scorn; Guru Nanak likewise makes conduct the test, though he measures it by truth rather than by public approval.
Greek Mythology
Arachne, a mortal weaver, challenges the goddess Athena and weaves the gods’ misdeeds; her presumption is punished and she is turned into a spider.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.1–145 [11]
Arachne did something that didn’t fit her place in the world, and she was punished for it.
Illustrates action out of keeping with one’s station meeting rejection, the Kural’s கொள்ளாத கொள்ளாது.
Philosophy
Adam Smith argues that we judge our own conduct by imagining how an impartial spectator would view it, and that we naturally desire not only to be praised but to be praiseworthy.
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), Part III [12]
Smith says a good check on our actions is to picture how a fair-minded observer would see them.
The Kural’s ‘act so that the world will not scorn’ is a version of Smith’s impartial-spectator test.

Real Life Example

Emperor Nero competes at the Olympic Games
67 CE · Olympia, Greece
During a tour of Greece, the Roman emperor Nero competed in the Greek games, adding contests in music and tragedy to the Olympic programme. At Olympia he drove a ten-horse chariot, fell from it and did not finish the race, yet was proclaimed the winner.
— Sources: [13] [14]
An emperor performing as a competitor and accepting a victory he had not earned became a lasting example of conduct unworthy of a ruler.
The kural says the world will not approve what does not suit one's own station, and Nero's games turned his rank into an object of ridicule.
