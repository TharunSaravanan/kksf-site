Kural 273

Inconsistent Conduct · Koodaavozhukkam · கூடாவொழுக்கம்

வலியில் நிலைமையான் வல்லுருவம் பெற்றம் புலியின்தோல் போர்த்துமேய்ந் தற்று.
Valiyil nilaimaiyaan valluruvam petram Puliyinthol porththumeindh thatru.

Meaning

Someone with no control over themselves who dresses up like a strict holy person is like a cow eating crops while wearing a tiger's skin.

Elementary

Picture a cow wearing a tiger costume so it can sneak into a farm and eat the crops. It looks scary, but it’s still just a cow munching grass. A person who dresses like a strong holy person but can’t control himself is the same — a costume on the outside, the same old habits inside.

University

The simile is doubly pointed: the cow is weak, not fierce, and the tiger-skin lends it a borrowed terror that lets it graze unchallenged on another’s field. வலியில் நிலைமையான் (“one lacking the strength” — traditionally, strength to restrain the senses) contrasts with வல்லுருவம் (“a mighty form”), the forbidding garb of tapas. The target is not ordinary weakness but the exploitation of a sacred appearance to feed appetite. The same fable circulated widely in the ancient world — the Panchatantra’s ass in the tiger-skin and Aesop’s ass in the lion’s skin — and in both the disguise ultimately betrays itself.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Panchatantra story “The Ass in the Tiger-Skin,” a washerman dresses his donkey in a tiger’s skin and turns it loose in the farmers’ fields at night; everyone flees from the ‘tiger’ while it eats the grain, until it brays and is recognised and killed.
— Panchatantra, Book 4, “The Ass in the Tiger-Skin” (tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [3]
A weak animal wearing a fearsome skin feeds on others’ crops until its real voice gives it away.
This is essentially the Kural’s image told as a story — the borrowed skin, the grazing on crops, and the inevitable exposure.
Christianity
“Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves.”
— Matthew 7:15 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns of religious teachers whose gentle outward appearance hides a predatory nature.
The same animal-disguise logic as the Kural, inverted: here the predator wears the harmless skin, but in both the costume misrepresents the real creature.
Islam
“And when you see them, their forms please you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech. [They are] as if they were pieces of wood propped up…”
— Qur’an 63:4 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes hypocrites whose impressive outward forms hide inner hollowness, like dry timber propped up to look solid.
Like the tiger-skinned cow, the hypocrite’s imposing exterior has nothing strong behind it.
Judaism
“Do not be afraid of the Pharisees, and neither [should you fear] from those who are not Pharisees; rather, beware of the hypocrites who appear like Pharisees, as their actions are like the act of the wicked Zimri and they request a reward like that of the righteous Pinehas.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 22b (William Davidson / Koren–Steinsaltz translation) [6]
King Yannai warns his wife that the real danger is people who look like pious Pharisees but act like sinners while demanding a saint’s reward.
Exactly the Kural’s type: someone borrowing the appearance of the devout while their deeds belong to a weaker, baser nature.
Buddhism
“There are many evil characters and uncontrolled men wearing the saffron robe. These wicked men will be born in states of woe because of their evil deeds.”
— Dhammapada 307 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that plenty of undisciplined people wear the monk’s robe, and their robe will not save them.
Matches the Kural’s ‘weak in self-control but strong in appearance’ figure.
Jainism
“As the poison Kâlakûta kills him who drinks it; as a weapon cuts him who awkwardly handles it; as a Vêtâla kills him who does not lay him; so the Law harms him who mixes it up with sensuality.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The text warns that taking up the monk's discipline while still giving in to the senses turns the discipline itself into a danger to the false monk.
Valluvar's cow grazing in a tiger's skin is a weakling hiding sensual appetite under a fierce ascetic guise; the Jain verse condemns the same mixture of religious garb and unmastered senses.
Sikhism
“Fareed, you wear your prayer shawl on your shoulders and the robes of a Sufi; your words are sweet, but there is a dagger in your heart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1380, Salok Sheikh Farid 50 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Shaikh Farid rebukes the false holy man who puts on the prayer shawl and robe of a Sufi and talks sweetly while hiding a dagger in his heart.
Valluvar likens the weak-willed man in an ascetic's fierce form to a cow in a tiger's skin grazing another's crop; Farid's false Sufi likewise uses borrowed holy dress to cover what he really is.
Greek Mythology
“An Ass found a Lion’s Skin, and dressed himself up in it. Then he went about frightening every one he met… Elated by the success of his trick, he loudly brayed in triumph.”
— Aesop, “The Ass in the Lion’s Skin” (tr. V. S. Vernon Jones) [10]
The ass in the lion’s skin scares everyone until its own braying reveals what it really is.
The Greek twin of Valluvar’s simile: a weak animal in a predator’s skin, exposed by its true nature.
Philosophy
“The boastful man, then, is thought to be apt to claim the things that bring glory, when he has not got them, or to claim more of them than he has…”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.7 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle defines the boaster as someone who claims admirable qualities he does not possess.
The tiger-skinned cow is a boaster in Aristotle’s sense: claiming the strength of austerity without having it.

Real Life Example

The Captain of Kopenick
16 October 1906 · Kopenick, near Berlin, German Empire (now Germany)
Wilhelm Voigt, an unemployed shoemaker with a criminal record, put together a Prussian army captain's uniform from second-hand pieces. Wearing it, he ordered a squad of about ten real soldiers to follow him to the town hall of Kopenick, where they arrested the mayor and the treasurer on his orders and he took about 4,000 marks from the town treasury. He was arrested ten days later and sentenced to four years in prison.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode became a byword for how a borrowed uniform could make soldiers and officials obey a man with no real authority at all.
The kural compares the weak man in the imposing garb of an ascetic to a cow grazing on crops in a tiger's skin, and Voigt fed himself from the town's treasury by wearing a captain's borrowed form.
