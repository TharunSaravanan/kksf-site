Kural 596

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

உள்ளுவ தெல்லாம் உயர்வுள்ளல் மற்றது தள்ளினுந் தள்ளாமை நீர்த்து.
Ulluva thellaam uyarvullal matradhu Thallinum thallaamai neerththu.

Meaning

Let all your thoughts be about rising higher. Even if you do not reach your goal, aiming high is as good as reaching it.

Elementary

If you aim to jump over a tall bar and only get halfway, you still jumped higher than if you’d aimed low. The Kural says always think big; even when you don’t reach it, the aiming itself counts.

University

The first line is a direct precept: ‘ulluvadhellaam uyarvu ullal’, let all that you think be the thinking of elevation. The second line is compressed and has produced different readings. Parimelazhagar (reflected in Pope’s ‘if it should be thrust from him by fate, it will have the nature of not being thrust from him’) takes ‘matradhu thallinum’ as ‘even if that (aim) is thwarted by fate (ool)’ and ‘thallaamai neerththu’ as ‘it has the nature (neermai) of not being thwarted’, since the nobility of the aim remains. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read the line as an imperative, ‘do not abandon such thinking even if it fails’, while Solomon Pappaiah adds that the great will praise the attempt, so it counts as fulfilled. Thirukkural.net compresses this as ‘failure then is as good as success’. The text is identical in the base sources; the interpretive variation lies in whether ‘thallaamai’ is descriptive or prescriptive.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, nor be attached to inaction.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna teaches that one’s task is the effort itself, not control of outcomes, and that giving up action is never right.
The Kural likewise says the lofty aim keeps its worth even when fate blocks the result.
Christianity
“…forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus.”
— Philippians 3:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Paul admits he has not yet arrived but keeps straining forward toward the highest goal.
His constant reaching for the ‘high calling’ is the Kural’s ‘let every thought be of rising’.
Islam
A hadith reported by al-Tabarani and others says that Allah loves lofty and noble matters and dislikes base and petty ones.
— al-Tabarani, al-Mu‘jam al-Kabir (hadith on ma‘ali al-umur) [5]
The Prophet teaches that believers should set their sights on noble, elevated aims rather than trivial ones.
This echoes the Kural’s command to let every thought be a thought of elevation.
Judaism
“It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you at liberty to neglect it;”
— Pirkei Avot 2:16 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon teaches that one must keep striving at a great task even if it cannot be completed in one’s lifetime.
Like the Kural, it values the pursuit of a high aim even when completion is blocked.
Buddhism
The Buddha recalls resolving before his awakening that he would gladly let his flesh and blood dry up, leaving skin, sinews and bones, rather than relax his energy before reaching what can be reached by human strength and effort.
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.5 [7]
The Buddha aimed at the highest goal and vowed never to lower his effort short of it.
This is the Kural’s ‘think only of rising’, carried to its utmost.
Jainism
“You have crossed the great ocean; why do you halt so near the shore? make haste to get on the other side; Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 10.34 (refrain from 10.1) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira urges his chief disciple Gautama not to settle for what he has achieved but to press on to the highest goal without a moment's slackness.
The Kural tells us to let every thought be of rising higher; Mahavira's charge to Gautama is the same refusal to rest short of the highest aim.
Sikhism
“If you desire to play this game of love with Me, then step onto My Path with your head in hand.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaraan Thay Vadheek, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak asks total commitment, even readiness to give one's life, from anyone who would walk the path of love for God.
The Kural asks that every thought be lofty and says such an aim keeps its worth even if fate defeats it; Guru Nanak's call to set out with head in hand, whatever the cost, is the closest Sikh counterpart.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads the young Heracles is approached by Vice, offering an easy, pleasant road, and Virtue, offering a steep, hard road to true glory; he chooses the harder, higher path.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 (Prodicus’ Choice of Heracles) [10]
Heracles deliberately picks the difficult road that leads upward rather than the comfortable one.
His choice embodies the Kural’s rule that one’s aims should always point toward elevation.
Philosophy
Seneca defends philosophers who fall short of their ideals, saying one should admire those who attempt great things even when they fail, because the attempt itself is noble.
— Seneca, On the Happy Life (De Vita Beata) 20 [11]
Seneca argues that striving toward a lofty goal deserves honour even without full success.
This is exactly the Kural’s second line: a high aim that is thwarted is as good as one achieved.

Real Life Example

Scott's party reaches the South Pole and dies on the return
17 January 1912 · South Pole and the Ross Ice Shelf, Antarctica
Captain Robert Falcon Scott led the British Antarctic (Terra Nova) Expedition, whose five-man polar party reached the South Pole on 17 January 1912, only to find that Roald Amundsen's Norwegian party had arrived there on 14 December 1911. All five men died on the return journey, Scott himself with Edward Wilson and Henry Bowers in late March, held up by a blizzard about 11 miles from a supply depot. A search party found their tent, bodies, diaries and photographs the following November.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Scott's party aimed at one of the hardest goals of their age and reached it, but fate denied them both priority and survival; they are still remembered for the height of their aim and their endurance.
The kural urges people to let every thought be of rising higher, and says that even if fate thwarts that aim, it keeps the worth of not having been thwarted, which is how Scott's expedition has been remembered.
