Kural 448

Seeking the Aid of Great Men · Periyaaraith Thunaikkodal · பெரியாரைத் துணைக்கோடல்

இடிப்பாரை இல்லாத ஏமரா மன்னன் கெடுப்பா ரிலானுங் கெடும்.
Idippaarai illaadha emaraa mannan Keduppaa rilaanung kedum.

Meaning

A king without anyone to scold and correct him has no protection, and he will be ruined even when no enemy is there to destroy him.

Elementary

A king with nobody brave enough to tell him he’s wrong is like someone walking without looking. Even if no enemy attacks him, he will trip over his own mistakes and fall.

University

This is the converse of 447. ‘Emaraa’ means unprotected or unsafeguarded — the missing protection is precisely the rebuker. The striking phrase ‘keduppaar ilaanum kedum’ (perishes even without destroyers) locates the source of ruin inside: unchecked error compounds until it destroys. Valluvar thus anticipates a well-known insight of political thought — that autocrats surrounded by yes-men fall by their own misjudgments more often than by foreign conquest.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Yuddha Kanda, Vibhishana urges his brother Ravana to return Sita to Rama; Ravana, surrounded by flattering ministers, insults and expels him, and Lanka is destroyed.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda [4]
Ravana drives away the one counselor willing to correct him and is ruined by his own obstinacy.
The king who rejects his rebuker brings about his own destruction.
Christianity
“He, that being often reproved hardeneth his neck, shall suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy.”
— Proverbs 29:1 (KJV) [5]
One who refuses correction again and again collapses suddenly and beyond repair.
Refusing rebuke leads to self-inflicted ruin.
Islam
The Prophet said that the religion is sincere advice (nasihah) — to God, His Book, His Messenger, the leaders of the Muslims and their common folk.
— Sahih Muslim 55 [6]
Sincere counsel to rulers is part of faith itself, implying that leaders deprived of it are endangered.
Supports the Kural’s view that a ruler needs honest advisers.
Judaism
“There is yet one man, Micaiah the son of Imlah, by whom we may enquire of the LORD: but I hate him; for he doth not prophesy good concerning me, but evil.”
— 1 Kings 22:8 (KJV) [5]
King Ahab prefers four hundred agreeable prophets to the one who tells him the truth; he goes to battle and is killed.
A king who shuts out his only honest critic perishes by his own choice.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that the one who admonishes, instructs and shields one from wrong is dear to the good and disliked by the bad.
— Dhammapada 77 [7]
Only those who are good welcome correction; those who reject the admonisher show their own ruin.
Implies the fate the Kural states outright for the uncorrected.
Jainism
“If admonished by a young or an old monk, by one above him or one of equal age, he should not retort against him, being perfectly free from passion; for one who is (as it were) carried away (by the stream of the Samsâra), will not get to its opposite shore.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.14.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga tells the monk to accept admonition from anyone, of any age or rank, and warns that one who rejects it is carried off by the current and never reaches the far shore.
Valluvar says the king with no one to rebuke him perishes even without enemies. The Jain verse likewise shows ruin coming from within, to one who refuses correction, with no outside destroyer needed.
Sikhism
“One who is attached to Maya is totally blind and deaf. He does not listen to the Word of the Shabad; he makes a great uproar and tumult.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 313, Raag Gauree (Gauree ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das describes the person ruled by Maya as deaf to the Guru's corrective Word, lost in his own noise.
The Kural says the king with no one to rebuke him perishes even without enemies; Guru Amar Das portrays the one who will not hear correction as destroying himself from within.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad 18, Polydamas urges Hector to withdraw the Trojans inside the walls now that Achilles has returned; Hector scorns him, the Trojans applaud Hector’s bad plan, and ‘no one praised Polydamas’ good counsel.’ The next day the Trojans are routed and Hector is killed.
— Homer, Iliad 18.249–313 [10]
Hector rejects his wise critic and brings disaster on himself and Troy.
The leader without a heeded rebuker falls through his own error.
Philosophy
“A prince who is not wise himself will never take good advice.”
— Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 23 (tr. W. K. Marriott) [11]
In his chapter on how to avoid flatterers, Machiavelli warns that a prince surrounded by flatterers, and unable to take counsel, is ruined.
The ruler without honest counsel undoes himself.

Real Life Example

Nicholas II takes personal command against his ministers' advice
1915-1917 · Russian Empire (now Russia)
In the late summer of 1915, with Russian armies in retreat, Tsar Nicholas II dismissed the commander in chief, Grand Duke Nicholas, and took supreme command himself, despite objections from his Council of Ministers. With the tsar away at headquarters, power in the capital passed largely to Empress Alexandra, and capable ministers were replaced by weaker figures, many favoured by Grigori Rasputin. The government lost the confidence of the country, and in March 1917 Nicholas was forced to abdicate.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By overruling and removing those who warned him, Nicholas cut himself off from correction at the moment his throne most needed it.
The kural says an unguarded king with no one to rebuke him perishes even without enemies to destroy him, and Nicholas's monarchy collapsed from within.
