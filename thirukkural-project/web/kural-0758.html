Kural 758

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

குன்றேறி யானைப்போர் கண்டற்றால் தன்கைத்தொன்று உண்டாகச் செய்வான் வினை.
Kundreri yaanaippor kandatraal thankaiththondru undaagach cheyvaan vinai.

Meaning

Starting a job with money already in hand is like watching an elephant fight from a hilltop. You are safe and can see everything.

Elementary

Imagine two huge elephants pushing and fighting in a field. If you stood in the middle, you could get hurt. But if you climb a hill and watch from the top, you are safe and can see everything. Valluvar says that starting a big job with savings in your pocket is like watching from that hill.

University

The simile draws on a familiar sight in Sangam India, the royal elephant fight, which was deadly to anyone on the ground and a pleasure to watch from high up. Thankaiththu ondru undaaga means ‘with something being in one’s own hand’, that is, having one’s own funds, and cheyvaan vinai is ‘the work of one who acts’. Parimelazhagar explains that such a person has no need to depend on others and so can watch the struggle of the undertaking without fear, while Kalaignar adds the image of not being caught between the fighting elephants. The sandhi-joined text reads கண்டற்றாற் றன்கைத்தொன் றுண்டாகச், which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Wealth brings about accessions of wealth, like elephants capturing (wild) elephants.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section VIII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna compares money that brings in more money to trained elephants used to capture wild ones.
Both texts use the elephant to show that someone who starts with wealth in hand is in a position of strength.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it? Lest haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all that behold it begin to mock him.”
— Luke 14:28-29 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses the builder who checks his funds before starting as an image of counting the cost of discipleship.
The builder with enough in hand is the Kural’s watcher on the hill, while the one without is caught and mocked.
Islam
“Nobody has ever eaten a better meal than that which one has earned by working with one’s own hands. The Prophet of Allah, David used to eat from the earnings of his manual labor.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2072 [5]
The Prophet praises living from one’s own earnings rather than depending on others.
The hadith’s pride in self-earned means matches the Kural’s trust in having one’s own wealth in hand.
Judaism
“And Rabbi Yitzḥak says: A person should always divide his money into three; he should bury one-third in the ground, and invest one-third in business, and keep one-third in his possession.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 42a (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud advises keeping part of one’s wealth liquid and at hand, so that one is never left without resources.
Rabbi Yitzhak’s third kept in hand is exactly the Kural’s wealth ‘in one’s own hand’ that makes a venture safe.
Buddhism
“One portion is to enjoy. Two parts invest in work. And the fourth should be kept for times of trouble.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha advises the householder to invest half his wealth in work and keep a quarter in reserve for hard times.
Working with capital and a reserve is the secure position the Kural pictures as the hilltop.
Jainism
“Vyayam ayochitam kurvan : Spending in proportion to one's income.” … “Balabalam janan : Aware of one's own strength and weakness.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, nos. 12 and 23, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, ‘Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code’ (jainworld.com) [8]
Among the Jain householder's practical virtues are measuring expenditure against income and knowing one's own strength and weakness before acting.
The Kural's man who starts a venture with money already in hand, watching safely as from a hilltop, reflects the same prudence the Jain householder code asks for in matching outlay to means and knowing one's capacity.
Sikhism
“Reliance on mortals is in vain - know this well.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 281, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani Sahib), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan teaches that leaning on other people for support is futile, and that the one Giver is God.
The Kural says one who undertakes a task with his own means in hand can watch the struggle safely, free of dependence on others; Guru Arjan likewise warns against depending on other people.
Greek Mythology
“But let it be your care to order your work properly, that in the right season your barns may be full of victual.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells Perses to plan his work so that his stores are full when they are needed.
Full barns before the season turns are the Kural’s funds in hand before a venture begins.
Philosophy
Aristotle tells how Thales, mocked for his poverty, foresaw a large olive harvest, used a small sum to reserve all the olive presses in Miletus and Chios in advance, and made a fortune when the harvest came.
— Aristotle, Politics, Book I, Part XI (trans. B. Jowett) [11]
Aristotle uses the story to show that a philosopher can make money easily when he chooses to, by having funds ready at the right moment.
Thales with deposits in hand, waiting calmly for the harvest, is the Kural’s watcher on the hill.

Real Life Example

Heinrich Schliemann uses his merchant fortune to excavate Hisarlik
1870 · Hisarlik, near Canakkale, Turkey
Heinrich Schliemann made a large fortune in trade, much of it as a military contractor during the Crimean War, and retired from business in his thirties to devote his money to archaeology. Frank Calvert, who owned part of the mound at Hisarlik and believed it was Troy, lacked the funds for further digging. Beginning in 1870, Schliemann paid for large excavations there himself, employing many workers, and he went on to uncover the remains of the ancient city.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Because he paid his own way, Schliemann could pursue an expensive, uncertain search that others could not afford to attempt.
The kural says that one who starts a venture with money already in hand is like a person watching an elephant fight from a hilltop, and Schliemann's private fortune let him undertake the dig without depending on anyone.
