Kural 762

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

உலைவிடத்து ஊறஞ்சா வன்கண் தொலைவிடத்துத் தொல்படைக் கல்லால் அரிது.
Ulaividaththu ooranjaa vankan tholaividaththuth tholpadaik kallaal aridhu.

Meaning

Only an army with a long history has the courage to stand firm after losses, unafraid of wounds even when its numbers have shrunk.

Elementary

When a team is losing badly, new players often give up, but players who have trained together for years keep going. They have been through hard games before. Valluvar says an old, experienced army is like that: it keeps its courage even when things are going wrong.

University

Ulaivu (உலைவு) means ‘reverse, setback’ and tolaivu (தொலைவு) ‘diminution, loss of numbers’, so the couplet describes valour (vaṉkaṇ, வன்கண்) that survives both defeat and attrition. Tolpaṭai (தொல்படை, ‘ancient army’) is read by Parimelazhagar as the hereditary core force (often called மூலப்படை in the commentarial tradition), as against hired or allied troops, a distinction that closely parallels Kautilya’s preference for hereditary troops. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar follow this reading, while Solomon Pappaiah extends tolpaṭai to native citizen-soldiers whose families have long lived in the land. The text is identical in both sources apart from sandhi (வன்கண் டொலைவிடத்துத் in the joined text).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Coming down directly, from father and grandfather (of the king), ever strong, obedient, happy in keeping their sons and wives well contented, not averse to making a long sojourn, ever and everywhere invincible, endowed with the power of endurance...”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book VI, Chapter 1 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya’s description of the best army begins with its hereditary character and stresses endurance and steadiness.
This is almost exactly Valluvar’s tolpaṭai: a hereditary army whose endurance holds when fortunes turn.
Christianity
“Thou therefore endure hardness, as a good soldier of Jesus Christ.”
— 2 Timothy 2:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Timothy that the mark of a good soldier is the capacity to endure hardship.
Endurance under hardship is the very valour the Kural assigns to a seasoned army.
Islam
“And how many a prophet [fought and] with him fought many religious scholars. But they never lost assurance due to what afflicted them in the cause of Allah, nor did they weaken or submit. And Allah loves the steadfast.”
— Qur’an 3:146 (Saheeh International) [5]
Revealed after the setback at Uhud, the verse holds up earlier companions of prophets who did not weaken when they were struck by losses.
It praises the same courage the Kural describes: steadiness when the fight is going badly.
Judaism
“Hear, O Israel, ye draw nigh this day unto battle against your enemies; let not your heart faint; fear not, nor be alarmed, neither be ye affrighted at them.”
— Deuteronomy 20:3 (JPS 1917, Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Before battle the priest addresses the army, telling it not to lose heart even when the enemy seems stronger.
The Kural’s ‘fearless valour’ is exactly what this exhortation tries to instil in Israel’s host.
Buddhism
“As an elephant in the battlefield withstands arrows shot from bows all around, even so shall I endure abuse.”
— Dhammapada 320 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares his patience to a trained war-elephant that holds its ground under a shower of arrows.
The image is the Kural’s ūṟañcā vaṉkaṇ, a seasoned fighter unshaken by wounds.
Jainism
“So a novice, who as yet has not suffered pains and is not yet used to a mendicant's life, believes himself a hero till he practises austerities.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.3.1.3 (cf. 1.3.1.7) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga sets the untried novice against the seasoned ascetic: only someone long practised in hardship keeps his courage when trouble comes, while the weak 'become disheartened like cowards in the battle' (1.3.1.7).
The Kural says courage that holds through defeat and losses is found only in a long-established army, and the Jain text likewise finds staying power only in those hardened by long endurance.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir's salok, kept in the Guru Granth Sahib, defines the true hero as one who holds the field even while being cut down piece by piece.
The Kural praises courage that stands firm even after defeat and heavy losses; Kabir's hero likewise does not leave the field however much he is cut down.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, when the Trojans drive the Achaeans back to their ships, Ajax son of Telamon stays at the sterns and keeps fighting off the attackers long after others have given way.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 15 [10]
Ajax is the Iliad’s great picture of defensive steadiness when the battle has turned against his side.
He embodies the Kural’s courage in the hour of defeat.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Laches, the general Laches first defines courage as staying at one’s post and not running away, then refines it to a kind of endurance of the soul; Socrates presses that it must be wise endurance.
— Plato, Laches 190e–192d [11]
The dialogue examines what military courage really is, rooted in steadfast endurance under danger.
Valluvar locates exactly this endurance in a veteran army facing defeat.

Real Life Example

The Ten Thousand march home after losing their generals
401-400 BCE · From Cunaxa near Babylon (Iraq) to Trapezus (Trabzon, Turkey)
A force of Greek mercenaries known as the Ten Thousand fought for Cyrus the Younger at Cunaxa in 401 BCE, but Cyrus was killed and the expedition failed. Their senior generals were then seized through treachery by the Persian satrap Tissaphernes during a parley. The soldiers elected new leaders, among them the Athenian Xenophon, and marched north through hostile mountains and snow-filled passes until they reached the Black Sea at Trapezus.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Stranded deep in enemy territory, leaderless and dwindling, the army kept its cohesion and fought its way out, a story Xenophon recorded in the Anabasis.
The kural says the courage to hold firm in defeat and loss of numbers is found mainly in a seasoned army, and the veteran Ten Thousand did exactly that after disaster had stripped them of their cause and their commanders.
