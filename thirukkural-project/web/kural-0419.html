Kural 419

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

நுணங்கிய கேள்வியர் அல்லார் வணங்கிய வாயினர் ஆதல் அரிது.
Nunangiya kelviyar allaar vanangiya Vaayinar aadhal aridhu.

Meaning

People who have not gained fine, deep learning by listening find it hard to speak humbly.

Elementary

People who have listened a lot to wise teachers usually speak kindly and humbly. People who haven’t listened much often find it hard to speak gently and politely.

University

Two adjectives govern the couplet: நுணங்கிய (fine, subtle) describes the quality of hearing, and வணங்கிய (bowed, humble) the quality of speech. The link is causal: refined hearing produces humility in speech, because the listener becomes aware of how much others know. Parimelazhagar reads வணங்கிய வாய் as speech that is modest and respectful. The kural thus connects the ear and the mouth: good speaking is an outcome of good listening — a theme echoed widely in wisdom literature that advises being quick to hear and slow to speak.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Gita defines austerity of speech as words that cause no distress, that are truthful, pleasant and beneficial, together with regular study and recitation of the scriptures (svādhyāya).
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 [4]
Gentle, truthful speech is paired with regular learning of the sacred texts.
Both link cultivated learning with courteous, humble speech.
Christianity
“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath:”
— James 1:19 (KJV) [5]
James makes hearing the precondition of restrained, gentle speech.
Listening first produces humble speech — the Kural’s causal link between hearing and mouth.
Islam
“And be moderate in your pace and lower your voice; indeed, the most disagreeable of sounds is the voice of donkeys.”
— Qur’an 31:19 (Sahih International) [6]
Part of the counsel of Luqman the Wise to his son; wisdom expresses itself as humble, lowered speech.
Wisdom received through a sage’s counsel issues in humble speech.
Judaism
“Keep thy foot when thou goest to the house of God, and be more ready to hear, than to give the sacrifice of fools… let thy words be few.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:1–2 (KJV) [5]
Ecclesiastes counsels readiness to hear and restraint of speech as marks of wisdom.
Hearing first, then few and humble words — close to the Kural.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada praises the monk who controls his tongue, speaks in moderation, is unassuming and explains the teaching in letter and spirit — his speech is pleasing.
— Dhammapada 363 [7]
Mastery of the teaching produces restrained, modest and sweet speech.
Refined learning yielding humble speech — as in the Kural.
Jainism
“But for the following fifteen good qualities he is called well-behaved: if he is always humble, steady, free from deceit and curiosity; if he abuses nobody; … if he listens to friendly advice; if he is not proud of his learning; … if he is enlightened, polite, decent, and quiet: then he is called well-behaved.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.10–13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on the very learned pairs real learning with humility, courtesy and restrained speech. It lists listening to advice, abusing no one, and being polite and quiet among the marks of the well-trained monk.
Valluvar says that without refined learning gained by hearing it is hard to have humble, courteous speech. The Uttaradhyayana likewise sets humility, polite speech and readiness to listen among the qualities that come with learning.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak names sweetness and humility, not size or show, as the heart of every virtue.
The Kural says only those refined by careful listening can speak humbly; Guru Nanak makes humble, sweet speech and bearing the essence of goodness.
Greek Mythology
Young Telemachus, sent by Athena to question Nestor, protests that he is inexperienced in subtle speech and that it is shameful for a young man to question an elder; Athena encourages him, and he addresses Nestor with deference.
— Homer, Odyssey 3.21–28, 79–101 [10]
Telemachus’ speech is modest precisely because he knows he has come to listen and learn.
The posture of a hearer produces respectful, humble speech.
Philosophy
Zeno of Citium, rebuking a talkative youth, said that we have two ears and one mouth so that we may listen more and speak less.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 7.23 [11]
The Stoic founder makes the proportion of hearing to speaking a matter of wisdom.
Listening more produces measured, humble speech — the Kural’s link.

Real Life Example

Gandhi spends a year listening before taking part in Indian politics
1915 · India
When Mohandas Gandhi returned to India from South Africa in January 1915, his political mentor Gopal Krishna Gokhale made him promise to spend a year travelling the country to gain experience, keeping his ears open and expressing no opinion on public questions until this period of probation was over. Gandhi kept the promise even after Gokhale died in February 1915, and spent much of the year touring India and meeting people of many regions and communities.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gandhi's later ability to speak to and for India's very diverse people rested partly on this deliberate period of listening before speaking.
The kural says that those without refined learning gained by hearing can hardly speak with humility, and Gokhale's counsel made Gandhi listen widely before he spoke, grounding his later public speech in what he had heard.
