Kural 102

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

காலத்தி னாற்செய்த நன்றி சிறிதெனினும் ஞாலத்தின் மாணப் பெரிது.
Kaalaththinaal seydha nandri siridheninum Gnaalaththin maanap peridhu.

Meaning

Help given at the right time, when it is truly needed, may be small, but it is worth more than the whole world.

Elementary

If you are thirsty in the middle of a race and someone hands you one small cup of water, that cup feels more valuable than a whole shop full of drinks later. Help that comes right when you need it is the biggest kind of help, even if it is tiny.

University

The operative word is காலத்தினால், 'by reason of the time', that is, timeliness. Valluvar shifts the measure of a benefit from its quantity (சிறிதெனினும், 'though small') to its moment, and then inflates it past ஞாலம், the whole earth, with the intensifier மாண ('exceedingly'). Kurals 102 to 104 form a graded triad of hyperbolic comparisons (world, sea, palmyra palm), each isolating a different factor that magnifies a benefit: timing, disinterestedness, and the recipient's discernment. The underlying intuition is almost economic: the value of help peaks at the point of greatest need, which is why Solomon Pappaiah glosses the phrase as help given in our time of crisis. Aristotle records the same argument from the benefactor's side, noting that givers insist their help came 'in times of danger or similar need' (Nicomachean Ethics 8.13).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Ravana carries Sita off, the aged vulture-king Jatayu flies at him and fights until he is mortally wounded. Dying, he tells Rama what happened; Rama grieves for him as for his own father and performs his funeral rites with his own hands.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda 49-51 and 67-68 (sarga numbering varies by edition) [3]
An old bird who could not really win still threw himself into the fight at the exact moment Sita needed help, and Rama honoured that help as if Jatayu were family.
Jatayu's help was small in outcome but came at the critical hour, and Rama treated it as immeasurably great, just as the Kural says.
Christianity
“For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in:”
— Matthew 25:35 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus's parable of the judgment, the King counts simple acts like a meal, a drink, or a bed given to someone in need as done to him, with eternal consequences.
Small, timely help given in someone's moment of need is valued far beyond its size, which is the Kural's point.
Islam
“So he (went down the well), filled his shoe with water, caught hold of it with his teeth and climbed up and watered the dog. Allah thanked him for his (good) deed and forgave him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2363 [5]
The Prophet told of a man who saw a dog dying of thirst, climbed down a well to fill his shoe with water for it, and was forgiven by God for that one small act.
A tiny act done at the exact moment of need is treated as enormous, even by God, just as the Kural says timely help outweighs the world.
Judaism
Rashi, following the Midrash, explains that Aaron rather than Moses struck the Nile to begin the plagues, because the river had sheltered Moses when he was placed in it as an infant; Moses would not strike what had once protected him.
— Rashi on Exodus 7:19 (drawing on Midrash Tanchuma) [6]
Even a river that once kept baby Moses safe in his moment of danger was owed gratitude, so Moses would not hit it.
Help given at the critical moment, even by water, earns lasting gratitude, far out of proportion to the 'size' of the act.
Buddhism
According to the Pali commentarial biography, in the second week after his awakening the Buddha stood gazing without blinking at the Bodhi tree that had sheltered him during his final struggle; the spot is honoured at Bodh Gaya as the Animisa (Animeshlochana) Cetiya, and later tradition reads the act as gratitude to the tree.
— Jataka Nidanakatha (commentarial biography); Mahabodhi Temple Complex, Bodh Gaya [7]
The Buddha spent a whole week simply looking at the tree that had given him shade while he struggled for enlightenment.
Shade is a small gift, but given at the decisive moment it was honoured as something immense, like the Kural's timely favour.
Jainism
“Master, even if this food is not suitable, nevertheless, accept it, you who delight only in benefits to others. Favor me.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X, ch. IV, parts 6-7 'Vīra's special vow' and 'The story of Candanā' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Mahāvīra had vowed to accept alms only under very exacting conditions, and kings, ministers and wealthy households failed to feed him for months; the chained Candanā finally offered him a little kulmāṣa (boiled pulse) from the corner of a winnowing basket, and the gods marked the moment with a rain of treasure.
The humblest possible gift, given at the one moment it was needed, ended a fast of almost six months that all the riches of Kauśāmbī could not, which illustrates Valluvar's point that timeliness makes a small help outweigh the world.
Sikhism
“Where there is no mother, father, children, friends or siblings - O my mind, there, only the Naam, the Name of the Lord, shall be with you as your help and support.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 264, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan praises the Naam as the help that arrives at the hour when every human support has fallen away.
The Kural prizes help given at the moment of real need above the whole world; Gurbani likewise exalts the support that comes precisely when no one else can help.
Greek Mythology
In the Aesopic fable of the Ant and the Dove, an ant falls into a stream and is drowning when a dove drops a leaf or twig beside it, letting it climb to safety; later the ant stings a bird-catcher's foot just as he is about to snare the dove.
— Aesop's Fables, The Ant and the Dove (Perry Index 235; G. F. Townsend trans.) [10]
A leaf is almost nothing, but dropped at the exact moment the ant was drowning it saved its life, and the ant never forgot it.
A tiny, timely favour is repaid as if it were enormous, which is the Kural's lesson.
Philosophy
Seneca argues that a benefit given promptly, before it has to be asked for, is received with far greater gratitude, while one that is delayed or extracted by long pleading loses much of its value; hesitation, he says, is close to refusal.
— Seneca, De Beneficiis 2.1-2 (Aubrey Stewart trans.) [11]
The Roman Stoic says the same gift is worth more when it comes quickly, right when needed, than when it arrives late.
Timing multiplies the worth of a favour, exactly the variable the Kural isolates.

Real Life Example

The little ships help evacuate Dunkirk
26 May - 4 June 1940 · Dunkirk, France, and the English Channel
With British, French and Belgian troops trapped around the French port of Dunkirk, the Royal Navy organised Operation Dynamo to bring them back across the Channel. Because large warships could not approach the shallow beaches, the British Admiralty appealed to the owners of small boats, and these little ships, many of them requisitioned and mostly crewed by naval personnel, ferried men from the beaches to the larger vessels offshore. More than 338,000 British and French soldiers were rescued, far more than the government had first expected.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Many of the vessels were small pleasure craft and launches, yet arriving at the one moment when the army's survival hung in the balance made them part of a rescue that kept Britain's trained army intact.
The kural says a favour done at the moment of need, however small, outweighs the world; the little ships mattered because of when they came, not because of their size.
