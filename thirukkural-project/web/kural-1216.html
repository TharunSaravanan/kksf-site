Kural 1216

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

நனவென ஒன்றில்லை ஆயின் கனவினால் காதலர் நீங்கலர் மன்.
Nanavena ondrillai aayin kanavinaal kaadhalar neengalar man.

Meaning

If there were no such thing as being awake, my love, who comes to me in dreams, would never leave me.

Elementary

Have you ever had a really happy dream and felt sad when the alarm clock rang? This woman wishes there were no alarm clock at all, no waking up, so that the husband she sees in her dreams could stay with her forever.

University

Naṉavu eṉa oṉṟu illai āyiṉ, ‘if there were no one thing called waking’, is a bold counterfactual that makes waking itself the villain; Pappaiah even calls it ‘a cruel sinner’ (koṭiya pāvi), and Kalaignar has it ‘suddenly coming to spoil’ the meeting. Nīṅkalar, ‘he would not depart’, describes the lover in the dream, and the final particle maṉ again marks an impossible wish, as in 1212. The couplet inverts the common wisdom that dreams are unreal and waking true: for the heroine, waking is what takes the beloved away. Pope: ‘And if there were no waking hour, my love / In dreams would never from my side remove.’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad says that in dreams there are no chariots, horses or roads, yet the self creates chariots, horses and roads, joys and delights, for itself, being their maker.
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.3.10 (paraphrase) [3]
The Upanishad describes the dream-world as one the self fashions and fully inhabits while it lasts.
The Kural’s heroine wishes she could live wholly in that self-made world where her beloved never departs.
Christianity
“I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, and by the hinds of the field, that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.”
— Song of Solomon 3:5 (KJV; also 2:7) [4]
The bride in the Song of Solomon repeatedly asks that love not be roused before its time, guarding a blissful repose.
Her plea not to wake echoes the Kural’s wish that waking would never come to break the lovers’ union.
Islam
“Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die [He takes] during their sleep. Then He keeps those for which He has decreed death and releases the others for a specified term.”
— Qur’an 39:42 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes sleep as a temporary taking of the soul, released again at waking.
For the Kural’s heroine the soul’s nightly absence is where she meets her beloved; its release into waking is the parting she laments.
Judaism
“And it shall be as when a hungry man dreameth, and, behold, he eateth, But he awaketh, and his soul is empty.”
— Isaiah 29:8 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Isaiah’s image captures the dreamer who is satisfied in sleep and left empty on waking.
This is the Kural’s complaint exactly: in the dream the beloved is present, and waking leaves her empty.
Buddhism
“Just as a man doesn’t see, on awakening, what he met in a dream, even so he doesn’t see, when they are dead, their time done, those he held dear.”
— Sutta Nipata 4.6, Jara Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha uses the vanishing of dream-companions at waking as the image of how loved ones are lost.
The Kural states the same experience from inside: waking is what makes the beloved met in the dream disappear.
Jainism
The Acaranga Sutra contrasts the unwise, who sleep, with the sages, who are always awake, making wakefulness the mark of spiritual life.
— Acaranga Sutra I.3.1 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Jain scripture prizes vigilance and treats ‘sleep’ as delusion.
The Kural’s heroine wishes waking away; Jainism, by contrast, would call her dream-bound love the true sleep from which one must awake.
Sikhism
“Enjoying the bliss of divine dream, I was woken up by the voice of rain-bird and that broke my celestial dream. The awe and marvel of love-filled state disappeared reawakening the pangs of separation. I was restless like a fish out of water.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabit Savaiye, Kabit 205 (English translation as given on BaniDB) [9]
Bhai Gurdas describes a dream of union with the Guru broken by the call of the rain-bird, so that waking brings back the pain of separation.
The Kural's wife says that if there were no such thing as waking, her beloved would never leave her; in Bhai Gurdas it is exactly the moment of waking that ends the meeting and leaves the lover like a fish out of water.
Greek Mythology
The ghost of Patroclus comes to Achilles in his sleep and asks for burial; Achilles stretches out his arms to embrace his friend, but the spirit vanishes like smoke beneath the earth, and Achilles springs up awake in amazement.
— Homer, Iliad 23.65-107 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows the beloved met in sleep slipping away at the very moment the dreamer reaches for him and wakes.
Achilles’ lost embrace is what the Kural’s heroine fears every dawn: waking is what makes the beloved leave.
Philosophy
Pascal remarks that if we dreamed the same thing every night, it would affect us as much as the objects we see every day; it is chiefly the continuity of waking life that makes us call it real.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées, fragment 386 (Brunschvicg numbering) (paraphrase) [11]
Pascal suggests that what separates dream from waking is only waking’s steady continuity.
The Kural imagines removing that very continuity, abolishing waking, so that the dream-beloved would become her whole reality.

Real Life Example

Coleridge's Kubla Khan and the person from Porlock
published 1816 · Somerset, England
Samuel Taylor Coleridge said that several hundred lines of a poem came to him in a dream while he was under the influence of laudanum. On waking he began writing them down but, by his account, was interrupted by a person on business from Porlock, after which the rest of the vision was gone. The surviving 54-line fragment, Kubla Khan, was published in 1816, and some scholars think the interruption story was an excuse.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The 'person from Porlock' has become a byword for the waking world breaking into, and ending, a precious vision.
The kural's heroine says that if there were no such thing as waking, the beloved who comes to her in dreams would never leave; Coleridge's lost poem is the classic case of waking chasing a dream away.
