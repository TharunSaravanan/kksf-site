Kural 32

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

அறத்தினூஉங்கு ஆக்கமும் இல்லை அதனை மறத்தலின் ஊங்கில்லை கேடு.
Araththinooungu aakkamum illai adhanai, Maraththalin oongillai kedu.

Meaning

Nothing helps you more than doing what is right, and nothing hurts you more than forgetting to do it.

Elementary

Think of brushing your teeth. Doing it is the best thing for your teeth, and forgetting to do it is the worst thing for them. This kural says goodness works the same way — doing it is the best thing possible, and forgetting about it is the worst.

University

The couplet is built on a deliberate chiasmus: ஆக்கம் (aakkam, increase/gain) is set against கேடு (kedu, ruin/loss), and the pivot between them is not 'doing evil' but மறத்தல் (marathal, forgetting). Valluvar's diagnosis of moral failure is therefore cognitive rather than volitional — Parimelazhagar glosses the forgetting as arising from மயக்கம் (delusion), not defiance. This makes the verse subtly different from ordinary two-path moralism: the opposite of virtue is not vice but inattention. The verse also repeats அறத்தினூஉங்கு from Kural 31, a rhetorical device (antadi-like linkage) binding the chapter's opening pair into a single argument.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Justice, being violated, destroys; justice, being preserved, preserves: therefore justice must not be violated, lest violated justice destroy us.”
— Manusmriti 8.15, trans. Georg Bühler (Sacred Books of the East, 1886) [3]
The Sanskrit maxim dharmo rakṣati rakṣitaḥ presents dharma as reciprocal: it guards whoever guards it and destroys whoever abandons it.
It states the Kural's exact two-sidedness — maximum gain from upholding virtue, maximum ruin from letting it go.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus sets total worldly acquisition against the loss of one's own self, arguing that no quantity of gain compensates for that loss.
Like the Kural, it measures gain and ruin on a single scale and concludes that moral loss is the greatest possible ruin.
Islam
“Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul, and doomed is the one who corrupts it!”
— Qur'an 91:9-10, Ash-Shams (Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The closing oath-sequence of Surah Ash-Shams distils all human outcomes into a single antithesis between purifying (zakkāhā) and corrupting (dassāhā) the soul.
Both couplets reduce human fortune to one binary: maintaining moral integrity is the whole of gain, abandoning it the whole of ruin.
Judaism
“I call heaven and earth to witness against you this day: I have put before you life and death, blessing and curse. Choose life—if you and your offspring would live—”
— Deuteronomy 30:19 (JPS/Sefaria Tanakh); cf. 30:15 [6]
Moses' closing charge presents obedience to the covenant not as one option among many but as the choice between existence and annihilation.
It gives the same stark two-sided accounting the Kural does: righteousness is all gain, its abandonment all ruin.
Buddhism
“Should a person commit evil, let him not do it again and again. Let him not find pleasure therein, for painful is the accumulation of evil. Should a person do good, let him do it again and again. Let him find pleasure therein, for blissful is the accumulation of good.”
— Dhammapada 117-118, Papavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
This paired verse presents merit and demerit as accumulating stocks whose eventual ripening is guaranteed, pleasant in one case and painful in the other.
Same symmetrical ledger as the Kural: the accumulation of good is the greatest gain, its opposite the greatest loss.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!” … “Thus the soul which suffers for its carelessness, is driven about in the Samsara by its good and bad Karman”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.1 and 10.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
In the 'Leaf of the Tree' lecture Mahavira warns his chief disciple Gautama, in a refrain repeated throughout, never to lapse into carelessness (pramada) even for a moment, because heedlessness is what keeps the soul drifting through rebirths.
The Kural places the worst ruin not in active vice but in forgetting virtue. Jainism likewise names inattentive carelessness, pramada, as the root of the soul's undoing.
Sikhism
“In your mind, you do not remember the One Lord-you fool! You have forgotten the Lord; your virtues shall wither away.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak pictures people sinking in a swamp of attachment and diagnoses their fall not as deliberate wickedness but as forgetting, warning that once God is forgotten, virtue itself withers.
It shares the Kural's striking claim that the deepest ruin comes from forgetting rather than from open defiance.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus' Eumenides the Furies, ancient goddesses of blood-vengeance, argue before the Athenian court that if Orestes goes unpunished every restraint on human wrongdoing will dissolve, and they warn the city that there are times when dread must sit enthroned over the heart, since neither anarchy nor tyranny follows where reverence is kept.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides, the Furies' choral ode (ll. 490-565) [10]
The Furies are making a civic argument: the forgetting of retributive justice is not a local error but the collapse of the moral order that holds a society together.
It dramatises the Kural's second line — the forgetting of dharma is itself the supreme catastrophe.
Philosophy
In Republic Book IV Socrates concludes that justice is the health, beauty and good condition of the soul while injustice is its disease, deformity and weakness — so that asking whether injustice pays is as absurd as asking whether it pays to have a ruined body.
— Plato, Republic IV.444d-445b [11]
The argument makes moral condition intrinsic rather than instrumental: virtue is what the soul's flourishing consists of, and vice is its destruction.
This is the Kural's claim rendered as an argument: nothing can outweigh virtue as gain, and nothing can rival its loss as ruin.

Real Life Example

The collapse of Charles Ponzi's scheme
1920 · Boston, Massachusetts, United States
In late 1919 Charles Ponzi, an Italian immigrant, opened a business in Boston promising investors a 50 percent return in 45 days from trading international postal reply coupons. In fact he paid earlier investors with money taken from later ones, and he became very rich within months. After journalists at the Boston Post investigated, the scheme collapsed in August 1920; Ponzi was arrested, convicted of mail fraud and imprisoned, several banks failed in the aftermath, and he was later deported to Italy, dying in poverty in Brazil in 1949.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ponzi's name became the standard term for this kind of fraud, and his rapid rise and total fall is one of the best-known warnings about wealth built on deceit.
The kural says there is no greater ruin than forgetting virtue, and Ponzi's fortune, built by setting honesty aside, ended in disgrace, prison and poverty.
