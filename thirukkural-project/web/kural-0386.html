Kural 386

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

காட்சிக் கெளியன் கடுஞ்சொல்லன் அல்லனேல் மீக்கூறும் மன்னன் நிலம்.
Kaatchik keliyan kadunjollan allanel Meekkoorum mannan nilam.

Meaning

If a king is easy for his people to meet and never speaks harshly, the whole world will praise his land.

Elementary

Imagine a school principal whose door is always open and who speaks kindly to every student. Everyone would say that’s a great school! A king who is easy to meet and never speaks rudely makes his whole country admired.

University

Kaatchikku eliyan — ‘easy to be seen’ — names the political virtue of accessibility: a ruler who can be reached by ordinary petitioners without layers of gatekeepers. The second condition, not being kadunjollan (a speaker of harsh words), concerns the manner of the encounter once access is granted. The reward is striking: it is the land (nilam), not merely the king, that the world exalts, implying that a ruler’s approachability shapes the reputation of the whole polity. Kautilya warns in almost identical terms that a king who makes himself inaccessible breeds confusion and public disaffection, and Islamic tradition records a hadith threatening divine seclusion from the ruler who secludes himself from his people.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When in the court, he shall never cause his petitioners to wait at the door, for when a king makes himself inaccessible to his people and entrusts his work to his immediate officers, he may be sure to engender confusion in business, and to cause thereby public disaffection, and himself a prey to his enemies.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.19 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya warns that a king who keeps people waiting and hides behind officials will make his people unhappy and his kingdom weak.
The exact virtue of ‘kaatchikku eliyan’ — being easy for subjects to reach.
Christianity
“Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls.”
— Matthew 11:28-29 (KJV) [4]
Jesus invites everyone who is tired or burdened to come to him, describing himself as gentle and humble.
Christ as king is openly approachable and gentle — both halves of the Kural’s ideal.
Islam
“If Allah puts anyone in the position of authority over the affairs of the Muslims, and he secludes himself (from them), not fulfilling their needs, wants, and poverty, Allah will keep Himself away from him, not fulfilling his need, want and poverty.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 2948 [5]
The Prophet warned that a ruler who hides from his people and ignores their needs will find God hiding from him.
A direct religious sanction for the Kural’s demand that a ruler be easy to approach.
Judaism
“Ye shall not respect persons in judgment; but ye shall hear the small as well as the great; ye shall not be afraid of the face of man; for the judgment is God’s:”
— Deuteronomy 1:17 (KJV) [4]
Moses instructs Israel’s judges to listen to ordinary people just as carefully as to important ones.
Hearing ‘the small as well as the great’ is the practical meaning of a ruler being easy to approach.
Buddhism
“...at any time, whether I am eating, in the women’s quarters, the bed chamber, the chariot, the palanquin, in the park or wherever, reporters are to be posted with instructions to report to me the affairs of the people so that I might attend to these affairs wherever I am.”
— Ashoka, Rock Edict VI (tr. S. Dhammika) [6]
Emperor Ashoka ordered that the people’s concerns be brought to him at any hour, wherever he was.
An actual Buddhist king institutionalising the Kural’s ideal of being always reachable.
Jainism
“As giving safety is the best of gifts, as the best of true speeches is that which causes no distress, as chastity is the highest of austerities, so is the Śramaṇa Jñātṛputra the highest of men.”
— Sutrakritanga I.6.23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In the hymn to Mahavira, the best speech is defined as true speech that causes no pain to others. This reflects the Jain vow of truthfulness, which is bound up with non-harm (ahimsa).
Valluvar's beloved king is one who is not kadunjollan, a speaker of harsh words. The Jain ideal of truthful speech that causes no distress is the same standard of gentle speech, though the verse says nothing about how accessible a ruler is.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak names sweetness of manner and humility as the very essence of every good quality.
The Kural praises the king who is never harsh of speech; Guru Nanak makes the same gentleness of manner the essence of virtue.
Greek Mythology
At the opening of Sophocles’ Oedipus the King, Oedipus comes out in person to the suppliants at his palace, saying he did not think it right to hear of their troubles from messengers but came himself.
— Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 1-13 [9]
King Oedipus goes out to meet his suffering people personally instead of sending servants.
Dramatises the approachable king the Kural praises — though the play then shows his temper toward Teiresias and Creon.
Philosophy
“A readiness to listen to those who had anything to propose for the common weal … He was also easy in conversation, and he made himself agreeable without any offensive affectation … There was in him nothing harsh, nor implacable, nor violent.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book I, §16 (tr. George Long) [10]
Marcus Aurelius describes his adoptive father, the emperor Antoninus Pius, as a ruler who listened to anyone with something useful to propose, was easy to talk to, and had nothing harsh about him.
This is the Kural’s praiseworthy king drawn from life: easy to approach and never harsh in speech.

Real Life Example

King Christian X's daily rides through occupied Copenhagen
1940-1945 · Copenhagen, Denmark
During the German occupation of Denmark, King Christian X rode on horseback through the streets of Copenhagen every day, where citizens could see and greet him. The rides became a public sign that he had not abandoned his claim to Danish sovereignty and won him great popular support. Later legends, such as the story that he wore a yellow star, are not true, but the daily rides are well documented.
— Sources: [11] [12]
By making himself visible and approachable to ordinary people at a time of national humiliation, the king strengthened the morale and unity of the country.
The kural praises a king who is easy for his people to see and approach, and Christian X's daily appearance among Copenhagen's citizens made the whole land admired for its quiet steadfastness.
