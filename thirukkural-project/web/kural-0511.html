Kural 511

Selection and Employment · Therindhu Vinaiyaadal · தெரிந்து வினையாடல்

நன்மையும் தீமையும் நாடி நலம்புரிந்த தன்மையான் ஆளப் படும்.
Nanmaiyum theemaiyum naadi nalampurindha Thanmaiyaan aalap padum.

Meaning

Give the job to the person who weighs the good and the bad in a matter and then naturally chooses to do what is good.

Elementary

Imagine picking a team captain. You don’t pick the kid who just does whatever pops into their head. You pick the one who stops, thinks “what could go right, what could go wrong?”, and then chooses the good way. That’s the kind of person you give a job to.

University

The chapter opens by naming the first qualification of a worthy agent: not raw talent but a settled disposition (தன்மை) that examines (நாடி) both benefit and harm and then inclines (புரிந்த) toward the good. Varadarasan and Kalaignar read it as a test of the candidate’s character, while Solomon Pappaiah reads it as an instruction to the appointee himself — once entrusted with a task, weigh its good and ill and do only what is good. Both readings converge on judgment joined to moral preference: discernment without a love of the good produces a clever but dangerous servant, and good intentions without discernment produce a well-meaning bungler. The pairing anticipates Aristotle’s prohairesis (deliberate choice) and the Upanishadic distinction between the good (śreyas) and the merely pleasant (preyas).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The good and the pleasant approach man: the wise goes round about them and distinguishes them. Yea, the wise prefers the good to the pleasant, but the fool chooses the pleasant through greed and avarice.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.2 (Max Müller, SBE vol. 15) [3]
Yama tells Nachiketas that both options present themselves to everyone; the wise person walks around them, examines them, and deliberately picks the good.
Exactly the Kural’s two-step: examine both sides (நாடி), then prefer the good (நலம்புரிந்த).
Christianity
“Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.”
— 1 Thessalonians 5:21 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Thessalonians to test everything rather than accept it blindly, and then cling to whatever proves good.
Testing plus holding to the good is the disposition the Kural says marks the employable person.
Islam
“Who listen to speech and follow the best of it. Those are the ones Allah has guided, and those are people of understanding.”
— Qur’an 39:18 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who hear all that is said, weigh it, and follow the best part of it.
Hearing everything and following the best mirrors weighing good and ill and choosing the good.
Judaism
“Give Thy servant therefore an understanding heart to judge Thy people, that I may discern between good and evil; for who is able to judge this Thy great people?”
— 1 Kings 3:9 (JPS 1917) [6]
Young King Solomon asks God not for wealth or long life but for the ability to tell good from evil so he can govern well.
Solomon names the very faculty the Kural treats as the first qualification for anyone who governs or serves the state.
Buddhism
“Not by passing arbitrary judgments does a man become just; a wise man is he who investigates both right and wrong.”
— Dhammapada 256 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says a person is truly just and wise only when they carefully investigate both sides before deciding.
Investigating both right and wrong is the Kural’s நன்மையும் தீமையும் நாடி.
Jainism
“After listening to scriptures, a person comes to know what is good and what is sinful, having thus known through listening one ought to perform what leads to welfare.”
— Saman Suttam v. 245 (Precepts on Right Knowledge; from Dasavaikalika Sutra 4.11; English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [8]
This Dasavaikalika verse, gathered in the Saman Suttam, teaches that one should come to know both the good and the harmful and then choose to do what leads to welfare.
The kural asks for the person who weighs both good and harm and is drawn to the good; the Jain verse describes exactly that act of knowing both sides and then choosing the beneficial.
Sikhism
“The swans peck at the diamonds and pearls, while the crane chases after frogs.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 960, Raag Raamkalee, Raamkalee Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan contrasts the swan, which by its nature picks out what is precious, with the crane, which by its nature goes after what is base.
The Kural says to employ the one who, after weighing good and harm, is by nature drawn to the good; Guru Arjan's swan is exactly such a nature, choosing pearls over frogs.
Greek Mythology
The Choice of Heracles: at a crossroads the young Heracles is approached by two women, Vice offering an easy, pleasant road and Virtue offering a hard but noble one; after hearing both, he chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus’ fable as retold in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 [10]
Heracles listens to both sides before choosing, and the choice shapes the labours he later carries out for others.
The hero who weighs both paths and chooses the good is the one fit for great tasks, just as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“...discern between good and evil, and so to choose always and everywhere the better life as he has opportunity.”
— Plato, Republic X, 618c (Jowett) [11]
At the end of the Republic, Socrates says the most important learning is the ability to tell good from evil and so always pick the better course.
Plato treats this power of discernment as the key qualification, just as the Kural makes it the first test of a worker.

Real Life Example

The Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances is signed
16 September 1987 · Montreal, Canada
Governments meeting in Montreal signed a protocol to cut and eventually phase out the production and use of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and other chemicals that deplete the stratospheric ozone layer, even though these chemicals were widely used in refrigeration, aerosols and industry. The treaty came into force in 1989, was strengthened several times, and became the first United Nations treaty to be ratified by every country. CFCs were phased out worldwide by 2010.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Faced with useful but harmful products, the world's governments weighed the benefit against the damage and chose the course that protected life.
The kural praises the one who weighs both the good and the harm in a matter and is drawn to do the good, which is what the parties to the Montreal Protocol did.
