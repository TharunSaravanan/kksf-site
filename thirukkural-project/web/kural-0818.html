Kural 818

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

ஒல்லும் கருமம் உடற்று பவர்கேண்மை சொல்லாடார் சோர விடல்.
Ollum karumam udatru pavarkenmai sollaadaar sora vidal.

Meaning

Some people pretend they cannot help and so spoil a job that could easily be done. Let your friendship with them quietly fade without saying anything.

Elementary

If a friend always says ‘I can’t’ whenever you need a small hand, even when they easily could, you don’t need to have a big fight. You can just slowly spend more time with friends who do help.

University

Ollum karumam (ஒல்லும் கருமம்) is ‘a task that can be done’; udatrupavar (உடற்றுபவர்) are those who ‘harass’ or ruin it, which Solomon Pappaiah and the classical English versions read as feigning inability to do what they could, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar as making the possible impossible. The remedy is notably mild: sollaadaar (சொல்லாடார்), ‘without speaking of it’, sora vidal (சோர விடல்), ‘let it slacken’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read sollaadaar as ‘without letting them know’, Puliyur Kesigan as ‘by ceasing to talk with them’. The counsel is gradual disengagement rather than confrontation, a tactful way to avoid making a fresh enemy. The texts agree; the sandhi-joined edition writes ‘ஒல்லுங் கரும முடற்று பவர்கேண்மை’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Shalya agrees to serve as Karna’s charioteer at Kurukshetra but, having privately promised Yudhishthira to weaken Karna, belittles him throughout the battle and undermines his morale.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva and Karna Parva (Shalya as Karna’s charioteer) [4]
The man whose job was to carry Karna’s chariot to victory uses his position to spoil the very task he was given.
Shalya is the Kural’s udatrupavar, the companion who ruins a doable task from the inside.
Christianity
“And one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?”
— James 2:16 (KJV) [5]
James mocks those who offer warm words to someone in need while doing nothing they could easily do.
This is the Kural’s friend who could help but finds a way not to.
Islam
“And they will swear by Allah, ‘If we were able, we would have gone forth with you,’ destroying themselves [through false oaths], and Allah knows that indeed they are liars.”
— Qur’an 9:42 (Sahih International) [6]
The hypocrites excuse themselves from the expedition by claiming they were unable, when in truth they did not want the hardship.
This is exactly the Kural’s pretended inability over a task that could be done.
Judaism
“Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it. Say not unto thy neighbour, Go, and come again, and to morrow I will give; when thou hast it by thee.”
— Proverbs 3:27-28 (Hebrew Bible, KJV rendering) [5]
Proverbs forbids putting off a neighbour with excuses when one is perfectly able to help now.
The Kural describes the friend who does exactly this; Proverbs names it as a fault.
Buddhism
“(iv) when opportunity for service has arisen, he expresses his inability.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha lists this as a mark of the ‘lip-service’ friend, a foe in the guise of a friend.
This is the Kural’s udatrupavar almost word for word: the friend who pleads inability when the chance to help arrives.
Jainism
“Though they adhere to one in friendship as closely as the small toes of a dog to one another, yet of what benefit is the love of those who do not help one even to the extent of the leg of a fly?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 8 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet asks what use there is in the love of friends who cling closely yet will not help even in the smallest way.
The Kural tells us to let friendship quietly slacken with those who spoil a task they could easily do, and the Naladiyar likewise questions the worth of close friends who will not lift a finger to help.
Sikhism
“If a fool does a job, he cannot do it right. Even if he does something right, he does the next thing wrong.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad observes that a foolish person spoils whatever task he takes up, so his help cannot be relied on.
The Kural speaks of those who ruin a task that could easily be done and advises letting their friendship slacken quietly; Guru Angad describes the same unreliable helper who spoils what he is given to do.
Greek Mythology
When the Greek leaders came to recruit Odysseus for the war against Troy, he pretended to be mad, ploughing a field with an ill-matched team; Palamedes exposed him by placing the infant Telemachus in the plough’s path.
— Apollodorus, Epitome 3.7 [10]
Odysseus feigned incapacity to escape a duty he was fully able to perform.
It is the Kural’s pretended inability in mythic form.
Philosophy
Cicero has Laelius endorse Cato’s advice that friendships which have gone wrong should be unstitched gradually rather than torn apart, unless some intolerable wrong makes an immediate break unavoidable.
— Cicero, De Amicitia (On Friendship) 76 [11]
Cicero prefers a gradual, quiet loosening to a public rupture that might turn a friend into an enemy.
This matches the Kural’s sollaadaar sora vidal, let it slacken without speaking of it, very closely.

Real Life Example

Bernadotte's corps takes part in neither Jena nor Auerstedt
14 October 1806 · Jena and Auerstedt, Thuringia, Germany
On the day Napoleon defeated the Prussians at Jena, Marshal Davout's single corps fought and beat the main Prussian army at nearby Auerstedt. Marshal Bernadotte's I Corps, positioned between the two battlefields, took part in neither battle, despite requests from Davout for support. Napoleon blamed Bernadotte for failing to help, but did not bring him to a court-martial; historians still debate whether Bernadotte disobeyed or was following ambiguous orders.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A capable marshal's corps stood idle while a colleague fought against heavy odds, and Napoleon's trust in him was never the same, though he made no public break.
The kural advises letting friendship quietly slacken, without announcement, with those who fail to do what they could easily have done, and Napoleon set aside formal punishment while his confidence in Bernadotte waned.
