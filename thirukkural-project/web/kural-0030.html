Kural 30

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

அந்தணர் என்போர் அறவோர்மற் றெவ்வுயிர்க்கும் செந்தண்மை பூண்டொழுக லான்.
Andhanar enbor aravormatru evvuyirkkum, Sendhanmai poondozhuga laan.

Meaning

The people called anthanar (holy, wise people) are the truly good ones, because they treat every living thing with a kindness that never stops.

Elementary

People sometimes think you are important because of the family you were born into. This kural says no: the title of the highest kind of person belongs to whoever treats every living creature — people, animals, everything — with real kindness, all the time.

University

The chapter's last couplet is also its most socially radical. “Anthanar,” the honorific ordinarily applied to Brahmins, is here redefined by conduct alone: anthanar are “aravor,” the virtuous, and the reason given is grammatical rather than incidental — the whole second line is a causal clause ending in “ozhuga-laan,” because they conduct themselves so. Traditional Tamil etymology reads anthanar as am (beautiful, gracious) + thanmai (coolness), and Valluvar exploits this by giving them “sen-thanmai,” upright or true coolness, a quality of unheated, unfailing graciousness extended to “ev-uyirkkum,” every living thing without exception — a phrase that takes the claim past human society and into the treatment of animals. Nothing about birth, ritual, thread or learning appears. The verse anchors the ahimsa ethic that Valluvar develops at length in the later chapters on compassion (Arul-udaimai) and abstaining from meat (Pulaal maruththal), and it has been a central text in modern Tamil arguments against caste, including in the work of the twentieth-century reform movements.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The humble sages, by virtue of true knowledge, see with equal vision a learned and gentle brāhmaṇa, a cow, an elephant, a dog and a dog-eater [outcaste].”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.18 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [4]
Krishna's list is constructed to cross every boundary at once — caste, species, and ritual purity — and to say that real knowledge dissolves the distinction between them.
Both verses redefine the brahmin by a levelling regard for all living beings rather than by birth or rank.
Christianity
Francis of Assisi, having given away his property, is remembered in the early lives for preaching to a flock of birds by the roadside, addressing them as his sisters and urging them to praise their creator, and for treating wolf, lamb and leper alike as creatures owed courtesy.
— Bonaventure, Legenda Major XII.3 (the sermon to the birds); cf. the Canticle of the Creatures [5]
The Franciscan tradition made the extension of charity beyond humanity to animals and the whole created order the signature of sanctity rather than an eccentric footnote to it.
It gives the Christian form of the Kural's test: the mark of the genuinely holy is a gracious bearing toward every creature, not a status.
Islam
“So, he went down the well again and filled his shoe with water and watered it. Allah thanked him for that deed and forgave him. The people said, ‘O Allah's Messenger! Is there a reward for us in serving the animals?’ He replied: ‘Yes, there is a reward for serving any animate (living being).’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2466 (Book of Oppressions) [6]
A man's sins are forgiven for the single act of climbing back into a well to bring water to a thirsty dog, and the Prophet generalises the principle to every creature with a moist liver.
The same moral test as the Kural: standing before God turns on gracious conduct toward every living thing, including the least regarded.
Judaism
“A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast: but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel.”
— Proverbs 12:10 (Tanakh, KJV) [7]
Proverbs uses treatment of animals as the diagnostic for righteousness, a verse the rabbis took as a scriptural anchor for the principle of tza'ar ba'alei chayim, the prohibition on causing animals distress.
Like the Kural, it identifies the truly virtuous person by an attentiveness that reaches past human beings to other living creatures.
Buddhism
“Not by matted hair, nor by lineage, nor by birth does one become a holy man. But he in whom truth and righteousness exist — he is pure, he is a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 393, Brahmanavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's closing chapter systematically strips the word brahmana of every inherited and ritual meaning and reassigns it on the basis of conduct alone.
This is the Kural's operation performed on the same word: anthanar / brahmana belongs to whoever lives rightly, not to whoever was born right.
Jainism
“He who thoroughly knows living beings, whether they move or not, and does not injure them in any of the three ways, him we call a Brahmana.” … “By one's actions one becomes a Brahmana, or a Kshattriya, or a Vaisya, or a Sudra.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.19-33, esp. 25.23 and 25.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The monk Jayaghosha tells a sacrificing priest that a true Brahmin is defined not by birth or ritual but by conduct, above all by not harming any living being, moving or unmoving, in thought, word or deed.
This mirrors the Kural's redefinition of 'anthanar' by unbroken kindness toward every living thing. The Jain text is even more explicit that the title belongs to non-injury, which reaches to beings that do not move, rather than to birth.
Sikhism
“Make compassion the cotton, contentment the thread, modesty the knot and truth the twist. This is the sacred thread of the soul; if you have it, then go ahead and put it on me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 471, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, the boy Nanak refused the Brahmin's sacred thread (janeu). He asked instead for a thread spun from compassion and truth, which cannot break, get dirty or burn.
Like the Kural's redefinition of anthanar, Guru Nanak redefines the mark of the twice-born as compassion lived out, not birth or ritual.
Greek Mythology
In the last book of the Metamorphoses, Ovid gives Pythagoras a long speech urging humanity to stop slaughtering animals for food, arguing that souls pass between all bodies and that the ox which has worked the fields should not be repaid with the knife.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses XV.60-478 (the speech of Pythagoras) [11]
The speech makes kinship with all animate life the ground of a specific ethical discipline, and presents it as the teaching of a sage set apart from ordinary appetite.
It is the nearest Greek-mythological articulation of the Kural's standard — the truly holy are known by a compassion that covers every living thing.
Philosophy
In On Abstinence from Animal Food, Porphyry argues against the Stoic position that justice applies only between rational beings, holding instead that animals perceive, suffer and reason after their fashion, and that the philosopher's justice must therefore extend to every ensouled creature.
— Porphyry, De Abstinentia III.18-26 [12]
Porphyry makes the scope of a person's justice the measure of their philosophical attainment, so that abstaining from harm to animals is not sentimentality but the completion of the ascetic life.
Same conclusion as the Kural: the person who deserves the highest title is the one whose gracious conduct excludes no living thing.

Real Life Example

Albert Schweitzer and Reverence for Life
1915 (idea); 1952 (Nobel Peace Prize) · Ogooue River and Lambarene, Gabon
Albert Schweitzer, a theologian, musician and doctor, went in 1913 to Lambarene on the Ogooue River in what is now Gabon, where he and his wife Helene built a hospital. In 1915, while travelling on the river, the phrase Reverence for Life came to him, and it became the centre of his ethics, extending moral concern to every living thing. He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1952, cited for his altruism, reverence for life and tireless humanitarian work.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Schweitzer turned a principle of compassion toward all life into decades of practical service, and the world honoured him for it.
The kural says the truly virtuous are those who wear unbroken compassion toward every living being, and Schweitzer's Reverence for Life is almost a restatement of that standard in modern form.
