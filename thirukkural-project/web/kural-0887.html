Kural 887

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

செப்பின் புணர்ச்சிபோல் கூடினும் கூடாதே உட்பகை உற்ற குடி.
Seppin punarchchipol kootinum kootaadhe utpakai utra kuti.

Meaning

A family with hidden hostility inside may look joined together like a box and its lid, but it is never truly united.

Elementary

A box and its lid fit together neatly, but they are still two separate pieces that can be pulled apart at any moment. A family that is secretly angry inside may look together at dinner, but deep down they are not really united.

University

The governing image is seppin punarchchi (செப்பின் புணர்ச்சி, 'the joining of a seppu'), a small casket whose lid sits flush on its body so that the two look like one object. A kudi (குடி, family, clan or community) that has suffered utpakai may koodinum (கூடினும், even though it comes together) yet kootaadhu (கூடாதே, will surely not unite). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read seppu as a lidded casket, and Pope translates 'casket with its cover'. Sivayogi Sivakumar instead reads seppu as copper used to join two different metals, an interpretive variant. Pappaiah extends kudi to the party and the state. The text is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The asura brothers Sunda and Upasunda, inseparable in everything and sharing one kingdom, fall into rivalry over the celestial maiden Tilottama and kill each other.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva (Sunda-Upasunda episode, trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Their seeming oneness hid a fault line that a single provocation split open.
Like the casket and lid, their union looked complete but did not hold once rivalry entered.
Christianity
“Burning lips and a wicked heart are like a potsherd covered with silver dross.”
— Proverbs 26:23 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs compares warm words over a hostile heart to cheap clay given a shiny coating.
Both texts use a crafted-object image for outward togetherness covering inner hostility.
Islam
“Their violence among themselves is severe. You think they are together, but their hearts are diverse.”
— Qur'an 59:14 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes a group that looks united from outside but is divided within.
This is almost exactly the Kural's casket-and-lid image of apparent but unreal union.
Judaism
“And when his brethren saw that their father loved him more than all his brethren, they hated him, and could not speak peaceably unto him.”
— Genesis 37:4 (KJV) [4]
Joseph's brothers live under one roof as one family while hatred divides them.
Like the Kural's family, they are together in form but not in heart.
Buddhism
At Kosambi, monks of a single community fell into a bitter dispute; they went on living in one monastery but refused to reconcile even when the Buddha himself urged them, and he finally withdrew to the forest alone.
— Majjhima Nikaya 128, Upakkilesa Sutta (opening); Vinaya, Mahavagga X [6]
The community remained outwardly one Sangha while being split within.
This matches the Kural's picture of a group joined in form but not in spirit.
Jainism
“He started as a pupil and ended as a frustrated rival of Mahavira.”
— T.U. Mehta, The Path of Arhat, 'Association with Gosala' (jainworld.com) [7]
Gośāla lived and wandered with Mahāvīra for six years as a self-declared disciple, then broke away, founded the Ājīvika order, and finally attacked Mahāvīra and two of his monks with his fiery power (tejolesyā).
The Kural's casket and lid look like one object without being one, and Gośāla's six years at Mahāvīra's side, outwardly together but never one in mind, ending in open attack, illustrate that false union.
Sikhism
“Those who have one thing in their heart, and something else in their mouth, are judged to be false.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, condemns those whose words and inner feelings do not match.
Like the casket whose lid sits flush yet remains a separate piece, people whose hearts and mouths differ may seem united while being divided inside, as the Kural says.
Greek Mythology
Atreus, pretending to be reconciled with his brother Thyestes, invites him to a feast of welcome and secretly serves him the flesh of his own sons.
— Apollodorus, Epitome 2.13 (trans. J. G. Frazer) [9]
A show of family reunion conceals the most savage enmity in Greek myth.
It is the Kural's casket and lid in tragic form: apparent union, total inner hostility.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that friendships based on utility or pleasure dissolve easily once the advantage ends, because the partners never loved each other for who they were.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Such friendships look like friendship but lack its inner substance.
This loosely matches the Kural's union that looks complete but is not real.

Real Life Example

The United Arab Republic of Egypt and Syria breaks apart
1958-1961 · Cairo, Egypt, and Damascus, Syria
On 1 February 1958 Egypt and Syria proclaimed their union as the United Arab Republic under Gamal Abdel Nasser, and the union was approved by plebiscites later that month. Egyptian dominance of the union antagonized many Syrians, and on 28 September 1961, after a military coup in Damascus, Syria declared itself independent again. Egypt kept the name United Arab Republic until 1971.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A union that looked complete on paper never became one in spirit and came apart within three and a half years.
The kural says a group divided by hidden enmity may look joined like a casket and its lid yet never truly unite, which describes the brief union of Egypt and Syria.
