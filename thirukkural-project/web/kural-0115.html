Kural 115

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

கேடும் பெருக்கமும் இல்லல்ல நெஞ்சத்துக் கோடாமை சான்றோர்க் கணி.
Kedum perukkamum illalla nenjaththuk Kodaamai saandrork kani.

Meaning

Loss and gain come in every life. The jewel of good people is a mind that stays fair in both.

Elementary

Some days you win, some days you lose; that happens to everyone. A good person plays fair on both kinds of days, and that fairness is their best jewel.

University

The first line states a plain fact: கேடும் பெருக்கமும் இல்லல்ல (“ruin and increase are not things that do not exist”), that is, they are inevitable. Parimelazhagar attributes them to past deeds (ūḻ), which is reflected in Pope’s “not mere accident” and Solomon Pappaiah’s “already ordained.” Given that fortune is outside our control, the only thing that distinguishes the சான்றோர் (saandror, “the accomplished/noble”) is நெஞ்சத்துக் கோடாமை, “not bending in the heart.” The verb கோடு (“to bend, to be crooked”) is the key image of the chapter and returns in Kurals 118 and 119. The ethic is close to Stoic doctrine, which separates externals from the governing faculty, and to the Gita’s samatva (evenness in gain and loss). The refrain கோடாமை சான்றோர்க்கு அணி is repeated word for word in Kural 118.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Do thou fight for the sake of fighting, without considering happiness or distress, loss or gain, victory or defeat – and by so doing you shall never incur sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.38 [4]
Krishna tells Arjuna to treat gain and loss, victory and defeat as equal and keep doing his duty.
Matches the Kural’s lābha-alābha (gain and loss) theme: stay steady in right action whatever fortune brings.
Christianity
“I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound”
— Philippians 4:11–12 (KJV) [5]
Paul writes from prison that he has learned to stay the same person in poverty and plenty.
Both portray inner steadiness through ups and downs as the mark of a mature person.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you.”
— Qur’an 57:23 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an explains that knowing all events are decreed should keep believers from both despair at loss and arrogance at gain.
Like the Kural (as Parimelazhagar reads it), it accepts loss and gain as ordained and asks for an unshaken heart in both.
Judaism
“till I die I will not remove mine integrity from me. My righteousness I hold fast, and will not let it go: my heart shall not reproach me so long as I live.”
— Job 27:5–6 (KJV) [5]
Job, having lost wealth, children and health, refuses to give up his integrity.
Job is the model of a heart that does not bend when ruin comes, the Kural’s kōdāmai.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada compares the wise to a rock that storms cannot move. The next verse adds that they show no elation or depression in happiness or sorrow.
The same picture of the noble person’s steady heart amid the “worldly winds” of gain and loss.
Jainism
“He was indifferent to success or failure (in begging), to happiness and misery, to life and death, to blame and praise, to honour and insult.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 19.90 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mṛgāputra, once a monk, holds the same even mind whether things go well or badly.
Valluvar grants that loss and gain will come and calls the unbent heart the ornament of the noble, and this verse shows the Jain ideal of a mind that stays level through both.
Sikhism
“That man, who in the midst of pain, does not feel pain, who is not affected by pleasure, affection or fear, and who looks alike upon gold and dust;”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 633, Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the person who stays even and unshaken through loss and gain, pain and pleasure.
The Kural says loss and gain will surely come and the noble person's ornament is a heart that does not bend in either; Guru Tegh Bahadur paints the same steadiness.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus, back in his own house disguised as a beggar and seeing the suitors’ outrages, restrains his rising heart by reminding it that it has endured worse before, and keeps to his course rather than acting rashly.
— Homer, Odyssey 20 (opening scene) [10]
Odysseus is the Greek model of a man who stays steady through twenty years of loss and reversal.
Shares the ideal of a heart that does not swerve under changing fortune, though Odysseus’ steadiness is about endurance, not fairness.
Philosophy
“Don’t demand that things happen as you wish, but wish that they happen as they do happen, and you will go on well.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 8 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus teaches that outcomes are not in our control, and that serenity comes from accepting them while governing our own choices.
The Kural’s structure is the same: fortune is not ours to command; the uprightness of the heart is.

Real Life Example

Justice H. R. Khanna's dissent in ADM Jabalpur
28 April 1976; 28 January 1977 · Supreme Court of India, New Delhi, India
During the national Emergency of 1975-77, the Supreme Court of India ruled 4 to 1 in ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla that detained citizens could not approach the courts to enforce their right to life and liberty while the Emergency lasted. Justice H. R. Khanna, next in line by seniority to become Chief Justice, was the lone dissenter, holding that life and liberty could not be at the mercy of the executive; he later wrote that before the judgment he had told his sister it would cost him the Chief Justiceship. In January 1977 a junior judge, M. H. Beg, was appointed Chief Justice instead, and Khanna resigned the same day; in 2017 a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court overruled the majority judgment.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Khanna knowingly gave up the highest judicial office in India rather than bend his reading of the law to the pressure of the moment.
The kural says loss and gain will come, and the ornament of the noble is a heart that does not bend in either; Khanna accepted the loss and did not bend.
