Kural 177

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

வேண்டற்க வெஃகியாம் ஆக்கம் விளைவயின் மாண்டற் கரிதாம் பயன்.
Vendarka vehkiyaam aakkam vilaivayin Maandar karithaam payan.

Meaning

Do not wish for riches that come from wanting what belongs to others. When those riches finally bear fruit, the result is hardly ever good.

Elementary

It’s like planting a seed you stole from your neighbour’s garden. It might grow, but the fruit tastes bitter because you know how you got it. This kural says wealth you got by grabbing others’ things never gives you a truly good harvest.

University

The kural uses an agricultural metaphor: வெஃகியாம் ஆக்கம் (vehkiyaam aakkam, ‘increase that comes through coveting’) is a crop, and விளைவயின் (vilaivayin, ‘at the time of its ripening’) its fruit is மாண்டற்கு அரிது (maandarku arithu, ‘hard to be excellent’). The argument concerns delayed consequences: ill-gotten gain may look like prosperity at first, but its maturation reveals its true nature. Some commentators (e.g., Solomon Pappaiah) read maandal as ‘goodness’ in enjoyment, while others read it as ‘glory’; either way the gain proves hollow. The theme of sweet-at-first, bitter-at-the-end fruit links this kural to the Gita’s account of rajasic happiness and to Proverbs’ ‘bread of deceit.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.38 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna describes pleasures of passion (rajas) as sweet at first but poisonous once they have run their course.
The Kural says the same of covetous gain: it looks good at first, but its ripened fruit is bad.
Christianity
“But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided? So is he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.”
— Luke 12:20-21 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus’ parable, a rich man plans bigger barns for his harvest, but he dies that night and never enjoys any of it.
Like the Kural, it warns that gain pursued through greed does not bear good fruit when the time comes.
Islam
“O Hakim! This property is like a sweet fresh fruit; whoever takes it without greediness, he is blessed in it, and whoever takes it with greediness, he is not blessed in it, and he is like a person who eats but is never satisfied.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1472 [5]
The Prophet tells Hakim ibn Hizam that wealth taken with greed carries no blessing and never satisfies.
Almost the same image as the Kural: wealth is a fruit, and when greed is how you get it, the fruit is not good.
Judaism
“Bread of deceit is sweet to a man; but afterwards his mouth shall be filled with gravel.”
— Proverbs 20:17 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says that what you gain dishonestly tastes sweet at first but later turns to gravel in your mouth.
The same arc as the Kural’s ripened fruit: sweet at first, bad when it matures.
Buddhism
“There is no satisfying sensual desires, even with the rain of gold coins. For sensual pleasures give little satisfaction and much pain.”
— Dhammapada 186-187 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says even a rain of gold could not satisfy desire, and what it brings is more pain than pleasure.
Like the Kural, it says gain chased by craving yields little good and much harm.
Jainism
“Pleasures are the thorn that rankles, pleasures are poison, pleasures are like a venomous snake; he who is desirous of pleasures will not get them, and will come to a bad end at last.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.53 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
King Nami tells Indra, who has urged him to multiply his gold and treasury, that grasping after objects of desire ends badly however good it looks at first.
Valluvar says gain born of coveting will rarely ripen into good fruit, and Nami likewise says the one who covets pleasures comes to a bad end at last.
Sikhism
“For the sake of this wealth, so many were ruined; because of this wealth, so many have been disgraced.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a hymn on the devastation of Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak reflects that wealth gathered and fought over has brought ruin and disgrace on many.
The Kural warns that prosperity won by coveting rarely bears good fruit when it ripens; Guru Nanak shows that harvest of ruin and disgrace.
Greek Mythology
King Midas asked Dionysus that everything he touched should turn to gold. His wish was granted, but his bread and wine turned to metal as he tried to eat, and he begged the god to take the gift back.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11 [9]
Midas got exactly the riches he craved, and they turned out to be useless, even deadly.
The Kural’s point in myth: greedy gain, once it ‘ripens’, gives no good fruit.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes those who take gain from shameful sources, such as petty extortion or exploiting others, as displaying ‘sordid love of gain’ (aischrokerdeia): they endure reproach for the sake of gain, and small gain at that.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (1121b-1122a) [10]
Aristotle says that people who profit dishonourably earn disgrace, not honour, and the profit is small anyway.
Matches the Kural’s maandarku arithu: such gain rarely yields anything excellent or honourable.

Real Life Example

Cicero prosecutes Verres for plundering Sicily
70 BCE · Rome, Italy, and Sicily
Gaius Verres, Roman governor of Sicily from 73 to 71 BCE, exploited the province ruthlessly, seizing money and art from its people. The Sicilians asked Cicero to prosecute him, and in 70 BCE Cicero gathered evidence across the island and opened the case with such force that Verres' defender, Hortensius, abandoned the defence. Before the hearing of evidence was over, Verres left Rome for exile in Massilia (Marseille), was condemned in his absence, and spent the rest of his life in exile.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
The fortune Verres extracted from Sicily brought him not enjoyment but disgrace and a lifetime of exile, and Cicero's speeches made his name a byword for corruption.
The kural warns that wealth gained by coveting seldom bears good fruit when it ripens, and Verres' plunder ripened into ruin and exile.
