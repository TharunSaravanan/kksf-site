Kural 228

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

ஈத்துவக்கும் இன்பம் அறியார்கொல் தாமுடைமை வைத்திழக்கும் வன்க ணவர்.
Eeththuvakkum inbam ariyaarkol thaamudaimai Vaiththizhakkum vankanavar.

Meaning

People with hard hearts keep their things to themselves, only to lose them in the end. Do they not know the joy of giving and of being glad about it?

Elementary

Imagine a kid who hides all his toys in a box so nobody else can play with them. The toys get old, break, or get lost, and he never had fun sharing them. This kural asks: doesn’t he know how happy it feels to give?

University

The couplet is a rhetorical question (அறியார்கொல், ‘do they not know?’) directed at the வன்கணவர், literally ‘the hard-eyed’, a Tamil idiom for the pitiless and miserly. Its irony lies in the verb pair வைத்து இழக்கும், ‘keep and lose’: the miser keeps wealth in order not to lose it, yet loses it anyway, through death, theft or decay, having missed the one pleasure it could have given. ஈத்து உவக்கும் இன்பம், ‘the joy of giving and delighting’, is presented as a real and distinct pleasure available only to the giver. The argument is almost psychological rather than moral: miserliness is not only wrong but self-defeating.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Isha Upanishad opens by teaching that everything in the world is pervaded by the Lord; one should enjoy it through renunciation and not covet anyone’s wealth, since wealth truly belongs to no one.
— Isha Upanishad, verse 1 [3]
The Upanishad teaches that clinging to possessions misunderstands their nature, since nothing we hold is ultimately ours.
Like the Kural, it exposes the futility of hoarding what cannot be kept; joy comes from letting go.
Christianity
“But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided? So is he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.”
— Luke 12:20-21 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the Rich Fool, a man hoards his harvest for himself and dies that very night, losing everything.
Exactly the Kural’s ‘keep and lose’: the hoarder stores up wealth only to lose it.
Islam
“And let not those who [greedily] withhold what Allāh has given them of His bounty ever think that it is better for them. Rather, it is worse for them. Their necks will be encircled by what they withheld on the Day of Resurrection.”
— Qur'an 3:180 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns misers that their hoarded wealth will become a burden around their necks.
Like the Kural, it says hoarding does not benefit the hoarder, even though he thinks it does.
Judaism
“There is a sore evil which I have seen under the sun, namely, riches kept for the owners thereof to their hurt. But those riches perish by evil travail: and he begetteth a son, and there is nothing in his hand.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Ecclesiastes laments wealth that is hoarded, then lost, leaving nothing behind.
Close parallel to the Kural’s ‘hoard and lose’ (வைத்திழக்கும்).
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada contrasts misers with the wise person who rejoices in giving.
Same contrast as the Kural: the miser misses the joy that the giver knows.
Jainism
“They who, vexing their own bodies by stinting them in food and raiment, perform not acts of that goodly charity which never faileth, but avariciously hoard up what they have gathered together, will lose it all. O Lord of the mountain land which toucheth the sky! the bees which are driven from the honey they have collected bear witness.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 1 ('Unstable Wealth'), st. 10 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaṭiyār compares misers who neither give nor enjoy their wealth to bees driven from the honey they stored. Like the bees, such hoarders lose everything in the end.
Valluvar mocks the hard-hearted who hoard only to lose it all, unaware of the joy of giving, and this stanza makes the same charge with the image of the robbed honeycomb.
Sikhism
“He hoards gold and silver, but this wealth is false and poisonous, nothing more than ashes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 937, Raag Raamkalee, Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Dakhni Oankar Guru Nanak warns that the gold and silver a person piles up is false wealth that turns to ash.
Valluvar mocks the hard-hearted who keep wealth only to lose it, and Guru Nanak says the same of hoarding: what is kept back is worthless in the end.
Greek Mythology
“For the man who gives willingly, even though he gives a great thing, rejoices in his gift and is glad in heart; but whoever gives way to shamelessness and takes something himself, even though it be a small thing, it freezes his heart.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 357-360 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod says willing givers feel gladness while grasping takers feel their hearts go cold.
Like the Kural, it names a real joy that belongs only to the giver.
Philosophy
“The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced.”
— Andrew Carnegie, “Wealth” (The Gospel of Wealth), 1889 [10]
Andrew Carnegie argued that the wealthy should give away their surplus fortunes during their lifetime rather than hold on to them until death.
Echoes the Kural’s charge that the hoarder only keeps his wealth to lose it at death, missing the chance to give.

Real Life Example

Alexander empties the Persian treasury at Persepolis
330 BCE · Persepolis, Persia (now Iran)
After defeating Darius III, Alexander the Great seized the vast Achaemenid treasuries, finding enormous reserves at Susa and then at Persepolis, where ancient writers say the stores of gold and silver had accumulated from the revenues of the Persian kings since Cyrus. Alexander carried the treasure away and used the wealth to pay and sustain his army.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Riches stored up by generations of kings passed in a single campaign to a conqueror, without ever having served the people from whom they were gathered.
The kural asks whether hoarders know that they keep their wealth only to lose it, and the Persian royal hoard was kept for centuries only to be lost at a stroke.
