Kural 678

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

வினையான் வினையாக்கிக் கோடல் நனைகவுள் யானையால் யானையாத் தற்று.
Vinaiyaan vinaiyaakkik kodal nanaikavul Yaanaiyaal yaanaiyaath thatru.

Meaning

Using one task to get another one done is like using a trained elephant to catch a wild one.

Elementary

When you go to the shop to buy milk and also post a letter on the way, one trip does two jobs. Long ago, people caught wild elephants by using a tame elephant to lead them in. Smart people let one job help with the next.

University

Nanaikavul, literally 'wet cheek', refers to an elephant in musth, whose temples stream with fluid; such tame elephants were used as decoys to lure and capture wild ones, a practice well attested in Tamil and Sanskrit sources on elephant-keeping. Vinaiyaan vinai aakkik kodal means 'to bring about a (further) task by means of a task'. Parimelazhagar specifies that the second undertaking should be of the same kind as the first, just as elephant catches elephant, while Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it as completing one task while doing another. The older sandhi text reads கோட னனைகவுள் / யானையால் யானையாத் தற்று. The elephant simile gives a vivid image of turning one achievement into the means of the next.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Rama helps Sugriva win back his throne from Vali, and Sugriva in return sends the monkey armies across the world to find Sita and later to build the bridge to Lanka.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda [3]
By completing one undertaking (Sugriva's restoration), Rama gains the means for his larger one (the rescue of Sita).
This is vinaiyaan vinai aakkik kodal on an epic scale: one completed task becomes the tool for the next.
Christianity
In the parable of the unjust steward, a manager about to lose his post uses his remaining authority over his master's accounts to win friends who will take him in later, and Jesus tells his hearers to use worldly wealth to make friends for the future.
— Luke 16:9 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the shrewd steward, Jesus praises using present resources to secure a future end.
The steward uses one piece of business to accomplish another, as the Kural's elephant is used to catch its fellow.
Islam
Joseph, now in charge of Egypt's grain stores, uses the distribution of grain to his visiting brothers to bring about their return with his brother Benjamin, and so to reunite his family.
— Quran 12:58-70 [5]
Surah Yusuf shows Joseph turning an administrative duty into the means of a deeper purpose.
Joseph uses one task (supplying grain) to achieve another (restoring his family), which is the Kural's method.
Judaism
“From and within the forest comes the ax to it,”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 39b (William Davidson trans., Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud cites this popular saying to explain why Obadiah, said to be an Edomite convert, was chosen to prophesy against Edom: the axe handle that fells the tree is cut from the forest itself.
Like the elephant used to catch an elephant, the forest supplies the tool for its own felling: like is taken by means of like.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that an unskilful thought can be driven out by focusing on a skilful one, as a skilled carpenter knocks out a coarse peg with a fine one.
— Majjhima Nikaya 20, Vitakkasanthana Sutta [7]
In the Vitakkasanthana Sutta the first method of calming the mind uses one thought to displace another.
The carpenter's peg, like the Kural's elephant, is a tool of the same kind used to accomplish the task.
Jainism
“Just as an impassioned elephant who is inflamed by carnal desires, is turned from his way by a female elephant (and is captured and at last killed in battle).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.89 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the lecture on the causes of carelessness, the elephant lured and captured by a decoy female is an image of how attachment to the senses leads a soul to ruin.
The Jain text knows the same decoy-elephant capture the kural uses, but turns it into a warning about the senses rather than a model of using one undertaking to win another.
Sikhism
“Servant Nanak begs for the dust of the feet of that GurSikh, who himself chants the Naam, and inspires others to chant it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 306, Raag Gauree, Gauri ki Vaar (salok), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das honours the Sikh whose own practice becomes the means of drawing others into the same practice.
The Kural pictures one achievement used to secure another, like a tamed elephant luring a wild one; the Gurbani image is of one who, having made the Name his own, draws others to it.
Greek Mythology
Set to clean the vast Augean stables in a single day, Heracles breaches the walls and diverts the rivers Alpheus and Peneus through them, letting one force of nature do the work.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5.5 [10]
Instead of doing the labour by hand, Heracles turns existing powers into his tool.
Heracles makes one thing (the rivers' flow) accomplish another (the cleansing), as the Kural's hunter uses one elephant to take another.
Philosophy
Francis Bacon writes that nature, to be commanded, must be obeyed: human power over nature comes from using nature's own laws and forces.
— Francis Bacon, Novum Organum I, aphorism 3 [11]
Bacon's programme for science rests on turning natural processes against one another to achieve human ends.
The idea that nature is mastered by nature matches the Kural's elephant-by-elephant image.

Real Life Example

The Great Exhibition's profits build the museums of South Kensington
1851 · London, United Kingdom
The Great Exhibition of 1851, held in the Crystal Palace in Hyde Park, made a surplus of about 186,000 pounds. Prince Albert proposed that the profit be used for educational institutions, and in December 1851 the Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851 began buying land in South Kensington. That estate became home to the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum, the Natural History Museum, Imperial College, the Royal Colleges of Art and Music and the Royal Albert Hall.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One successful undertaking was deliberately used as the engine for another, and the exhibition of a single summer produced institutions that have lasted more than 170 years.
The kural says that using one undertaking to achieve another is like using a tamed elephant to capture a wild one, and the Great Exhibition's surplus was harnessed to capture a far larger prize in the museums and colleges of South Kensington.
