Kural 351

Knowledge of the True · Meyyunarthal · மெய்யுணர்தல்

பொருளல்ல வற்றைப் பொருளென்று உணரும் மருளானாம் மாணாப் பிறப்பு.
Porulalla vatraip porulendru unarum Marulaanaam maanaap pirappu.

Meaning

Thinking that things which are not real are real is a mistake of the mind. From this mistake come unhappy births, again and again.

Elementary

Imagine chasing a shiny puddle on a hot road, sure it’s water, when it’s only a mirage. You’d run and run, get more and more thirsty, and never drink. This kural says that when we treat things that don’t last as if they were the real treasure, we keep chasing mirages again and again, life after life.

University

The chapter opens with a diagnosis rather than a prescription: the root of bondage is marul (delusion, confusion), specifically the cognitive error of taking porul-alladhu (what is not the real) for porul (the real). Commentators such as Parimelazhagar read maanaa-p-pirappu (‘inglorious birth’) as the whole cycle of transmigration, which turns the couplet into an epistemological account of samsara: rebirth is caused less by particular sins than by misperception. This closely matches the Advaita Vedanta analysis of adhyasa (superimposition, the classic rope mistaken for a snake) and the Buddhist teaching that avijja (ignorance) is the first link of dependent origination. Because the Tamil ‘porul’ is richly ambiguous (thing, wealth, meaning, reality), the couplet also carries an ethical undertone: whoever treats wealth and possessions as the ultimate ‘thing’ has mistaken the counterfeit for the coin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“From the unreal lead me to the real! From the darkness lead me to the light! From death lead me to immortality!”
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.3.28 (Pavamana Mantra), trans. Patrick Olivelle [3]
This ancient Upanishadic prayer asks to be led away from what is unreal toward what is truly real. The Upanishad itself explains that the ‘unreal’ and ‘darkness’ mean death, and the ‘real’ and ‘light’ mean immortality.
Valluvar names the same wrong turn (taking the unreal for the real) and the same cost: being caught in death and birth again and again.
Christianity
“While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal.”
— 2 Corinthians 4:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Christians not to fix their eyes on visible things, which pass away, but on unseen realities, which last forever.
Same distinction the Kural draws between passing things wrongly treated as ‘real’ and the lasting reality; the Kural adds rebirth as the consequence of getting it wrong.
Islam
“Know that the life of this world is but amusement and diversion and adornment and boasting to one another and competition in increase of wealth and children … And what is the worldly life except the enjoyment of delusion.”
— Qur’an 57:20 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes worldly life as a passing show, like a plant that flourishes after rain and then dries into debris, and calls its enjoyment ‘delusion’.
The Kural’s ‘marul’ (delusion) and the Qur’an’s ‘enjoyment of delusion’ both describe treating the fleeting world as the real prize.
Judaism
“Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.”
— Ecclesiastes 1:2 (KJV) [4]
The Hebrew word translated ‘vanity’ (hevel) literally means vapor or breath. Ecclesiastes says that what people chase as solid and lasting is really as thin as breath.
Like the Kural, Ecclesiastes warns that what looks substantial is not the real thing; unlike the Kural, it does not tie this mistake to rebirth.
Buddhism
“Those who mistake the unessential to be essential and the essential to be unessential, dwelling in wrong thoughts, never arrive at the essential.”
— Dhammapada 11 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that people who confuse the worthless with the valuable, and the valuable with the worthless, are stuck in wrong thinking and never reach what truly matters.
Almost the same sentence as the Kural: mistaking the non-essential for the essential is the error that keeps beings in bondage.
Jainism
“But they do not cross the Flood of Life, who, ignoring the true relation of things, and not versed in the true Law, hold the above heretical opinions.” … “They do not put an end to birth, who, &c.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.1.1.20 and 23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 239) [7]
After surveying views it considers false, the Sūtrakṛtāṅga concludes that anyone who misreads the true nature of things stays in the flood of saṃsāra and cannot end birth. In Jain terms, wrong view (mithyātva) heads the causes of bondage.
The Kural traces inglorious birth to the delusion that takes the unreal for the real. The Jain text likewise ties the failure to end birth to ignorance of 'the true relation of things'.
Sikhism
“False doubts and dream objects - man believes them to be true.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 485, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Namdev (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Namdev says that people take illusions and the objects of a dream for real things, which is the root of their bondage.
The Kural names taking what is not real for the real as the delusion from which lowly birth arises; Namdev names the same error of believing false things to be true.
Greek Mythology
Ixion, invited to dine among the gods, desired Hera. Zeus shaped a cloud (Nephele) into Hera’s likeness; Ixion embraced the phantom as if it were real and was punished by being bound to an ever-turning wheel.
— Pindar, Pythian Ode 2 [9]
Ixion fell for an illusion made to look like the real thing, and his punishment was an endless, spinning wheel.
The Kural’s picture of mistaking a phantom for reality and being trapped in an endless cycle is strikingly mirrored in Ixion’s cloud and wheel.
Philosophy
“To them, I said, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images.”
— Plato, Republic VII, 515c (trans. Jowett) [10]
In Plato’s Allegory of the Cave, prisoners chained since birth see only shadows on a wall and take them for the whole of reality.
Exactly the Kural’s diagnosis: people take what is not real (shadows) for the real, and that mistake defines the whole condition they live in.

Real Life Example

Piltdown Man is exposed as a forgery
1912-1953 · Piltdown, Sussex, and the Natural History Museum, London, England, United Kingdom
In 1912 the amateur geologist Charles Dawson and the palaeontologist Arthur Smith Woodward announced skull and jaw fragments from a gravel pit at Piltdown as an early human ancestor, and many leading scientists accepted it as a missing link between apes and humans. For about forty years the find shaped debate about human origins, and it was one reason why Raymond Dart's genuine 1925 Taung fossil from South Africa was widely doubted. In November 1953 Joseph Weiner, Kenneth Oakley and Wilfrid Le Gros Clark showed that the jaw came from a modern ape, that its teeth had been filed down and that the bones had been stained to look ancient.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A counterfeit taken for the real thing did more than fool individuals: it sent a whole field of research down a false path for decades and caused genuine evidence to be dismissed.
The kural says that mistaking what is not real for the real is the delusion from which an unworthy chain of consequences is born, and the Piltdown forgery, accepted as real, produced forty years of misdirected science.
