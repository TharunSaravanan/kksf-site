Kural 1137

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

கடலன்ன காமம் உழந்தும் மடலேறாப் பெண்ணின் பெருந்தக்க தில்.
Kadalanna kaamam uzhandhum madalaeraap pennin perundhakka thil.

Meaning

There is nothing as great as a woman who suffers a love as big as the sea and still never rides the palm-stem horse.

Elementary

Imagine two friends who both miss someone very much. One shouts about it all over town; the other keeps going quietly and patiently. The poet says the quiet, patient strength the woman shows is one of the greatest things in the world.

University

The couplet turns the chapter: kaṭal aṉṉa kāmam (கடலன்ன காமம்), 'love like the sea', is borne (uḻantum, 'even while suffering') by the woman who nonetheless does not ride the madal (maṭal ēṟā). This echoes the Tolkāppiyam rule that the madal is never proper for women, and the commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah, Kalaignar) read it as praise of the endurance of women; Kesikan sharpens it into a comparison: no man has such worth. The speaker is disputed: some commentators give it to the hero, conceding the heroine's greater restraint, while others read it as the heroine's own words, and many hear the remaining couplets of the chapter in her voice. Perun-takkatu il (பெருந்தக்கது இல்) is 'there is nothing of greater worth'. Pope: 'There's nought of greater worth than woman's long-enduring soul, / Who, vexed by love like ocean waves, climbs not the horse of palm.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Held captive in Ravana's Ashoka grove, Sita endures long separation from Rama, refuses every threat and temptation, and keeps her dignity and faith until Hanuman finds her.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (paraphrase) [3]
The Sundara Kanda makes Sita's quiet endurance in separation the very picture of a noble woman's strength.
Sita's vast grief borne in dignified restraint is the kind of nobility the Kural praises.
Christianity
“Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes true love as patient endurance that does not give way.
The Kural praises exactly this love that endures all things without breaking out in a public display.
Islam
When Jacob hears that Joseph has been lost, he answers that 'beautiful patience' (sabrun jamil) is his course, and he endures his grief with dignity.
— Qur'an 12:18 (paraphrase) [5]
The Qur'an holds up patient, uncomplaining endurance of heartbreak as a mark of nobility.
The Kural's woman shows the same 'beautiful patience' in bearing a sea-wide love without display.
Judaism
“She said to him: If he would listen to me, he would sit and study for another twelve years.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 63a (William Davidson Edition, Sefaria) [6]
Rachel, wife of Rabbi Akiva, endures twelve years of separation and, when mocked as a 'widow of a living man', declares she would bear twelve more for his sake.
Her long, uncomplaining endurance of separation is the kind of nobility the Kural assigns to women.
Buddhism
Buddhist tradition tells that after Prince Siddhartha left home, Yasodhara chose to live as he did, wearing simple robes and eating once a day, enduring her loss with dignity and later becoming a nun.
— Jataka Nidanakatha and later Pali commentarial tradition (paraphrase) [7]
Yasodhara is remembered for bearing abandonment without bitterness or display.
Like the Kural's woman, Yasodhara bore a great love and loss with restraint.
Jainism
“Firm and decided she cut off her tresses which were black like bees and dressed with a brush and comb.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Forsaken by Arishtanemi, Rajimati does not complain or make a display; she calmly takes up the life of a nun.
Like the Kural's woman, Rajimati carries a great love with steadiness instead of public protest, though she turns it toward renunciation.
Sikhism
“Let patience be your purpose in life; implant this within your being. In this way, you will grow into a great river; you will not break off into a tiny stream.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 117) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid praises patience (sabar) firmly held within, which makes a person as deep and broad as a great river instead of a broken trickle.
The Kural honours the woman who carries a love as vast as the sea yet never rides the madal; Farid likewise honours the patient endurance that holds a river-like fullness without breaking out.
Greek Mythology
Penelope waits twenty years for Odysseus, putting off the suitors with the trick of the shroud she weaves by day and unravels by night, weeping in private but never giving way.
— Homer, Odyssey 2 and 19 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer makes her patient, controlled fidelity the model of a noble wife.
Penelope bears a sea of longing without display, as the Kural's woman does.
Philosophy
Aristotle distinguishes endurance (karteria), the ability to hold out against pains and pressures most people give way to, from softness, which yields.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.7 (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, enduring what most would not is a mark of strength of character.
The Kural ranks the woman who endures love's sea without yielding to the madal above all others, the same esteem for endurance.

Real Life Example

Princess Margaret announces she will not marry Peter Townsend
October 1955 · London, United Kingdom
Princess Margaret, sister of Queen Elizabeth II, wished to marry Group Captain Peter Townsend, a former equerry who was divorced, at a time when the Church of England did not accept remarriage after divorce. Townsend was posted abroad as an air attaché, and after they met again in October 1955 she issued a public statement that she had decided not to marry him. In it she cited the Church's teaching that Christian marriage is indissoluble and her duty to the Commonwealth.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A young woman bore a deep and long-standing love but chose restraint over defiance, and made the choice herself in public.
The kural praises the woman who suffers a love as vast as the sea yet never rides the palm-stem horse; Margaret kept her love within the limits she felt bound by rather than breaking them openly.
