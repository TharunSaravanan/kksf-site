Kural 864

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

நீங்கான் வெகுளி நிறையிலன் எஞ்ஞான்றும் யாங்கணும் யார்க்கும் எளிது.
Neengaan vekuli niraiyilan enjnaandrum yaanganum yaarkkum elidhu.

Meaning

A person whose anger never goes away and who cannot control themselves is easy for anyone to beat, at any time and in any place.

Elementary

If a player gets angry every time and can’t keep the team’s plan a secret, the other team just has to tease him and listen in. He makes himself easy to beat. Staying calm and keeping your plans to yourself is a kind of armour.

University

Neengaan vekuli (நீங்கான் வெகுளி) is anger that does not depart, a settled irascibility rather than a passing flare. Nirai (நிறை) is the key term: Varadarasanar glosses it as the capacity to hold and govern one’s own mind, while Parimelazhagar’s tradition, followed by Solomon Pappaiah and the classic English rendering (‘neither refrains from anger nor keeps his secrets’), reads it as guarding secrets. Both senses describe a leak in self-command through which the enemy can enter. The triple universal of the second line, enjnaandrum yaanganum yaarkkum (எஞ்ஞான்றும் யாங்கணும் யார்க்கும், at all times, in all places, to all), makes the vulnerability total. This anticipates chapter 31 (vekulaamai, avoiding anger), here recast as political danger. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna traces a chain of ruin: from anger comes delusion, from delusion confusion of memory, from that the loss of intelligence, and with intelligence lost, a person is destroyed.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.63 [3]
The Gita shows anger as the first link in a collapse of judgement.
Valluvar’s permanently angry man has lost exactly the judgement that would protect him.
Christianity
“He that hath no rule over his own spirit is like a city that is broken down, and without walls.”
— Proverbs 25:28 (KJV) [4]
The proverb compares a person without self-control to an undefended city that anyone can enter.
It is almost the Kural’s image: the man without nirai is open to any attacker.
Islam
“The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength, but the strong is the one who controls himself while in anger.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 [5]
The Prophet redefines strength as mastery of one’s own anger.
By that measure, Valluvar’s ever-angry man is the weakest of all.
Judaism
Ben Zoma asks, ‘Who is mighty?’ and answers: the one who conquers his own impulse.
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 [6]
The Mishnah locates true power in self-mastery rather than in force.
Valluvar’s man without nirai has failed at exactly this kind of might.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada calls the one who checks rising anger as a charioteer checks a speeding chariot a true charioteer; others merely hold the reins.
— Dhammapada 222 [7]
The Buddha praises active control of anger as real mastery.
Valluvar’s man whose anger never leaves has let go of the reins.
Jainism
“He should not give way to his anger, and bear with indifference pleasant and unpleasant occurrences. Subdue your Self, for the Self is difficult to subdue; if your Self is subdued, you will be happy in this world and in the next. Better it is that I should subdue my Self by self-control and penance, than be subdued by others with fetters and corporal punishment.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.14–16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahāvīra's discipline joins the curbing of anger with mastery of the self, and warns that whoever does not subdue himself will be subdued by others.
The Kural says a man of lasting anger and no inner restraint (nirai) can be beaten by anyone, and these verses give the same pairing, adding that the self-mastered person cannot be overpowered from outside.
Sikhism
“O anger, you are the root of conflict; compassion never rises up in you. You take the corrupt, sinful beings in your power, and make them dance like monkeys.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sahaskriti (salok 47), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Salok Sahaskriti Guru Arjan addresses anger directly: it breeds strife and takes control of those it possesses, making them dance like performing monkeys.
The Kural says the man whose anger never leaves him and who lacks self-restraint is easy for anyone to defeat; Guru Arjan likewise shows the angry man as no longer his own master but led about by his passion.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ Ajax, the hero’s rage at losing Achilles’ armour drives him into a delusion in which he slaughters livestock, believing them to be his enemies, and ends in disgrace.
— Sophocles, Ajax [10]
Ajax’s unrelenting anger destroys him more completely than any enemy could.
He is the Kural’s man of unrelenting anger, undone without a battle.
Philosophy
Seneca calls anger a brief madness, an emotion that cannot govern itself, forgets decency and is deaf to reason.
— Seneca, De Ira I.1 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
For Seneca, the angry man has handed control of himself to a passion.
This is why Valluvar says the angry man is easy prey: he is no longer in command.

Real Life Example

Zinedine Zidane is sent off in the World Cup final
9 July 2006 · Berlin, Germany
In extra time of the 2006 FIFA World Cup final between France and Italy, French captain Zinedine Zidane head-butted Italian defender Marco Materazzi in the chest after an exchange of words, and the referee sent him off. The match ended 1-1 and Italy won the penalty shoot-out 5-3, with France's David Trezeguet missing his kick. It was the last match of Zidane's playing career, and FIFA later fined and banned both players.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
One of the greatest players of his era ended his career not in defeat by skill but by a moment of uncontrolled anger, removing France's leader before the decisive shoot-out.
The kural says one whose anger never leaves him and who lacks inner restraint can be overcome by anyone, anywhere, and a single provoked outburst cost Zidane his final match.
