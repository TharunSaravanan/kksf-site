Kural 905

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

இல்லாளை அஞ்சுவான் அஞ்சுமற் றெஞ்ஞான்றும் நல்லார்க்கு நல்ல செயல்.
Illaalai anjuvaan anjumat regngnaandrum nallaarkku nalla seyal.

Meaning

A man who is afraid of his wife will always be afraid even to do good things for good people.

Elementary

If you are scared to share your snack with a hungry classmate because someone might be cross with you, fear is stopping you from being kind. Kindness needs a little courage.

University

Parimelazhagar glosses nallaar (நல்லார், the good) as gods, ascetics, the wise, one's two elders (parents) and worthy guests, and nalla seyal (நல்ல செயல்) as giving them what they need; even when the wealth is his own earning, the fearful husband hesitates. He supports the reading with a parallel from the Jain Tamil epic Civaka Cintamani (மண்மகள் 217): 'illaalai anji virunthin mugam kondra nenjin pullaalan', a man who, fearing his wife, turned away the face of a guest. Manakkudavar sums the verse as meaning such a man cannot practise aram (virtue). Kalaignar inverts the sense, reading that a man anxious not to err in doing good will heed a watchful wife, a strikingly egalitarian modern reinterpretation. The text is identical across the four sources. Critics note that the verse presumes hospitality and charity are the husband's prerogative, a presumption that modern readers can retain as a call to generosity without its gendered frame.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Taittiriya Upanishad's parting instruction to students includes treating mother, father, teacher and guest as divine.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.2 [5]
Honouring and serving guests and elders is part of the householder's sacred duty.
The 'good deeds to the good' that the fearful husband neglects are precisely these duties of hospitality and reverence.
Christianity
Herod, who feared John the Baptist as a just and holy man, has him beheaded after a promise made at a banquet, at the request of Herodias through her daughter, though he was exceedingly sorry.
— Mark 6:17-28 [6]
A ruler who respected a righteous man nonetheless harms him rather than lose face and displease his wife's faction.
This is close to the Kural: fear of displeasing those at home overrides doing right by the good.
Islam
The Qur'an condemns Abu Lahab together with his wife, the 'carrier of firewood', for their joint hostility to the Prophet.
— Qur'an 111 [7]
A husband and wife are portrayed as reinforcing each other in opposing a righteous messenger.
Loosely parallels the Kural's concern that domestic influence can turn a man against the good.
Judaism
When Job's wife urges him to curse God and die, Job answers that she speaks foolishly, and he does not sin with his lips.
— Job 2:9-10 [6]
Job holds to integrity even against the counsel of his closest companion.
Job is the opposite of the Kural's fearful husband: he will not let domestic pressure divert him from righteousness.
Buddhism
In the Vessantara Jataka, Prince Vessantara gives away everything in perfect generosity, and his wife Maddi supports and shares his gifts and exile.
— Vessantara Jataka (Jataka 547) [8]
Husband and wife together embody unstinting generosity.
A counterpoint to the Kural: a spouse can strengthen rather than inhibit good deeds to others.
Jainism
Among Hemachandra's thirty-five qualities of an ideal Jain householder is 'Yathavat atithau sadhau dine cha pratipatti-krt', rendered by Sangave as diligent in helping ascetics, the righteous and the needy. It goes with the lay vow of atithi-samvibhaga, sharing one's food with ascetics.
— Hemachandra, Yogashastra, list of 35 householder qualities, no. 19, as rendered in V.A. Sangave, 'Jaina ethics' (jainworld.com) [9]
In Jainism, a householder is expected to look after ascetics, good people and guests freely and as a regular duty.
Kural 905 condemns the wife-fearing man who hesitates to do good to good people (the commentator lists ascetics and guests), which is exactly the duty Hemachandra's householder must not neglect.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Vaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak describes the true way as honest work (kirat karni) and sharing its fruits with others (vand chhakna).
The Kural regrets a man too afraid of his wife to give from his own earnings to the deserving; for Sikhs, sharing what one honestly earns is a basic duty.
Greek Mythology
The aged couple Baucis and Philemon together welcome disguised gods into their humble home and are rewarded.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 [11]
A husband and wife share in generous hospitality toward strangers.
This shows the alternative to the Kural's scenario: a marriage in which good deeds to the worthy are done jointly.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes liberality as the virtue of giving the right amounts to the right people at the right time, with pleasure rather than pain.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV [12]
A generous person gives readily and gladly to worthy recipients.
The Kural's fearful husband lacks this readiness, giving to the good with fear rather than gladness.

Real Life Example

Emperor Gaozong's senior statesmen are removed after Wu Zetian becomes empress
655-660 · Chang'an, Tang China
In 655 the Tang emperor Gaozong had his empress Wang deposed and appointed Wu Zetian in her place, against the opposition of senior court officials who objected that she did not come from one of the great noble families. Within a few years those officials were dismissed, exiled or put to death, among them the emperor's own uncle Zhangsun Wuji, who had been one of the dynasty's leading statesmen. By 660 the empress had triumphed over all her opponents.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The emperor did not protect the experienced counsellors who had served his family, once they stood in the way of his wife.
The kural says a man who fears his wife will be afraid even to do good to good people, and Gaozong's failure to shield his loyal elder statesmen illustrates that loss of nerve.
