Kural 1326

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

உணலினும் உண்டது அறல்இனிது காமம் புணர்தலின் ஊடல் இனிது.
Unalinum undadhu aral-inidhu kaamam Punardhalin oodal inidhu.

Meaning

Digesting food is sweeter than eating it. In the same way, in love, sulking is sweeter than being together.

Elementary

After a big lunch, the cosy, full feeling afterwards can feel even nicer than the eating. The poet says love is similar: the little pause of a pretend quarrel can be sweeter than simply being together.

University

A striking analogy from the body: uṇaliṉum (‘more than eating’) uṇṭatu aṟal (‘the digesting of what has been eaten’, aṟal from aṟu, to be consumed or settle) is iṉitu (‘sweet, pleasant’); likewise in kāmam (love), ūṭal is sweeter than puṇartal (union). Commentators read the parallel as saying that, just as digestion readies one for the next meal, ūṭal renews the appetite of love; Puliyur Kesikan speaks of the pleasure gained ‘by sulking’ exceeding that gained ‘by uniting’. Kalaignar and Pope keep the comparison directly. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; ytamizh hyphenates aṟal-iṉitu in its transliteration.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Natyashastra divides the erotic sentiment (śṛṅgāra rasa) into two kinds, love in union (sambhoga) and love in separation (vipralambha), and treats both as full forms of the same sentiment.
— Natyashastra, ch. 6 (tr. Manomohan Ghosh; paraphrase) [3]
Indian poetics gives the longing of a quarrel or separation a full place in the aesthetics of love, not merely a lack of union.
The Kural goes one step further and ranks the separation of a sulk above union itself.
Christianity
“Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby.”
— Hebrews 12:11 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews says that what is unpleasant at the time brings sweetness afterwards.
Like the Kural’s digestion after a meal, the sweetness comes after, not during, the experience.
Islam
Rumi’s Masnavi opens with the reed flute lamenting that it was cut from its reed-bed, and saying that whoever is parted from his source longs to return to the time of union.
— Rumi, Masnavi I.1-4 (tr. R. A. Nicholson; paraphrase) [5]
For Rumi, separation is what gives love its voice and its sweetness of longing.
The Kural similarly finds in a short separation a sweetness that union alone does not give.
Judaism
“The full soul loatheth a honeycomb; But to the hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet.”
— Proverbs 27:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that sweetness depends on appetite: the full refuse honey, while the hungry relish even bitter food.
The Kural uses the same food logic: a pause in love restores the appetite that makes love sweet.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada warns that a person who is sluggish and gluttonous, sleeping and rolling about like a hog fed on grain, keeps returning to birth again and again.
— Dhammapada 325 (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist teaching counsels moderation in eating and in all the pleasures of the senses.
The Kural uses eating and digesting to praise the rhythm of love’s pleasures; the Buddhist verse uses food to warn against indulgence.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain text says a pleasure that is sweet in the eating turns to poison in the digesting.
The Kural and the Jain verse use the same eating-and-digestion contrast, but the Kural finds the digesting sweet while the Jain sutra finds it deadly.
Sikhism
“Your actions seem so sweet to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 394, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan accepts whatever the Lord does, near or distant, as sweet, because it comes from the Beloved.
The Kural finds the sulk sweeter than union itself, as digesting is sweeter than eating; Gurbani similarly finds sweetness even in the Beloved's withholding, though it makes no such ranking.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls Eros the limb-loosener and a ‘sweetbitter’ (glukupikron) creature that cannot be resisted.
— Sappho, fragment 130 (Lobel-Page; paraphrase) [10]
Greek love poetry names the mixture of sweetness and pain at the heart of desire.
The Kural’s sulk is just such a bittersweet ingredient, and it says this makes love sweeter than union alone.
Philosophy
Epicurus wrote that although every pleasure is good, not every pleasure is to be chosen, and that we often prefer pains when enduring them for a while brings a greater pleasure afterwards.
— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus 129 (tr. R. D. Hicks; paraphrase) [11]
Epicurus calculates pleasures over time rather than moment by moment.
The Kural makes the same calculation: the brief pain of a sulk is worth it for the greater sweetness that follows.

Real Life Example

Much Ado About Nothing, with its quarrelling lovers, is published
1600 · London, England
William Shakespeare's comedy Much Ado About Nothing was first published in 1600. In it Beatrice and Benedick keep up a running battle of wit and insults, kept apart by pride and mutual antagonism, until their friends trick each of them into believing the other is secretly in love. Even at the end they carry on teasing each other, but when love poems in their own handwriting are produced, they agree to marry.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Beatrice and Benedick became the model for countless later stories in which lovers' quarrelling turns out to be the very spice of their love.
This kural says that in love the quarrel is sweeter than the union, and Shakespeare's pair show how sparring can make love livelier than calm agreement.
