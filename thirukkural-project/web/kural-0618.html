Kural 618

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

பொறியின்மை யார்க்கும் பழியன்று அறிவறிந்து ஆள்வினை இன்மை பழி.
Poriyinmai yaarkkum pazhiyandru arivarindhu Aalvinai inmai pazhi.

Meaning

Having bad luck is nothing to be ashamed of. The real shame is knowing what needs to be done and still not making the effort.

Elementary

Nobody laughs at you if it rains on sports day. That’s just bad luck. But if you knew you had a test and didn’t study at all, that’s on you. What matters is whether you tried.

University

The couplet turns on பழி (blame, disgrace) and on the contested word பொறி. Parimelazhagar takes பொறி as ஊழ், favourable destiny, so ‘to lack good fortune is no disgrace’; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar follow him, while Solomon Pappaiah (and, differently, Sivayogi Sivakumar) read பொறி in its other sense of bodily organ or faculty, making the point that physical disability is no disgrace but failure to exert oneself is. Both readings yield the same moral: blame attaches only to what lies in our power, namely அறிவறிந்து ஆள்வினை, effort guided by knowing what ought to be known. The chapter title word ஆள்வினை appears here explicitly, and the couplet prepares for 619-620’s direct confrontation with fate. The text is the same in both editions (sandhi: பழியன் றறிவறிந் / தாள்வினை யின்மை).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Everything can be secured by Exertion: but nothing can be gained through Destiny alone, by a man that is wanting in personal Exertion.”
— Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Section VI (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Asked whether destiny or exertion is stronger, Bhishma relays Brahma’s answer that destiny is like seed and exertion like the tilled field, and neither yields without the other.
Like the Kural, the Mahabharata puts responsibility on the exertion that is within a person’s power.
Christianity
“Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him. I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work.”
— John 9:3-4 (KJV) [4]
Asked whose sin caused a man’s blindness, Jesus denies that the affliction is anyone’s fault and turns at once to the duty of working while there is time.
An unchosen affliction is no disgrace; neglecting the work one should do is what counts, as in the Kural, especially on Pappaiah’s ‘bodily faculty’ reading.
Islam
“A strong believer is better and is more lovable to Allah than a weak believer, and there is good in everyone, (but) cherish that which gives you benefit (in the Hereafter) and seek help from Allah and do not lose heart, and if anything (in the form of trouble) comes to you, don’t say: If I had not done that, it would not have happened so and so, but say: Allah did that what He had ordained to do”
— Sahih Muslim 2664 (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet commands striving for what benefits and forbids losing heart, while accepting misfortune as decree rather than as grounds for self-reproach.
Misfortune is accepted without disgrace, but slackness in striving is not, which is the Kural’s own division.
Judaism
“For a righteous man falleth seven times, and riseth up again, But the wicked stumble under adversity.”
— Proverbs 24:16 (JPS 1917) [6]
The proverb says the righteous are marked not by never falling but by rising again.
Falling (misfortune) brings no shame; what matters is the effort to rise, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
“The idler who does not exert himself when he should, who though young and strong is full of sloth, with a mind full of vain thoughts — such an indolent man does not find the path to wisdom.”
— Dhammapada 280 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha condemns the one who has youth and strength and still will not exert himself at the right time.
Having capacity and knowing the time yet not striving is exactly the Kural’s ‘disgrace’.
Jainism
“Acknowledging the truth about bondage and liberation, but talking only, not acting (in accordance with these tenets), they seek comfort for themselves in mighty words. Clever talking will not work salvation…”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.9-10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira condemns those who know the truth but do not act on it; knowledge without exertion saves no one.
The Kural's real disgrace, knowing what should be done and not making the effort, is exactly the 'false ascetic' of Uttaradhyayana 6, who knows but does not act.
Sikhism
“Dadda: Do not blame anyone else; blame instead your own actions.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 433, Raag Aasaa (Patti), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his acrostic Patti, Guru Nanak teaches that a person should not lay blame on others but look at the record of his own deeds.
The Kural says there is no disgrace in bad luck, only in failing to make the effort; Guru Nanak likewise places responsibility on one's own actions, not on outside causes.
Greek Mythology
“Work is no disgrace: it is idleness which is a disgrace.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 311 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells Perses that shame belongs not to labour or to poverty itself but to the idle.
Hesiod places disgrace precisely where Valluvar does: in the lack of effort.
Philosophy
Epictetus begins his Handbook by dividing things into those within our power, our judgements, impulses and actions, and those not in our power, such as body, property and reputation.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 [11]
For the Stoic, only what is in our power can be the object of praise or blame.
Unlucky externals carry no blame; neglecting what is in our power does, as the Kural says.

Real Life Example

Frida Kahlo teaches herself to paint after her bus accident
1925-1926 · Mexico City, Mexico
On 17 September 1925, eighteen-year-old Frida Kahlo was severely injured when the bus she was riding in was struck by a streetcar in Mexico City, and she spent months in plaster casts, largely confined to bed. Her mother had a special easel made so she could paint lying down, and a mirror was fixed above her bed so she could study her own face. Teaching herself, she produced her first known work, Self-Portrait Wearing a Velvet Dress, in 1926, and went on to become one of the most celebrated painters of the twentieth century.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Kahlo did not choose her injuries, which affected her for the rest of her life, but she chose to work within their limits, and that work made her name.
The kural says misfortune is no disgrace, only the failure to make an effort is, and Kahlo's accident brought no shame on her while the effort she made from her bed brought lasting honour.
