Kural 1136

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

மடலூர்தல் யாமத்தும் உள்ளுவேன் மன்ற படல்ஒல்லா பேதைக்கென் கண்.
Madaloordhal yaamaththum ulluvaen mandra padalollaa paedhaikken kan.

Meaning

Even at midnight I think about riding the palm-stem horse, since that innocent girl keeps my eyes from closing in sleep.

Elementary

Have you ever been so worried or excited that you couldn't fall asleep? The speaker lies awake in the middle of the night, thinking about the girl he loves and about his big plan to tell the whole village.

University

Yāmam (யாமம்) is the deep watch of the night, midnight; uḷḷuvēṉ (உள்ளுவேன்) 'I shall think, brood on', with maṉṟa (மன்ற) 'certainly' as an intensifier. Paṭal ollā (படல் ஒல்லா), 'not consenting to close', describes eyes that will not shut, and pētai (பேதை), literally 'innocent, simple one', is a conventional endearment for the heroine (also a technical term for the youngest of the seven stages of womanhood). Commentators in Parimelazhagar's line take it as the hero assuring the confidante that his resolve is not a passing mood but something he dwells on even at midnight. The ytamizh.com text prints படல்ஒல்லா without a space, thirukkural.net the same; editions vary only in spacing. Pope: 'Of climbing horse of palm in midnight hour, I think; / My eyes know no repose for that same simple maid.'

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Damayanti, having heard of King Nala's virtues, falls in love with him before they meet; she grows pale and thin, sighs, and loses her rest, so that her companions notice her changed state.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
The Nala episode is the classic Sanskrit account of a lover kept awake and wasting through longing.
Like the Kural's lover, Damayanti cannot rest because of the one she loves.
Christianity
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Solomon 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The beloved lies awake at night longing for the one she loves.
Both lovers are awake in the night, their thoughts fixed on the beloved.
Islam
Rabi'a al-'Adawiyya is remembered as praying through the night, saying that while lovers are alone with their beloveds, she is alone with her Lord.
— Farid al-Din 'Attar, Tadhkirat al-Awliya, life of Rabi'a (paraphrase) [5]
The great woman saint of Basra turned the lover's sleepless night into an act of devotion.
The Kural's lover is also awake at midnight because of the one he loves; Rabi'a gives that wakefulness a mystical meaning.
Judaism
“I sleep, but my heart waketh.”
— Song of Songs 5:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved says that even when her body rests, her heart stays awake for the one she loves.
The Kural's lover is in the same state: his eyes will not close because his heart is with her.
Buddhism
“Long is the night to the sleepless; long is the league to the weary.”
— Dhammapada 60 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha uses the sleepless person's endless night as an image for the long round of existence of those without wisdom.
The Kural's lover is that sleepless one, and the Buddhist verse sees his long night as a symptom of attachment.
Jainism
Jacobi notes that Rajimati's lament after Arishtanemi's renunciation is the subject of a later Jain Sanskrit poem, the Nemiduta of Vikrama, which borrows the last line of each stanza from Kalidasa's poem of a separated lover, the Meghaduta.
— Hermann Jacobi, note to Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28 (SBE 45), on Vikrama's Nemiduta [8]
Jain poets themselves took up the longing of the abandoned bride as a theme of separation poetry.
Like the Kural's sleepless lover, Rajimati in this tradition is shown dwelling on the beloved who is gone.
Sikhism
“Separation from my Lord keeps my body awake, O my dear beloved; I cannot sleep at all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 452, Raag Aasaa, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das gives the bride-soul's cry that the pain of separation (birha) will not let her body rest, so the night passes without sleep.
The Kural's lover broods even at midnight because his eyes will not close; the Sikh bride-soul likewise lies awake through the night, kept up by longing.
Greek Mythology
In the Argonautica, Medea lies awake through the night, torn between shame, which holds her back, and love for Jason, which urges her on to help him against her own father.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.C. Seaton) [10]
Apollonius shows a young woman's night-long struggle between modesty and desire.
This is the heart of the chapter: a lover awake at night, weighing reserve against love.
Philosophy
“And in her madness can neither sleep by night nor abide in her place by day.”
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-e (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates describes the soul of a lover separated from the beautiful one as unable to rest by night or day.
The Kural's lover is in exactly this state: his eyes will not close at midnight.

Real Life Example

Kafka's hundreds of letters to Felice Bauer
1912-1917 · Prague (now Czech Republic) and Berlin, Germany
Franz Kafka met Felice Bauer on 13 August 1912 at the home of his friend Max Brod in Prague and wrote her his first letter on 20 September 1912. Over the next five years he sent her more than five hundred letters and postcards, a romance carried on mostly on paper because they met in person only rarely. They were engaged twice, first in 1914, and both engagements were broken off; the final break came in 1917.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kafka's restless letter-writing shows how a love that cannot be settled keeps the mind turning over the same longing again and again.
The kural's lover cannot close his eyes even at midnight for thinking of the girl; Kafka's flood of letters shows the same sleepless, unending preoccupation with a beloved he could neither win nor give up.
