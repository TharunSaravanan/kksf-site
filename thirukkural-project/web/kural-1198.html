Kural 1198

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

வீழ்வாரின் இன்சொல் பெறாஅது உலகத்து வாழ்வாரின் வன்கணார் இல்.
Veezhvaarin insol peraaadhu ulakaththu vaazhvaarin vankanaar il.

Meaning

No one in the world is as hard-hearted, or as brave, as a woman who keeps on living without even one kind word from the one she loves.

Elementary

Imagine waiting every day for a letter from someone you love, and none ever comes, yet you keep getting up and going to school. That takes a very strong heart. The Kural says it is almost too strong.

University

Vaṉkaṇār means both ‘the hard-hearted, cruel’ and ‘the brave, enduring’, and the couplet exploits the ambiguity with self-irony: the heroine says that those who go on living in the world (ulakattu vāḻvār) without receiving (peṟāatu) even a sweet word (iṉcol) from their beloved (vīḻvār) are the most vaṉkaṇ of all. Parimelazhagar and Varadarasanar read vaṉkaṇmai as the stony endurance of separation; Pappaiah’s ‘no one is more cruel’ and Kalaignar’s ‘no one has so stony a heart’ stress self-reproach, while Pope chooses the heroic sense: ‘Who hear from lover’s lips no pleasant word from day to day, / Yet in the world live out their life,- no braver souls than they!’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Abandoned by her husband Nala in the forest while she sleeps, Damayanti wanders alone through wild country and strange cities for years, without word from him, yet keeps herself alive and devises a way to find him again.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
The Nala episode is the great Indian tale of a wife who endures long separation without any message from her husband.
Damayanti is the Kural’s ‘brave, hard heart’ who goes on living without a single kind word from her beloved.
Christianity
“Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life.”
— Proverbs 13:12 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs observes how long-delayed hope wears down the heart, while its fulfilment restores life.
The Kural’s heroine lives in this ‘hope deferred’, waiting for a sweet word that never comes.
Islam
“so patience is most fitting. And Allāh is the one sought for help against that which you describe.”
— Qur’an 12:18 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob, told that his beloved son Joseph is dead and given no true word of him, chooses beautiful patience.
Jacob’s years of patient endurance without news of Joseph parallel the heroine’s enduring life without a word from her beloved.
Judaism
Proverbs teaches that a person’s spirit can sustain him through sickness, but a broken spirit is something no one can bear.
— Proverbs 18:14 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [6]
The proverb distinguishes bodily suffering, which the spirit can endure, from the wound of the spirit itself.
The Kural marvels that the heroine goes on living with exactly such a broken spirit.
Buddhism
The nun Isidasi recounts how, though she served each husband with complete devotion, she was rejected again and again without cause, until she renounced household life and, as a nun, came to understand the past deeds behind her suffering.
— Therigatha 15.1, Isidasi (paraphrase) [7]
Isidasi’s verses tell of a faithful wife who received no love in return, yet endured and found release.
Isidasi endured, as the Kural’s heroine does, a life without kind words from those she loved.
Jainism
In the Jain Ramayana, Sita is sent away into the forest by Rama on account of public rumour, though she is pregnant and innocent; she survives, raises her sons, and when at last recalled to prove her purity she passes the ordeal and chooses to become a nun.
— Vimalasuri, Paumacariya (Jain Ramayana), Sita’s banishment and renunciation (paraphrase) [8]
The Jain telling shows Sita enduring long abandonment by her husband with dignity and then turning to renunciation.
Sita’s survival without word from Rama mirrors the Kural’s heroine, though the Jain story ends in renunciation rather than longing.
Sikhism
“Many talk of the pain and suffering of separation; O pain, you are the ruler of all. Fareed, that body, within which love of the Lord does not well up - look upon that body as a cremation ground.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 36) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid calls the pain of separation the ruler of all and says that a body which can live untouched by longing for the Beloved is no better than a cremation ground.
The Kural's wife says that anyone who goes on living without even one kind word from the beloved must be the hardest of hearts; Farid likewise sees a life untouched by longing for the Beloved as already dead.
Greek Mythology
For twenty years Penelope receives no word from Odysseus; she weeps nightly for him, weaving and unweaving the shroud to hold off her suitors, and tells the disguised stranger that she wastes her heart away in longing.
— Homer, Odyssey 19.124-163 and 19.512-534 (paraphrase) [10]
Penelope’s endurance without any message from her husband is the Greek model of faithful waiting.
Penelope is the Kural’s ‘brave soul’ who lives on without a single sweet word from her beloved.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that distance does not dissolve friendship outright but interrupts its activity, and quotes the saying that lack of conversation has broken many a friendship.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.5, 1157b (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, love lives through shared words and company, and withers when communication stops.
The Kural’s heroine endures exactly what Aristotle says love can hardly survive: no conversation, not even a kind word.

Real Life Example

Beethoven's letter to the Immortal Beloved
6–7 July 1812 · Teplitz, Bohemia (now Teplice, Czech Republic)
In July 1812, at the spa town of Teplitz, Beethoven wrote a passionate ten-page letter in pencil to a woman he addressed only as his Immortal Beloved. The letter was apparently never sent; it was found among his belongings after his death on 26 March 1827, and scholars still do not know for certain who it was meant for. Beethoven never married.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter shows a great love that, as far as the record shows, never became a shared life, and Beethoven went on working for fifteen more years without it.
The kural calls those who go on living without a sweet word from their beloved the hardest, or bravest, of all, and Beethoven's unsent letter points to that kind of endurance.
