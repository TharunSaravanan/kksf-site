Kural 108

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

நன்றி மறப்பது நன்றன்று நன்றல்லது அன்றே மறப்பது நன்று.
Nandri marappadhu nandrandru nandralladhu Andre marappadhu nandru.

Meaning

It is not good to forget a kindness done to you. But it is good to forget a wrong done to you that very same day.

Elementary

Keep the good things people do for you in your memory box forever. But if someone is mean to you, try to let it go before you go to bed that night.

University

The couplet is a celebrated piece of Valluvar's wordplay, circling the root நன்று (good) through நன்றி (a benefit), நன்றன்று (not good) and நன்றல்லது (what is not good, i.e. an injury), and pivoting on அன்றே, 'that very day'. It proposes an asymmetric economy of memory: benefits are to be retained indefinitely, injuries released at once. Ethically, this protects gratitude from being eroded by later grievances, a theme the next couplet (109) develops; psychologically, it anticipates modern research that treats gratitude and forgiveness as complementary dispositions. The instruction to let go of a wrong 'that very day' closely parallels Ephesians 4:26 ('let not the sun go down upon your wrath'), while the Psalmist's 'forget not all his benefits' supplies the other half of the Kural's pairing.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After Ravana's defeat, Hanuman offers to punish the demon women who had tormented Sita during her captivity, but Sita forbids it, saying they only acted under orders, that a noble person shows compassion even to wrongdoers, and that there is no one who never errs.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (Hanuman's report to Sita; commonly sarga 113-116 depending on edition) [3]
Sita refuses revenge on the guards who frightened and bullied her for months and lets the wrong go as soon as she is free.
Sita forgets the injury immediately, the second half of the Kural's teaching.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath:”
— Ephesians 4:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul says anger itself may happen, but you should not let it last past sunset.
'Not letting the sun go down' is almost exactly the Kural's 'forget it that very day' (அன்றே).
Islam
“And let not those of virtue among you and wealth swear not to give [aid] to their relatives and the needy and the emigrants for the cause of Allah, and let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allah should forgive you? And Allah is Forgiving and Merciful.”
— Qur'an 24:22 (Saheeh International) [5]
Revealed, according to the commentators, when Abu Bakr vowed to stop supporting a relative who had joined in slandering his daughter Aisha, the verse told him to pardon, overlook, and keep giving.
It tells the believer to drop the injury rather than let it cancel the relationship of kindness, the Kural's balance of forgetting wrongs and keeping goodness alive.
Judaism
“Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits:”
— Psalm 103:2 (KJV) [4]
The Psalmist tells his own soul to praise God and never forget any of the good things God has done.
'Forget not all his benefits' is the Kural's first half, நன்றி மறப்பது நன்றன்று; Leviticus 19:18's ban on bearing a grudge supplies the second.
Buddhism
“‘He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.’ Those who do not harbor such thoughts still their hatred.”
— Dhammapada 4 (Acharya Buddharakkhita trans.) [6]
The Buddha says that people who stop replaying the wrongs done to them are the ones whose hatred fades.
Not harbouring the memory of injury is the Kural's 'forget the wrong'.
Jainism
“If a monk hears bad words, cruel and rankling ones, he should silently overlook them, and not take them to heart.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Among the twenty-two hardships a Jain monk must endure, Mahāvīra lists abuse, which is to be let go of at once rather than nursed as a grievance.
This matches the second half of the Kural, letting go of a wrong that very day, though the Jain verse does not pair it with the duty to remember a kindness.
Sikhism
“Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir 155 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Kabir places God's presence wherever there is forgiveness, khima, one of the virtues Gurbani asks of the Sikh.
The Kural asks us to forget a wrong the same day it is done; Kabir's verse treats such forgiveness as the very place where God dwells.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Achilles ends his long wrath against Agamemnon, telling the assembled Achaeans that they should let what is past be past, painful as it was, and master the anger in their hearts.
— Homer, Iliad 19.56-73 [9]
After nursing his grudge for most of the war, Achilles finally decides to let it go.
It shows the release of an injury that the Kural recommends, though Achilles gets there far too late.
Philosophy
Seneca writes that of all the ungrateful, the most ungrateful is the one who has forgotten a benefit, since someone who has let it slip from memory has clearly never thought about repaying it.
— Seneca, De Beneficiis 3.1 (Aubrey Stewart trans.) [10]
The Stoic says that forgetting a kindness is the worst kind of ingratitude, because it shows you never even tried to pay it back.
This is the Kural's first half, 'forgetting a benefit is not good', stated as philosophy.

Real Life Example

Pope John Paul II forgives the man who shot him
17 May 1981; 1983 · Rome, Italy (Gemelli Hospital and a Rome prison)
On 13 May 1981 Pope John Paul II was shot and seriously wounded in St Peter's Square by Mehmet Ali Agca. Four days later, in a message recorded at the Gemelli hospital and broadcast in St Peter's Square, he said that he was praying for the brother who had shot him, whom he had sincerely forgiven. In 1983 he met Agca in prison and offered him forgiveness in person.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The Pope declared his forgiveness within days, while still recovering from his wounds, rather than waiting for the trial or for time to soften the injury.
The kural says it is good to let go of a wrong on the very day it is done; John Paul II released the gravest of injuries almost at once.
