Kural 1058

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

இரப்பாரை இல்லாயின் ஈர்ங்கண்மா ஞாலம் மரப்பாவை சென்றுவந் தற்று.
Irappaarai illaayin eerngkanmaa nyaalam marappaavai sendruvan thatru.

Meaning

If there were no people who ask for help, all the coming and going in this cool, wide world would be like wooden puppets moving back and forth.

Elementary

Imagine a toy theatre where wooden puppets bounce around on strings. They move, but they don't really care about anything. Valluvar says that if no one ever needed help, people would be like those puppets, busy but with no kindness to show.

University

Eerngkanmaa nyaalam (ஈர்ங்கண்மா ஞாலம், 'the cool, wide, great earth') and marappaavai (மரப்பாவை, 'wooden doll') give the image: Mu. Varadarasanar explains it as a wooden puppet moved by strings, so that human activity without askers would be mechanical motion without inner meaning. The commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar takes the point to be that giving is what gives life its moral content, and giving needs askers. Kalaignar reads the couplet differently, making those who keep the poor at a distance no different from operated puppets. Pope: 'The mighty earth ... Will be a stage where wooden puppets come and go.' The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree (சென்றுவந் தற்று in both).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He feeds no trusty friend, no man to love him. All guilt is he who eats with no partaker.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.6 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Vedic hymn says that someone who eats without sharing is 'all guilt', his life empty of bonds of love.
A life with no one to share with has lost its point, which comes close to the Kural's world of puppets.
Christianity
“Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.”
— Matthew 25:40 (KJV) [4]
In the judgment scene, Christ says that what is done for the hungry, the stranger and the poor is done for him.
Without the needy there would be no meeting with Christ through them, an echo of the Kural's claim that askers give the world its meaning.
Islam
“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allāh is like a seed [of grain] which grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains.”
— Qur'an 2:261 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an compares charity to a seed that multiplies into hundreds of grains.
Giving is what makes wealth grow and bear fruit, and there is no giving without someone to receive.
Judaism
“the world stands upon three things: the Torah, the Temple service, and the practice of acts of piety.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:2 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Shimon the Righteous names acts of kindness (gemilut chasadim) as one of the three pillars that hold up the world.
If kindness is a pillar of the world, a world with no one to receive it would lose something essential, close to the Kural's image.
Buddhism
In the recollection of the Sangha recited in the Dhajagga Sutta, the community of noble disciples is called worthy of gifts and of hospitality and 'an unsurpassed field of merit for the world'.
— Dhajagga Sutta, SN 11.3 (recollection of the Sangha) (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist teaching treats the alms-receiving community as the 'field' in which the world's giving bears fruit.
Without such a field the world's generosity would have nowhere to take root, near the Kural's claim that askers give the world meaning.
Jainism
Jain teaching on householder conduct (vaiyavrittya and gifts to ascetics) describes householders and mendicants as depending on each other: monks rely on pure alms freely given, and householders gain merit and spiritual progress by giving.
— Samantabhadra, Ratnakaranda Shravakachara (section on vaiyavrittya / gifts) (paraphrase) [8]
In Jain practice, lay giving finds its fullest meaning in supporting the mendicant order.
A world with no one to give to would empty this exchange, roughly what the Kural pictures.
Sikhism
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, the young Nanak was given money by his father to make a profitable bargain, but spent it feeding a group of hungry holy men, calling this the true bargain (sacha sauda).
— Janamsakhi tradition, as retold in Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion, Vol. 1 (1909) (paraphrase) [9]
The story presents giving to those in need as the only trade that gives worldly dealings real worth; it is hagiography rather than documented history.
The Kural says that without askers the world's busy comings and goings would be mere puppet-motion; the Sacha Sauda story likewise implies that commerce gains meaning only when it meets the needs of those who ask.
Greek Mythology
“we know the gods go about disguised in all sorts of ways as people from foreign countries, and travel about the world to see who do amiss and who righteously.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
A suitor warns Antinous that a beggar may be a god in disguise, testing who behaves rightly.
In the Greek view the beggar at the door is how a person's character is shown, a sense close to the Kural's claim that askers give human life its meaning.
Philosophy
Seneca opens On Benefits by treating the giving and receiving of benefits as the practice that, more than any other, holds human society together.
— Seneca, On Benefits, Book I (tr. Aubrey Stewart) (paraphrase) [11]
For Seneca, benefits are a social bond, not a private transaction.
Without people in need of benefits that bond would vanish, like the Kural's world of puppets.

Real Life Example

Marcel Mauss publishes The Gift
1925 · Paris, France
The French sociologist and anthropologist Marcel Mauss published Essai sur le don (The Gift) in 1925 in the journal L'Annee sociologique. Comparing forms of exchange in Melanesia, Polynesia and northwestern North America, he examined the religious, legal, economic and mythological sides of giving, receiving and repaying, and argued that such exchanges bind people together through mutual obligation.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The essay became one of the most influential works in the social sciences, showing that the giving and receiving of gifts is a basic thread of social life rather than a side matter.
The kural says that without people who ask, the busy world would be wooden puppets moving to and fro, and Mauss argued that the exchange of giving and receiving is what gives human society its living bonds.
