Kural 645

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

சொல்லுக சொல்லைப் பிறிதோர்சொல் அச்சொல்லை வெல்லுஞ்சொல் இன்மை அறிந்து.
Solluka sollaip pirithorsol achchollai Vellunjol inmai arindhu.

Meaning

Say what you have to say only after making sure that no one can say something that beats your words.

Elementary

Before you give your answer in a debate, check it from every side, like testing a bridge before you walk on it. If nobody can knock it down, then say it.

University

The couplet is famous for its sound play: col (word) appears five times in two lines, in collu-ka, collai, piṟitōr col, accollai and vellum col, so the verse enacts the verbal skill it recommends. The sense is that before speaking one should know (aṟintu) that no other word (piṟitu ōr col) that would overcome (vellum) that word exists (inmai). Parimelazhagar applies this to the minister in council, whose proposal must be answer-proof before it is offered to the king; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar say the same. The older edition shows sandhi forms (பிறிதோர்சொ லச்சொல்லை / வெல்லுஞ்சொ லின்மை யறிந்து), with no difference in meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At King Janaka’s great debate, the sage Yajnavalkya answers challenger after challenger (Ashvala, Artabhaga, Gargi, Uddalaka and others) until none can refute him, and Gargi declares that no one will defeat him in argument about Brahman.
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, Book 3 [3]
The Upanishad presents Yajnavalkya’s answers as so exact that every counter-argument fails.
Speech that no other speech can defeat is exactly what Yajnavalkya shows in Janaka’s court.
Christianity
“For I will give you a mouth and wisdom, which all your adversaries shall not be able to gainsay nor resist.”
— Luke 21:15 (KJV) [4]
Jesus promises his disciples that when they are put on trial they will be given words their opponents cannot answer.
Words no adversary can gainsay are the Kural’s speech that no other word can overcome.
Islam
“And We strengthened his kingdom and gave him wisdom and discernment in speech.”
— Qur’an 38:20 (Sahih International) [5]
David is given faṣl al-khiṭāb, which exegetes understand as decisive, conclusive speech that settles a matter.
Decisive speech that ends dispute is the Kural’s speech that cannot be defeated.
Judaism
Rabbi Eleazar teaches in Pirkei Avot to be diligent in study and to know what to answer an unbeliever (apikoros).
— Pirkei Avot 2:14 [6]
The sage urges preparing answers in advance so that one’s reply to a challenger is sound.
Preparing a reply that will hold against challenge is the discipline the Kural asks for before speaking.
Buddhism
The debater Saccaka boasts that no one could face him in debate without trembling, but when he challenges the Buddha his own theses are refuted, and he is left silent and sweating before the assembly.
— Majjhima Nikaya 35 (Cūḷasaccaka Sutta) [7]
The sutta contrasts a boastful debater with the Buddha, whose carefully built answers cannot be overturned.
The Buddha’s unanswerable replies show the kind of speech the Kural means; Saccaka shows what happens without it.
Jainism
“Well considering (what one is to say), speaking with precision, one should employ language in moderation and restraint.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.4.1.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Acaranga asks the monk to weigh every word, down to number, gender and tense, before speaking, so that what he says is exact and cannot be faulted.
The kural says to speak only after making sure no other word can defeat one's own; the Jain rule of careful, precise speech aims at the same unassailable word.
Sikhism
At Gorakhmata, Macauliffe relates from the Sikh biographies, Siddhs pressed the young Guru Nanak to put on the yogi's dress; he answered with a hymn saying religion lies not in patched coats, earrings or ashes but in living pure amid the impurities of the world, and the Siddhs, unable to answer him, made obeisance.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion, vol. 1 (1909), Life of Guru Nanak, ch. V, pp. 59-61 [9]
Guru Nanak met the yogis on their own ground with a reply so complete that it ended the dispute, a pattern repeated in his recorded discourses with the Siddhs.
The Kural advises speaking only after making sure no other word can defeat yours; in this account Guru Nanak's answer to the Siddhs left them no rejoinder.
Greek Mythology
After Achilles dies, Ajax and Ulysses (Odysseus) compete by speech for his armour before the Greek chiefs, and Ulysses’ carefully built argument defeats Ajax’s appeal to his strength.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 13.1-398 [10]
In Ovid’s retelling, the armour goes to the man whose speech the council cannot answer.
Ulysses wins because no counter-speech could overcome his, the Kural’s test for speaking.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that the rhetorician must be able to argue both sides of a question, not to argue for the worse, but so that he can see how things stand and refute anyone who argues unfairly.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 1.1, 1355a [11]
For Aristotle, good rhetoric means anticipating every counter-argument.
Knowing the opposing case in advance is how one makes sure that no word can defeat one’s own.

Real Life Example

Gandhi pleads guilty at the Great Trial in Ahmedabad
18 March 1922 · Circuit House, Ahmedabad, India
Charged with sedition for three articles in his weekly Young India, Mohandas Gandhi pleaded guilty before Judge Robert Broomfield, leaving the prosecution nothing to prove. He then read a written statement explaining how he had turned from loyalty to the British Empire to non-cooperation, and said he was there to submit to the highest penalty the law could impose for what he regarded as the highest duty of a citizen. The judge, acknowledging that many regarded Gandhi as a great patriot, sentenced him to six years and said no one would be better pleased than he if the government later reduced the sentence.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By admitting the facts and accepting the penalty in advance, Gandhi left no ground for argument against him and turned the trial into an examination of British rule.
The kural advises speaking only after making sure no other word can defeat one's own, and Gandhi's carefully prepared statement left the court with nothing to refute.
