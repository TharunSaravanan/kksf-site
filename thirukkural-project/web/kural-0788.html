Kural 788

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

உடுக்கை இழந்தவன் கைபோல ஆங்கே இடுக்கண் களைவதாம் நட்பு.
Udukkai izhandhavan kaipola aange idukkan kalaivadhaam natpu.

Meaning

When a person’s clothes slip, the hand rushes to catch them. In the same way, a true friend rushes right away to end a friend’s trouble.

Elementary

If your trousers started slipping in front of the whole class, your hand would grab them right away. You wouldn’t stop to think or wait to be asked; your hand just does it. A true friend helps you in trouble that fast and that naturally, as if they were your own hand.

University

This is one of the most quoted images in the Kural. Udukkai (உடுக்கை) is a garment and izhandhavan (இழந்தவன்) the one losing it, so the picture is of clothing slipping, which Solomon Pappaiah and others place ‘in front of many people’ (பலர் முன்னே) to bring out the urgency of shame. The key word is āngē (ஆங்கே, ‘there and then, at once’): the hand acts by reflex, without being asked and without thinking. Parimelazhagar notes that the hand acts before the mind decides, and so friendship should remove idukkaN (இடுக்கண், distress) before the friend has to ask. The image also implies that a friend is part of one’s own body. Both web sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Draupadi is dragged into the Kuru assembly and Duhshasana begins to strip off her garment, she calls on Krishna, and in the popular and later textual tradition her sari is miraculously and endlessly extended so that she cannot be stripped.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva, Section LXVIII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Draupadi, who calls herself ‘the friend of Vasudeva’ in the same scene, is rescued at the very moment her garment is being pulled away.
The image is almost the same as the Kural’s, a garment slipping in public and instant help, with Krishna as the friend who acts at once.
Christianity
“For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up.”
— Ecclesiastes 4:10 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher says two are better than one because, when one falls, the other is there to lift him up straight away.
Like the Kural’s hand, the companion is right there to catch his fellow at the moment of falling.
Islam
“The similitude of believers in regard to mutual love, affection, fellow-feeling is that of one body; when any limb of it aches, the whole body aches, because of sleeplessness and fever.”
— Sahih Muslim 2586a [5]
The Prophet compares believers to one body that reacts as a whole when any part is hurt.
Both texts use the body’s instinctive response to describe how friends respond to each other’s pain without stopping to think.
Judaism
As soon as Abram hears that his kinsman Lot has been taken captive, he arms his 318 trained men, pursues the raiding kings through the night as far as Dan, and brings Lot back.
— Genesis 14:14-16 [6]
Abram acts the moment the news reaches him, without waiting or being asked.
Abram’s instant rescue is the hand flying to the slipping garment.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should a helpmate be understood as a warm-hearted friend: (i) he guards the heedless, (ii) he protects the wealth of the heedless, (iii) he becomes a refuge when you are in danger”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha describes the true friend as one who protects you when you are off guard and becomes your refuge in danger.
Guarding the heedless is just what the hand does for the man who does not notice his garment slipping.
Jainism
“If I stretch not out my hand and deliver my whole soul without hesitation to my friend who is in distress, may I be cast into the hell where the wretch is punished who has violated the chaste wife of his friend, and may I be scoffed at throughout the far-famed earth!”
— Naladiyar, ch. 24 'Improper Friendship', stanza 8 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The speaker swears a solemn oath that he will reach out at once, without hesitating, to a friend in distress, and calls down the worst punishment on himself if he fails.
The Kural pictures friendship as a hand that flies to catch a slipping garment, and the Naladiyar's friend likewise stretches out his hand without hesitation to relieve a friend's distress.
Sikhism
“Great is His greatness, as He gives without being asked.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 463, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak praises God as the giver who meets our needs before we ask.
The Kural's friendship, like a hand catching a slipping garment, removes a friend's distress at once, without being asked; Guru Nanak praises the same unasked help in God.
Greek Mythology
When Heracles goes down to Hades, he finds his friend Theseus held fast to the Chair of Forgetfulness and, taking him by the hand, pulls him free and brings him back to the living.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5.12 [10]
Heracles takes the chance to free his friend the moment he finds him trapped.
The rescuing hand of Heracles is the Kural’s hand reaching out to remove a friend’s distress.
Philosophy
“without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods … Or how can prosperity be guarded and preserved without friends? The greater it is, the more exposed is it to risk.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle opens his discussion of friendship by saying no one would choose life without friends, since friends are what guard us in fortune and in danger.
Aristotle sees friends as the protection that keeps a life from coming apart, the role the hand plays in the Kural.

Real Life Example

Joshua Speed offers Abraham Lincoln a place to sleep
15 April 1837 · Springfield, Illinois, United States
On 15 April 1837 Abraham Lincoln, newly arrived in Springfield to practise law, walked into Joshua Speed's store and asked the price of bedding, but found he could not afford it and asked for credit. Speed, taken with the stranger, at once offered to share his own living quarters above the store, and Lincoln accepted on the spot. The two shared those quarters for several years and became close and lasting friends.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A moment of spontaneous generosity began one of the most important friendships of Lincoln's life, one that later helped him through a period of deep despair.
The kural compares friendship to the hand that flies to catch a slipping garment without being asked, and Speed solved a stranger's embarrassment immediately, before Lincoln could even finish asking for credit.
