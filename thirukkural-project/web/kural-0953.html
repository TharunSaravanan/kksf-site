Kural 953

Lineage · Kudimai · குடிமை

நகைஈகை இன்சொல் இகழாமை நான்கும் வகையென்ப வாய்மைக் குடிக்கு.
Nagai eegai insol igazhaamai naangum vagaiyenba vaaymaik kudikku.

Meaning

A smiling face, giving to others, kind words, and never looking down on anyone: people say these four are the marks of a truly good family.

Elementary

When a friend visits, a kind family smiles, shares their snacks, speaks nicely and never makes fun of anyone. Valluvar says those four habits show you what a truly good family looks like.

University

The couplet lists four marks (naangum, நான்கும்): nagai (நகை, a smiling or cheerful countenance), eegai (ஈகை, giving, the subject of chapter 23), insol (இன்சொல், pleasant speech, chapter 10) and igazhaamai (இகழாமை, not scorning or belittling). Vaaymaik kudi (வாய்மைக் குடி) means a family that is genuinely, not nominally, noble; Puliyur Kesikan reads it as a lineage that never changes its character, and he applies the four marks especially to one's conduct towards the poor. Vagai (வகை) is 'kind' or 'proper quality', and enba (என்ப, 'they say') marks the list as received wisdom. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu tells the householder to say what is true and what is pleasing, not to speak an unpleasant truth nor a pleasant falsehood, and calls this the eternal law.
— Manusmriti 4.138 (tr. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
The Laws of Manu make pleasant, truthful speech a standing duty of the cultured householder.
It matches the Kural's insol, pleasant speech, as a mark of good breeding.
Christianity
“Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks the Corinthians to give freely and gladly rather than under pressure.
The cheerful giver unites two of the Kural's marks, the smile and the gift.
Islam
“Kind speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allah is Free of need and Forbearing.”
— Qur'an 2:263 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an ranks gentle words above a gift spoiled by insult or reminders of the favour.
It joins three of the Kural's four marks: giving, kind words, and not humiliating the recipient.
Judaism
“Shammai used to say: make your [study of the] Torah a fixed practice; speak little, but do much; and receive all men with a pleasant countenance.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:15 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Shammai, famed for strictness, still teaches that every person should be greeted with a warm face.
His 'pleasant countenance' is the Kural's nagai, the cheerful welcome.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches the young householder Sigala the four bases of sympathy that hold society together: generosity, kind speech, helpful conduct and impartial treatment of all.
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
These four sangaha-vatthu are the Buddhist householder's tools for winning and keeping goodwill.
Like the Kural, it gives a list of four social virtues, with generosity and kind speech in common.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika teaches the monk purity of speech: he must not use harsh or hurtful words even when they are true, and must not call a one-eyed man one-eyed or a sick man sick, but speak in gentle, fitting terms.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra, ch. 7 (Vakyasuddhi, purity of speech) [8]
Jain discipline treats wounding speech as a form of violence (himsa) and so forbids mocking or demeaning words.
This is the Kural's igazhaamai and insol, never belittling and always speaking kindly.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all. Do not break anyone's heart; these are all priceless jewels.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 129) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, included in the Granth, forbids harsh speech and wounding anyone, because God lives in every person and each is a priceless jewel.
Two of the Kural's four marks, kind words and never belittling others, are exactly what Farid asks for, and he gives a reason: God is in everyone.
Greek Mythology
When the disguised Odysseus arrives as a ragged beggar, the swineherd Eumaeus welcomes him warmly, kills a pig for him and says it would not be right to slight any stranger, since strangers and beggars come from Zeus; later we learn Eumaeus was himself born a king's son.
— Homer, Odyssey 14-15 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Eumaeus, a prince sold into slavery, keeps the gracious manners of his birth even as a servant.
His smile, gift, kind words and refusal to scorn the poor match all four of the Kural's marks of true nobility.
Philosophy
“How much better is it to join kind words to kind actions, and to enhance the value of our gifts by a civil and gracious commendation of them!”
— Seneca, On Benefits (De Beneficiis) II.3, with II.2 and II.4 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
In this part of On Benefits, Seneca says the ready giver 'gives cheerfully and shows his disposition in his countenance' (II.2). He urges joining kind words to the gift (II.3) and warns that harsh words and a contemptuous manner make even kindnesses odious (II.4).
Seneca's good giver shows the Kural's four marks together: a cheerful face, generosity, kind words and no contempt for the one who receives.

Real Life Example

The Maharaja of Travancore issues the Temple Entry Proclamation
12 November 1936 · Travancore (now in Kerala), India
On 12 November 1936 Maharaja Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma of Travancore issued a proclamation opening the temples under his government to all Hindus, removing the ban that had kept people of the so-called lower castes out of them. The proclamation followed years of campaigning, including the Vaikom Satyagraha of 1924-25. It was hailed as one of the first measures of its kind in India, and the Government of Kerala still marks its anniversary.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A ruler of an old royal house used his authority to end a practice of exclusion, treating all his subjects as equally welcome.
Among the four marks of a noble family the kural lists generosity and never belittling others, and the proclamation was a royal act against the belittling of a whole section of society.
