Kural 907

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

பெண்ணேவல் செய்தொழுகும் ஆண்மையின் நாணுடைப் பெண்ணே பெருமை உடைத்து.
Penneval seydhozhukum aanmaiyin naanutaip penne perumai utaiththu.

Meaning

A woman who has modesty and self-respect is worth far more than a man whose so-called manliness is spent doing whatever his wife orders.

Elementary

Being a grown-up does not mean being bossy, and it does not mean just following orders either. A person with self-respect, whoever they are, deserves more admiration than someone who has given up thinking for themselves.

University

Pen-eval (பெண்ணேவல்) is 'a woman's command', and the man who 'conducts himself doing it' (seydhu ozhugum) is contrasted with naanutaip penne (நாணுடைப் பெண்ணே), the woman possessed of naan (நாண், modesty, sense of shame). Parimelazhagar notes that 'modest woman' is added deliberately as the opposite of the shameless man: the wife who issues commands is not the point of comparison, but rather womanhood marked by naan, which he regards as superior to such manhood. He also observes the emphatic separation of 'penne' at the line's start. Manakkudavar reads that such men are not respected by others. The verse is notable because, within its gendered frame, it affirms that a woman's perumai (பெருமை, greatness) can exceed a man's; modern feminist readers note, however, that it values women for modesty while denying men the role of following a woman. Text is identical across sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the forest exile, Draupadi rebukes Yudhishthira for his passivity after the dice game and urges him to reclaim his kingdom by righteous force.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva [5]
The epic gives a woman the voice of spirited dignity against a husband's resignation.
This supports the Kural's second half: a woman's dignity can surpass a man's, while complicating the first by showing a wife's counsel as valuable.
Christianity
Barak refuses to go into battle unless the prophetess Deborah goes with him, and she tells him the honour will therefore go to a woman, as Jael later kills Sisera.
— Judges 4:8-9 [6]
A commander's dependence on a woman means the glory of victory passes to women.
As in the Kural, a woman's dignity outshines that of a man who will not act on his own, though the text honours Deborah and Jael rather than shaming women's leadership.
Islam
The Queen of Sheba consults her chiefs, who defer to her judgment, and she chooses a wise and peaceful course before submitting to God with Solomon.
— Qur'an 27:29-44 [7]
The Qur'an portrays a woman ruler as prudent and her counsellors as rightly deferring to her.
A counterpoint: following a woman's lead here is wise, and her dignity is affirmed.
Judaism
Abigail, wife of the boorish Nabal, wisely intercedes with David and averts bloodshed, earning his praise for her discretion.
— 1 Samuel 25 [6]
A wife's good judgment far exceeds her husband's, and the narrative honours her.
This agrees with the Kural that a woman's dignity can surpass a man's, while showing a woman's initiative as praiseworthy.
Buddhism
The Therigatha preserves the verses of early Buddhist nuns who attained liberation, celebrating their insight and resolve.
— Therigatha [8]
Early Buddhism records women's spiritual accomplishment in their own voices.
It affirms, as the Kural's second line does, that a woman's greatness can be real and exemplary.
Jainism
“But when they have captured him, they send him on all sorts of errands.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.2.4, cf. 1.4.2.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
A long passage of the Sutrakritanga then lists the woman's orders (fetch fruit, rub my back, bring the mirror) and says such women 'order their husbands about like slaves' (1.4.2.15).
It is nearly a one-to-one match for Kural 907's man who 'conducts himself doing a woman's errands' (pen-eval).
Sikhism
Mai Bhago (Mata Bhag Kaur) openly rebuked forty Sikhs who had deserted Guru Gobind Singh during the siege of Anandpur; shamed, they followed her back, and they fought beside her at Khidrana (Muktsar) in 1705, where the Guru honoured the fallen as the Chali Mukte, the Forty Liberated Ones.
— Wikipedia, 'Mai Bhago' (drawing on the Oxford Encyclopedia of Women in World History and other works) [10]
A woman's courage and sense of honour exposed and then redeemed the failure of men who had abandoned their duty.
The Kural says a modest woman's dignity is worth more than the so-called manhood of a man who has lost his own; Mai Bhago's story shows a woman's honour shaming and restoring men who had faltered.
Greek Mythology
Heracles, as penance, serves Queen Omphale of Lydia, and later tradition depicts him spinning wool in women's dress while she wears his lion-skin and club.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.6.3; Ovid, Heroides 9 [11]
The greatest hero, performing a woman's commands, becomes a classical emblem of reversed roles.
This is the closest mythic image of the Kural's 'manhood that does a woman's bidding'.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates argues that women of the guardian class should receive the same education and share the same duties as men.
— Plato, Republic, Book V [12]
Plato's Socrates holds that capacity, not sex, should determine one's role in the city.
A counterpoint: Plato's argument undercuts the assumption that a man following a woman is inherently shameful.

Real Life Example

Empress Theodora counsels Justinian to stand firm in the Nika revolt
January 532 · Constantinople (Istanbul, Turkey)
During the Nika riots in Constantinople, the emperor Justinian's advisers urged him to flee the city. His wife, Empress Theodora, advised him to stay and defend his throne, and Justinian remained. His general Belisarius then crushed the revolt, and Justinian went on to rule for more than three decades.
— Sources: [13] [14]
At the decisive moment the empress showed more resolve and sense of honour than the emperor and his counsellors.
The kural says the dignity of a woman of honour is worthier than the manhood of a man who has lost his own, and Theodora's firmness outshone the counsel to flee.
