Kural 567

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

கடுமொழியும் கையிகந்த தண்டமும் வேந்தன் அடுமுரண் தேய்க்கும் அரம்.
Kadumozhiyum kaiyigandha thandamum vaendhan Adumuran thaeykkum aram.

Meaning

Harsh words and punishments that go too far are like a file that slowly wears down a king’s power to beat his enemies.

Elementary

A metal file rubs a little bit off a key every time you use it, until one day the key no longer works. The kural says that every cruel word and every too-big punishment rubs away a little of a leader’s strength, until it is gone.

University

கையிகந்த தண்டம் is punishment that has ‘gone beyond the hand’, exceeding its proper limit (Mu. Varadarasanar: முறைகடந்த தண்டனை). அடுமுரண் is the destroying strength, the capacity to crush enemies, and அரம் is a file, so the metaphor is of gradual erosion rather than sudden collapse, complementing the ‘swift ruin’ of 563–566. The couplet echoes 566 (harsh words) and 561 (proportionate punishment), making excess, not punishment itself, the target. The word தண்டம் (Sanskrit daṇḍa) links Valluvar to the wider Indian daṇḍanīti tradition of measured punishment. thirukkural-thamizh.org prints the sandhi form ‘அடுமுரண் டேய்க்கு மரம்’, identical in wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If the king is not wrathful, if he is not addicted to evil practices and not severe in his punishments, if he succeeds in keeping his passions under control, he then becomes an object of confidence unto all like the Himavat mountains.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section LVII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Bhishma teaches that freedom from wrath and from severe punishment makes a king as secure and trusted as the Himalayas.
The reverse of the Kural’s file: restraint builds the king’s strength where excess wears it down.
Christianity
“Fathers, provoke not your children to anger, lest they be discouraged.”
— Colossians 3:21 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns those in authority at home that excessive harshness breaks the spirit of those under them.
Excessive harshness erodes the bond that gives authority its strength, as the Kural’s file does.
Islam
“And the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation - his reward is [due] from Allāh. Indeed, He does not like wrongdoers.”
— Quran 42:40 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran caps retribution at an equal measure and ranks pardon above it; going beyond the measure is itself wrongdoing.
‘கையிகந்த தண்டம்’, punishment beyond measure, is what this verse rules out.
Judaism
“Forty stripes he may give him, and not exceed: lest, if he should exceed, and beat him above these with many stripes, then thy brother should seem vile unto thee.”
— Deuteronomy 25:3 (KJV) [4]
The Torah sets a strict upper limit on corporal punishment to protect the dignity of the one punished.
The Torah forbids exactly the punishment ‘beyond the hand’ that the Kural says erodes a king.
Buddhism
“While being completely law-abiding, some people are imprisoned, treated harshly and even killed without cause so that many people suffer. Therefore your aim should be to act with impartiality.”
— Ashoka, Kalinga (Separate) Rock Edict I (trans. S. Dhammika) [6]
Ashoka rebukes his judicial officers for excessive and unjust punishment, naming anger and cruelty as its causes and non-anger and patience as its cure.
Ashoka identifies and forbids the excessive punishment that the Kural calls a file on royal power.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra lists nine graded kinds of expiation (prayascitta) for faults, running from confession and repentance through penance and temporary suspension up to expulsion and reinitiation, so that the correction can be matched to the gravity of the fault.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.22 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Jain monastic discipline uses a graded scale of remedies rather than one severe penalty, starting from the mildest step of confession.
The Kural says punishment beyond measure wears down a king's power like a file; the Jain scale of expiation is built on the opposite principle of measured correction.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, speaking insipid words, the body and mind become insipid.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak teaches that harsh, graceless speech gradually drains the speaker himself, body and mind.
The Kural says harsh words and excessive punishment are a file that slowly wears away a king's strength; Guru Nanak likewise sees harsh speech as wearing down the one who uses it.
Greek Mythology
“He repealed the laws of Draco, all except those concerning homicide, because they were too severe and their penalties too heavy.”
— Plutarch, Life of Solon 17.1 (trans. B. Perrin, Loeb) [9]
Plutarch reports that Draco’s laws punished almost every offence with death, so harshly that they were said to be written in blood, and Solon swept them away.
Punishment beyond measure could not last; it wore away the authority of the laws themselves.
Philosophy
Seneca argues in On Clemency that frequent and cruel punishment does not secure a ruler but multiplies his enemies, since every execution creates new kin who hate him, while mercy makes his power safe.
— Seneca, De Clementia 1.8 and 1.24–26 [10]
For Seneca, cruelty is politically self-defeating: it drains the ruler’s security instead of adding to it.
Seneca’s argument is the Kural’s file metaphor spelled out: excessive severity wears away the ruler’s strength.

Real Life Example

England's Bloody Code is dismantled
c. 1690-1830s · England, United Kingdom
Between the 1690s and about 1750 Parliament passed many laws making specified crimes, including some thefts of small value, punishable by death, a body of law later known as the Bloody Code. In 1832 opponents of capital punishment argued in Parliament that such severity discouraged victims from prosecuting and juries from convicting, though others disputed this. Robert Peel, as Home Secretary from 1822, carried a broad consolidation and reform of the criminal law between 1825 and 1830, and most of the capital statutes were repealed in the 1820s and 1830s.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Punishments far beyond the measure of the offence did not strengthen the law; reformers argued that they weakened it, and Parliament eventually abandoned them.
The kural calls punishment beyond measure a file that slowly wears away a ruler's strength, and the Bloody Code's long, gradual discrediting shows excess eroding rather than reinforcing authority.
