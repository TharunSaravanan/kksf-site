Kural 1176

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

ஓஒ இனிதே எமக்கிந்நோய் செய்தகண் தாஅம் இதற்பட் டது.
Ohoh inidhae emakkinnoi seydhakan thaa-am itharpat tadhu.

Meaning

Oh, how sweet this is! The eyes that gave me this sickness have now fallen into the same suffering themselves.

Elementary

Imagine a friend talked you into jumping into a cold pool, and then they slipped and fell in too. You might shiver and giggle, ‘Ha! Now you know how it feels!’ The lady says that to her eyes: they got her into this sadness, and now they are stuck in it too.

University

The couplet opens with the long exclamation ōo (ஓஒ), an alapeṭai (prosodic lengthening) expressing intense feeling, and closes with the lengthened tāam (தாஅம், ‘they themselves’), both stretched for metre and emphasis. Iṉitē, ‘it is sweet’, is ironic satisfaction: the eyes that ‘made this sickness for us’ (emakku innōy ceyta kaṇ) have ‘fallen into it’ (itaṟpaṭṭatu). Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah specify that the eyes now cannot sleep and weep, while Kalaignar renders the tone as ‘this is a joy to me’. Pope: ‘Oho! how sweet a thing to see! the eye / That wrought this pain, in the same gulf doth lie.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One must deliver himself with the help of his mind, and not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that one’s own faculties can act as one’s friend or as one’s enemy, depending on how they are governed.
The heroine has discovered her eyes to be her enemy, and takes grim pleasure that the enemy now suffers along with her.
Christianity
“Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap.”
— Galatians 6:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul teaches that every action returns upon the one who did it.
The eyes sowed this sickness and now reap it, which is why the heroine calls their suffering ‘sweet’.
Islam
“So let them laugh a little and [then] weep much as recompense for what they used to earn.”
— Qur’an 9:82 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says that those who delighted briefly will weep at length as the fitting return for their deeds.
The Kural sees the same justice in the eyes: they delighted once, and now they weep as their due.
Judaism
“Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein; And he that rolleth a stone, it shall return upon him.”
— Proverbs 26:27 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs teaches that harm one causes tends to come back upon oneself.
The eyes dug the pit of love-sickness and have fallen into it themselves, which the heroine finds fitting.
Buddhism
“Fools of little wit are enemies unto themselves as they move about doing evil deeds, the fruits of which are bitter.”
— Dhammapada 66 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that people who act heedlessly become their own enemies, reaping bitter fruit from their own actions.
The eyes acted as the heroine’s enemy and now taste the bitter fruit themselves.
Jainism
“Thus the objects of the senses and of the mind cause pain to passionate men, but they never in the least cause any pain to the dispassionate.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.100 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that the senses, once passionately attached, bring pain back on the one who indulges them.
The heroine’s eyes, having been passionate, now feel the pain that passion brings, just as the sutra says.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, that human body, in which there is no pain of separation from the Lord-take that body and burn it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 89, Sri Raag ki Vaar (salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad says the pain of separation from the Lord is so precious that a body without it is worth only burning.
The Kural's heroine calls it sweet that the eyes which gave her this sickness have fallen into the same suffering; Guru Angad likewise treats the pain of separation as something to be welcomed rather than escaped.
Greek Mythology
When Oedipus learns the truth, he blinds himself, saying that his eyes shall no longer see the evils he has suffered and done.
— Sophocles, Oedipus the King, lines c. 1268-1274 (messenger’s report) (paraphrase) [10]
In Sophocles’ play, Oedipus turns on his own eyes as the guilty organs and punishes them.
The heroine likewise treats her eyes as culprits deserving to suffer, though only in a lover’s hyperbole.
Philosophy
In the Gorgias, Socrates argues that it is better for a wrongdoer to pay the penalty than to escape it, because just punishment is a cure for the soul.
— Plato, Gorgias 476a-479e (paraphrase) [11]
For Plato’s Socrates, suffering that answers to one’s own wrongdoing is fitting, even good.
The heroine’s ‘How sweet!’ treats the eyes’ suffering as a just and fitting penalty in exactly this spirit, if with more humour.

Real Life Example

Cleopatra meets Mark Antony at Tarsus
41 BCE · Tarsus, Cilicia (now in Turkiye)
In 41 BCE the Roman general Mark Antony summoned Cleopatra, queen of Egypt, to Tarsus to answer charges that she had helped his enemies. According to Plutarch, she sailed up the river Cydnus in a gilded barge with purple sails, and Antony was so taken with her that he returned with her to Alexandria. Their alliance ended in defeat at the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE, and both died in Egypt the following year.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Cleopatra came to Tarsus to win over Antony, and the attachment she created drew her, as well as him, into the same disaster.
The kural takes grim satisfaction that the eyes which caused the heroine's love-sickness have fallen into the same suffering, much as Cleopatra, who captivated Antony, was caught in the ruin that their love brought on them both.
