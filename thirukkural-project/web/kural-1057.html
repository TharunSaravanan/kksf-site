Kural 1057

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

இகழ்ந்தெள்ளாது ஈவாரைக் காணின் மகிழ்ந்துள்ளம் உள்ளுள் உவப்பது உடைத்து.
Igazhndhellaadhu eevaaraik kaanin magizhndhullam ullul uvappadhu udaiththu.

Meaning

When someone who asks meets givers who give without looking down on them or mocking them, their heart is filled with deep joy.

Elementary

If you ask for help and the person helps you with a kind smile instead of laughing at you, you feel warm and happy inside. How we give matters as much as what we give.

University

Igazhndhu ellaadhu (இகழ்ந்து எள்ளாது, 'without despising, without mocking') names the two insults a giver can add to a gift, contempt and derision; Pappaiah glosses them as அவமதிக்காமல், இழிவாகப் பேசாமல். The response is doubled for emphasis: magizhndhu (மகிழ்ந்து, 'rejoicing') and ullul uvappadhu (உள்ளுள் உவப்பது, 'delighting within the innermost heart'). The commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar places the joy in the asker, and the next chapter, Iravachcham, will show the opposite case, where contempt makes asking unbearable. Pope: 'The minds of askers, through and through, will thrill with joy.' The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And charity performed at an impure place, at an improper time, to unworthy persons, or without proper attention and respect is said to be in the mode of ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.22 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
The Gita places at the lowest grade the gift given without respect (asat-kṛtam) and with disdain (avajñātam).
The Gita's avajñātam, contempt, is exactly the scorn (igazhchi, ellal) that the Kural says spoils a gift.
Christianity
“If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him.”
— James 1:5 (KJV) [4]
James invites believers to ask God freely, because God gives generously and does not reproach the one who asks.
'Giveth liberally, and upbraideth not' is almost word for word the Kural's 'gives without scorn or ridicule'.
Islam
“Kind speech and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury. And Allāh is Free of need and Forbearing.”
— Qur'an 2:263 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an ranks a kind word above a gift that is followed by hurt or humiliation.
The Kural's joy at a gift given without scorn is the other side of the Qur'an's warning against gifts that injure.
Judaism
“Whenever a person gives charity to a poor person with an unpleasant countenance and with his face buried in the earth, he loses and destroys his merit even if he gives him 1000 gold pieces. Instead, he should give him with a pleasant countenance and with happiness, commiserating with him about his troubles”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:4 (tr. Eliyahu Touger, via Sefaria) [6]
Maimonides rules that a gift given with a sour face loses all merit, while a gift given cheerfully and with sympathy is the proper way.
This states the Kural's lesson as law: the manner of a gift decides whether the poor person's heart is gladdened.
Buddhism
In the Sappurisadana Sutta the Buddha lists the gifts of a person of integrity: given with conviction, attentively and with respect, at the right time, with an empathetic heart, and without harming oneself or others, and each brings its own good result.
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.148, Sappurisadana Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha teaches that a gift given respectfully and with empathy differs in kind from one carelessly thrown.
Giving with respect and empathy is what produces the asker's inner joy in the Kural.
Jainism
In the Uttaradhyayana's twelfth lecture, the outcaste-born monk Harikesa comes begging to a Brahmin sacrifice and is mocked and beaten. A yaksha who serves him intervenes, and the Brahmins finally honour him and give him alms.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 12 (Harikesa) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [8]
The story condemns those who insult a mendicant and shows that a gift offered with respect is what honours both sides.
It is a negative example of the Kural's lesson: scorn spoils the giving, and respect brings joy.
Sikhism
In the Sikh langar, as Britannica describes it, everyone sits together in a straight line, neither ahead to claim higher status nor behind to mark inferiority; the institution began as a protest against caste, and under Guru Amar Das visitors ate in the casteless langar.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Sikhism: Sikh practice' (sections on the rejection of caste and the worship service) and 'Guru Amar Das' [9]
The langar gives food freely and without any mark of contempt or rank: rich and poor, high and low caste sit side by side and are served the same meal as equals.
The Kural says the asker's heart rejoices when givers give without scorn or ridicule; the langar is built precisely so that no one who receives food is looked down upon.
Greek Mythology
“With these words he threw a footstool at him, and hit him on the right shoulder-blade near the top of his back.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Antinous answers the disguised Odysseus's request by throwing a footstool at him, and even the other suitors are shocked.
This is the opposite of the giver the Kural praises, and it shows why a gift without scorn gladdens the heart.
Philosophy
Seneca reports that Fabius Verrucosus called a favour granted harshly by an unfeeling man 'bread full of stones': welcome to a hungry man, but painful to swallow.
— Seneca, On Benefits 2.7 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) (paraphrase) [11]
Seneca holds that the manner of giving can spoil the benefit itself.
A gift given with scorn is the stony bread the Kural's asker cannot enjoy, while one given kindly makes the heart rejoice.

Real Life Example

Mother Teresa opens Nirmal Hriday for the dying destitute
1952 · Kalighat, Calcutta (Kolkata), West Bengal, India
In 1952 Mother Teresa opened Nirmal Hriday, a hospice at Kalighat in Calcutta, for destitute people who were dying and whom many hospitals of the time would not admit. Residents were washed, fed and cared for so that they could die with dignity, and she required workers to respect each person's own religious beliefs; the Nobel Committee later described the dying destitute there as received with warm compassion devoid of condescension.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nirmal Hriday became known worldwide for giving the poorest people care without contempt, and it was a central part of the work recognised by the 1979 Nobel Peace Prize.
The kural says the heart of the one in need rejoices deep within when givers give without scorn or ridicule, and Nirmal Hriday was founded on giving the destitute care with respect rather than condescension.
