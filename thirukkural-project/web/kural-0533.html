Kural 533

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

பொச்சாப்பார்க் கில்லை புகழ்மை அதுஉலகத்து எப்பால்நூ லோர்க்கும் துணிவு.
Pochchaappaark killai pukazhmai adhuulakaththu Eppaalnoo lorkkum thunivu.

Meaning

People who are careless and forgetful never become famous. The writers of every kind of book in the world agree on this.

Elementary

Ask a coach, a music teacher, a doctor, or a chef, and they will all tell you the same thing: people who keep forgetting and not paying attention never become great at anything. When everyone agrees on something, it's worth listening!

University

புகழ்மை means ‘the state of having fame’, and துணிவு here means a settled conclusion, not courage. Parimelazhagar takes எப்பால் நூலோர் to mean the authors of treatises of every kind, not only of polity (நீதி நூல்). He explains that the rule holds for everyone, not only kings: anyone pursuing any of the four aims of life (aṟam, poruḷ, iṉpam, vīṭu) will fail if heedless, and so will have no fame. Pavanar agrees that the rule is universal but argues that உலகம் here means the Tamil world and that எப்பால் நூல் refers to the branches of Tamil learning, much of which has since been lost; he points out that Valluvar does not say the northern (Sanskrit) texts agree. Manakkudavar reads the couplet simply as saying that heedlessness brings no fame. Parimelazhagar groups 533-535 as describing the harm (ஏதம்) that comes to the heedless.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The six faults should be avoided by a person who wisheth to attain prosperity, viz., sleep, drowsiness, fear, anger, indolence and procrastination.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 33 (Vidura Niti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
Vidura's statecraft maxims, in the nīti tradition, list the forms of inattention (sleep, drowsiness, idleness, delay) among the faults that rule out prosperity.
This confirms the Kural's claim that the treatise writers agree: the nīti texts too hold that the heedless cannot prosper.
Christianity
“Lest coming suddenly he find you sleeping. And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch.”
— Mark 13:36-37 (KJV) [5]
Jesus ends his discourse on the last things by extending the command to watch beyond his disciples to everyone.
Like the Kural, it presents watchfulness as a rule that holds universally, not a counsel for a few.
Islam
“They have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. Those are like livestock; rather, they are more astray. It is they who are the heedless.”
— Qur'an 7:179 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur'an names the heedless (al-ghāfilūn) as the lowest of the low: people who have every faculty and use none.
Like the Kural, it places heedlessness (ghaflah) beneath all honour.
Judaism
In Mesillat Yesharim, Moshe Chaim Luzzatto builds his ladder of spiritual ascent on the baraita of Rabbi Pinchas ben Yair (Avodah Zarah 20b), which starts from watchfulness (zehirut). He makes watchfulness the first rung and argues that a person who goes through life without examining his ways is like someone walking blind along a riverbank.
— Moshe Chaim Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim, chs. 1-2 (on Zehirut); Babylonian Talmud, Avodah Zarah 20b [7]
Luzzatto draws together a classic rabbinic chain of virtues in which careful watchfulness is the foundation for every higher attainment.
Like the Kural's ‘all treatises agree’, the Jewish ethical tradition treats vigilance as the agreed starting point without which nothing worthy is achieved.
Buddhism
“By Heedfulness did Indra become the overlord of the gods. Heedfulness is ever praised, and heedlessness ever despised.”
— Dhammapada 30, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [8]
The Dhammapada states a universal verdict: heedfulness is always praised and heedlessness always despised, and it cites even Indra's rise as proof.
This is almost the Kural's ‘settled conclusion of all’: the heedless get no praise.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
This lecture repeats the single command 'be careful all the while' (do not be heedless, pamaya) at the end of nearly every verse, which makes heedfulness the constant burden of Mahavira's teaching.
The Kural says every school of thought agrees that the heedless win no lasting glory; in the Jain canon heedfulness is likewise the teaching the master repeats without exception.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, those who forget the Naam-what will happen when they go to the Court of the Lord?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 17, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak asks what standing the forgetful can possibly have at the final reckoning.
The Kural says the heedless never win lasting fame; Guru Nanak agrees that those who forget have no standing where it finally counts.
Greek Mythology
Some of Odysseus's men eat the honey-sweet lotus and forget all thought of home. They want only to stay among the Lotus-eaters, and Odysseus has to drag them weeping back to the ships and tie them down.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 [11]
The lotus stands for any pleasure that wipes out memory of one's purpose. The men who ate it would have lost their homecoming (nostos), and with it any share in the hero's glory.
The Kural says the forgetful win no fame, and those who forget their homecoming vanish from the story.
Philosophy
Pierre Hadot argued that prosoche, continuous attention to oneself and to the present moment, was the fundamental spiritual attitude of the Stoics, and that practices of vigilance were shared across the ancient schools of philosophy.
— Pierre Hadot, Philosophy as a Way of Life, ed. Arnold I. Davidson (Blackwell, 1995), essay ‘Spiritual Exercises’ [12]
Hadot's reading of ancient philosophy as a set of ‘spiritual exercises’ puts attention at the core of the Stoic and wider philosophical way of life.
Hadot's account supports the Kural's claim that the ‘authors of every kind of treatise’ agree on the value of unforgetting vigilance.

Real Life Example

Henri Becquerel develops the plates he had put away
February-March 1896 · Paris, France
Henri Becquerel was testing whether uranium salts, after exposure to sunlight, gave off rays like the newly discovered X-rays, by placing the salts on photographic plates wrapped in black paper. When cloudy weather in late February 1896 interrupted the experiments, he stored the salts and wrapped plates together in a drawer, and on 1 March he developed the plates anyway and found clear images. Further tests showed that the uranium gave off penetrating rays without any sunlight, the discovery of radioactivity, for which he shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre and Marie Curie.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Becquerel's lasting fame rests on a careful check of plates that seemed unlikely to show anything at all.
The kural says the heedless never win lasting fame, and Becquerel's enduring name shows the reverse: fame that came from refusing to overlook a small, apparently pointless step.
