Kural 1243

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

இருந்துள்ளி என்பரிதல் நெஞ்சே பரிந்துள்ளல் பைதல்நோய் செய்தார்கண் இல்.
Irundhulli enparidhal nenje parindhullal paidhalnoai seydhaarkan il.

Meaning

My heart, why stay here thinking of him and suffering? The one who gave us this sickness of sorrow has no kind thoughts for us.

Elementary

Think of sitting by the window all day thinking about a friend who moved away, while that friend is busy and not thinking about you. You might ask yourself, “Why am I sitting here feeling sad when they aren’t even missing me?”

University

The heart is questioned for sitting idle: iruntu uḷḷi (‘remaining here and thinking’) eṉ paritaltal (‘why grieve?’), while parintu uḷḷal (‘thinking with tender pity’) is absent in the one who caused paital nōy (‘the sickness of sorrow’; paital = distress). Solomon Pappaiah unpacks iruntu as ‘neither going to where he is, nor dying here’, a reading that sharpens the reproach of passive pining. Pope’s explanation on ytamizh.com prints ‘there are no lewd thoughts (of you) in him’, which looks like a transcription slip for ‘loving thoughts’; all four Tamil glosses render parintu uḷḷal as anpu or irakkam (love, pity). The commentary tradition treats the line as the heroine voicing a suspicion she does not finally believe. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While speaking learned words, you are mourning for what is not worthy of grief.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.11 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna gently rebukes Arjuna for indulging a grief that true understanding would not sustain.
The Kural’s heroine rebukes her heart in the same way: why sit here grieving over one who is not grieving for you?
Christianity
“Hope deferred maketh the heart sick: but when the desire cometh, it is a tree of life.”
— Proverbs 13:12 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs observes that waiting endlessly for what one longs for makes the heart literally ill.
The Kural’s heroine sits pining for a husband who does not come, and names her state a ‘sickness of sorrow’.
Islam
“Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of Allah. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured.”
— Qur’an 13:28 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that the heart finds true rest in remembering God.
In contrast to the Kural’s heart, which finds only sickness in dwelling on an absent man, the Qur’an names a remembrance that brings peace.
Judaism
“Return, O my soul, unto Thy rest; For the LORD hath dealt bountifully with thee.”
— Psalms 116:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist tells his own soul to stop its restless distress and come back to rest.
The Kural’s heroine likewise tells her heart to stop sitting and pining in vain.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha notes that being apart from a loved one is itself a form of suffering.
The Kural’s heroine is living exactly this pain of ‘not seeing the beloved’.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra lists ārta-dhyāna, ‘sorrowful brooding’, as an inauspicious form of meditation, one kind being the constant thought of regaining a cherished person or thing from which one has been separated.
— Tattvartha Sutra ch. 9, sutras on ārta-dhyāna (tr. Nathmal Tatia, paraphrase; sutra numbers differ between Svetambara and Digambara recensions) [8]
Jain psychology classes repeated grieving over a lost beloved as a harmful mental state that binds karma.
The Kural’s heroine, ‘sitting and thinking’ of the husband who has gone, is caught in exactly this kind of brooding, which her own heart questions.
Sikhism
“My body is cooking like an oven; my bones are burning like firewood. If my feet become tired, I will walk on my head, if I can meet my Beloved.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 119) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid describes a longing so fierce that he would go to his Beloved even on his head once his feet gave out.
The Kural's wife asks her heart why it sits here pining for one who has no tender thought for them; Farid shows the opposite of idle pining, a lover ready to go to the Beloved at any cost.
Greek Mythology
Ariadne, waking on the island of Naxos to find Theseus has sailed away, writes to him from the shore, lamenting that she still thinks of him while he has no thought or pity for her.
— Ovid, Heroides 10 (Ariadne to Theseus) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid gives voice to an abandoned heroine whose love is met only by the beloved’s indifference.
The Kural’s heroine, too, sits grieving for one who ‘has no tender thought’ for her.
Philosophy
Epictetus begins his handbook by dividing things into those within our power, such as our judgments and desires, and those not within our power, which we must not let disturb us.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (paraphrase) [11]
Stoic practice trains the mind to stop agonising over what lies in another’s control.
Whether the absent husband thinks of her is not in the heroine’s power, which is why her reason questions her heart’s pining.

Real Life Example

Petrarch first sees Laura in Avignon
1327 · Avignon, France
In 1327, at Avignon, the Italian poet Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch) first saw the woman he called Laura. She became the idealised subject of his chaste love and the main subject of his poetry for the rest of his life, and he gathered his lyrics into the Canzoniere, which eventually held 366 poems.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Petrarch's lifelong poetry for a love he could never have shaped the European love sonnet for centuries.
The kural's heroine asks her heart why it sits thinking and suffering over someone who gives no tender thought in return, and Petrarch spent decades pining in verse for a woman his love could never possess.
