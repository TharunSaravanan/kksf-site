Kural 1267

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

புலப்பேன்கொல் புல்லுவேன் கொல்லோ கலப்பேன்கொல் கண்அன்ன கேளிர் விரன்.
Pulappaen-kol pulluvaen kolloa kalappaenkol kan-anna kaelir viran.

Meaning

When he comes back, he who is as dear to me as my own eyes, should I sulk, or hug him, or do both? I cannot decide.

Elementary

Imagine your best friend went away for a long time without visiting. When they finally come back, part of you wants to say “Hmph, where were you?” and part of you wants to give them a big hug. The lady in this verse feels both at once, and she just can’t decide which to do first.

University

Three rhetorical questions, each with the doubt particle kol: pulappēṉ (shall I show pulavi, the lover’s feigned sulk), pulluvēṉ (shall I embrace), kalappēṉ (shall I mingle the two, or simply unite). Kaṇ aṉṉa kēḷir, ‘the beloved who is like my eyes’, and viraṉ (‘should he come’) set the scene. Solomon Pappaiah reads kalappēṉ as ‘doing both together’ and Kalaignar adds that she is too overwhelmed even to know; the couplet anticipates the later chapters on ūṭal (lovers’ quarrel, chapters 131-133). Textual note: ytamizh.com’s ‘English Couplet’ slot for 1267 contains a prose explanation, and its ‘Couplet Explanation’ (‘This malady (of lust) is manured by the talk of women…’) belongs to Kural 1147, not this couplet. The Tamil text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, when Krishna comes to Radha at dawn after a night away, she rebukes him in wounded pride, and only later, after his pleading, does her anger melt into reconciliation.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Radha’s rebuke and reconciliation (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva gives Radha the full sequence of māna (proud sulk) followed by reunion.
The Kural’s heroine sees both moves ahead of her, the sulk and the embrace, and cannot choose between them.
Christianity
“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying, Open to me… I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them?”
— Song of Solomon 5:2-3 (KJV) [4]
The bride’s heart wakes for her beloved, yet she hesitates and makes excuses before rising.
Like the Kural’s heroine, she is caught between eager love and a reluctance that holds her back.
Islam
“I know when you are pleased with me or angry with me… When you are pleased with me, you say, ‘No, by the Lord of Muhammad,’ but when you are angry with me, then you say, ‘No, by the Lord of Abraham.’” Aisha replies that even then she leaves nothing but his name.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5228 (sunnah.com English) [5]
The Prophet gently notices Aisha’s small sulks, and she answers that even in anger her love for him remains.
This is the Kural’s mix of pulavi and embrace: the sulk is only on the surface, and the love beneath it never leaves.
Judaism
“And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him; and they wept.”
— Genesis 33:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
After twenty years of estrangement and grievance, Esau meets Jacob not with reproach but with an embrace.
The Kural’s heroine wonders whether her grievance or her embrace will win; in Esau’s case the embrace wins.
Buddhism
“From affection springs grief, from affection springs fear. For one who is wholly free from affection there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 213 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha traces both grief and fear to the root of affection.
The heroine’s sulk and her embrace both spring from one root, her love, which is just what the verse names as the source of turmoil.
Jainism
“Having heard the Buddha’s well-delivered sermon, adorned by illustrations, Gautama cut off love and hatred and reached perfection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching names love (raga) and hatred (dvesha) as the twin bonds to be cut for liberation (‘Buddha’ here is a title for Mahavira).
The heroine is caught between those very two, the pull to sulk and the pull to embrace, which Jainism counsels cutting away together.
Sikhism
“whether your Lord and Master is angry with you or in love with you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 338, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In this hymn Kabir speaks of the devotee's relationship with the Master as one that holds both displeasure and love.
The wife wonders whether, when her husband returns, she will sulk, embrace him, or both; Kabir's line likewise holds anger and love together in one bond.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus stands before her at last, Penelope sits silent, pondering whether to question her husband from a distance or to go to him and kiss his head and hands, and Telemachus scolds her for her hardness of heart.
— Homer, Odyssey 23 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer gives Penelope a long moment of doubt between reserve and embrace before she yields.
This is the Kural’s dilemma almost exactly: hold back or embrace, or somehow both.
Philosophy
Spinoza calls it vacillation of mind when the same thing is at once the cause of both joy and sorrow, so that we love and hate it at once.
— Baruch Spinoza, Ethics III, Proposition 17 and Scholium (paraphrase) [11]
Spinoza explains how one object can stir opposite feelings at the same moment.
The heroine’s undecided ‘shall I sulk or embrace?’ is exactly such a vacillation toward one beloved person.

Real Life Example

Heloise writes to Abelard with reproach and love
c. 1130s · The Paraclete, France
After Peter Abelard, then abbot of Saint-Gildas in Brittany, wrote an account of his misfortunes, Heloise, by then head of the convent of the Paraclete, wrote to him. In her letter she reproached him for not comforting her, either in person or by letter, since they had both entered religious life. In the same letter she declared that she had always loved him and that her heart still belonged to him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Heloise's letter is famous for holding complaint and devotion together in the same breath.
The kural's heroine cannot decide whether to sulk at her returning beloved, embrace him, or do both; Heloise's letter does both at once.
