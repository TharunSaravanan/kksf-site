Kural 1268

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

வினைகலந்து வென்றீக வேந்தன் மனைகலந்து மாலை அயர்கம் விருந்து.
Vinaikalandhu vendreeka vaendhan manaikalandhu maalai ayarkam virundhu.

Meaning

Let the king fight on and win the war. Then I will join my wife at home, and we will have a feast together this evening.

Elementary

Now the husband speaks. He is far away helping the king in a big contest. He thinks: “Let us finish this job and win, and then I can rush home and have a happy dinner with my wife tonight.” He misses her just as much as she misses him.

University

Here the speaker changes: it is the husband, serving with the king (vēntaṉ) on campaign, who voices his own vitumpal, which is why the chapter is ‘mutual’. Viṉai kalantu veṉṟīka, ‘may [the king] engage in the task and win’, is followed by maṉai kalantu, ‘joining my home (wife)’, and mālai ayarkam viruntu, ‘we shall hold a feast in the evening’. The parallel kalantu…kalantu sets the king’s battle beside the husband’s reunion. Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read the hero’s eagerness for victory as eagerness to be home; Puliyur Kesikan specifies ‘with her in the evening’. Text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
During the monsoon at Kishkindha, Rama, separated from Sita, laments that the rains delay the campaign to recover her, and longs for the season to end so that he can march and win her back.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda, Rama’s lament in the rains (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki shows a hero whose impatience for the campaign is really impatience to be reunited with his wife.
Like the Kural’s husband, Rama wants the fighting done because his heart is with his wife.
Christianity
“Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the life of thy vanity, which he hath given thee under the sun… for that is thy portion in this life, and in thy labour which thou takest under the sun.”
— Ecclesiastes 9:9 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher names joy with one’s beloved wife as the true portion of life amid all labour.
The Kural’s husband feels the same: the campaign is labour, and the evening with his wife is his real reward.
Islam
When Abdullah ibn Amr fasted every day and prayed all night, the Prophet told him not to, saying that his body, his eyes and his wife each have a right over him.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5199 (sunnah.com English; paraphrase) [5]
The Prophet restrains even devout effort that crowds out a man’s duty of companionship to his wife.
The Kural’s husband feels the same claim: his wife’s right draws him home once the task is done.
Judaism
“When a man taketh a new wife, he shall not go out in the host, neither shall he be charged with any business; he shall be free for his house one year, and shall cheer his wife whom he hath taken.”
— Deuteronomy 24:5 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The Torah exempts a newly married man from war so that he can make his wife happy.
Torah law gives priority to exactly what the Kural’s husband longs for: leaving the campaign to be with his wife.
Buddhism
“When, after a long absence, a man safely returns from afar, his relatives, friends and well-wishers welcome him home on arrival.”
— Dhammapada 219 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha uses a traveller’s homecoming as a picture of joyful welcome.
This is the homecoming the Kural’s husband is already imagining: the work done, a welcome and a feast at home.
Jainism
“Give up your wealth and your wife; you have entered the state of the houseless; do not, as it were, return to your vomit; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira tells a monk not to look back toward the household and wife he has left.
The Kural’s husband longs to hurry home to his wife; Jain teaching for the renouncer points the other way, though it does not forbid this to the householder.
Sikhism
“My sought-after Husband Lord has come home, and now, congratulations are pouring in.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 704, Raag Jaithsree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes the joy of the soul-bride when the Husband Lord she longed for finally comes home, and everyone celebrates.
In the Kural the husband longs to finish the campaign and come home to feast with his wife; Guru Arjan pictures the happy homecoming itself and its celebration.
Greek Mythology
On Calypso’s island, Odysseus sits on the shore day after day weeping, longing for his homecoming and his wife, though a goddess offers him immortality to stay.
— Homer, Odyssey 5 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer’s hero values the return to his home and wife above everything else on offer.
The Kural’s husband shares this pull: whatever the task, his heart is set on the evening at home with his wife.
Philosophy
Aristotle writes that we are busy in order to have leisure, and make war in order to live in peace.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 10.7 (tr. W.D. Ross; paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, work and war are not ends in themselves but are for the sake of the life lived afterward.
The Kural’s husband sees the king’s war the same way: it is for the sake of the evening feast at home.

Real Life Example

George Washington writes to Martha on taking command
18 June 1775 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
Days after the Continental Congress made him commander of the new army, George Washington wrote to his wife Martha from Philadelphia. He told her he had not sought the command, partly because he did not want to be parted from her and the family, and that he would find more real happiness in one month with her at home than in the most distant prospect abroad, even if his stay lasted seven times seven years. The letter is one of the very few between them to survive, because Martha is believed to have destroyed most of their correspondence.
— Sources: [12] [13]
At the start of a long war, the new commander's private letter shows that his heart was at home with his wife.
The kural gives the husband's voice: let the king's campaign be won, and he will go home to feast with his wife; Washington, taking up command of a war, wrote that a month at home with Martha outweighed any glory abroad.
