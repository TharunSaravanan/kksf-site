Kural 536

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

இழுக்காமை யார்மாட்டும் என்றும் வழுக்காமை வாயின் அதுவொப்பது இல்.
Izhukkaamai yaarmaattum endrum vazhukkaamai Vaayin adhuvoppadhu il.

Meaning

If a person can stay alert and careful toward everyone and at all times, nothing else is as good as that.

Elementary

Being careful once is easy. Being careful with everyone, every day, even when things are going great, is the real superpower. If you can do that, it's better than almost any other gift you could have.

University

The couplet plays on two near-synonyms: இழுக்காமை (‘not slipping’, that is, unforgetfulness) must be present வழுக்காமை (‘without a lapse’). Parimelazhagar explains யார்மாட்டும் as reaching even the king's own people, the servants who do his work and his kin, and என்றும் as meaning ‘in days of growth and of decline alike’. He parses வாயின் as a conditional form built on the verbal-noun stem வாய் (‘to come right, to fall into place’). Pavanar adds that வழுக்காமை means applying this without fail in every task, and he explicitly rejects Parimelazhagar's analysis of வாயின், citing Tolkāppiyam and Kural 737 to argue that வாய் is an intransitive verb. Manakkudavar reads the couplet as a rule for the careful administration of justice. Together with 537, this couplet forms Parimelazhagar's pair on the excellence of unforgetfulness.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Therefore, Arjuna, you should always think of Me in the form of Kṛṣṇa and at the same time carry out your prescribed duty of fighting. With your activities dedicated to Me and your mind and intelligence fixed on Me, you will attain Me without doubt.”
— Bhagavad Gita 8.7 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is, A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
The Sanskrit sarveṣu kāleṣu anusmara means ‘remember at all times’. Krishna asks for unbroken remembrance to go along with, not replace, active duty.
The Kural's ideal is likewise remembrance that never lapses, ‘at all times’, while one carries on one's work.
Christianity
“Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour:”
— 1 Peter 5:8 (KJV) [5]
Peter calls for constant sobriety and alertness because the danger is always prowling nearby.
Like the Kural, it treats unbroken vigilance as the essential safeguard.
Islam
“And remember your Lord within yourself in humility and in fear without being apparent in speech - in the mornings and the evenings. And do not be among the heedless.”
— Qur'an 7:205 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur'an asks for remembrance (dhikr) morning and evening, as a standing guard against ghaflah, heedlessness.
Like the Kural, it treats unbroken remembrance as the positive virtue that defeats heedlessness.
Judaism
“Only take heed to thyself, and keep thy soul diligently, lest thou forget the things which thine eyes saw, and lest they depart from thy heart all the days of thy life;”
— Deuteronomy 4:9 (JPS 1917) [7]
Moses commands a watchfulness that lasts ‘all the days of thy life’ so that what has been learned is never forgotten.
This is the Kural's unforgetfulness kept up at all times.
Buddhism
“Just as the footprints of all living beings with legs can be encompassed by the footprint of the elephant, and the elephant's footprint is declared to be supreme among them in terms of its great size; in the same way, heedfulness is the one quality that keeps both kinds of benefit secure — benefits in this life & benefits in lives to come.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 3.17 (Appamada Sutta), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [8]
King Pasenadi asks the Buddha whether one quality secures every kind of welfare, and the Buddha names heedfulness (appamāda) as supreme among them all.
This is the Kural's ‘nothing equals it’, stated almost word for word in Buddhist terms.
Jainism
“Though others sleep, be thou awake! Like a wise man, trust nobody, but be always on the alert; for dangerous is the time and weak the body. Be always watchful like a Bhârunda bird!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira asks for unbroken vigilance at every moment and toward everyone, comparing it to the mythical two-necked Bharunda bird that can never relax its watch.
The Kural praises unfailing watchfulness toward everyone and at all times as the highest good; this verse asks for exactly that constant, all-round alertness.
Sikhism
“One who does not forget the Naam, for a moment or even an instant-how rare is such a person in this world!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 21, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak praises remembrance that never lapses even for an instant, and notes how rare it is.
The Kural says nothing equals watchfulness kept without a lapse towards everyone and at all times; Guru Nanak prizes the same never-lapsing attention, directed to the Name.
Greek Mythology
Hera sets Argus Panoptes, the hundred-eyed watchman, to guard Io. Only some of his eyes ever sleep at a time while the rest keep watch, until Hermes lulls every eye shut with his pipes and a long story and kills him.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.622-721 (trans. A. S. Kline) [11]
Argus is the Greek image of constant vigilance. His death shows that one lapse undoes everything.
The Kural values watchfulness that never lapses at any time, and Argus's end shows why ‘always’ matters.
Philosophy
In his discourse on attention (prosoche), Epictetus warns that once you relax your attention you should not imagine you can recover it whenever you like. Each lapse makes the next one easier and builds a habit of neglect, so attention has to be kept up continuously.
— Epictetus, Discourses 4.12 (‘On Attention’) [12]
For Epictetus vigilance is not a single act but an unbroken state, and any intermission costs more than it seems to.
The Kural's ideal of never slipping, towards anyone, at any time, matches Epictetus's demand exactly.

Real Life Example

Walsingham's watchfulness uncovers the Babington Plot
1586 · England
Sir Francis Walsingham, secretary of state to Elizabeth I, kept a network of agents and informers to protect the queen. In 1586, learning through a double agent of a Catholic conspiracy, later named after Anthony Babington, to assassinate Elizabeth and put Mary, Queen of Scots, on the throne, Walsingham had Mary's secret letters, smuggled in beer barrels, intercepted, deciphered by his codebreaker Thomas Phelippes and resealed before delivery. The correspondence exposed the plot, and the conspirators were arrested and tried.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Watchfulness extended even to letters their writers thought secure protected the queen from a plot that might otherwise have succeeded.
The kural says nothing equals unfailing watchfulness kept towards everyone and at all times, and Walsingham's constant vigilance is a classic example of it in statecraft.
