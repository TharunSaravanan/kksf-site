Kural 1086

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

கொடும்புருவம் கோடா மறைப்பின் நடுங்கஞர் செய்யல மன்இவள் கண்.
Kodumpuruvam kodaa maraippin nadungagnar seyyala manival kan.

Meaning

If her curved eyebrows would only stay straight and shade her eyes, her eyes would not make me tremble and suffer like this.

Elementary

When the sun is too bright, a cap with a peak shades your eyes so you are not dazzled. The young man wishes her eyebrows would act like that cap and shade her eyes, so her look would not make him so nervous.

University

Kodum-puruvam (கொடும்புருவம்) means the 'curved' or 'cruel' brows; kodum carries both senses, so the curve itself is accused. Kodaa (கோடா, 'without bending') and maraippin (மறைப்பின், 'if they hid') give the wish: if the brows stayed straight and screened the eyes. Nadung-agnar (நடுங்கஞர்) is 'trembling distress', and man (மன்) adds regret, 'surely they would not...'. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both read it as 'if the bent brows stood straight and hid them, her eyes would not cause me trembling sorrow'. The image draws on the Indian convention of brows as a drawn bow from which the eyes shoot. Pope: 'If cruel eye-brow's bow, / Unbent, would veil those glances now.' Identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Katha Upanishad compares the body to a chariot, the senses to horses and the mind to the reins: only a person who keeps the reins firm stops the senses from running wild.
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.3-6 (paraphrase) [3]
Hindu teaching treats control over what reaches us through the senses as the key to a steady mind.
The Kural wishes for a 'rein' on the eyes, the brows acting as a screen that would spare him distress.
Christianity
“Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me: thy hair is as a flock of goats that appear from Gilead.”
— Song of Solomon 6:5 (KJV) [4]
The lover asks the beloved to turn her eyes away because they overpower him.
The Kural makes the same wish: if only her eyes were screened, he would stop trembling.
Islam
“And she certainly determined [to seduce] him, and he would have inclined to her had he not seen the proof [i.e., sign] of his Lord.”
— Qur'an 12:24 (Sahih International) [5]
Joseph is kept from yielding only because a sign from God came between him and temptation.
The Kural wishes for a screen between him and her eyes; Joseph is saved by a divine screen.
Judaism
“Your eyes are like doves / Behind your veil.”
— Song of Songs 4:1 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs praises the beloved's eyes as they appear softened behind a veil.
The Kural wishes her eyes were veiled by her brows, so their power would be softened.
Buddhism
The Visuddhimagga tells of the elder Mahatissa, who passed a woman laughing on the road and, guarding his senses, noticed only her teeth, which became for him an object of meditation on the body.
— Visuddhimagga I.55 (tr. Bhikkhu Nanamoli; paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist sense restraint means not letting the eye seize on beauty, so it cannot disturb the mind.
The Kural wishes for a screen over her eyes; the monk builds that screen inside his own mind.
Jainism
“Not to look at, nor to long for, not to think of, nor to praise, womankind: this is becoming the meditation of the noble ones, and it is always wholesome to those who delight in chastity.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana makes not looking the first step of a monk's chastity.
The Kural's lover wishes the sight could be screened off; Jain discipline screens it by choice.
Sikhism
“The soul-bride and the Husband Lord live together as one, but the hard wall of egotism has come between them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1263, Raag Malar, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Using the bride-soul image, Guru Ram Das says the soul and God already live together, but ego stands between them like a hard wall.
The Kural's lover wishes the curved brows would screen the girl's eyes; Gurbani also speaks of a screen between lover and Beloved, but there the screen is ego, and the devotee longs for it to be removed.
Greek Mythology
“...from whose eyes as they glanced flowed love that unnerves the limbs: and beautiful is their glance beneath their brows.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 910-911 (tr. Hugh G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod says that love flows from the Graces' eyes and singles out their glance beneath their brows.
The Kural, too, places the danger in the eyes beneath curved brows.
Philosophy
Pascal remarked that if Cleopatra's nose had been shorter, the whole face of the earth would have changed.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées (Brunschvicg 162; paraphrase) [11]
Pascal's point is that a tiny feature of a face can decide enormous outcomes.
The Kural, too, makes everything depend on one small feature, the curve of her eyebrows.

Real Life Example

Frida Kahlo paints Diego on My Mind
1943 · Mexico City, Mexico
In 1943, a few years after she and Diego Rivera remarried in 1940, Frida Kahlo painted Diego on My Mind (Self-Portrait as Tehuana). She shows herself in a Tehuana headdress of starched lace framing her face, with a small portrait of Rivera placed on her forehead just above her dark, joined eyebrows. The painting is now in the Jacques and Natasha Gelman Collection.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The work is a famous image of a love so consuming that the beloved occupies the painter's very brow and thoughts.
The kural dwells on the beloved's brow and eyes as the source of the lover's trouble; Kahlo set her husband's image on her own forehead, between her brows, to show how he filled her mind.
