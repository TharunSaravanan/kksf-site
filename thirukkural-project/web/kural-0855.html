Kural 855

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இகலெதிர் சாய்ந்தொழுக வல்லாரை யாரே மிக்லூக்கும் தன்மை யவர்.
Ikaledhir saaindhozhuka vallaarai yaare mikalookkum thanmai yavar.

Meaning

Who could ever beat people who can turn away from hostility whenever it rises inside them?

Elementary

Picture someone trying to start a fight with a kid who just smiles and won’t fight back. After a while, there is nothing to fight about, and the bully gives up. The poet says nobody can beat a person who never lets anger take over.

University

The key phrase is ikal edhir saaindhu ozhuga (இகல் எதிர் சாய்ந்து ஒழுக): to ‘lean away against’ hostility, i.e. to recoil from it and conduct oneself in the opposite direction when it arises within. Puliyur Kesigan specifies ‘when hostility arises in one’s own heart’, so the victory is first over oneself; mikal ūkkum (to aspire to overcome) then describes would-be conquerors, of whom ‘who’ (yaare) implies none. Textual note: both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print the second line as ‘மிக்லூக்கும்’ while their own romanizations read ‘mikalookkum’ and thirukkural.net’s song version prints ‘மிகலூக்கும்’; the word is mikal + ūkkum, and the printed form appears to be a shared digital orthographic slip. The same form recurs in 858. The text is otherwise identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Before giving up this present body, if one is able to tolerate the urges of the material senses and check the force of desire and anger, he is well situated and is happy in this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.23 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that the one who can check the force of anger becomes steady and secure.
The Kural calls such a person unconquerable.
Christianity
“Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.”
— Romans 12:21 (KJV) [4]
Paul says the way to win against evil is not to be dragged into it but to answer with good.
The Kural likewise makes refusal of hostility the secret of being unconquered.
Islam
“The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength, but the strong is the one who controls himself while in anger.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 [5]
The Prophet redefines strength as mastery over one’s own anger.
Valluvar’s unconquerable person is exactly this kind of strong one.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city.’”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria), citing Proverbs 16:32 [6]
The sage Ben Zoma defines true might as self-mastery, quoting Proverbs on slowness to anger.
Both texts place real power in conquering one’s own hostility.
Buddhism
“Overcome the angry by non-anger; overcome the wicked by goodness; overcome the miser by generosity; overcome the liar by truth.”
— Dhammapada 223 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that non-anger is itself the means of victory.
The Kural’s unconquerable person wins by the same non-anger.
Jainism
“They indeed had no effect whatever on the Lord whose power was great. Is a wind, even great, able to shake Meru?”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X (Mahāvīracaritra), ch. III, part 10 'Episode of Caṇḍakauśika' (tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The furious serpent Caṇḍakauśika burns Mahavira with its poisonous gaze and bites him again and again. The Lord stays unmoved and without hostility, and the serpent, overcome by his calm, gives up its anger altogether.
The Kural asks who could ever defeat those who turn away from hostility, and Caṇḍakauśika's venom cannot even shake Mahavira, who wins not by fighting back but by staying free of enmity.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib (pauri 28), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib Guru Nanak makes kinship with all people the true spiritual order and says that whoever conquers his own mind has conquered the world.
The Kural asks who could ever defeat those who turn away from hostility when it rises within; Guru Nanak likewise makes victory over one's own mind, held in brotherhood with all, the victory over the world.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the North Wind and the Sun, the Wind blows harder and harder but cannot strip a traveller of his cloak, while the Sun’s gentle warmth makes him take it off.
— Aesop, The North Wind and the Sun (Townsend tr.) [10]
Gentleness succeeds where force and aggression fail.
The Kural likewise says the one who refuses hostility cannot be overcome.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius writes that kindness is invincible if it is genuine, since even the most insolent person can do nothing to one who stays kind and gently corrects him.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.18 [11]
The Stoic emperor holds that sincere goodwill disarms every attacker.
Valluvar’s ‘who could overcome them?’ makes the same claim.

Real Life Example

Martin Luther King Jr. calms the crowd after his home is bombed
30 January 1956 · Montgomery, Alabama, United States
During the Montgomery bus boycott, Martin Luther King Jr.'s home was bombed on the evening of 30 January 1956 while he was speaking at a mass meeting; his wife Coretta and their baby daughter were unhurt. He returned to find a large, angry crowd, many of them armed, gathered outside and refusing police orders to leave. Standing on his damaged porch, King urged them to love their enemies and to go home calmly, and what could have become a riot ended peacefully.
— Sources: [12] [13]
King's refusal to answer violence with violence kept the boycott nonviolent, and the movement went on to win the desegregation of Montgomery's buses later that year.
The kural asks who could ever overcome those who turn away from hostility whenever it rises, and King turned himself and his supporters away from retaliation at the very moment it was most provoked, making the movement unbeatable.
