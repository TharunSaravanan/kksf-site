Kural 1311

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

பெண்ணியலார் எல்லாரும் கண்ணின் பொதுஉண்பர் நண்ணேன் பரத்தநின் மார்பு.
Penniyalaar ellaarum kannin podhu-unpar nannen paraththa-nin maarpu

Meaning

You unfaithful man! All women feast their eyes on your broad chest as if it belongs to everyone. So I will not come near it.

Elementary

Imagine your favourite teddy bear being passed around the whole class until it no longer feels like yours. A wife in this poem playfully tells her husband that since everyone admires him, she will pretend she does not want to sit close to him.

University

The chapter title pulavi nuṇukkam means ‘the fine points (nuṇukkam) of love-sulking (pulavi)’: in the kaṟpu (wedded love) section, Valluvar sketches how a loving wife turns the smallest innocent word or act of her husband into a pretext for a mock quarrel, and Parimelazhagar’s tradition takes this first couplet as the wife’s own speech. She addresses him as paratta (‘man of wandering ways’) and says that all of feminine nature (peṇ-iyalār) ‘eat’ his chest with their eyes as a common possession (potu uṇpar), so she will not approach (naṇṇēn) it. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read the eye-feasting literally as other women’s admiring glances, while Solomon Pappaiah sharpens it into a charge that he is ‘shared’ with women of the street; Pope renders it ‘From thy regard all womankind enjoys an equal grace; O thou of wandering fickle mind, I shrink from thine embrace.’ The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the eighth canto of Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda, Krishna comes to Radha at dawn still showing the signs of a night spent with another cowherd girl, and Radha, cast as the wounded heroine (khandita), sends him away with bitter teasing: go to the one you were with.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 8 (paraphrase) [3]
Radha’s anger is the classic Sanskrit picture of a beloved who refuses an embrace because her lover has been shared with others.
Like Radha, the Kural’s wife declares she will not come near a chest that, in her playful accusation, belongs to every woman.
Christianity
“The wife hath not power of her own body, but the husband: and likewise also the husband hath not power of his own body, but the wife.”
— 1 Corinthians 7:4 (KJV) [4]
Paul teaches that in marriage each spouse belongs to the other, so neither is a common possession.
The wife’s complaint rests on the same idea: her husband should be hers, not shared by every admiring eye.
Islam
“Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them. Indeed, Allah is Acquainted with what they do.”
— Qur’an 24:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an asks believers, with a parallel command for women in the next verse, to lower their gaze rather than feast their eyes on others.
The wife objects to exactly the kind of looking the verse forbids: other women feasting their eyes on her husband.
Judaism
“Because the LORD hath been witness between thee and the wife of thy youth, against whom thou hast dealt treacherously, though she is thy companion, and the wife of thy covenant.”
— Malachi 2:14 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Malachi rebukes husbands who betray the wife of their youth and reminds them that she is their covenant partner.
The Kural’s wife voices, in play, the same claim Malachi makes in earnest: a husband owes himself to his own wife.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha lists the ways a husband should care for his wife, including by honouring her, not despising her, and not being unfaithful to her.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (tr. Narada Thera; paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist lay ethics make faithfulness one of a husband’s plain duties to his wife.
The wife’s teasing charge of ‘wandering’ presumes this same duty of faithfulness.
Jainism
“If you fall in love with every woman you see, you will be without hold like the Haṭha-plant, driven before the wind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The nun Rajimati rebukes Rathanemi, warning that a heart which goes after every woman it sees has no anchor.
The Kural’s wife teases her husband as a ‘wanderer’ whose chest belongs to everyone, the same rootless love Rajimati condemns.
Sikhism
“The One God is our father; we are the children of the One God. You are our Guru.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 611, Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan teaches that God belongs equally to all people, who are all His children.
The Kural's wife will not come near a chest she says all women gaze on as common property; Gurbani, by contrast, rejoices that the Lord is shared by all, with no room for jealousy.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, the goddess Calypso offers Odysseus immortality if he stays with her, but he tells her that, though Penelope cannot match her beauty, he still longs every day to go home.
— Homer, Odyssey 5.203-224 (paraphrase) [10]
Odysseus keeps his heart for his own wife even when another, more dazzling partner wants him.
The Kural’s wife wants exactly this: a husband who belongs to her, not to every woman who admires him.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Pausanias distinguishes a ‘common’ (Pandemos) love, which attaches to anyone and loves the body more than the soul, from a ‘heavenly’ love that is faithful and lasting.
— Plato, Symposium 180c-181d (paraphrase) [11]
Pausanias contrasts love that belongs to everyone and no one with love that is constant to one person.
The wife’s word potu (‘common’) makes the same contrast: she refuses a chest she pretends has become common property.

Real Life Example

The Beatles appear on The Ed Sullivan Show
9 February 1964 · New York City, United States
On the evening of 9 February 1964 the Beatles made their debut on the CBS programme The Ed Sullivan Show. About 73 million people tuned in, and the band played to a studio audience filled with screaming teenage fans. The appearance helped spread the craze that British newspapers had already named Beatlemania across the United States.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It is one of the best-documented moments when millions of admirers fixed their eyes on the same few performers at once, a kind of shared adoration that television made possible.
The wife in this kural refuses to come near a man whom every woman already gazes at as if he belonged to all, and the screaming crowds of 1964 show what that kind of shared, public admiration looks like.
