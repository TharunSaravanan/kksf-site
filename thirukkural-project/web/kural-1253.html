Kural 1253

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

மறைப்பேன்மன் காமத்தை யானோ குறிப்பின்றித் தும்மல்போல் தோன்றி விடும்.
Maraippaenman kaamaththai yaanoa kurippindrith thummalpoal thoandri vidum.

Meaning

I try to hide my love, but it gives no warning. Like a sneeze, it just bursts out.

Elementary

Have you ever tried to stop a sneeze in a quiet room? You hold your nose, but out it comes anyway. The heroine says her love is like that: she tries to keep it secret, but it pops out all by itself.

University

The homely simile of the sneeze (tummal) is one of the most memorable images in the Kāmattuppāl: the heroine would conceal (maṟaippēṉ) her kāmam, but it appears kuṟippiṉṟi, ‘without regard to my intention’ or ‘without warning’. The particle maṉ in maṟaippēṉmaṉ marks an intention that fails (‘I would indeed hide it, but…’), a construction repeated in 1254 (eṉpēṉmaṉ). Mu. Varadarasanar glosses kuṟippiṉṟi as ‘not staying as I intend’ (என் குறிப்பின்படி நிற்காமல்), while Solomon Pappaiah reads ‘without my even knowing’, stressing involuntariness. Pope’s rendering on ytamizh.com (‘like a sneeze / It shows itself, and gives no warning sign’) keeps the image. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Act 3 of Kalidasa’s play, Shakuntala, wasting with love for King Dushyanta, tries to keep her feelings to herself, but her friends Anasuya and Priyamvada read the signs on her body and draw the confession from her.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 3 (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa shows a young woman’s love becoming visible to those around her despite her silence.
Like the Kural’s sneeze, Shakuntala’s love shows itself before she chooses to reveal it.
Christianity
“Whosoever hideth her hideth the wind, and the ointment of his right hand, which bewrayeth itself.”
— Proverbs 27:16 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says some things simply cannot be hidden: trying is like hiding the wind, or perfume on your hand that gives itself away.
The Kural’s sneeze is the same kind of image: something that reveals itself however you try to hide it.
Islam
Ibn Hazm devotes chapters to the signs of love and to the keeping of the lover’s secret, observing that lovers try to hide their passion but are betrayed by their glances, their confusion and their changing colour.
— Ibn Hazm, The Ring of the Dove (Tawq al-Hamama), chapters on the signs of love and on concealing the secret (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Arberry) [5]
The Andalusian treatise on love treats concealment as a duty the lover attempts and often fails.
The Kural’s heroine attempts the same concealment, and her love likewise breaks through without warning.
Judaism
“And if I say: ‘I will not make mention of Him, nor speak any more in His name’, then there is in my heart as it were a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I weary myself to hold it in, but cannot.”
— Jeremiah 20:9 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Jeremiah resolves to stay silent, but what is in his heart forces its way out.
The Kural’s heroine resolves to hide her love and likewise cannot hold it in.
Buddhism
In the Ummadanti Jataka, King Sivi glimpses the beautiful Ummadanti and is so struck that he cannot hide his condition: he becomes lovesick and absent-minded, and his longing becomes plain to those around him.
— Ummadanti Jataka (Jataka 527) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Jataka shows a king’s concealed passion making itself known despite his position and self-command.
Like the Kural’s heroine, the king finds that love shows itself without his consent.
Jainism
When the monk Rathanemi sees Rajimati sheltering in a cave from the rain, his peace of mind is disturbed and he at once speaks his desire aloud to her, unable to keep it to himself.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.33-38 (paraphrase of tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana shows a disciplined monk whose sudden passion breaks out in words the moment it arises.
Rathanemi’s passion, like the heroine’s love, bursts out before restraint can contain it.
Sikhism
“Now the moon emerged in the sky of heart cannot remain hidden in spite of efforts to cover its light with flour kneading basin.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 13, Pauri 6 (English translation as given on BaniDB) [9]
In a stanza describing the delight of those drenched in love, Bhai Gurdas says such love shows itself however hard one tries to cover it, like moonlight under an upturned bowl.
The Kural's wife says she tries to hide her love but it bursts out without warning like a sneeze; Bhai Gurdas likewise says love that has risen in the heart cannot be kept covered.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Hippolytus, Phaedra explains that she first tried to bear her love in silence and hide it, then to master it by self-control, and only when both failed resolved to die rather than be shamed.
— Euripides, Hippolytus, Phaedra’s speech to the women of Troezen (c. lines 373-430; paraphrase) [10]
Euripides dramatizes a woman’s doomed effort to conceal a love that keeps showing itself in her wasting body.
Phaedra’s first strategy is the Kural heroine’s ‘I would hide it’, and both find that love will not stay hidden.
Philosophy
La Rochefoucauld observes that there is no disguise that can long hide love where it exists, nor feign it where it does not.
— La Rochefoucauld, Maxims, no. 70 (paraphrase) [11]
The French moralist states as a general maxim that real love cannot be kept hidden.
This is the Kural’s sneeze turned into a maxim: love reveals itself whatever the lover intends.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth Barrett's Sonnets from the Portuguese
written 1845-1846; published 1850 · London, England, and Florence, Italy
Elizabeth Barrett wrote a sequence of 44 love sonnets during her courtship with the poet Robert Browning, whom she met in 1845. She kept them as a private record and gave them to him only after their secret marriage and move to Italy in 1846. When they were published in her Poems of 1850, she titled them Sonnets from the Portuguese, a disguise that made them look like translations and hid how personal they were.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Barrett tried to keep her love private and then to veil it with a misleading title, yet the feeling in the poems made them some of the most famous love poems in English.
The kural's heroine would hide her love but it bursts out like a sneeze; Barrett's love, written in secret and then disguised, still came out for all to read.
