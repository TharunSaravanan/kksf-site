Kural 1152

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

இன்கண் உடைத்தவர் பார்வல் பிரிவஞ்சும் புன்கண் உடைத்தால் புணர்வு.
Inkan udaiththavar paarval pirivanjum punkan udaiththaal punarvu.

Meaning

Once, even his look was pure joy. Now even our hugs are full of pain, because I fear the parting that is coming.

Elementary

Think of the last day of a wonderful holiday. You are still at the beach, the sun is shining, but you keep thinking, ‘Tomorrow we go home,’ and the fun feels a little sad. The wife in this couplet feels that way: her husband is still with her, but knowing he will leave makes even being close to him hurt.

University

The couplet contrasts the past and present of the same relationship: avar pārval (அவர் பார்வல், ‘his look’) once had iṉkaṇ (இன்கண், ‘sweetness, joy’), but now even puṇarvu (புணர்வு, ‘union, being together’) has puṉkaṇ (புன்கண், ‘distress’) because of pirivu añcum, ‘fearing separation’. The two rhyming nouns iṉkaṇ and puṉkaṇ carry the turn from joy to grief. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read pārval as his loving look, while Solomon Pappaiah contrasts his pleasing look with his conduct, which signals that he is about to leave; Kalaignar sharpens the contrast between gazing and embracing. Pope: ‘It once was perfect joy to look upon his face; / But now the fear of parting saddens each embrace.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gopi Gita, the cowherd women tell Krishna that when he goes off to the forest by day, a single moment without seeing him feels like an age to them.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.31, Gopi Gita (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana makes the gopis’ longing for the sight of Krishna the model of devotion, and their pain in his absence its measure.
The gopis share the Kural’s sense that the sight of the beloved is pure joy, and that the shadow of his going spoils it.
Christianity
“I held him, and would not let him go, until I had brought him into my mother’s house.”
— Song of Solomon 3:4 (KJV) [4]
The bride in the Song of Songs, having found her beloved after searching for him, clings to him for fear of losing him again.
Her clinging embrace is shaped by the same fear of loss that, in the Kural, turns being together into anxiety.
Islam
“[Jacob] said, ‘Indeed, it saddens me that you should take him, and I fear that a wolf would eat him while you are of him unaware.’”
— Qur’an 12:13 (Sahih International) [5]
Jacob is grieved by the mere proposal that Joseph leave his side, even for a day.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Jacob is pained in advance; the loved one is still present, but the coming separation already hurts.
Judaism
“The LORD watch between me and thee, when we are absent one from another.”
— Genesis 31:49 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Laban and Jacob name the cairn Mizpah as they part, asking God to keep watch over the bond while they are apart.
The Mizpah words show a meeting already shaped by the coming absence, which is the mood of the Kural’s embrace.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that the same affection that delights us is the root of the grief and fear of losing what we love.
The Kural describes this exact process: the joy of his presence has become the fear of his absence.
Jainism
“All singing is but prattle, all dancing is but mocking, all ornaments are but a burden, all pleasures produce but pains.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Citra warns his former brother, King Brahmadatta, that every pleasure carries the seed of its own suffering.
The Kural’s heroine finds out the same thing in her own life: the delight of union has already turned into pain.
Sikhism
“Previously in my spiritual tranquility, I had the happiness and bliss of being near Him, but I am now crying with pangs of separation.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabitt Savaiye 670, English translation as given in the BaniDB database [9]
Bhai Gurdas, the early Sikh scholar and scribe of the Adi Granth, contrasts the bliss the devotee once knew in the Beloved's presence with the weeping of separation now. His Kabitt Savaiye are early exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural's heroine says that his look was once pure delight but that dread of parting now fills even their embrace with pain; Bhai Gurdas sets the same before-and-after of nearness turned to grief.
Greek Mythology
Orpheus is allowed to lead Eurydice back from the underworld on condition he does not look at her until they reach the light; near the end, afraid of losing her, he turns to look, and she slips away from him a second time.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10 (Orpheus and Eurydice) (paraphrase) [10]
In Ovid’s telling, it is Orpheus’s anxious love and fear of losing her that make him look back.
As in the Kural, the beloved’s presence and the lover’s look are spoiled by dread of separation.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises that when you kiss your child or your wife, you should remind yourself that you are kissing a human being, so that if they are taken from you, you will not be disturbed.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 3 (paraphrase) [11]
The Stoic exercise deliberately brings the thought of separation into moments of affection, in order to free the mind from dread.
The Kural’s heroine has the same thought of parting in the middle of an embrace, but in her it brings only pain, which is exactly the disturbance Epictetus hopes to remove.

Real Life Example

Fridtjof Nansen leaves his wife and baby daughter for the Fram expedition
24 June 1893 · Lysaker, near Christiania (Oslo), Norway
The explorer Fridtjof Nansen, married to the singer Eva Nansen since 1889, left his home at Lysaker in June 1893 to begin the Fram expedition into the Arctic Ocean. In his published journal he described looking back at his home, seeing his small daughter Liv at an upstairs window, wishing he could turn back, and calling that parting the darkest hour of the whole journey. The voyage took just over three years, and the Fram returned in 1896.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Nansen's own record shows that, for him, the hardest moment of a dangerous polar voyage was not the ice but the farewell at his own door.
The kural says that the shadow of a coming parting turns even the joy of being together into pain, which is the feeling Nansen set down on the day he left his family.
