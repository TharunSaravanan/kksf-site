Kural 196

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

பயனில்சொல் பாராட்டு வானை மகன்எனல் மக்கட் பதடி யெனல்.
Payanilsol paaraattu vaanai makanenal, Makkat padhadi yenal.

Meaning

Do not call someone who loves to show off with empty words a real person. Call them the useless husk among people.

Elementary

When farmers harvest rice, the good grain is kept and the light, empty husks called chaff blow away in the wind. This kural says a person who only talks empty talk is like that chaff: they look like a person on the outside, but inside there is nothing useful.

University

Valluvar here turns from consequence to classification. பாராட்டுவான் (paaraattuvaan) means one who displays, cherishes or keeps repeating; Mu. Varadarasan glosses it as ‘saying many times’. The one who does this with பயனில்சொல் (empty words) is denied the name மகன் (makan, ‘man, human being’) and given instead மக்கட்பதடி (makkat-padhadi, ‘the chaff of humanity’). The agricultural metaphor is precise: chaff (பதர்) has the outward form of grain without its kernel, so the babbler has the form of a person without the substance that makes speech human. (One web edition prints ‘பராட்டு’ here, evidently a typo; the standard reading ‘பாராட்டு’ is followed.) The image anticipates later critiques of inauthentic ‘idle talk’, such as Heidegger’s Gerede, in which speech circulates without anyone really grasping what it is about.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrihari’s Nītiśataka says that a man without literature, music and the arts is a beast in plain sight, lacking only a tail and horns.
— Bhartrihari, Nītiśataka (verse ‘sāhitya-saṅgīta-kalā-vihīnaḥ’; numbering varies by edition) [3]
The poet argues that what makes someone fully human is cultivated inner substance, not mere bodily form.
Both texts deny full humanity to one who has the outward human form but lacks inner substance: the Kural calls him chaff, Bhartrihari a hornless beast.
Christianity
“The prophet that hath a dream, let him tell a dream; and he that hath my word, let him speak my word faithfully. What is the chaff to the wheat? saith the LORD.”
— Jeremiah 23:28 (KJV) [4]
God contrasts the empty words of false prophets (chaff) with the true word (wheat).
Both texts use chaff as the image for words without substance, and in both the speakers of such words are judged hollow.
Islam
“The most disliked among you to me, and the one sitting furthest from me on the Day of Judgement are the Thartharun, and the Mutashaddiqun and the Muthafaihiqun.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2018 (graded hasan, sunnah.com) [5]
Tirmidhi explains that al-tharthār is ‘the one who talks a great deal’. The prophet ranks such chatterers among those furthest from him.
Both texts assign the compulsive talker the lowest rank: chaff among men, or furthest from the Prophet.
Judaism
“Morning sleep, midday wine, children’s talk and sitting in the assemblies of the ignorant put a man out of the world.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:10 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Dosa ben Harkinas lists habits, including idle ‘children’s talk’, that drain a grown person’s life of substance.
Both texts see adult idle talk as a way of losing one’s standing as a full person.
Buddhism
“Sweep away the sweepings, throw away the trash, then remove the chaff: non-contemplatives who think they’re contemplatives.”
— Sutta Nipata 2.6, Dhammacariya Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells the Sangha to winnow out harsh-mouthed, deluded monks who have the outward form of monks but none of the substance.
Both texts call people chaff when they have the outward form (a man, a monk) without the inner grain.
Jainism
“He who is ignorant of the truth, egoistical, greedy, without self-discipline, and who talks loosely, is called ill-behaved and void of learning.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana counts loose talk among the marks that strip a monk of the name of a disciplined and learned person.
Valluvar refuses to call the man who parades empty words a real man and calls him chaff, and the Jain text likewise denies the loose talker the standing of a disciplined, learned person.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, those people are really donkeys; they have no virtue or merit, but still, they are very proud.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1411, Salok Vaaraan Thay Vadheek, Salok 15, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok, Guru Nanak refuses the dignity of the name human to people who show off with no virtue behind it, calling them donkeys.
The Kural will not call the man who delights in displaying empty words a real man, but the chaff among human beings; Guru Nanak likewise demotes the hollow show-off below the human.
Greek Mythology
Echo, punished by Juno for distracting her with endless chatter, loses her own speech and can only repeat others’ last words. Pining for Narcissus, she wastes away until nothing remains but her voice.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.356-401 [10]
A nymph whose talk had no purpose of its own is reduced to a voice with no substance.
Echo’s fate turns the Kural’s ‘chaff’ into a story: a person reduced to empty sound without a kernel.
Philosophy
Heidegger describes ‘idle talk’ (Gerede) as speech that passes along what ‘one says’ without any real grasp of its subject, and he links it to an inauthentic, ‘fallen’ mode of human existence.
— Martin Heidegger, Being and Time §35 (‘Idle Talk’) [11]
For Heidegger, chatter is a symptom of losing oneself in the anonymous ‘they’ (das Man), existing as a person without being truly oneself.
Heidegger’s idle talker, like the Kural’s ‘chaff among men’, has the form of a speaking human but is hollow at the core.

Real Life Example

The Sokal hoax
May 1996 · New York, United States
Physicist Alan Sokal of New York University submitted an article titled Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity to the cultural studies journal Social Text, which published it in its spring/summer 1996 issue without any review by a physicist. Shortly afterwards Sokal revealed in the magazine Lingua Franca that the article was a parody, deliberately filled with impressive-sounding jargon and nonsense. The affair set off a worldwide debate about standards of rigour in academic writing.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The hoax showed that language can have all the outward form of learned argument while containing nothing of substance.
The kural calls the person who shows off empty words the chaff of humanity, grain-shaped but without a kernel, and Sokal's article was built to be exactly such a husk, to show how easily it could pass for the real thing.
