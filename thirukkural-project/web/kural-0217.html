Kural 217

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

மருந்தாகித் தப்பா மரத்தற்றால் செல்வம் பெருந்தகை யான்கண் படின்.
Marundhaagith thappaa maraththatraal selvam Perundhagai yaankan padin.

Meaning

When riches belong to a truly good person, they are like a healing tree that always works, with every part of it used as medicine for others.

Elementary

Some trees, like the neem tree, can be used as medicine — the leaves, the bark, even the roots. A great-hearted person's money is like that tree: every part of it helps someone feel better.

University

Parimelazhagar spells out what thappaa (‘not failing’) excludes: a tree fails if it grows where it is hard to reach, if it is hidden, if it bears only in some seasons, or if it is useless — so the ideal benefactor is accessible, open, reliable, and helpful in every part. He adds that such a person relieves everyone's distress without regard to his own lack. Manakkudavar glosses thapputhal as ‘hiding’: the medicinal tree does not conceal itself from those who seek it. With this third image Parimelazhagar closes the triad on how the benefactor's wealth is used, escalating from water (sustenance) to fruit (nourishment) to medicine (healing).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Just see how these trees are maintaining every living entity! Their birth is successful. Their behavior is just like that of great personalities, for anyone who asks anything from a tree never goes away disappointed.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.22.33 [4]
Krishna praises trees for supporting all creatures, comparing them to great souls who never send a seeker away empty-handed.
Almost the Kural's image in reverse: the tree is like a great person, just as the great person's wealth is like the tree.
Christianity
“and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations.”
— Revelation 22:2 (KJV) [5]
In John's vision of the new Jerusalem, the tree of life bears fruit every month and its leaves heal the nations.
Shares the image of a tree whose parts are medicine for all people.
Islam
“Have you not considered how Allah presents an example, [making] a good word like a good tree, whose root is firmly fixed and its branches [high] in the sky? It produces its fruit all the time, by permission of its Lord.”
— Qur'an 14:24–25 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an compares a good word to a firmly rooted tree that yields fruit at all times.
Like the Kural's thappaa maram, the good tree never fails to give.
Judaism
“and the fruit thereof shall be for meat, and the leaf thereof for medicine.”
— Ezekiel 47:12 (KJV) [5]
Ezekiel foresees trees along a sacred river whose fruit feeds and whose leaves heal.
Shares the Kural's image of a tree whose parts serve as food and medicine for others.
Buddhism
“For all those ailing in the world, Until their every sickness has been healed, May I myself become for them The doctor, nurse, the medicine itself.”
— Shantideva, Bodhicaryavatara 3.7 (Padmakara Translation Group version) [7]
Shantideva's bodhisattva vows to become the very medicine that cures the world's sick.
Same idea as the Kural: the great-hearted person becomes medicine for all.
Jainism
“Charity (dāna) is the giving of one's objects to another for mutual benefit–anugraha.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.38 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Umāsvāti defines charity as giving one's own wealth so that both sides benefit: the giver gains merit (puṇya) and the receiver is helped toward right knowledge and the like.
Valluvar pictures a noble person's wealth as a healing tree that never fails others; the Jain definition of dāna also treats a good person's possessions as given out for others' benefit.
Sikhism
Britannica records that Guru Har Rai, the seventh Guru, had apparently once assisted the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh, possibly curing him of poisoning; the Guru consistently sought peaceful relations with the Mughal dynasty.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Har Rai (paraphrase) [9]
Sikh memory of Guru Har Rai includes his help in healing someone from outside the community.
The Kural likens the wealth of a noble person to a medicinal tree whose every part is a cure for others; Guru Har Rai's reported help to Dara Shikoh is an example of healing offered beyond one's own circle.
Greek Mythology
Chiron, the wise centaur renowned for his skill in healing, taught medicine to Asclepius; when wounded incurably by Heracles' arrow, he gave up his immortality, which passed to Prometheus.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5.4 and 3.10.3 [10]
Chiron is the mythic healer-teacher whose knowledge benefited humanity through his pupils, and who finally gave away even his immortality.
A noble figure whose whole being serves healing, close to the Kural's medicinal tree.
Philosophy
“no physician, in so far as he is a physician, considers his own good in what he prescribes, but the good of his patient”
— Plato, Republic I, 342d (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates argues that a true art, like medicine, serves the good of those it cares for, not the practitioner's own gain.
Like the Kural's healing tree, true skill or wealth exists for the benefit of others.

Real Life Example

Nicolas Rolin and Guigone de Salins found the Hotel-Dieu of Beaune
4 August 1443 (founded); opened 1 January 1452 · Beaune, Burgundy, France
In 1443 Nicolas Rolin, chancellor to Duke Philip the Good of Burgundy, and his wife Guigone de Salins founded the Hotel-Dieu at Beaune as a hospital for the poor, at a time when much of the town's population was destitute and the area had recently suffered an outbreak of plague. The building was dedicated on 31 December 1451 and received its first patients the next day, cared for by a community of nursing sisters, and Guigone de Salins continued to direct it after her husband's death until her own death in 1470. The hospital went on caring for patients for more than five centuries before becoming a museum.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The fortune of one of the most powerful men in Burgundy was turned into a hospital that served the sick poor for over five hundred years.
The kural compares the wealth of a noble person to a medicinal tree that never fails to heal, and the Hotel-Dieu turned the Rolins' wealth into healing for generation after generation.
