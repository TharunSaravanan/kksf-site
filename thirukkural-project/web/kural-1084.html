Kural 1084

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

கண்டார் உயிருண்ணும் தோற்றத்தால் பெண்டகைப் பேதைக்கு அமர்த்தன கண்.
Kandaar uyirunnum thottraththaal pendakaip pethaikku amarththana kan.

Meaning

This gentle, innocent girl has eyes that seem to drink the life of anyone who sees them. Her eyes do not match her soft nature.

Elementary

Think of a kitten with huge, shining eyes: it is small and gentle, yet one look can make everyone in the room stop what they are doing. The young man sees that the girl is shy and gentle, but her eyes are so powerful that they seem to belong to someone much fiercer.

University

Uyir-unnum (உயிருண்ணும்) means 'devouring life', and kandaar (கண்டார்) 'those who see', so her eyes have a look that eats the life of every viewer. Pethai (பேதை) here means an innocent young girl, not a 'fool', and pen-takai (பெண்டகை) is womanly grace. The key verb amarththana (அமர்த்தன) is read by Mu. Varadarasanar as 'were at variance with one another', i.e. the eyes contradict her gentle nature; Solomon Pappaiah adds that they 'war against' her character and understanding. Pope renders: 'In sweet simplicity, / A woman's gracious form hath she; / But yet those eyes, that drink my life, / Are with the form at strife!' Identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And as he gazed on that lady of sweet smiles. Nala's love increased, but desirous of keeping his truth, he suppressed his passion.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 55 (Nalopakhyana; tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
When Nala sees Damayanti, her sweet beauty stirs a powerful love that he must struggle to master.
As in the Kural, a gentle, smiling beauty has an overwhelming effect on the one who looks.
Christianity
“Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse; thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes, with one chain of thy neck.”
— Song of Solomon 4:9 (KJV) [4]
The lover says one look from the beloved's eyes has carried his heart away.
The Kural makes the same claim: her eyes take the viewer's very life.
Islam
“Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them.”
— Qur'an 24:30 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an tells believers to lower their gaze, recognising how much power the eyes have over the heart.
The Kural describes the force of a look; the Qur'an answers that force with restraint of the gaze.
Judaism
“Turn your eyes away from me, / For they overwhelm me!”
— Song of Songs 6:5 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover begs the beloved to look away because her eyes overpower him.
The Kural's eyes that 'drink life' are the same overwhelming eyes.
Buddhism
“The Destroyer brings under his sway the person of distracted mind who, insatiate in sense desires, only plucks the flowers (of pleasure).”
— Dhammapada 48 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada warns that the Destroyer (death) takes control of the person carried away by sense desire.
The Kural's 'life-devouring' eyes describe that same capture from inside the lover's experience.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of 'colours,' will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira's teaching says that passion for what the eye sees destroys a person the way a flame draws a moth to its death.
The Kural's eyes that 'drink the life' of the viewer are the flame, and the lover is the moth.
Sikhism
“The Bani of the Lord's Love is the pointed arrow, which has pierced my mind, O Lord King.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 449, Raag Asa, Chhant, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a chhant in Raag Asa, Guru Ram Das describes the Guru's word of divine love as a sharp-pointed arrow that has wounded his mind.
The Kural marvels that a gentle, innocent girl has eyes that seem to drink the life of anyone who sees them; Guru Ram Das likewise finds that something sweet, the word of love, wounds like a sharp arrow.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid's version, Medusa was once a beautiful maiden; after her transformation her gaze turned everyone who looked at her to stone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.770-803 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth makes a woman's eyes literally deadly to anyone who meets them.
The Kural's 'life-devouring' eyes are a playful version of the deadly gaze, set against the girl's gentleness.
Philosophy
In Plato's Symposium, Diotima describes Eros as a paradox: poor and soft in one way, yet a bold and mighty hunter who is always weaving snares.
— Plato, Symposium 203c-d (paraphrase) [11]
Plato sees love as made of opposites, gentle and fierce at once.
The Kural finds the same opposition in the girl, whose gentle nature contrasts with her conquering eyes.

Real Life Example

Vermeer paints Girl with a Pearl Earring
about 1665 · Delft, Dutch Republic (now the Netherlands)
Around 1665 Johannes Vermeer painted a young woman in a turban and a large pearl earring, turning to look out at the viewer with her lips softly parted. The Mauritshuis in The Hague explains that it is a tronie, a study of an imaginary figure rather than a portrait of a named person. Its simple setting, light and direct gaze have made it one of the most recognised paintings in the world.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The painting shows how a gentle, quiet figure can hold every viewer through the power of a look.
The kural speaks of a gentle, innocent girl whose eyes seem to seize all who see them; Vermeer's quiet young woman draws in viewers above all through her gaze.
