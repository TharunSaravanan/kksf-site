Kural 279

Inconsistent Conduct · Koodaavozhukkam · கூடாவொழுக்கம்

கணைகொடிது யாழ்கோடு செவ்விதுஆங் கன்ன வினைபடு பாலால் கொளல்.
Kanaikodidhu yaazhkodu sevvidhuaang kanna Vinaipadu paalaal kolal.

Meaning

An arrow is straight but does harm. A lute (a stringed instrument) is bent but makes sweet music. So judge people by what they do, not by how they look.

Elementary

An arrow looks perfectly straight, but it’s made for hurting. A guitar has a curvy neck, but it makes lovely music. So don’t decide who is good just by how they look — watch what they actually do.

University

Here Valluvar moves from condemnation to method: how can one tell the hypocrite from the saint? The paired images turn the usual metaphors upside down — கணை (arrow) is straight yet கொடிது (cruel), while யாழ்கோடு (the curved neck of the yazh, the ancient Tamil harp-lute) is bent yet செவ்விது (upright, good). Since செம்மை and கொடுமை mean both ‘straight/crooked’ and ‘good/cruel’, the kural exploits the double meaning to show that visual straightness and moral straightness are different things. The conclusion — வினைபடு பாலால் கொளல், ‘assess by the quality of deeds’ — is a pragmatic epistemology of character that parallels Jesus’ ‘by their fruits ye shall know them’ and the Yaksha’s answer that conduct, not birth or learning, makes a Brahmin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is neither birth, nor study, nor learning, that is the cause of Brahmanahood, without doubt, it is behaviour that constitutes it.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 311 (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Yudhishthira tells the Yaksha that a person is truly a Brahmin by conduct alone, not by birth or scholarship.
Same standard as the Kural: judge people by their actions, not by outward markers of status.
Christianity
“Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles?”
— Matthew 7:16 (KJV) [4]
Right after warning about wolves in sheep’s clothing, Jesus says people are known by what they produce.
Almost identical rule to the Kural’s: ignore the look, judge by the results.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth but He looks to your heart and to your deeds.”
— Sahih Muslim 2564c [5]
The Prophet says God judges by hearts and actions, not by appearance or riches.
Shares the Kural’s rejection of appearance as a measure; adds the heart alongside deeds.
Judaism
“Rabbi said: don’t look at the container but at that which is in it: there is a new container full of old wine, and an old [container] in which there is not even new [wine].”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 4:20 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah teaches that the vessel’s look tells you nothing about its contents.
Like the straight arrow and curved lute, the outward shape misleads; what matters is what the thing really holds and does.
Buddhism
“Even though he be well-attired, yet if he is poised, calm, controlled and established in the holy life, having set aside violence towards all beings — he, truly, is a holy man, a renunciate, a monk.”
— Dhammapada 142 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says someone dressed like an ordinary layperson can still be a true holy man if his conduct is right.
The ‘curved lute’ case: the wrong look, but the right deeds — judge by conduct.
Jainism
“By one's actions one becomes a Brâhmana, or a Kshattriya, or a Vaisya, or a Sûdra.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jayaghosha teaches that a person's real standing comes from conduct, not from birth or outward marks.
Valluvar says to judge people by their deeds and not by appearance, as the straight arrow is cruel and the bent lute sweet; the Jain verse likewise makes deeds, not outward signs, the measure of a person.
Sikhism
“The simmal tree is straight as an arrow; it is very tall, and very thick. … Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak notes that the silk-cotton tree is straight, tall and imposing, yet birds that come to it leave disappointed, and concludes that sweetness and humility are what make a person good.
Valluvar says the arrow is straight but cruel and the lute bent but sweet, so people must be judged by their deeds, not their looks; Guru Nanak's straight-as-an-arrow tree yields nothing, while humble sweetness is the real worth.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus returns to Ithaca, Athena disguises him as a ragged old beggar; the suitors mock and abuse him, but he is in fact the rightful king, revealed by his deeds when he strings the great bow.
— Homer, Odyssey 13 and 21 [10]
A shabby appearance hides a hero, whose true identity is shown by what he does.
The ‘bent lute’ half of the Kural: an unimpressive look that hides real worth, known only through action.
Philosophy
Alcibiades compares Socrates to the statues of Silenus sold in shops — grotesque figures that open up to reveal images of the gods inside — saying that Socrates’ ugly, satyr-like exterior hides divine virtue within.
— Plato, Symposium 215a–b, 216d–217a [11]
Plato presents Socrates as proof that an unappealing outside can contain the finest character.
Exactly the Kural’s curved lute: a crooked-looking form that produces something beautiful.

Real Life Example

Joseph Merrick, the Elephant Man, finds a home at the London Hospital
1886-1890 · London Hospital, Whitechapel, London, England
Joseph Merrick was born with severe physical deformities and was exhibited in a freak show before the surgeon Frederick Treves arranged for him to live at the London Hospital in 1886. Treves at first assumed from his appearance and difficult speech that he was mentally impaired, but came to recognise him as intelligent and sensitive; Merrick could read and write and enjoyed books. Public donations allowed him to remain at the hospital until his death in April 1890 at the age of 27.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Merrick's story is a lasting lesson that a person's appearance can mislead even an expert observer about the person within.
The kural says the bent-necked lute is sweet while the straight arrow is cruel, so people must be judged by their deeds and not their looks, and Merrick's life taught that lesson to Victorian London.
