Kural 1005

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

கொடுப்பதூஉம் துய்ப்பதூஉம் இல்லார்க்கு அடுக்கிய கோடியுண் டாயினும் இல்.
Kotuppadhooum Thuyppadhooum Illaarkku Atukkiya Kotiyun Taayinum Il

Meaning

For people who neither give their money away nor enjoy it themselves, even crores upon crores (huge piles of money) are as good as nothing.

Elementary

If you have a big box of crayons but never draw with them and never lend them to a friend, it is just like having no crayons at all. Valluvar says money works the same way: if you never use it and never share it, you are really poor.

University

The couplet names the two proper uses of wealth, koduppadhu (கொடுப்பது, 'giving') and thuyppadhu (துய்ப்பது, 'enjoying, consuming'), with the emphatic lengthening -ūum (கொடுப்பதூஉம், துய்ப்பதூஉம்) marking each as indispensable. Adukkiya kodi (அடுக்கிய கோடி) means 'crores stacked one upon another'; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses 'many crores ever increasing'. The verdict il (இல், 'there is not') says such people are effectively without wealth; Pope renders the sense as 'they are poor'. Solomon Pappaiah adds the qualifier that giving should be to those in need and enjoying should be as need requires, guarding against reading the couplet as licence for extravagance. The two-fold scheme parallels the Sanskrit gnomic triad of dāna, bhoga and nāśa (giving, enjoyment, destruction) found in Bhartrihari. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrihari's well-known stanza says that wealth has only three possible courses, giving, enjoyment and destruction; for the man who neither gives nor enjoys, the third course is what becomes of it.
— Bhartrihari, Nītiśataka (dānaṃ bhogo nāśas tisro gatayo bhavanti vittasya; verse numbering varies by edition) [3]
Wealth not given or used is bound simply to be lost.
This is the Kural's own scheme of giving and enjoying, with the same conclusion that otherwise the wealth amounts to nothing.
Christianity
Paul tells Timothy to charge the rich not to trust in uncertain riches but in God, who gives us richly all things to enjoy, and to be rich in good works, ready to distribute and willing to share.
— 1 Timothy 6:17–18 [4]
Wealth is meant both to be enjoyed with gratitude and to be shared generously.
It names the same two uses, enjoying and giving, that the Kural says give wealth its meaning.
Islam
“A servant says, My wealth. my wealth, but out of his wealth three things are only his: whatever he eats and makes use of or by means of which he dresses himself and it wears out or he gives as charity, and this is what he stored for himself (as a reward for the Hereafter), and what is beyond this (it is of no use to you) because you are to depart and leave it for other people.”
— Sahih Muslim 2959a (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that only what one consumes or gives away is truly one's own; the rest is merely left behind.
Exactly the Kural's teaching: wealth neither used nor given is as good as nothing to its owner.
Judaism
Proverbs observes that one person gives freely and yet grows richer, while another withholds more than is right and only comes to want.
— Proverbs 11:24 [6]
Withholding does not make one rich; generosity does.
The Kural likewise says the one who never gives is poor despite heaped-up crores.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells Anathapindika that rightly earned wealth should be used to bring pleasure to oneself, to parents, family and workers, to friends, to protect against losses, and to make offerings to relatives, guests, the dead, kings, devas and contemplatives.
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.41, Adiya Sutta [7]
Wealth's benefits lie in using it for one's own and others' good, not in storing it.
This is the Kural's pairing of enjoyment and giving as the purpose of riches.
Jainism
“Those who, though utterly poor, will not beg, are the truly rich. Even the rich are poor if they give not.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 27 ('Riches without goodness'), st. 10 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil quatrain redefines riches by conduct: the non-giver is poor whatever he owns.
It restates the Kural's verdict that crores amount to nothing for one who does not give.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Sarang, Sarang ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak names the right use of earnings: live on what you have earned by honest work and give part of it to others. This salok is the scriptural root of kirat karni (honest work) and vand chhakna (sharing).
The Kural names enjoying and giving as the two uses of wealth, without which crores are worth nothing; Guru Nanak names the same two, eating what one earns and giving some away.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop's fable a miser buries his gold and visits it daily; when a thief steals it and he laments, a neighbour advises him to bury a stone in its place, since it will serve him just as well as the gold he never used.
— Aesop's Fables, 'The Miser and His Gold' [10]
Wealth that is never used has no more value than a stone.
This is the Kural's point that unused, ungiven crores amount to nothing.
Philosophy
Aristotle remarks that being rich consists in using things rather than in owning them, since it is the actual use of possessions that constitutes wealth.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric I.5, 1361a23–24 [11]
Wealth is defined by its use, not by mere ownership.
The Kural likewise denies that unused, ungiven riches are wealth at all.

Real Life Example

Russell Sage dies with 70 million dollars after a lifetime of tight-fistedness
22 July 1906 · New York, United States
Russell Sage, a financier who helped organise American railroads and telegraphs and became a major Wall Street moneylender, died in 1906 with a fortune estimated at about 70 million dollars. He was known for his frugality, and over a marriage of 37 years he and his wife made only three major charitable gifts. His widow, Margaret Olivia Sage, then gave the fortune away, beginning with 10 million dollars in 1907 to establish the Russell Sage Foundation for the improvement of social and living conditions in the United States.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Sage's fortune did almost nothing for anyone in his own lifetime; it became a force for good only once it passed out of his hands.
The kural says that for those who neither give nor enjoy, wealth counts for nothing even if they own crores heaped upon crores, and Sage's millions achieved nothing until his widow gave them away.
