Kural 1123

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

கருமணியிற் பாவாய்நீ போதாயாம் வீழும் திருநுதற்கு இல்லை இடம்.
Karumaniyir paavaainee poadhaayaam veezhum thirunudharku illai idam.

Meaning

Little image in the pupil (the dark center) of my eye, go away! There is no room in there for the girl with the lovely forehead whom I love.

Elementary

When you look closely into someone's eye you can see a tiny picture of yourself in it. The poet tells that tiny picture to move out, because he wants his eye to hold only the person he loves.

University

The conceit rests on paavai (பாவை, 'doll, image'), the tiny reflected figure visible in the karumani (கருமணி, the dark pupil); the lover orders it to leave (poadhaai) because there is no idam (இடம், 'room') for thiru-nudhal (திருநுதல், 'she of the lovely brow'), a standard epithet for the heroine. The idea is that he wants to hold her image permanently in his sight. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar agree; Pope: 'For her with beauteous brow, the maid I love, there place is none; / To give her image room, O pupil of mine eye, begone!' Pulavar Kesikan's gloss adds 'there is no other place for her to stay', heightening the hyperbole. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Chandogya Upanishad, Prajapati first teaches that the person seen in the eye is the Self, a lesson Indra later finds incomplete.
— Chandogya Upanishad 8.7.4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. F. Max Müller, SBE 1) [3]
The figure reflected in the pupil was an old Indian puzzle, and the Upanishad uses it as a first step toward knowing the true Self.
The Kural plays with the same figure in the eye but wants to replace it with the beloved, turning a metaphysical image into a lover's one.
Christianity
“Keep me as the apple of the eye, hide me under the shadow of thy wings”
— Psalm 17:8 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist asks God to guard him as carefully as one guards the pupil of the eye.
Both texts treat the pupil as the most cherished place, the place where the dearest one is kept.
Islam
“Our Lord, grant us from among our wives and offspring comfort to our eyes”
— Qur'an 25:74 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an praises those who pray that their spouses and children be qurrat a'yun, a delight or coolness to their eyes.
The Kural's lover wants his eyes filled with his beloved; the Qur'an calls a spouse the comfort of one's eyes.
Judaism
“When our love was strong, we could have slept on a bed that was the width of a sword. Now that our love is not strong, a bed of sixty cubits is not sufficient for us.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 7a (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud records a popular saying about marriage: love makes the smallest space enough, and its lack makes any space too small.
The Kural's lover finds no room in his eye except for her; the Talmudic saying likewise measures love by how much space it needs.
Buddhism
In the Therigatha a man becomes infatuated with the nun Subha's eyes; she answers that the eye is only a ball of tissue and fluid and, to cure his obsession, plucks one out and gives it to him.
— Therigatha 14.1, Subha Jivakambavanika (paraphrase) [7]
The story uses the eye, the seat of attraction, to teach how infatuation fixes on a part of the body.
The Kural's lover fills his eye with his beloved; Subha's story treats such fixation as the illusion to be cut through.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of 'colours,' will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain sermon warns that the eye, captured by what it loves to see, leads the soul into ruin.
The Kural's lover wants his eye wholly taken up by the beloved's form; the Jain text calls that capture the moth's error.
Sikhism
“The crows have searched my skeleton, and eaten all my flesh. But please do not touch these eyes; I hope to see my Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 91) (BaniDB English translation) [9]
Sheikh Farid, wasted by longing, gives up his whole body to the crows but keeps his two eyes back, because they are reserved for the sight of the Beloved.
Like the Kural's lover who orders even the little image in his pupil to leave so that the eye holds only his beloved, Farid keeps his eyes for the Beloved alone.
Greek Mythology
Narcissus falls in love with the image he sees in a pool and cannot bear to leave it.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.339-510 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth turns on an image seen with the eyes that captures the viewer completely.
The Kural's lover banishes the image of himself from his pupil to make room for his beloved, exactly the reverse of Narcissus's fixation on himself.
Philosophy
Socrates points out that when a person looks into another's eye, his face is reflected in the pupil as in a mirror, which the Greeks call the 'kore', the girl or doll.
— Plato, Alcibiades I 132e-133a (paraphrase) [11]
Plato uses this to argue that the soul comes to know itself by looking into another soul.
The Greek kore and the Tamil paavai are the same 'little doll' in the eye that the Kural's lover tells to leave.

Real Life Example

The Prince of Wales sends Maria Fitzherbert a portrait of his eye
November 1785 · England and France
George, Prince of Wales (later King George IV), fell in love with Maria Fitzherbert, a twice-widowed Catholic, in 1784, but the law and his father barred him from marrying her, and she left for the Continent. In November 1785 he sent her a proposal accompanied not by a ring but by a miniature painting of his own eye, made by the miniaturist Richard Cosway. She returned, and in December 1785 they married in a secret ceremony that was not legally valid because it lacked royal consent.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The gift helped start a fashion for lover's eye miniatures, small portraits of a single eye worn as private love tokens.
The kural's lover tells the little image in his pupil to leave so that only his beloved may dwell in his eye; the prince built his love token on the same idea of the eye as love's dwelling place, sending his own eye so that his gaze would always be with her.
