Kural 1089

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

பிணையேர் மடநோக்கும் நாணும் உடையாட்கு அணியெவனோ ஏதில தந்து.
Pinaiyer madanokkum naanum udaiyaatku aniyevano ethila thanthu.

Meaning

She already has the gentle look of a deer and a natural shyness. What use are other jewels, which are not part of her, on a girl like her?

Elementary

A flower in a garden is already beautiful; you do not need to paint it or stick glitter on it. The young man feels the same about the girl: her kind eyes and shyness are her real jewels, so ordinary jewellery adds nothing.

University

Pinai-er (பிணையேர்) means 'like a doe', mada-nokku (மடநோக்கு) 'an innocent, gentle look', and naanum (நாணும்) 'and modesty'. Naanam (நாணம்) is one of the classical feminine virtues in Tamil poetics, alongside madam (innocence), accham (fear) and payirppu (delicacy). Ethila (ஏதில) is 'alien, unrelated', so other ornaments are foreign to her true beauty. Mu. Varadarasanar asks why one should put unrelated jewels on her; Kalaignar contrasts natural (iyarkai) and artificial (seyarkai) ornaments. Pope: 'Like tender fawn's her eye; / Clothed on is she with modesty; / What added beauty can be lent / By alien ornament?' Identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Act I of Kalidasa's Shakuntala, King Dushyanta notices that Shakuntala's rough bark garment does not dim her beauty, and reflects that a lovely form is adorned by anything, as a lotus stays lovely though covered with moss.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 1 (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa makes the point that true beauty needs no costly ornament.
The Kural asks the same question: what can outside ornaments add to natural grace?
Christianity
“Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; But let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price.”
— 1 Peter 3:3-4 (KJV) [4]
Peter teaches that a gentle, quiet spirit is the true ornament, worth more than gold or fine clothes.
The Kural, too, names gentleness and modesty as her real ornaments.
Islam
“O children of Adam, We have bestowed upon you clothing to conceal your private parts and as adornment. But the clothing of righteousness - that is best.”
— Qur'an 7:26 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an ranks the inner 'clothing' of righteousness above every outward garment and adornment.
The Kural ranks inner modesty above outward jewels in the same way.
Judaism
“Grace is deceptive, / Beauty is illusory; / It is for her fear of GOD / That a woman is to be praised.”
— Proverbs 31:30 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The poem of the 'woman of valour' places a woman's worth in her character rather than in outward charm.
The Kural, too, values her inner qualities over anything added from outside.
Buddhism
“Of all the fragrances — sandal, tagara, blue lotus and jasmine — the fragrance of virtue is the sweetest.”
— Dhammapada 55 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada ranks virtue above every costly perfume.
The Kural ranks modesty above costly ornaments in the same spirit.
Jainism
“The daughter of the best king, true to self-control and her vows, maintained the honour of her clan and family, and her virtue...”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; excerpt) [8]
Rajimati, praised earlier as beautiful, is finally honoured for her self-control and virtue.
The Kural, too, makes modesty and virtue a woman's chief adornment.
Sikhism
“Humility is the word, forgiveness is the virtue, and sweet speech is the magic mantra. Wear these three robes, O sister, and you will captivate your Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 127) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid tells the soul-bride that humility, forgiveness and sweet speech are the only dress she needs to win her Lord.
The Kural asks what use other ornaments are to a girl who already has a doe-like look and natural modesty; Farid likewise says inner virtues, not outward finery, are the bride's true adornment.
Greek Mythology
In Hesiod, the gods adorn the first woman, Pandora, with golden necklaces, a crown of flowers and fine clothing, yet her outward splendour hides the trouble she brings.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 60-82 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth sets dazzling outward adornment against what lies within.
The Kural reverses Pandora: her inner qualities are the real beauty and outward ornaments add nothing.
Philosophy
“We should, therefore, fix our attention on making them sensible that they are valued for the appearance of decent, modest and discreet behavior.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 40 (tr. Elizabeth Carter, as revised on classics.mit.edu) [11]
Epictetus objects that young women are taught to rely on adornment, and says they should know they are valued for modesty and good conduct.
The Kural, too, says modesty is worth more than any jewel.

Real Life Example

Marie Curie's plain wedding dress
July 1895 · France
When Marie Sklodowska married Pierre Curie in July 1895, they held a simple civil ceremony with no religious service, and they did not exchange rings. Instead of a white bridal gown she wore a dark blue outfit, chosen so that she could wear it afterwards in the laboratory.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The wedding is remembered as a sign of the Curies' simplicity and their devotion to each other and to their work.
The kural asks what use ornaments are to one who already has natural grace and modesty; Marie Curie married without a gown or rings and needed neither.
