Kural 463

Acting after Due Consideration · Therindhu Seyalvakai · தெரிந்து செயல்வகை

ஆக்கம் கருதி முதலிழக்கும் செய்வினை ஊக்கார் அறிவுடை யார்.
Aakkam karudhi mudhalizhakkum seyvinai, Ookkaar arivutai yaar.

Meaning

Wise people will not take on a project that risks losing the money they already have just in the hope of making more later.

Elementary

Smart people don’t risk everything they already have just because they hope to win something bigger. They protect what they have first.

University

The pairing ஆக்கம் (gain, increase) and முதல் (principal, capital) is commercial vocabulary and continues the ledger of 461. The verb ஊக்கார் (‘they will not be eager to / will not set about’) matters: Valluvar does not ban all risk, but says the wise will not be enthusiastic for ventures where the principal itself is at stake. This is a rule of prudence rather than timidity. It tells a ruler not to gamble the treasury or the kingdom’s security on speculative expansion. Later chapters praise energy and persistence (ஊக்கம், chapter 60), so the teaching is best read as ‘secure the base before seeking growth’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A saying attributed to Chanakya warns that whoever abandons what is certain in pursuit of what is uncertain loses the certain, and the uncertain is lost as well.
— Chanakya Niti 1.13 [4]
If you drop what you surely have to chase something you only might get, you can end up with nothing.
Almost exactly the Kural’s warning against losing capital while chasing gain.
Christianity
The thoughts of the diligent tend only to plenteousness; but of every one that is hasty only to want.
— Proverbs 21:5 (KJV) [5]
Careful planners end up with plenty, but people who rush after quick gain end up poor.
Like the Kural, it contrasts steady, careful increase with hasty ventures that lead to loss.
Islam
On wine and gambling (maysir) the Qur’an says there is great sin in them and some benefit for people, but their sin is greater than their benefit.
— Qur’an 2:219 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an admits gambling can bring some gain, but says the harm is bigger, so it should be given up.
Gambling is the clearest case of risking what one has for hoped-for profit, which the Kural tells the wise to avoid.
Judaism
Rabbi Isaac taught that a person should always divide his money into three parts: a third in land, a third in merchandise, and a third kept at hand.
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 42a [7]
The Talmud advises spreading your wealth so no single venture can wipe you out.
Protecting the principal while still seeking gain is the practical heart of the Kural.
Buddhism
The Buddha advises the householder Sigala to divide his wealth into four parts: one to enjoy, two to invest in his work, and the fourth to keep in reserve against misfortune.
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 [8]
Even the Buddha told lay people to save part of their money for hard times instead of putting it all at risk.
Keeping a reserve so that the principal survives is the Kural’s concern.
Jainism
“As a man for the sake of one Kâkinî (risks and) loses a thousand (Kârshâpaṇas), or as the king lost his kingdom (and life) by eating a mango-fruit which he was strictly forbidden (by his physician)”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Two short examples show the folly of staking something great for a trifling gain: a thousand coins thrown away for a copper, a kingdom lost for one forbidden mango.
The Kural warns the wise not to put their capital at risk for a hoped-for gain, and the Jain verse pictures exactly that bad bargain.
Sikhism
“The self-willed manmukhs lose their capital, and still, they demand profits. How can they earn any profit?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1131, Raag Bhairao, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das uses the trader's language of capital and profit: the self-willed squander their principal and still expect a return.
The Kural warns against losing one's capital in the hope of future profit; Guru Amar Das calls it folly to lose the capital and still expect a gain.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable, a dog carrying a piece of meat across a stream sees its reflection, snaps at the ‘bigger’ piece in the water, and drops the real one.
— Aesop’s Fables, ‘The Dog and Its Reflection’ (Perry 133) [11]
The greedy dog lost the meat it had by grabbing for a bigger piece that wasn’t real.
A vivid picture of losing capital while reaching for imagined gain.
Philosophy
“It is, therefore, only a madman who, in a calm, would pray for a storm; a wise man's way is, when the storm does come, to withstand it with all the means at his command, and especially, when the advantages to be expected in case of a successful issue are greater than the hazards of the struggle.”
— Cicero, De Officiis (On Duties), Book I, §83 (tr. Walter Miller (Loeb, 1913)) [12]
Cicero warns against exposing oneself to danger needlessly and says a risk is worth running only when the gain to be expected from success outweighs what is hazarded.
Shares the Kural's rule that the wise weigh what may be lost against what may be gained, and do not court ruin for the sake of a hoped-for profit.

Real Life Example

Long-Term Capital Management's leveraged bets collapse
1998 · New York, United States
The hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management earned thin margins on each trade and borrowed heavily to magnify them, holding about 30 dollars of debt for every dollar of its own capital by the end of 1997. After Russia's debt default in August 1998 set off market turmoil, the fund lost 44 percent of its value in that month alone. In September the Federal Reserve Bank of New York brought together fourteen banks and brokerages, which put about 3.6 billion dollars into the fund so that its holdings could be wound down in an orderly way.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The fund's pursuit of extra profit through borrowing put its own capital, and that of its lenders, at risk, and the losses came far faster than the gains had.
The kural says the wise will not chase future profit by risking the capital they already hold, which is exactly the risk that heavy borrowing created for this fund.
