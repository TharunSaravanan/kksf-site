Kural 381

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

படைகுடி கூழ்அமைச்சு நட்பரண் ஆறும் உடையான் அரசருள் ஏறு.
Padai kudi koozh amaichu natparan aarum Udaiyaan arasarul eru.

Meaning

A ruler who has all six parts of a strong country (an army, loyal people, plenty of food and money, ministers, friendly allies and strong forts) is like a lion among kings.

Elementary

Think of a strong table that needs all its legs to stand. A kingdom needs six “legs”: soldiers, happy people, food and money, good helpers, friends, and strong walls. A king who has all six is like the lion — the strongest of all the kings.

University

Valluvar opens the Book of Polity not with the king’s personality but with a structural inventory of the state: padai (army), kudi (citizenry), koozh (food/revenue), amaichu (ministry), natpu (alliances), and aran (fortification). This six-limbed model closely parallels the saptanga (“seven-limbed”) theory of the state found in Kautilya’s Arthashastra, minus the king himself — who in the Kural is the one who ‘possesses’ the six, making him the integrating seventh element. The image of the ‘eru’ (a male lion or bull, the pre-eminent male of its kind) frames kingship as excellence in a competitive field of rulers rather than divine right. The chapter structure of the Porutpaal then follows this list, with later chapters devoted to each limb (army, fortress, friendship, ministers), so this kural functions as a table of contents for the whole book of statecraft.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya lists the constituent elements of sovereignty as the king, the minister, the country, the fort, the treasury, the army, and the ally (Arthashastra 6.1, the saptanga theory).
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 6.1 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya’s classic Indian theory says a state is made of seven parts that must all be healthy for the kingdom to survive.
The Kural’s six possessions of a great king are almost exactly Kautilya’s list once the king himself is set aside.
Christianity
“In the multitude of people is the king’s honour: but in the want of people is the destruction of the prince.”
— Proverbs 14:28 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says a king’s glory depends on having many people under him; without them his rule collapses.
Matches the Kural’s ‘kudi’ (the people) as a core limb of royal greatness, though it names only one of the six elements.
Islam
The Qur’an instructs the community to prepare whatever strength and war-horses it can muster so that enemies are deterred (Qur’an 8:60).
— Qur’an 8:60 (Sahih International, paraphrased) [5]
Muslims are told to be ready with military strength so that enemies think twice about attacking.
Parallels the ‘padai’ (army) and ‘aran’ (defence) limbs of the Kural’s strong state.
Judaism
Solomon’s reign is described with an organised cabinet of officials (1 Kings 4:1-7), district officers supplying provisions (4:7, 4:22-23), vast stables of horses and chariots (4:26), and a treaty of friendship with Hiram of Tyre (5:12).
— 1 Kings 4-5 (KJV) [4]
The Bible pictures Solomon’s kingdom as great because it had ministers, food supplies, an army, and allies all at once.
Solomon’s kingdom is a narrative illustration of nearly every limb the Kural names: ministers, provisions, army, and alliance.
Buddhism
The wheel-turning monarch (cakkavatti) is said to possess seven ‘treasures’, including the elephant and horse treasures (military might), the householder treasure (wealth), and the counsellor treasure (wise minister) (e.g. Mahasudassana Sutta, DN 17).
— Digha Nikaya 17 (Mahasudassana Sutta) [6]
Buddhist texts imagine the ideal world-ruler as owning seven special treasures that make his rule complete.
Like the Kural, it defines the supreme king by a checklist of possessions — army, wealth, and counsel among them.
Jainism
“As a universal monarch with his fourfold army and great power, the possessor of the fourteen attributes of a king, (has no equal), neither has a very learned monk.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.22 (cf. 11.20) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The Uttaradhyayana uses the cakravartin (universal monarch), with his fourfold army and fourteen royal treasures, as a byword for unmatched sovereign strength. A few verses earlier (11.20) it uses the other image found in this kural: the proud lion that is 'superior to all animals'.
Valluvar's king with all six limbs of state stands among rulers like a lion among beasts. The Jain text draws on the same picture of the fully equipped monarch who has no equal, though it uses it to praise the learned monk.
Sikhism
After the martyrdom of his father Guru Arjan, Guru Hargobind took up two swords at his succession, standing for temporal (miri) and spiritual (piri) authority. He trained in arms, built up a Sikh army, fortified his towns, built the Lohgarh fort near Amritsar, and in 1609 founded the Akal Takht as a seat where both the spiritual and the temporal affairs of the community could be settled.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Hargobind' (paraphrase) [8]
Guru Hargobind gave the Sikh community the means of a self-governing body, with an army, fortifications and a seat of authority, alongside its spiritual life.
The Kural lists army, people, resources, counsel, allies and fortification as the parts of a strong state; Guru Hargobind's miri-piri reforms gave the young Panth several of these same limbs.
Greek Mythology
In Hesiod’s account, Zeus wins and keeps his kingship over the gods only by gathering allies — the Cyclopes who forge his thunderbolt and the Hundred-Handed ones who fight beside him against the Titans.
— Hesiod, Theogony 139-141, 501-506, 617-735 [9]
Even Zeus needed weapons, fighters, and friends to become and stay king of the gods.
Illustrates the Kural’s point that a ruler’s supremacy rests on army and allies, not on personal power alone.
Philosophy
“First, there must be food; secondly, arts, for life requires many instruments; thirdly, there must be arms … fourthly, there must be a certain amount of revenue, both for internal needs, and for the purposes of war; fifthly, or rather first, there must be a care of religion … sixthly, and most necessary of all …”
— Aristotle, Politics, Book VII, ch. 8 (1328b) (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Setting out what a state cannot exist without, Aristotle lists six requisites, among them food, arms, revenue and, last and most necessary, a power of deciding what is just and expedient among citizens.
Like Valluvar’s six limbs of the state, Aristotle’s list gives six things a polity must have, including the army and the treasury, though the two lists differ item by item.

Real Life Example

R. Shamasastry rediscovers Kautilya's Arthashastra
1905 · Mysore (Mysuru), Karnataka, India
In 1905 the Sanskrit scholar Rudrapatna Shamasastry, working as a librarian at the Oriental Library in Mysore, identified a manuscript of the Arthashastra, the ancient Indian treatise on statecraft attributed to Kautilya, which had been known only through later references and was thought to be lost. He published the Sanskrit text in 1909 and an English translation in 1915. The publication brought the treatise to the attention of scholars around the world.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The rediscovery gave historians a detailed ancient Indian account of how a state should be built and run, including its army, revenue, ministers, alliances and fortifications. It transformed the study of early Indian political thought.
The kural lists the parts a ruler must possess, army, people, resources, ministers, allies and forts, and the rediscovered Arthashastra is the classic Indian treatise that analyses the state through closely similar elements.
