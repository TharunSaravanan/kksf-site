Kural 872

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

வில்லேர் உழவர் பகைகொளினும் கொள்ளற்க சொல்லேர் உழவர் பகை.
Viller uzhavar pakaikolinum kollarka soller uzhavar pakai.

Meaning

Even if you make enemies of soldiers who use the bow as their plow, never make enemies of learned people who use words as their plow.

Elementary

A bully with big muscles can only hurt you while he is near you. But a clever person who writes and speaks well can tell your story to the whole town and to people years from now. So the poet says: if you must quarrel with anyone, never quarrel with the people who are good with words.

University

The couplet is built on a celebrated agricultural metaphor: villēr uḻavar (வில்லேர் உழவர்) are ‘ploughmen whose plough is the bow’, that is, warriors, while collēr uḻavar (சொல்லேர் உழவர்) are ‘ploughmen whose plough is the word’. Mu. Varadarasanar reads the latter as scholars, Solomon Pappaiah as writers, Kalaignar as learned people of verbal power; the older commentarial tradition, reflected in Pope’s gloss ‘ministers whose ploughs are words’, takes them as counsellors skilled in policy. The concessive kolinum (‘even if you incur’) does not recommend war with soldiers; it ranks dangers, implying that the harm of hostile counsel, satire or reputation outlasts and outreaches the harm of arms. The metaphor also suggests that both kinds of men ‘cultivate’ a harvest, and the harvest of words is wider. No variant readings appear between ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Parikshit, irritated while hunting, draped a dead snake on the silent sage Shamika; the sage’s young son Shringi, hearing of the insult, pronounced a curse that the king would die by the serpent Takshaka within seven days, and it came to pass.
— Bhagavata Purana 1.18 [3]
A mighty king who could command armies was undone by the spoken word of a sage’s son.
It dramatizes the Kural’s ranking: the enmity of those whose weapon is the word is more dangerous than that of armed men.
Christianity
“Even so the tongue is a little member, and boasteth great things. Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth!”
— James 3:5 (KJV) [4]
James warns that the tongue, though small, can set a whole forest ablaze.
Both texts recognise that words can do more damage than their size suggests.
Islam
“Satirise against the (non-believing amongst the) Quraish, for (the satire) is more grievous to them than the hurt of an arrow.”
— Sahih Muslim 2490 [5]
The Prophet asked the poet Hassan ibn Thabit to answer the Quraysh in verse, saying satire wounded them more than arrows.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s comparison: the word cuts deeper than the bow.
Judaism
Proverbs teaches that death and life are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit.
— Proverbs 18:21 [4]
Speech is presented as carrying literal life-and-death power.
The Kural’s caution about enemies who wield words rests on the same recognition.
Buddhism
“Speak not harshly to anyone, for those thus spoken to might retort. Indeed, angry speech hurts, and retaliation may overtake you.”
— Dhammapada 133 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha warns that hostile speech provokes hostile speech in return and brings harm back on the speaker.
Provoking those who answer in words invites a retaliation the Kural warns is especially costly.
Jainism
“You may as well dig rocks with your nails, or eat iron with your teeth, or kick fire with your feet, as treat contemptuously a monk.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
When the priests' young men beat the sage Harikeśa, Bhadrā warns them that to make an enemy of a holy man of disciplined mind is like attacking rock, iron and fire: only the attacker is hurt.
The Kural warns never to make enemies of those whose plough is the word, and Bhadrā's warning likewise makes the wise sage the one opponent it is ruinous to provoke.
Sikhism
“Slandering the Saints, one’s life is cut short.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan warns that turning against the holy by slandering them shortens one's very life.
The Kural warns never to make enemies of the wise whose plough is the word; Guru Arjan likewise warns that turning against the saints, people of the Word, brings ruin on oneself.
Greek Mythology
After Achilles’ death, Ajax the mighty warrior and Ulysses the eloquent speaker both claimed his armour; the Greek leaders, swayed by Ulysses’ speech, awarded it to him, and the power of eloquence was shown to outweigh strength of arm.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 13.1-398 [9]
Ovid stages a contest between the man of the sword and the man of words, and the man of words wins.
It matches the Kural’s point that the one who ploughs with words is the more formidable opponent.
Philosophy
At his trial Socrates says he fears his old accusers, including a comic poet, more than his present prosecutors, because their talk had filled the jurors’ minds with slander since childhood.
— Plato, Apology 18a-d [10]
Socrates recognised that reputations shaped by skilled talkers and writers are harder to fight than any formal charge.
It illustrates the Kural’s warning that the enmity of wordsmiths is the most damaging kind.

Real Life Example

Voltaire's campaign for Jean Calas
1762-1765 · Toulouse and Ferney, France
In March 1762 the Parlement of Toulouse condemned and executed the Huguenot merchant Jean Calas, convicted of murdering his son to stop him converting to Catholicism. Voltaire took up the case and, through a vigorous campaign of letters and pamphlets, including his Treatise on Tolerance of 1763, convinced much of European opinion that the judges had been swayed by anti-Protestant prejudice. A 50-judge royal panel reversed the conviction on 9 March 1765 and the government paid the family an indemnity.
— Sources: [11] [12]
One writer armed only with a pen discredited a powerful court before all of Europe and helped launch the movement for religious toleration and legal reform in France.
The Toulouse judges learned that the hostility of a man whose plough is the word reaches further and lasts longer than that of any warrior.
