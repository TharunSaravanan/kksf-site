Kural 332

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

கூத்தாட்டு அவைக்குழாத் தற்றே பெருஞ்செல்வம் போக்கும் அதுவிளிந் தற்று.
Koothaattu avaikkuzhaath thatre perunjelvam Pokkum adhu vilindhatru.

Meaning

Great riches come together like a crowd gathering to watch a show, and they leave like that crowd going home when the show is over.

Elementary

Think of people crowding in to watch a street show. When the show ends, everyone walks away at once and the place is empty. This kural says money is like that crowd: it comes in bit by bit, and it can leave all at once.

University

Valluvar builds the couplet on a single theatrical simile with two halves: the assembling of an audience (koothaattu avaik kuzhaam) and its dispersal when the performance ends (adhu vilindhatru). Commentators note the asymmetry the image implies: an audience trickles in gradually but leaves together, just as wealth is accumulated slowly and can vanish in a single reversal. The theatre is also suggestive, since the crowd was never attached to the patron but to the spectacle, much as wealth is never truly bound to its holder. The kural therefore relocates wealth from the category of possession to that of event, something that happens around a person for a time. Comparable ideas appear in the Greek theme of the reversal of fortune (Solon and Croesus) and in the Stoic teaching that external goods are on loan.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Offered wealth, long life, horses, and dancing and singing by Yama, the boy Nachiketas refuses, saying such things last only until tomorrow and wear out the senses, and tells Death to keep his dances and songs.
— Katha Upanishad 1.1.26-27 [3]
In the Katha Upanishad, a young seeker rejects every worldly prize because it is short-lived and asks instead for knowledge of what lies beyond death.
Nachiketas sees wealth and entertainment for what the Kural says they are, a show that ends and a crowd that disperses.
Christianity
“Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy;”
— 1 Timothy 6:17 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Timothy to warn the rich not to rely on wealth, because riches are uncertain.
‘Uncertain riches’ is the Kural's point in a phrase: wealth can leave as quickly as a crowd after a show.
Islam
“Know that the life of this world is but amusement and diversion and adornment and boasting to one another and competition in increase of wealth and children - like the example of a rain whose [resulting] plant growth pleases the tillers; then it dries and you see it turned yellow; then it becomes [scattered] debris.”
— Quran 57:20 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran compares worldly life and the race for wealth to entertainment and to crops that flourish and then dry up and scatter.
Both texts use the language of amusement and spectacle, and both say the gathering of wealth ends in scattering.
Judaism
“Wilt thou set thine eyes upon that which is not? for riches certainly make themselves wings; they fly away as an eagle toward heaven.”
— Proverbs 23:5 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns against fixing your gaze on riches, which suddenly grow wings and fly away.
The Kural's crowd scatters and Proverbs' riches fly off; both describe how suddenly wealth can leave.
Buddhism
“One who looks upon the world as a bubble and a mirage, him the King of Death sees not.”
— Dhammapada 170 [6]
The Buddha teaches seeing the world, and the things in it, as a bubble or mirage: real enough to appear, too fleeting to hold.
A bubble and a theatre crowd are both brief gatherings that vanish; the wise, in both texts, are not taken in by them.
Jainism
“Then at one time, his manifold savings are a large treasure. Then at another time, his heirs divide it, or those who are without a living steal it, or the king takes it away, or it is ruined in some way or other, or it is consumed by the conflagration of the house.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.2.3.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [7]
The Ācārāṅga describes wealth that accumulates into a great treasure and then scatters through heirs, thieves, the king, ruin or fire.
The Kural likens wealth to a crowd that gathers for a show and disperses when it ends; the Ācārāṅga traces the same gathering and dispersal of riches.
Sikhism
“That wealth, which he believes to be his own, in an instant, belongs to another.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1008, Raag Maaroo, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Tegh Bahadur observes how quickly possessions that seem securely one's own pass to someone else.
The Kural pictures great wealth arriving like a gathering crowd and leaving like the crowd after the show; Guru Tegh Bahadur makes the same point about how suddenly wealth departs.
Greek Mythology
The Athenian sage Solon refused to call the fabulously rich King Croesus the happiest of men, saying no one should be called happy until his life has ended well. Croesus later lost his kingdom to Cyrus and, on the pyre, cried out Solon's name.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.30-33, 1.86 [9]
Herodotus' story shows the richest man in the world losing everything in a single reversal.
Croesus' treasure gathered over a lifetime and scattered in a day, just as the Kural's crowd leaves when the show ends.
Philosophy
“Never say of anything, "I have lost it"; but, "I have returned it." ... Is your estate taken away? Well, and is not that likewise returned?”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 11 (Elizabeth Carter translation) [10]
Epictetus teaches that property is never truly ours: it is lent for a while and eventually given back.
If wealth is only on loan, its leaving is as natural as an audience going home after the show, which is the Kural's point.

Real Life Example

John Law's Mississippi Bubble bursts
1720 · Paris, France
The Scottish financier John Law founded a note-issuing bank in Paris and merged it with the company holding France's trading rights in the Mississippi Valley; as controller general of finances in 1720 he presided over intense speculation that drove the company's shares from about 500 livres to many times that value. In the summer of 1720, as shareholders rushed to turn their paper into gold and silver, share prices collapsed and the bank notes lost their value. Law, politically isolated, left France in December 1720.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Fortunes built up over months of speculation vanished within weeks once the crowd of investors turned and left together. The crash destroyed public confidence in paper money and companies in France for a generation.
The kural compares great wealth to a theatre crowd that gathers gradually and scatters all at once when the show ends, just as the Mississippi investors poured in and then fled together.
