Kural 140

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

உலகத்தோடு ஒட்ட ஒழுகல் பலகற்றும் கல்லார் அறிவிலா தார்.
Ulagaththoadu otta ozhugal pala-katrum Kallaar arivilaa thaar.

Meaning

People who have not learned to live in step with the world are still unlearned and foolish, no matter how much they have studied.

Elementary

Someone could read every book in the library, but if they cannot get along with people, share, or be polite, they have not really learned the most important lesson.

University

The chapter closes on a paradox: pala-katrum kallaar, ‘having learned much, they have not learned’. What they lack is ulagaththoadu otta ozhugal, conduct that ‘sticks to’ or fits with the world. Commentators differ on what ‘the world’ means. Parimelazhagar and Varadarasanar take it as the worthy (uyarndhor). Pappaiah boldly reads it as adapting the old ethical texts to present circumstances and dropping what no longer fits, which turns the couplet into almost a charter for moral reasoning over rote rule-following. On any reading, social wisdom is the test of learning. This echoes the rabbinic principle that derech eretz (right conduct) comes before Torah, and Aristotle's scorn for people who ‘take refuge in theory’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Professors and pupils, in fact, all who study the scriptures, if addicted to wicked habits, are to be regarded as illiterate wretches. He only is learned who performeth his religious duties.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 311 (Yaksha Prashna), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Yudhishthira tells the Yaksha that scholars without good conduct are really unlearned, and that only right action makes a person learned.
This is nearly identical to the Kural's ‘having learned much, they have not learned’.
Christianity
“Ever learning, and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth.”
— 2 Timothy 3:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes people who keep studying but never reach real understanding.
It captures the Kural's paradox of much learning without true knowledge.
Islam
“The example of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not take it on is like that of a donkey who carries volumes [of books].”
— Qur'an 62:5 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an compares those who carry scripture without living by it to a donkey loaded with books.
This is the same point as the Kural: knowledge carried but not lived counts as no knowledge at all.
Judaism
“Where there is no Torah, there is no right conduct; where there is no right conduct, there is no Torah.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:17 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah makes learning and derech eretz (proper conduct in the world) depend on each other.
Both hold that learning without right conduct in the world is empty.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares the learned person who does not practice to a herdsman counting cattle he does not own.
This is the Kural's paradox exactly: much learning, no real gain.
Jainism
“Acknowledging the truth about bondage and liberation, but talking only, not acting (in accordance with these tenets), they seek comfort for themselves in mighty words. Clever talking will not work salvation; how should philosophical instruction do it? Fools, though sinking lower and lower through their sins, believe themselves to be wise men.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.8–10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Mahāvīra condemns those who know the doctrine but do not live it, calling them fools who think themselves wise.
Valluvar says those who have learned much but cannot live in harmony with the world are ignorant; the Jain verse likewise judges learning without matching conduct to be no wisdom at all.
Sikhism
“If an educated person is a sinner, then the illiterate holy man is not to be punished.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that learning counts for nothing at the reckoning; a learned wrongdoer is judged by his deeds, while an unlettered good person is safe.
The Kural says the much-read who do not live well with the world remain ignorant; Guru Nanak likewise refuses to let book-learning stand in for right living.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod names Eunomia (Good Order), with her sisters Dike (Justice) and Eirene (Peace), as a daughter of Zeus and Themis. She personifies the lawful harmony that holds human communities together.
— Hesiod, Theogony 901–903 [10]
For the Greeks, living in well-ordered harmony with the community was itself a divine gift.
This loosely matches the Kural's ideal of conduct in harmony with the world.
Philosophy
“But most people do not do these, but take refuge in theory and think they are being philosophers and will become good in this way, behaving somewhat like patients who listen attentively to their doctors, but do none of the things they are ordered to do.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2.4, 1105b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle mocks people who think that studying ethics makes them good without practising it.
Both hold that learning without conduct is not real knowledge.

Real Life Example

Ignaz Semmelweis fails to win over his colleagues
1847-1861 · Vienna, Austria, and Pest, Hungary
In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, a doctor at the maternity clinic of the Vienna General Hospital, required staff to wash their hands in a chlorinated lime solution before examining patients, and deaths from childbed fever fell sharply. Many colleagues rejected his explanation, partly because it implied that doctors themselves were carrying the disease to their patients. He set out his findings in a full book only in 1861, and after unfavourable reviews he answered his critics with harshly worded open letters; his doctrine was generally accepted only later.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Semmelweis was right on the science, yet his discovery saved far fewer lives in his own time than it might have, in part because he could not bring the medical world along with him.
The kural says that those who cannot live in harmony with the world remain ignorant however much they have learned; Semmelweis's case shows how knowledge without the skill of persuading others can fail to do its good.
