Kural 1228

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

அழல்போலும் மாலைக்குத் தூதாகி ஆயன் குழல்போலும் கொல்லும் படை.
Azhalpoalum maalaikkuth thoodhaaki aayan kuzhalpoalum kollum padai.

Meaning

Evening burns like fire, and the cowherd's flute is its messenger. Even that flute has become a weapon that kills me.

Elementary

Sometimes a song you used to love can make you sad if it reminds you of someone who is far away. The herdsman plays his flute every evening; it used to sound sweet to the wife, but now it tells her that the lonely evening is coming, so it hurts to hear.

University

At dusk the herdsman (āyaṉ) plays his flute (kuḻal) as he brings the cattle home, and in classical Tamil poetics this belongs to the mullai landscape, whose mood is patient waiting (iruttal) and whose hour is evening. The flute becomes the tūtu (herald, envoy) of a mālai that burns like aḻal (fire), and so a paṭai (weapon) that kills; the repeated pōlum is read by the commentators as a particle of emphasis (‘the flute too’), not as ‘like’. Solomon Pappaiah notes that the flute once sounded sweet, and Karunanidhi likens its notes to the clang of a weapon. Pope: ‘The shepherd’s pipe is like a murderous weapon, to my ear, / For it proclaims the hour of ev’ning’s fiery anguish near.’ Both web sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Venu-gita, the gopis of Vraja, hearing the flute that the cowherd Krishna plays as he wanders with the cows, are so overwhelmed by longing that they can only describe to one another, in agitation, the power of its music.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.21 (Venu-gita) (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata makes the cowherd’s flute the very sound of love’s longing for those separated from him.
The Kural’s āyaṉ kuḻal is exactly a cowherd’s flute; for the gopis as for the wife, its music stirs unbearable longing.
Christianity
“My harp also is turned to mourning, and my organ into the voice of them that weep.”
— Job 30:31 (KJV) [4]
Job laments that the instruments which once gave him joy now sound only grief.
The herdsman’s flute, once sweet to the Kural’s wife, has likewise turned into a voice of mourning, even a weapon.
Islam
“HEARKEN to the reed-flute, how it complains, Lamenting its banishment from its home: ‘Ever since they tore me from my osier bed, My plaintive notes have moved men and women to tears.’”
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book 1, Prologue (tr. E.H. Whinfield, 1898) [5]
Rumi opens the Masnavi with the reed flute whose music is a lament of separation, read in Sufi tradition as the soul’s longing for God.
The Kural’s flute and Rumi’s reed both sing of separation and wound those who hear it.
Judaism
“Upon the willows in the midst thereof We hanged up our harps. For there they that led us captive asked of us words of song, And our tormentors asked of us mirth: ‘Sing us one of the songs of Zion.’ How shall we sing the LORD’S song In a foreign land?”
— Psalms 137:2-4 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The exiles by the rivers of Babylon find that music, once joyful, has become unbearable in separation from home.
For the separated wife too, music that once delighted now only deepens her pain.
Buddhism
The Buddha’s half-brother Nanda, ordained as a monk, grows discontented and wants to return to lay life because he keeps remembering the parting words of his beautiful Sakyan bride, who asked him to hurry back.
— Udana 3.2 (Nanda Sutta) (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Udana shows how a single remembered sound of the beloved can keep longing alive and painful.
The flute’s notes act on the Kural’s wife as the bride’s parting words act on Nanda, reviving longing each time they are recalled.
Jainism
“As an impassioned deer allured (by a song) rushes into death, without being satisfied with the sound.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; given in Jacobi’s summary note to verses 35-47) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana compares one enslaved by the sense of hearing to a deer drawn to its death by music.
The Kural’s wife finds the herdsman’s music literally a ‘killing weapon’; the Jain verse uses the same image of a song that leads to death.
Sikhism
“Blessed, blessed is that flute which the Lord plays.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 988, Raag Maalee Gauraa, Bhagat Namdev (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Namdev celebrates the flute of the Lord, the pastoral image of Krishna, as the sound of divine sweetness.
In the Kural the herdsman's flute at dusk heralds the burning evening and wounds the lonely wife; in Namdev the cowherd's flute is a blessing, so the two use the same instrument with opposite feeling.
Greek Mythology
The nymph Syrinx flees the god Pan and is turned into marsh reeds; as Pan sighs over them, the wind in the reeds makes a soft, mournful sound, and he binds reeds of different lengths into the pipe that bears her name.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.689-712 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid tells how the shepherd’s pipe was born from a love that ended in loss.
The herdsman’s pipe in the Kural also carries the sound of love’s loss, which is why it wounds the waiting wife.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates proposes to banish the dirge-like musical modes, such as the Mixolydian, because music of lament makes the listener’s soul sorrowful and soft.
— Plato, Republic 398d-399a (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato holds that musical modes have direct power over the emotions of those who hear them.
The Kural shows that power at its extreme: the flute’s evening music can wound the grieving wife like a weapon.

Real Life Example

Taps is first sounded at Harrison's Landing
July 1862 · Harrison's Landing, Virginia, United States
In July 1862, after the Seven Days battles, Union Brigadier General Daniel Butterfield had his brigade bugler Oliver W. Norton sound a new call in place of the usual Lights Out at the end of the day. The call, now known as Taps, spread through the Army of the Potomac and was soon being played at military funerals, and Army regulations made it mandatory at funerals in 1891. Some scholars think Butterfield revised an older call rather than composing it from scratch.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A simple tune that marked the close of each day became, almost at once, the sound of mourning and loss.
The kural's herdsman's flute, which announces the evening, becomes a weapon that wounds the lonely wife, just as Taps, the bugle call of nightfall, became the call that announces grief.
