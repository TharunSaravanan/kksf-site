Kural 1202

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

எனைத்தொன்று இனிதேகாண் காமம்தாம் வீழ்வார் நினைப்ப வருவதொன்று இல்.
Enaiththondru inidhekaan kaamamdhaam veezhvaar ninaippa varuvadhondru il.

Meaning

Love is sweet in every way. When you think of the one you love, no pain of being apart comes to you.

Elementary

When your best friend moves away, you miss them. But when you think about the funny games you played together, the sad feeling goes away for a while. The poet says love is like that: just thinking of the person you love can chase the sadness off.

University

The couplet explains why love is sweet ‘in every way’ (eṉaittoṉṟu): to think of those one desires (tām vīḻvār) brings no suffering (varuvatu oṉṟu il), so love delights both in union and in separation; Parimelazhagar, who treats it as a continuation of the previous couplet’s speaker, says Valluvar called it sweet ‘in any measure’ because it is equally sweet when lovers are together and apart. Manakkudavar and Parip Perumal instead make it the heroine’s rejoinder to a confidante who warns that her pining will harm her. There is a genuine textual variant: Perundevanar, commenting on the Virasoliyam, quotes the second line as niṉaikka varuvatu oṉṟu uṇṭu (‘there is something that comes when one thinks’), whereas Parimelazhagar reads niṉaippa varuvatu oṉṟu il, the standard text (as given on kuralthiran.com). Both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print the head-word with typographical slips (eṉaittoṉaṟu, and ēl for il), which are corrected here against kuralthiran.com and the commentaries’ glosses.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gopi Gita the cowherd women, searching for the vanished Krishna, tell him that the nectar of his words and of stories about him gives life to those burning with separation and removes their sins.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.31.9 (Gopi Gita) (paraphrase; cf. vedabase.io) [4]
The gopis find that dwelling on Krishna, through his words and his stories, revives them even while he is absent.
The Kural’s claim that thinking of the beloved banishes separation’s pain is exactly the gopis’ comfort.
Christianity
“My soul shall be satisfied as with marrow and fatness; and my mouth shall praise thee with joyful lips: When I remember thee upon my bed, and meditate on thee in the night watches.”
— Psalm 63:5-6 (KJV) [5]
The psalmist, far from the sanctuary, finds his soul satisfied simply by remembering God through the night.
As in the Kural, remembrance of the one loved turns absence into satisfaction instead of pain.
Islam
“Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of Allāh. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allāh hearts are assured.”
— Qur’an 13:28 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an teaches that hearts find rest and assurance in remembering God (dhikr).
The Kural finds that remembering the beloved leaves no room for pain; the Qur’an finds the same calming power in remembering God.
Judaism
“And Jacob served seven years for Rachel; and they seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her.”
— Genesis 29:20 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [7]
Jacob’s love for Rachel made seven years of labour and waiting feel like a few days.
Jacob shows the Kural’s truth that love takes the sting out of waiting and separation.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells his monks that if fear or terror arises when they are alone in the forest, they should recollect him, the Teacher, and their fear will vanish, just as Sakka’s warriors lose fear by looking at their king’s banner.
— Dhajagga Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 11.3 (paraphrase) [8]
Recollection of the Buddha (buddhanussati) is taught as a remedy that dispels fear and distress when one is alone.
As in the Kural, calling to mind one who is dear removes the pain of being alone, though the Buddhist object is the Teacher, not a lover.
Jainism
Manatunga tells the Jina that, let alone hymns of praise, even the telling of his story destroys the sins of living beings, as the sun’s light, though far away, opens lotuses in the pond.
— Manatunga, Bhaktamara Stotra, verse 9 (paraphrase) [9]
The Bhaktamara Stotra teaches that merely calling the Jina to mind, through his story, has a cleansing, healing effect.
The Kural’s comfort in thinking of the beloved has a Jain devotional echo in the healing power of remembering the Jina, though the Jina neither returns nor responds.
Sikhism
“Remembering Him in meditation, happiness comes, and all sorrows and pains simply vanish.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 518, Raag Goojaree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan teaches that simran, remembering the Lord, brings joy and makes all pain disappear.
The Kural says love is sweet in every way because thinking of the beloved brings no pain at all; Gurbani likewise says that remembering the Beloved drives every sorrow away.
Greek Mythology
Sappho recalls a girl who wept as she left her, and answers her farewell by telling her to go happily and remember, reminding her of the garlands, perfumes and soft beds they once shared.
— Sappho, fragment 94 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Anne Carson) [11]
Sappho uses shared memories as the consolation for parting.
The Kural, too, offers memory of the loved one as the cure for separation’s pain.
Philosophy
Dying in great pain, Epicurus writes to Idomeneus that against all his suffering he set the gladness of mind that came from remembering their past conversations.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 10.22 (letter of Epicurus to Idomeneus) (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.D. Hicks) [12]
Epicurus held that recollected joys can outweigh present pain.
The Kural’s speaker uses the same remedy: the memory of the beloved leaves no room for the pain of separation.

Real Life Example

John and Abigail Adams sustain their marriage through letters
1762–1801 · Braintree, Massachusetts; Philadelphia; and Europe
John and Abigail Adams, who married in 1764, exchanged more than 1,100 letters, nearly all now held by the Massachusetts Historical Society. His service in the Continental Congress from 1774 to 1777 and his diplomatic posts in Europe from 1778 to 1784 kept them apart for years at a time, and the letters carried both family news and the business of the Revolution. They continued writing to each other into his presidency, until 1801.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Through years of separation the couple kept their partnership alive by constantly thinking of and writing to each other.
The kural says that when one thinks of the person one loves, the pain of separation does not come, and the Adams letters show long absence softened by steady, loving thought of each other.
