Kural 806

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

எல்லைக்கண் நின்றார் துறவார் தொலைவிடத்தும் தொல்லைக்கண் நின்றார் தொடர்பு.
Ellaikkan nindraar thuravaar tholaividaththum thollaikkan nindraar thodarpu.

Meaning

People who keep to the rules of true friendship will not leave old friends, even when that friendship causes them loss.

Elementary

Suppose your oldest friend accidentally makes your team lose the big game. A true friend doesn’t say, ‘We’re done.’ They say, ‘We’ve been friends forever, and one bad game won’t change that.’

University

The couplet balances ellai (எல்லை, ‘limit, boundary’) against tholl-ai (தொல்லை, ‘antiquity’): ellaikkan nindraar are those who stand within the limit (of friendship’s ethic), and thollaikkan nindraar are those who have stood with us from long ago. Tholaividaththum (தொலைவிடத்தும்) is read by most commentators as ‘even in loss or ruin’, from tholai- ‘to be destroyed’. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all take it as ‘even when the old friend’s acts bring harm’. Puliyur Kesigan reads it spatially, ‘even when they have gone to distant places’, and also takes thollai in its modern sense of ‘trouble’, giving ‘those who stood by us in our troubles’. The two readings change the image but keep the ethic of not abandoning old friends. Both base sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Before the war, Krishna tells Karna that he is Kunti’s eldest son and could be king of the Pandavas. Karna refuses: Duryodhana gave him honour and friendship when others mocked his birth, and he will not desert him now, even knowing the Kaurava side will lose.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Karna’s reply to Krishna), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
In the Udyoga Parva, Karna chooses an old friend’s cause over a throne, knowing it will cost him his life.
He embodies the Kural’s ‘those who will not give up long-standing ties even in ruin’.
Christianity
Paul blesses the household of Onesiphorus, who often refreshed him, was not ashamed of his chains, and searched Rome diligently until he found him, while others in Asia turned away.
— 2 Timothy 1:15-18 [4]
Onesiphorus stayed loyal when association with the imprisoned Paul was costly.
He kept the bond when others broke it, as the Kural’s friend keeps faith in loss.
Islam
During the flight from Mecca, Abu Bakr hid with the Prophet in the cave of Thawr while their enemies searched for them; the Qur’an remembers him as ‘the second of the two’ to whom the Prophet said not to grieve, for God was with them.
— Qur’an 9:40 [5]
Abu Bakr stayed with his long-time companion at the moment of greatest danger.
His steadfastness is the Kural’s refusal to give up an old bond when it brings peril.
Judaism
“Thine own friend, and thy father's friend, forsake not; neither go into thy brother's house in the day of thy calamity: for better is a neighbour that is near than a brother far off.”
— Proverbs 27:10 (King James Version) [4]
Proverbs urges loyalty to one’s own friends and to friends inherited from one’s father, even above distant kin.
‘Forsake not’ is the Kural’s thuravaar (‘they will not give up’) applied to old, even inherited, friendships.
Buddhism
“When misfortunes strike, he doesn't abandon you. When you're down & out, he doesn't look down on you.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.35, Mitta Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
Two of the seven marks of a friend worth keeping are loyalty in misfortune and respect in decline.
This matches the Kural’s friend who does not let go even in loss.
Jainism
“Even though they do what is disagreeable, one should preserve as gold those who ought not to be forsaken. Daily do men seek for fire and keep it in their house, though it has consnmed both their good house and gold.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 5 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The poet says that a true friend should be kept like gold even when he causes harm, just as people keep fire in the house though fire has burned houses and gold.
The Kural says those who keep within friendship's bounds will not give up old friends even when the bond brings them loss, and the Naladiyar's fire that has cost house and gold yet is still kept is the same teaching.
Sikhism
“Kabir! If the mortal continues to love the Lord in the end, as he pledged in the beginning, no poor diamond, not even millions of jewels, can equal him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir 153 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir praises love that stays the same at the end as it was at the beginning, and says such constancy is worth more than any treasure.
The Kural says those who keep to the true bounds of friendship never give up long-standing friends even when it costs them; Kabir prizes the same constancy of a love kept to the end as it was pledged.
Greek Mythology
Through twenty years of Odysseus’ absence, the swineherd Eumaeus keeps faith with his master, refuses to serve the suitors’ interests, and welcomes the disguised stranger kindly, at cost and risk to himself.
— Homer, Odyssey 14-16 [9]
Eumaeus’ loyalty to an old bond holds up through loss, hardship and pressure from those in power.
He is the Kural’s ‘one who stands within the bounds’ and does not give up an old tie.
Philosophy
“Surely he should keep a remembrance of their former intimacy, and as we think we ought to oblige friends rather than strangers, so to those who have been our friends we ought to make some allowance for our former friendship, when the breach has not been due to excess of wickedness.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Even where a friendship can no longer continue, Aristotle says past intimacy still creates obligations.
Aristotle, like Valluvar, holds that old friendship keeps its claim even after the friend has changed.

Real Life Example

Vice President Truman attends the funeral of Tom Pendergast
January 1945 · Kansas City, Missouri, United States
Tom Pendergast, the Kansas City political boss who had backed Harry Truman's early career, was convicted of income tax evasion in 1939 and died on 26 January 1945. Truman, Vice President of the United States for little more than a week, attended the funeral despite considerable public controversy. In a statement he said that Pendergast had always been his friend and that he had always been Pendergast's.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Truman accepted damage to his own reputation at the start of his national career rather than abandon an old friend who had fallen into disgrace.
The kural says those who keep to the true bounds of friendship will not give up a long-standing bond even when it brings them loss, and Truman stood by Pendergast's memory at real political cost.
