Kural 954

Lineage · Kudimai · குடிமை

அடுக்கிய கோடி பெறினும் குடிப்பிறந்தார் குன்றுவ செய்தல் இலர்.
Adukkiya kodi perinum kudippirandhaar kundruva seydhal ilar.

Meaning

Even if they could get crores upon crores (huge piles of money), people from good families will not do anything that brings their family down.

Elementary

Imagine someone offers you a giant pile of money if you will just cheat once. A person from a good family says no, because no pile of money is worth losing their good name.

University

Adukkiya kodi (அடுக்கிய கோடி) is 'crores stacked one upon another', hyperbole for limitless wealth, and perinum (பெறினும், 'even if they obtain') concedes the strongest temptation. Kundruva (குன்றுவ) are acts that diminish, literally 'that make small', and the commentators unanimously take the diminished thing to be the family's honour: Mu. Varadarasanar speaks of faults that would lower the family's distinction, and Kalaignar of deeds that would spoil that excellence. The couplet thus sets the chapter's characteristic contrast between wealth (Porul) and the inherited good name that the section on family (Kudiyiyal) values above it. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, tested by the sage Vishvamitra, gives away his whole kingdom, sells his wife and son and himself becomes a servant at a cremation ground rather than break his given word.
— Markandeya Purana, chs. 7-8 (Harishchandra episode) [3]
The Puranic story makes Harishchandra the ideal of a king of the Solar line who will lose every possession before he loses his truth.
Like the Kural's well-born, he refuses any gain that would require a shameful act.
Christianity
“Again, the devil taketh him up into an exceeding high mountain, and sheweth him all the kingdoms of the world, and the glory of them; And saith unto him, All these things will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me. Then saith Jesus unto him, Get thee hence, Satan.”
— Matthew 4:8-10 (KJV) [4]
Offered every kingdom on earth in exchange for one act of false worship, Jesus refuses outright.
This is the Kural's 'crores upon crores' offered and declined.
Islam
“And do not exchange the covenant of Allah for a small price. Indeed, what is with Allah is best for you, if only you could know.”
— Qur'an 16:95 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an warns that any worldly price, however large it seems, is small next to keeping faith.
Both texts call any payment for a dishonourable act too low.
Judaism
Rabbi Yose ben Kisma tells how a man offered him a thousand thousand gold dinars, precious stones and pearls to come and live in his town, and he replied that even for all the silver and gold in the world he would live only in a place of Torah.
— Pirkei Avot 6:9 [6]
The sage turns down a fortune rather than leave the setting that keeps him true to his calling.
His 'thousand thousand' dinars echo the Kural's piled-up crores, refused for something worth more.
Buddhism
“He is indeed virtuous, wise, and righteous who neither for his own sake nor for the sake of another (does any wrong), who does not crave for sons, wealth, or kingdom, and does not desire success by unjust means.”
— Dhammapada 84 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada defines the righteous person as one who will not seek wealth or power by wrong means.
Like the Kural, it sets wealth gained through wrongdoing below the person's integrity.
Jainism
When Indra, in disguise, urges King Nami to increase his gold, silver and treasure before renouncing, Nami answers that even countless mountains of gold and silver as large as Kailasa would not satisfy a greedy man, for desire is as endless as the sky.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 9 (The Pravrajya of King Nami), tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45 [8]
The royal sage Nami refuses to let any amount of treasure turn him from his chosen path.
His mountains of gold are the Jain counterpart to the Kural's piled crores, and both are refused.
Sikhism
“To take what rightfully belongs to another, is like a Muslim eating pork, or a Hindu eating beef.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Majh, Majh ki Vaar, salok of Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Majh ki Vaar, Guru Nanak says that taking what is not rightfully one's own is as forbidden as the strongest food taboo of each community.
The Kural says the noble will not do anything base even for crores of wealth; Guru Nanak makes ill-gotten gain something to recoil from absolutely.
Greek Mythology
In Prodicus's fable, the young Heracles at a crossroads is met by Vice, who promises him every pleasure without toil, and by Virtue, who promises honour through hard work, and he chooses Virtue.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1 (Prodicus's Choice of Heracles) [10]
The hero turns down an easy life of luxury for the harder road that keeps his honour.
Like the Kural's well-born, Heracles will not trade his worth for easy gain.
Philosophy
At his trial Socrates tells the jury that he will not stop doing philosophy even to be acquitted, and that virtue does not come from money, but money and every other good come from virtue.
— Plato, Apology 29d-30b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates refuses to buy his life by abandoning what he thinks right.
He shows the Kural's resolve: no gain, even life itself, is worth a deed that lowers one's worth.

Real Life Example

King Albert I of Belgium rejects the German ultimatum
2-3 August 1914 · Brussels, Belgium
On 2 August 1914 Germany delivered an ultimatum demanding free passage for its armies across neutral Belgium, offering assurances that Belgium would keep its independence and possessions after the war if it agreed. The Belgian government, meeting under King Albert I, rejected the demand on 3 August, stating that to accept would sacrifice the nation's honour and betray its duty. German forces invaded the next day, and the violation of Belgian neutrality brought Britain into the war.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Belgium chose to defend its pledged neutrality at enormous cost rather than accept a safe bargain that would have dishonoured it.
The kural says the nobly born will not do a lowering deed even for untold riches, and King Albert would not trade his country's honour for the guarantees Germany offered.
