Kural 629

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

இன்பத்துள் இன்பம் விழையாதான் துன்பத்துள் துன்பம் உறுதல் இலன்.
Inpaththul inpam vizhaiyaadhaan thunpaththul Thunpam urudhal ilan.

Meaning

A person who does not chase after pleasure in good times will not be crushed by sadness in bad times.

Elementary

If you jump up and down screaming when you win a game, you might cry just as hard when you lose. But if you enjoy the win calmly, you can handle the loss calmly too. The Kural says staying balanced in good times keeps you steady in bad times.

University

The couplet is built as a strict parallel, iṉpattuḷ iṉpam / tuṉpattuḷ tuṉpam, ‘in pleasure, pleasure’ and ‘in sorrow, sorrow’, so that the inner response mirrors the outer situation in each case. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses the first half as not cherishing and clinging to pleasure when it comes, and Kalaignar summarises it with a Tamil jingle, not dancing in joy and not wilting in sorrow, as an example of regarding both alike. It differs from 628 in that it concerns one’s attitude within pleasure already present rather than the pursuit of pleasure. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net, and Pope’s ‘Mid joys he yields not heart to joys’ control’ captures the sense of inner restraint.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Established in God, having a firm understanding of divine knowledge and not hampered by delusion, they neither rejoice in getting something pleasant nor grieve on experiencing the unpleasant.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.20 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna describes the knower of Brahman as one who neither exults (na prahṛṣyet) at the pleasant nor is shaken by the unpleasant.
The Gita gives both halves of the Kural in the same order: no elation in pleasure, and so no grief in pain.
Christianity
“I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need.”
— Philippians 4:12 (KJV) [4]
Paul says he has learned to stay the same person whether he has plenty or nothing.
Paul’s evenness in abundance and need is the Kural’s balance that refuses to be carried away in either direction.
Islam
“Strange are the ways of a believer for there is good in every affair of his … for if he has an occasion to feel delight, he thanks (God), thus there is a good for him in it, and if he gets into trouble and shows resignation (and endures it patiently), there is a good for him in it.”
— Sahih Muslim 2999 [5]
The Prophet describes the believer who meets good times with gratitude and bad times with patience, so that neither overturns him.
Like the Kural, the hadith prescribes a measured response in joy that makes a measured response in sorrow possible.
Judaism
“In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity consider; God hath made even the one as well as the other, to the end that man should find nothing after him.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:14 (JPS 1917) [6]
Koheleth teaches that good and bad days both come from God, so neither should be treated as permanent or ultimate.
Koheleth, like the Kural, pairs prosperity and adversity and asks for a measured, thoughtful response to each.
Buddhism
“The good renounce (attachment for) everything. The virtuous do not prattle with a yearning for pleasures. The wise show no elation or depression when touched by happiness or sorrow.”
— Dhammapada 83 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada describes the wise as neither lifted up by happiness nor cast down by sorrow.
‘No elation or depression when touched by happiness or sorrow’ is almost a paraphrase of this Kural’s balanced pair.
Jainism
“He was indifferent to success or failure (in begging), to happiness and misery, to life and death, to blame and praise, to honour and insult.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 19.90 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
After renouncing, Mrigaputra is described as unmoved equally by happiness and misery, success and failure.
The Kural says one who does not revel in good times will not be sunk in bad times; the Jain ideal of equal indifference to happiness and misery is the same balance.
Sikhism
“One who is not affected by pleasure or pain, who looks upon friend and enemy alike”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahala 9, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks at the close of the Guru Granth Sahib, Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the liberated person as one whom neither joy nor sorrow can sway.
The Kural says one who does not revel in good times will not sink in bad times; Guru Tegh Bahadur praises the one whom neither pleasure nor pain can move.
Greek Mythology
Niobe, queen of Thebes, exulted so boastfully in her fourteen children that she set herself above the goddess Leto; when Apollo and Artemis struck them all down, her grief was so boundless that she turned to stone and weeps forever.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.146-312 [10]
Ovid’s Niobe is the cautionary opposite of the Kural’s ideal: unrestrained delight in good fortune followed by unrestrained despair in loss.
Her story shows the Kural in reverse: one who revels without measure in joy is drowned without measure in sorrow.
Philosophy
In the Consolation of Philosophy, Lady Philosophy shows Boethius that Fortune’s nature is to change, so whoever lets himself be lifted up by her gifts has already set himself up to be crushed when she withdraws them.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book II [11]
Boethius learns that attachment to good fortune is what gives bad fortune its sting.
Philosophy teaches Boethius the Kural’s lesson: restraint in the enjoyment of good fortune is protection against despair in bad.

Real Life Example

Su Shi remains cheerful through exile to Huangzhou and Hainan
1079-1100 · Huangzhou (Hubei) and Hainan Island, China
Su Shi, one of China's greatest poets and a successful official, was banished in 1079 to Huangzhou because of his opposition to the reforms of the minister Wang Anshi. There he farmed a plot on an eastern slope, took from it the name Dongpo, by which he is still known, and wrote some of his best-known works, including the Red Cliff rhapsodies of 1082. He showed the same optimism and lack of bitterness when he was banished again to Hainan Island in 1094, and he stayed on friendly terms with Wang Anshi, exchanging poems with him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Su Shi's equanimity under disgrace became part of his fame, and the works he wrote in exile are among the best loved in Chinese literature.
The kural says the one who does not greedily revel in good times will not be sunk in sorrow in bad times, and Su Shi, who moved from high office to a farmer's life without bitterness, shows the evenness of mind the couplet describes.
