Kural 1002

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

பொருளானாம் எல்லாமென்று ஈயாது இவறும் மருளானாம் மாணாப் பிறப்பு.
Porulaanaam Ellaamendru Eeyaadhu Ivarum Marulaanaam Maanaap Pirappu

Meaning

Thinking money can do everything, a person holds it tight and gives nothing away. That foolish mistake leads to a low, worthless birth.

Elementary

Some people think, 'If I just have enough money, I'll have everything!' So they grip their money tight and never share. Valluvar says this is a big mistake, like a confused traveller holding the wrong map, and it leads them to a very sad place.

University

The first line reports the miser's creed, poruLaal aam ellaam ('by wealth everything is achieved'), and his conduct, eeyaadhu ivaRum ('not giving, he clings'); ivaRu- denotes tight-fisted holding, the same root as இவறல் (miserliness) condemned among royal faults in Kural 432. The second line names the result: maruL (மருள், 'delusion, bewilderment') produces maaNaap piRappu, 'an un-excellent birth'. Mu. Varadarasanar keeps this general ('a birth without distinction'), Kalaignar reads it this-worldly ('a base creature'), while Solomon Pappaiah and Pope's explanation ('will be born a demon') follow the classical line that specifies a ghost or demon birth (பேய்ப் பிறப்பு); Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as an endless, graceless cycle of births. The phrase மருளானாம் மாணாப் பிறப்பு also closes Kural 351, where delusion about what is real is said to cause such a birth, so Valluvar here treats the miser's faith in money as a species of metaphysical error. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna describes the demoniac mentality that says, so much wealth is mine today and more will come tomorrow; deluded by such thoughts and bound by attachment, these persons fall into a foul hell and take birth again and again in demoniac wombs.
— Bhagavad Gita 16.13–16 and 16.19–20 [3]
The Gita traces degraded rebirth to the delusion of treating possessions as the measure of life.
The Kural likewise says the delusion that wealth is everything produces a debased, even demonic, birth.
Christianity
Jesus tells of a rich man who feasted every day while the beggar Lazarus lay starving at his gate; after death Lazarus is comforted while the rich man is in torment, reminded that he received his good things in his lifetime.
— Luke 16:19–25 (The Rich Man and Lazarus) [4]
The parable shows a lifetime of withholding from the needy leading to a terrible state after death.
Like the Kural, it links refusal to give with a fallen condition in the life to come.
Islam
“Woe to every scorner and mocker / Who collects wealth and [continuously] counts it. / He thinks that his wealth will make him immortal. / No! He will surely be thrown into the Crusher.”
— Qur'an 104:1–4, Al-Humazah (Saheeh International, via quran.com) [5]
The surah condemns the man who hoards and counts his money, imagining it makes him invulnerable.
This is the Kural's deluded miser who believes wealth is everything and so earns a degraded destiny.
Judaism
The Mishnah lists among human characters the one who says, what is mine is mine and what is yours is yours, calls it an ordinary type, and records that some call it the character of Sodom.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 5:10 [6]
Refusing ever to share, even when it costs nothing, is identified with the proverbially wicked city.
The Kural's miser who gives nothing displays exactly this Sodom-like closed-handedness.
Buddhism
The Petavatthu, a canonical collection of ghost stories, repeatedly explains that beings were reborn as hungry ghosts (petas) because in their human lives they were stingy and refused to give, even to ascetics who came for alms.
— Petavatthu (Khuddaka Nikaya), stories of misers reborn as petas [7]
Buddhist teaching makes miserliness (macchariya) a direct cause of rebirth in the ghost realm.
It closely matches the classical reading of the Kural that the miser's delusion leads to a ghost-like birth.
Jainism
“If the whole world and all treasures were yours, you would still not be satisfied, nor would all this be able to save you.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Queen Kamalâvatî rebukes King Ishukâra for wanting to seize a renouncer's abandoned property, teaching that no amount of wealth can satisfy or protect the soul.
It directly contradicts the miser's creed in the Kural that wealth can accomplish everything.
Sikhism
“People gather and hoard their possessions, and give nothing to anyone else - the poor fools think that everything is theirs.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 155, Raag Gauri, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that the hoarder gives nothing away because he is blind: he thinks everything he holds belongs to him.
The Kural blames the delusion that wealth is everything for the miser's refusal to give; Guru Nanak likewise ties the refusal to give to a blind belief that it is all his own.
Greek Mythology
King Croesus of Lydia shows Solon his treasuries and asks who is the happiest of men, expecting to be named; Solon instead names obscure Athenians who lived and died well, and warns that no one should be called happy until his end is seen.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.30–33 (Solon and Croesus) [10]
Croesus's confidence that riches equal happiness is exposed as an illusion, later borne out by his fall.
Croesus embodies the Kural's mistaken belief that wealth is everything.
Philosophy
“But meanness is both incurable (for old age and every disability is thought to make men mean) and more innate in men than prodigality; for most men are fonder of getting money than of giving.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross, MIT Internet Classics Archive) [11]
Aristotle judges stinginess a deeper and harder vice to cure than wastefulness.
The Kural similarly treats tight-fisted clinging to wealth as a deep-rooted error with grave consequences.

Real Life Example

Didius Julianus buys the support of the Praetorian Guard
28 March - 1 June 193 CE · Rome, Roman Empire (now Italy)
After the Praetorian Guard killed the emperor Pertinax in March 193, the wealthy senator Didius Julianus competed with Pertinax's father-in-law, Sulpicianus, for the Guard's backing by promising each soldier a cash payment, and won with the larger offer. The Guard proclaimed him emperor, but his rule was insecure from the start, and when the general Septimius Severus marched on Rome with the Danube legions, support for Julianus collapsed. He was put to death on 1 June 193, about two months after his accession.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Julianus's wealth bought him the title of emperor but none of the loyalty or legitimacy needed to keep it.
The kural describes the delusion of thinking that wealth can do everything, and Julianus's brief, doomed reign shows how badly that belief can end.
