Kural 357

Knowledge of the True · Meyyunarthal · மெய்யுணர்தல்

ஓர்த்துள்ளம் உள்ளது உணரின் ஒருதலையாப் பேர்த்துள்ள வேண்டா பிறப்பு.
Orththullam ulladhu unarin orudhalaiyaap Perththulla vendaa pirappu.

Meaning

If the mind has carefully studied and firmly understood what is truly real, there is no need to think about being born again.

Elementary

Once you truly understand that the monster under the bed was only a shadow, you don’t have to be scared of it again tomorrow night. This kural says that once your mind really and firmly understands what is real, you don’t have to worry about coming back to be born again.

University

Ulladhu, literally ‘that which is’, is one of the most philosophically loaded words in the chapter. It corresponds to the Sanskrit sat (Being) and later gave its name to Ramana Maharshi’s Tamil work Ulladhu Narpadhu (‘Forty Verses on That Which Is’). Orththu (having reflected or investigated) and oru-thalaiyaa (with complete, one-pointed certainty) spell out the method: truth is reached by sustained inquiry until conviction is total. Traditional glosses (Solomon Pappaiah speaks of holding fully in mind ‘what was heard from the great’) connect this to the Vedantic sequence of hearing, reflection and contemplation (shravana, manana, nididhyasana). The language of ‘what is’ invites comparison with Parmenides, for whom Being is ‘uncreated and indestructible’, and with Maimonides, for whom knowing the Primary Being is ‘the foundation of all foundations’. Valluvar’s distinctive claim is soteriological: to grasp Being is to end becoming.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The fetter of the heart is broken, all doubts are solved, all his works (and their effects) perish when He has been beheld who is high and low.”
— Mundaka Upanishad 2.2.8 (trans. Max Müller) [3]
The Upanishad says that when the ultimate Reality is directly seen, every bond, every doubt, and all karma that would cause future births fall away.
Just as the Kural says, firm realization of ‘ulladhu’ removes the karmic cause of rebirth; the same Upanishad describes aiming the mind ‘at that which is’.
Christianity
“And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent.”
— John 17:3 (KJV) [4]
In his prayer before his arrest, Jesus defines eternal life itself as knowing the one true God.
Both teach that knowing the ultimate Reality is itself the deathless state.
Islam
“Who remember Allāh while standing or sitting or [lying] on their sides and give thought to the creation of the heavens and the earth, [saying], ‘Our Lord, You did not create this aimlessly; exalted are You [above such a thing]; then protect us from the punishment of the Fire.’”
— Qur’an 3:191 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who reflect deeply on creation, reach certainty of its purpose and its Creator, and so seek salvation.
Like the Kural’s ‘orththu’ (having reflected), careful contemplation leads to grasping the Real and being saved from a dreaded fate.
Judaism
“The foundation of all foundations and the pillar of wisdom is to know that there is a Primary Being who brought into being all existence.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 1:1 (trans. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides opens his code of Jewish law by making knowledge of the one true Being, from whose truth everything exists, the first commandment and the basis of wisdom.
The Kural’s ‘ulladhu’ (That Which Is) and Maimonides’s ‘Primary Being’ both place knowing true Being at the summit of wisdom.
Buddhism
“There is, monks, an unborn — unbecome — unmade — unfabricated. If there were not that unborn — unbecome — unmade — unfabricated, there would not be the case that escape from the born — become — made — fabricated would be discerned.”
— Udana 8.3 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha affirms that there really is an unborn, unconditioned reality, and that because it exists, escape from birth is possible.
Echoes the Kural’s logic: realizing what truly ‘is’ (the unborn) removes the need to be born again.
Jainism
“1. jîva, Soul; 2. ajîva, the inanimate things; 3. bandha, the binding of the soul by Karman; 4. puṇya, merit; 5. pâpa, demerit; 6. âsrava … 7. saṃvara … 8. the annihilation of Karman; 9. final deliverance: these are the nine truths (or categories). He who verily believes the true teaching of the (above nine) fundamental truths, possesses righteousness.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 28.14-15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 154) [8]
For Jainism, 'that which is' is spelled out as the nine tattvas, and firm conviction in them is the right view with which the path to the end of rebirth begins.
Valluvar says that once the mind has examined and firmly grasped That Which Is, no further birth need be feared. The Jain counterpart is firm conviction in the real categories (tattvārtha), which is where the soteriological path starts.
Sikhism
“True In The Primal Beginning. True Throughout The Ages. True Here And Now. O Nanak, Forever And Ever True.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, opening salok, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
The opening salok of Japji Sahib declares that the One is Sat, true and real, in the beginning, through the ages, now and forever.
The Kural speaks of the mind that has examined and grasped That Which Truly Is; the Japji salok names that Ulladhu, the One who simply is, in every time.
Greek Mythology
In the Myth of Er, souls about to be reborn must drink from the River of Unmindfulness (Lethe) on the Plain of Forgetfulness. Those not protected by wisdom drink more than the measure and forget everything before entering a new life.
— Plato, Republic X, 621a–b (Myth of Er) [10]
In this Greek picture of reincarnation, forgetting the truth goes hand in hand with being reborn; wisdom is what limits that forgetting.
Inverse parallel: in the Kural, firmly remembering and knowing the truth is what makes rebirth unnecessary.
Philosophy
“In this path are very many tokens that what is is uncreated and indestructible; for it is complete, immovable, and without end.”
— Parmenides, Fragment 8 (DK B8; trans. John Burnet) [11]
Parmenides argues that true Being (‘what is’) never comes into being or passes away; birth and death belong only to mortal opinion.
The Kural’s ‘ulladhu’ is Parmenides’s ‘what is’: to grasp the ungenerated is to stand outside birth.

Real Life Example

Ramana Maharshi's self-inquiry in Madurai
1896 · Madurai, Tamil Nadu, India
In 1896 the teenage student Venkataraman, later known as Ramana Maharshi, was overcome in his uncle's house in Madurai by a sudden fear of death. Lying still as if dead, he inquired into what it was that dies and concluded that his true self was not the body; Britannica describes this as the experience from which he derived his method of self-inquiry, vichara. About six weeks later he left home for the holy hill of Arunachala at Tiruvannamalai, where he lived until his death in 1950.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By his own account the fear of death never returned after that single inquiry, and the rest of his life and teaching grew from it.
The kural says that once the mind has examined and firmly grasped That Which Is, there is no more need to think of birth, and Ramana's inquiry into the self, the root of his later teaching, is perhaps the closest documented illustration.
