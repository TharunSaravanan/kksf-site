Kural 809

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

கெடாஅ வழிவந்த கேண்மையார் கேண்மை விடாஅர் விழையும் உலகு.
Kedaaa vazhivandha kenmaiyaar kenmai vidaaar vizhaiyum ulagu.

Meaning

The world loves people who never leave friends whose friendship has lasted, unbroken, for many years.

Elementary

People trust the kid who has had the same best friend since kindergarten. If you stick with old friends, others think, ‘I would like a friend like that too.’

University

Kedaaa (கெடாஅ) and vidaaar (விடாஅர்) carry isai-nirai alapedai, a metrical lengthening that also adds emphasis: ‘never ruined’ and ‘never letting go’. Vazhivandha kenmaiyaar are ‘those whose friendship has come down along an unbroken path’. The main verb vizhaiyum ulagu, ‘the world desires or loves’, makes loyalty to old friends a source of public esteem. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read ‘the world praises’, while Puliyur Kesigan reads ‘everyone in the world will want such a person as a friend’. Solomon Pappaiah adds the qualification ‘even if the old friend does harm’, linking this couplet to 807. The text is the same in both base sources; the sandhi-split ytamizh form keeps the alapedai spellings.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Hitopadesha’s first book, Mitralabha, tells how a crow, a mouse, a tortoise and a deer become firm friends and rescue one another again and again from hunters and snares; their steadfastness is held up as the model of winning friends.
— Hitopadesha, Book I: Mitralabha [3]
The fable collection offers lasting friendship as practical wisdom that everyone admires.
The Kural says the world loves the faithful friend, and the Hitopadesha teaches that faithfulness as a model.
Christianity
Proverbs teaches that a friend loves at all times and that a brother is born for adversity.
— Proverbs 17:17 [4]
Constant love in every season is presented as the mark of the true friend.
The Kural’s unbroken friendship is this ‘love at all times’, and it wins admiration.
Islam
In a hadith qudsi, God asks on the Day of Resurrection where those are who loved one another for His glory, and promises to shade them on the day when there is no shade but His.
— Sahih Muslim, Book of Virtue, Good Manners and Joining of Ties [5]
Friendship kept for the sake of God is promised the highest honour.
The Kural promises the world’s love to the steadfast friend; the hadith promises divine honour.
Judaism
After the story of Honi the Circle-Drawer, who slept seventy years and woke to find no one who knew him, the Talmud records the saying ‘either companionship or death’ (o chavruta o mituta).
— Babylonian Talmud, Taanit 23a [6]
Life without one’s long-known companions is portrayed as not worth living.
This shows how much is lost when old bonds are broken, and so why the Kural honours those who keep them.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells Sigala that a true friend is the same in happiness and in sorrow: he shares one’s troubles and is ready even to give up his life for one’s sake.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta [7]
The sutta portrays the loyal companion as someone to be cherished as a mother cherishes her child.
Cherishing the steadfast friend parallels the Kural’s ‘the world loves’ the loyal.
Jainism
“Men cleave not to those to whom their hearts cleave not, within a short space of time; but will the friendship which cherishes the memory of those who are intertwined with one's heart be abandoned, though they are absent from us for a long time?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 4 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet contrasts quick attachments with the deep friendship that holds on to the memory of a friend even through long absence.
The Kural says the world loves those who never let go of friends whose bond has come down unbroken through the years, and the Naladiyar likewise praises a friendship that is not abandoned over long years apart.
Sikhism
Bhai (later Baba) Buddha met Guru Nanak as a boy and served the Gurus for the rest of a very long life: he applied the tilak at the installation of Guru Angad and of the four Gurus after him, helped dig the tank at Amritsar, became the first granthi of the Harimandar in 1604, and trained the young Hargobind. When he died in 1631, Guru Hargobind was at his bedside and gave his shoulder to the bier.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, ed. Harbans Singh (Punjabi University, Patiala), entry 'Buddha, Baba (1506-1631)' by Gurdip Singh Randhawa (paraphrase) [9]
Baba Buddha's unbroken loyalty across six Gurus made him one of the most venerated figures of early Sikhism, honoured by the Guru himself at his death.
The Kural says the world loves those who never let go of friendships handed down through the years; Baba Buddha, who kept faith with the Gurus' house for over a century, is remembered with exactly that love and honour.
Greek Mythology
Orestes and Pylades, companions from childhood who stand together through exile, matricide and danger in Tauris, became the proverbial pair of perfect friends for later Greek and Roman writers.
— Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris; later classical reception [10]
Their unbroken loyalty earned them lasting fame as the model of friendship.
The world’s admiration of them is exactly the Kural’s vizhaiyum ulagu.
Philosophy
“And the proverb is a true one, "You must eat many a peck of salt with a man to be thorough friends with him.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 19 (§67), trans. E. S. Shuckburgh [11]
Laelius quotes the proverb that only long shared life makes a complete friendship.
The Kural’s ‘friendship come down an unbroken path’ is a friendship of many pecks of salt.

Real Life Example

Lafayette's farewell tour and his visit to Washington's tomb
1824-1825 · United States, including Mount Vernon, Virginia
At President James Monroe's invitation, the Marquis de Lafayette returned to the United States in August 1824 and over the next year visited all twenty-four states, greeted by parades and crowds, including an estimated two-thirds of New York City on his arrival. On 17 October 1824 he visited the tomb of his old friend and mentor George Washington at Mount Vernon, leaving it in tears, and he renewed friendships with Jefferson, Madison and other veterans of the Revolution. Congress granted him 200,000 dollars and land, and towns and squares across the country were named in his honour.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nearly fifty years after the Revolution, Lafayette's unbroken loyalty to Washington and to his American comrades made him the most celebrated guest the young nation had ever received.
The kural says the world loves those who never let go of friendships that have come down unbroken through the years, and a whole nation turned out to honour Lafayette's lifelong fidelity to Washington and his old companions.
