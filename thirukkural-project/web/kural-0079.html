Kural 79

The Possession of Love · Anbudaimai · அன்புடைமை

புறத்துறுப் பெல்லாம் எவன்செய்யும் யாக்கை அகத்துறுப்பு அன்பி லவர்க்கு.
Puraththurup pellaam evanseyyum yaakkai, Agaththuruppu anbi lavarkku.

Meaning

What use are all the outer parts of the body to people who lack love, the body’s most important inner part?

Elementary

A guitar can have perfect strings, shiny wood and beautiful tuning pegs — but if there's no hollow space inside it, it makes no music. This kural says love is the inside part of a person. Without it, all the outside parts don't accomplish anything.

University

Valluvar builds the couplet on a precise anatomical contrast: புறத்து உறுப்பு (external organ/limb) against அகத்து உறுப்பு (internal organ), and then makes the startling classificatory claim that அன்பு is itself an உறுப்பு — a bodily organ, not a sentiment. யாக்கை (yaakkai) is the physical frame or corporeal body. The rhetorical question எவன் செய்யும் ("what will they accomplish?") is not asking whether the limbs are pleasant but whether they can function at all, on the analogy of a body whose vital inner organ has failed. Commentators split on the referent: Parimelalhagar and Solomon Pappaiah extend it to the "external organs" of a household — its wealth, servants and property — arguing that a home without love is equally inoperative, while Varadarajan and Karunanidhi keep it to the individual body. Both readings converge on the same deflationary verdict about outward equipment, and the verse reads as a direct ancestor of the argument in Kural 80.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who restrains the senses of action but whose mind dwells on sense objects certainly deludes himself and is called a pretender.”
— Bhagavad Gītā 3.6, trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna distinguishes genuine discipline from its imitation: outwardly immobilised organs of action mean nothing if what is inside is unchanged.
Both verses judge the body's outward apparatus worthless when the inner condition is absent.
Christianity
“And though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:3 (in context of 13:1–3), King James Version [4]
Paul concedes every impressive outward performance — eloquence, prophecy, total almsgiving, martyrdom — and declares each one to be worth exactly nothing without love.
This is Valluvar's எவன் செய்யும் answered in full: the external apparatus, however complete, accomplishes nothing without the inner organ.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your bodies nor to your faces but He looks to your hearts.”
— Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2564c, narrated by Abū Hurayra [5]
The Prophet, pointing to his chest as he spoke, denies that outward form or status carries any weight in divine assessment, which attends only to the inward state.
Precisely the Kural's hierarchy: the outward parts count for nothing beside the inner organ.
Judaism
“…for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart.”
— 1 Samuel 16:7, King James Version [4]
Samuel is about to anoint Jesse's tallest and most impressive son when he is stopped and told that stature and countenance are not what is being assessed.
The verse states the Kural's principle as a rule of judgement: outward endowments are simply not the relevant organ.
Buddhism
“Not by a shaven head does an undisciplined man who utters lies become a monk. How can he who is full of desire and greed be a monk? He who wholly subdues evil deeds, both small and great, is called a monk, because he has overcome all evil.”
— Dhammapada 264–265, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [6]
The Dhammapada refuses to let the visible markers of the monastic life — the shaved head, the robe — count for anything without the inward transformation they are supposed to signify.
Same verdict as the Kural: the outward apparatus, however correct, accomplishes nothing without the inner quality.
Jainism
“One does not become a Sramana by the tonsure, nor a Brâhmana by the sacred syllable ôm, nor a Muni by living in the woods, nor a Tâpasa by wearing (clothes of) Kusa-grass and bark. One becomes a Sramana by equanimity, a Brâhmana by chastity, a Muni by knowledge, and a Tâpasa by penance.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.31–32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
Jayaghosha tells the priest that outward marks make no one holy; only the inner qualities behind them do.
Valluvar asks what use the outer limbs are without love, the inner organ; this sutra likewise rejects outward marks that lack the inner quality.
Sikhism
“Without her Husband, the soul-bride's youth and ornaments are useless and wretched.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 58, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak uses the bride-soul image: without the inner bond of love with the Husband Lord, all outward beauty and adornment serve no purpose.
As the Kural asks what good the outer limbs are without love as the inner organ, Gurbani declares outer endowments useless without the inner relationship.
Greek Mythology
Pygmalion carves an ivory woman more beautiful than any living one, dresses her, lays her on a couch and speaks to her — but she remains cold ivory until Venus, moved by his prayer, grants her life and her flesh grows warm under his hand.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses X.243–297 (Pygmalion) [9]
Ovid dwells on the statue's flawless external form precisely to make the point that flawless form is not the same as a living body; something has to be given from within.
The statue is Valluvar's body with every outward organ and no inner one — perfectly equipped and entirely inert.
Philosophy
“For without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods … for what is the use of such prosperity without the opportunity of beneficence, which is exercised chiefly and in its most laudable form towards friends?”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book VIII, ch. 1 (1155a) (tr. W. D. Ross) [10]
Opening his treatment of friendship (philia), Aristotle argues that wealth, office and power are worthless to someone with no bond of affection through which to use them.
Like the Kural’s question of what use the outer limbs are without love, Aristotle asks what use the outer goods are without friendship, and the answer in both is: none.

Real Life Example

Harry Harlow presents The Nature of Love
1958 · University of Wisconsin-Madison, United States
In his 1958 presidential address to the American Psychological Association, titled The Nature of Love, the psychologist Harry Harlow reported experiments in which infant rhesus monkeys were given a bare wire surrogate mother and a cloth-covered one. The infants went to the wire figure only to feed, if it held the bottle, and spent their time clinging to the soft cloth figure, especially when frightened. The work showed that comfort and contact, not feeding alone, were the basis of attachment.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Harlow's experiments demonstrated that meeting a creature's bodily needs is not enough; an inner bond of comfort is what makes development possible.
The kural asks what use the outer limbs are without love, the body's inner organ, and Harlow showed that the food-giving apparatus was nearly worthless to the infants without the comfort of contact.
