Kural 497

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

அஞ்சாமை அல்லால் துணைவேண்டா எஞ்சாமை எண்ணி இடத்தால் செயின்.
Anjaamai allaal thunaivendaa enjaamai Enni idaththaal seyin.

Meaning

If you think everything through carefully and act from the right place, you need no other helper except your own courage.

Elementary

If you have practised your speech, checked every page, and are standing in the right spot on stage, the only thing left to bring is courage. This kural says good planning and the right place leave you needing only a brave heart.

University

‘Enjaamai enni’ means to think things through without remainder (enjuthal, to be left over), so planning comes first. ‘Idaththaal seyin’ means acting from, or by means of, the right place; the instrumental ‘-aal’ makes place the means of action. ‘Anjaamai allaal thunai vendaa’ says no ally is needed except fearlessness, so courage takes the place of all outside help. Parimelazhagar notes that thorough deliberation and the right ground remove the need for allies (thunai), which were discussed earlier under ‘Valiyarithal’ (ch. 48, thunaivali). Kalaignar’s gloss is about thorough method and leaves out place, a lighter reading than the traditional one.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna lists abhayam, fearlessness, first among the divine qualities (daivī sampad) that lead to freedom.
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1 [3]
In the Gita, fearlessness heads the list of virtues belonging to a person of divine nature.
The Kural likewise makes fearlessness the one companion needed once planning and positioning are done.
Christianity
“Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand. Stand therefore…”
— Ephesians 6:13–14 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to prepare fully, then hold their ground firmly.
‘Having done all, to stand’ matches the Kural’s thorough preparation followed by steadfast courage in position.
Islam
“Those to whom people said, ‘Indeed, the people have gathered against you, so fear them.’ But it [merely] increased them in faith, and they said, ‘Sufficient for us is Allāh, and [He is] the best Disposer of affairs.’”
— Quran 3:173 (Sahih International) [5]
After Uhud, believers who were warned of a gathering enemy answered with fearlessness instead of alarm.
The refusal to be frightened by numbers matches the Kural’s ‘no ally but fearlessness’.
Judaism
“Be strong and of a good courage; be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.”
— Joshua 1:9 (KJV) [4]
God charges Joshua to be courageous as he leads Israel into the land and its campaigns.
Courage as the leader’s essential quality going into the field fits the Kural’s ‘anjaamai’.
Buddhism
“There is no fear for an awakened one, whose mind is not sodden (by lust) nor afflicted (by hate), and who has gone beyond both merit and demerit.”
— Dhammapada 39 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
For the Dhammapada, a well-trained mind no longer knows fear.
The kural’s fearlessness as one’s only needed companion has an inner counterpart here.
Jainism
“Be without fear, O king; but grant safety to others also; in this transient world of living beings, why are you addicted to cruelty?”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 18.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The sage Gardabhali tells the frightened King Sanjaya, who had killed a deer in the monk's park, to be fearless and to grant fearlessness to all beings.
The kural names fearlessness as the only ally a well-prepared person needs; Jainism also prizes fearlessness, but joins it to the gift of safety to others (abhaya-dana), whereas the kural is about attacking an enemy.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahalla 9, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In this salok Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the truly wise person as one who neither threatens others nor gives way to fear of them.
The Kural says that once a plan is complete and the ground is right, fearlessness is the only ally needed; Guru Tegh Bahadur makes fearlessness the mark of the wise.
Greek Mythology
At Thermopylae, when the Spartan Dieneces was told the Persian arrows were so many they would hide the sun, he answered that this was good news, because they would then fight in the shade.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.226 (tr. A. D. Godley) [9]
Herodotus records the reply as an example of fearlessness in a well-chosen position against huge odds.
Once the ground was chosen, fearlessness was the only thing the Greeks still needed, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“The natural formation of the country is the soldier’s best ally; but a power of estimating the adversary, of controlling the forces of victory, and of shrewdly calculating difficulties, dangers and distances, constitutes the test of a great general.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War X.21 (tr. Lionel Giles) [10]
Sun Tzu calls terrain an ally in itself, provided the general calculates everything carefully.
Both texts treat the right ground plus full calculation as removing the need for other allies.

Real Life Example

Admiral Yi Sun-sin's stand at the Myeongnyang Strait
1597 · Myeongnyang Strait (Uldolmok), off southwestern Korea
During Japan's second invasion of Korea, Admiral Yi Sun-sin, recently restored to command after being demoted, had only about thirteen warships left against a Japanese fleet of more than a hundred and thirty. He chose to fight in the narrow Myeongnyang Strait, whose name means roaring currents, where the channel and fast tidal flow kept many ships from passing at once. The Japanese ships were hampered and fell into confusion, and the Japanese fleet withdrew after heavy losses.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Careful choice of a battleground that neutralised enemy numbers allowed a tiny fleet to turn back a large one.
The kural says that one who thinks everything through and acts from the right place needs no ally but fearlessness, and Yi had no allies, only his plan, the strait and his courage.
