Kural 947

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

தீயள வன்றித் தெரியான் பெரிதுண்ணின் நோயள வின்றிப் படும்.
Theeyala vandrith theriyaan peridhunnin noyala vindrip padum.

Meaning

A person who carelessly eats far more than their stomach's fire (the body's power to digest) can handle will get sicknesses without end.

Elementary

A small campfire can cook a small pot. If you pile a whole tree on it, the fire chokes and smoke goes everywhere. Valluvar says your tummy has a little fire too, and if you overload it, troubles pile up.

University

The couplet turns on a wordplay of measure: thee alavu (தீ அளவு, 'the measure of the fire') against noi alavu indri (நோய் அளவின்றி, 'diseases without measure'). The fire is the pasi-thee (பசித்தீ), the 'fire of hunger' or digestive fire, as Varadarasanar and Kesikan gloss it; it corresponds to the Ayurvedic jatharagni, whose strength determines how much food can be digested. Theriyaan (தெரியான்) is 'one who does not discern', and Kesikan adds that he ignores both the time and the quantity. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads thee alavu instead as an analogy to one who does not know how to use fire in measure, a minority reading. Parimelazhagar's tradition takes the metaphor medically, so that the couplet states a law: exceed the fire's measure and illness exceeds all measure. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Charaka teaches that one should eat in proper measure, and that the right quantity of food depends on the strength of the digestive fire (agni).
— Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 5.3-4 (matrashitiya), paraphrase [3]
In Ayurveda the digestive fire sets the limit of how much food can be taken without harm.
This is the Kural's thee alavu, the measure of the fire, stated as medical doctrine.
Christianity
“For excess of meats bringeth sickness, and surfeiting will turn into choler.”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 37:30 (KJV Apocrypha) [4]
Ben Sira states plainly that eating too much produces sickness and biliousness.
Excess of food bringing sickness is the Kural's rule that eating beyond measure brings disease beyond measure.
Islam
The Qur'an describes those who reject faith as enjoying themselves and eating as grazing cattle eat, without thought.
— Qur'an 47:12, paraphrase [5]
Thoughtless, animal-like eating is presented as the mark of a heedless life.
The Kural's theriyaan, the undiscerning eater, is likewise one who eats without thought of measure.
Judaism
“Overeating is like poison to anyone's body. It is the main source of all illness. Most illnesses which afflict a man are caused by harmful foods or by his filling his belly and overeating, even of healthful foods.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:15 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides traces most human illness to overeating, even of good food.
This matches the Kural's claim that eating beyond measure brings diseases beyond measure.
Buddhism
“One who gets drowsy from overeating, fond of sleep, rolling round the bed like a great hog stuffed with grain: that dullard returns to the womb again and again.”
— Dhammapada 325 (tr. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Dhammapada, traditionally linked to King Pasenadi's overeating, pictures the glutton as a sluggish, stuffed hog.
The thoughtless over-eater of the Kural is this stuffed, dull figure, though the Buddhist cost is rebirth rather than disease.
Jainism
“As in a forest, full of fuel, a fire fanned by the wind cannot be extinguished, so the fire (as it were) of the senses of him who eats as he lists; it does not benefit any chaste man.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana uses the image of fire fed with too much fuel for the senses inflamed by unrestrained eating.
Both texts reach for fire to describe eating without measure, the Kural for the digestive fire and the Jain text for the fire of the senses.
Sikhism
“Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 155) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir names lobh (greed), one of the five thieves of Sikh teaching, as the bringer of death, and sets it against forgiveness.
The Kural warns that one who thoughtlessly eats beyond measure will suffer diseases beyond measure; Kabir warns more broadly that wherever greed rules, ruin follows.
Greek Mythology
Ovid tells how Erysichthon, cursed by Ceres with Hunger for felling her sacred oak, eats ever more and craves ever more, as fire never refuses fuel, until he consumes his fortune and at last his own flesh.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Erysichthon), paraphrase [10]
The myth makes boundless eating a self-devouring fire that destroys the eater.
Erysichthon is the extreme of the Kural's man who eats beyond the fire's measure and suffers without measure.
Philosophy
“Growing bodies have the most innate heat; they therefore require the most food, for otherwise their bodies are wasted. In old persons the heat is feeble, and therefore they require little fuel, as it were, to the flame, for it would be extinguished by much.”
— Hippocrates, Aphorisms I.14 (tr. Francis Adams) [11]
Hippocrates measures how much food a body needs by its inner heat, and warns that too much fuel smothers a weak flame.
This is the Kural's thee alavu exactly: food must match the measure of the body's fire.

Real Life Example

King Henry I of England dies after a meal of lampreys
1 December 1135 · Lyons-la-Forêt, Normandy (now France)
Henry I, king of England and duke of Normandy, fell ill and died at Lyons-la-Forêt in Normandy on 1 December 1135. The chronicler Henry of Huntingdon recorded that the king's last illness followed a meal of lampreys, a fish he was fond of, eaten against medical advice. His death without a surviving legitimate son led to a long succession war between his nephew Stephen and his daughter Matilda.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The account of the king's last meal became a byword in English history for the danger of indulging an appetite against good advice.
The kural warns that one who thoughtlessly eats beyond what the body can digest will suffer disease beyond measure, and the chroniclers presented Henry's fatal illness in just those terms.
