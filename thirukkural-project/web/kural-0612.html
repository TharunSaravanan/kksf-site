Kural 612

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

வினைக்கண் வினைகெடல் ஓம்பல் வினைக்குறை தீர்ந்தாரின் தீர்ந்தன்று உலகு.
Vinaikkan vinaikedal ombal vinaikkurai Theerndhaarin theerndhandru ulagu.

Meaning

Once you start a task, be careful not to let your effort drop. The world leaves behind people who leave their work half-done.

Elementary

If you start building a sandcastle and walk away halfway, the waves wash it away and nobody even knows what you were making. Friends want to team up with the kid who finishes things. Keep going until it’s done.

University

The couplet plays on வினை three times: in the course of வினை (the undertaking), guard against கெடல் (the failing) of வினை (the exertion), because the world has already தீர்ந்தது (let go of) those who have let go of வினைக்குறை, the unfinished remainder of the work. Parimelazhagar reads the first வினை as the undertaking and the second as the effort (முயற்சி), which Mu. Varadarasanar follows; Solomon Pappaiah paraphrases the rhetorical jingle of the second line (தீர்ந்தாரின் தீர்ந்தன்று) as ‘the world lets go of those who let go’. Kalaignar glosses it with the proverb of the man who ‘leapt half-way across the well’, a vivid way of saying that half an effort is no effort at all. The text agrees in both editions consulted, thirukkural-thamizh.org merely printing the sandhi forms வினைகெட லோம்பல் and தீர்ந்தாரிற் றீர்ந்தன் றுலகு.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari’s Nitishataka sorts people into three kinds: the lowest never begin a task for fear of obstacles, the middling begin but stop when obstacles strike, and the best, though struck again and again by obstacles, never abandon what they have begun.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka (verse beginning prārabhyate na khalu) [3]
The famous Sanskrit verse (prārabhyate na khalu vighnabhayena nīcaiḥ) makes persistence through obstruction the mark of the noblest person.
The Kural’s warning against letting effort fail midway is precisely Bhartrhari’s distinction between the middling and the best.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it? Lest haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all that behold it begin to mock him, Saying, This man began to build, and was not able to finish.”
— Luke 14:28-30 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that a half-built tower makes its builder a public laughing-stock, so one must be ready to see a commitment through.
The mocking onlookers are the Kural’s ‘world’ that turns away from those who leave work unfinished.
Islam
“Do good deeds properly, sincerely and moderately and know that your deeds will not make you enter Paradise, and that the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant even if it were little.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6464 (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that steady, continuous action is dearer to God than bursts of effort that are then abandoned.
Both texts prize constancy of effort over enthusiastic beginnings that lapse.
Judaism
“Better is the end of a thing than the beginning thereof; And the patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:8 (JPS 1917) [6]
Koheleth values the completion of a matter above its start, and patient endurance above proud self-assurance.
The Kural likewise measures a person by whether the work is carried to its end.
Buddhism
The Buddha recalls that he resolved: let skin, sinews and bones remain and flesh and blood dry up, but there would be no relaxing of his persistence until he had attained what can be reached by human firmness, human persistence and human striving.
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.5 [7]
In the Anguttara Nikaya the Buddha names ‘not shrinking back in exertion’ as one of the two qualities that brought him to awakening.
The Pali purisa-viriya, ‘human (literally manly) effort’, is an exact counterpart of ஆள்வினை, and the refusal to relax midway is this kural’s whole point.
Jainism
“You have crossed the great ocean; why do you halt so near the shore? make haste to get on the other side; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira tells Gautama not to slacken when the goal is nearly won: stopping near the shore wastes the whole crossing.
The Kural's warning not to let effort slacken halfway, since the world abandons those who leave work unfinished, is matched by Mahavira's rebuke for halting near the shore.
Sikhism
“Let self-control be the furnace, and patience the goldsmith.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 8, Japji Sahib (pauri 38), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the closing pauri of Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak pictures the shaping of a true life as a goldsmith's long work at the furnace, carried out with patience and steady control.
The Kural warns against letting effort slacken halfway through a task; Japji Sahib makes patience the craftsman who keeps working until the piece is finished.
Greek Mythology
When some of Odysseus’ men eat the lotus and lose all wish to go home, he drags them weeping back to the ships and ties them under the benches so the voyage can go on.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.82-104 [10]
In Odyssey 9 the lotus-eaters’ gift is forgetfulness of the goal; Odysseus refuses to let the homeward labour stop halfway.
The lotus is the Kural’s வினைகெடல, the slackening of effort in mid-course, and Odysseus guards against it by force.
Philosophy
“In every affair consider what precedes and follows, and then undertake it. Otherwise you will begin with spirit; but not having thought of the consequences, when some of them appear you will shamefully desist.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion, ch. 29 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus warns that people who start eagerly without weighing the effort required give up in disgrace when difficulties appear, and compares them to children who play at one role after another but are nothing with their whole soul.
Both texts call it shameful to drop a task halfway, though Epictetus places the remedy in weighing the work before starting rather than in keeping one's effort up once started.

Real Life Example

Diderot carries the Encyclopedie through to completion after it is banned
1751-1772 · Paris, France
Denis Diderot was co-editor of the Encyclopedie, whose first volume appeared in 1751. In 1758 his co-editor Jean le Rond d'Alembert withdrew, and in 1759 the royal privilege was revoked, the volumes already published were condemned, and the pope placed the work on the Index. Diderot nevertheless kept working, the last ten volumes of text were issued together in 1765, and the final volumes of plates appeared in 1772.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Abandoned by his partner and banned by the authorities at the halfway point, Diderot spent more than a decade finishing one of the defining works of the Enlightenment.
The kural warns against letting effort slacken in the middle of a task, and Diderot, faced with every reason to stop halfway, carried the Encyclopedie through to its last volume.
