Kural 90

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

மோப்பக் குழையும் அனிச்சம் முகந்திரிந்து நோக்கக் குழையும் விருந்து.
Moppak kuzhaiyum anichcham mugandhirindhu, Nokkak kuzhaiyum virundhu.

Meaning

The anicham flower (a very delicate flower) wilts when you just smell it. A guest wilts just as fast when you look at them with a cold, turned-away face.

Elementary

There's a flower so delicate that it droops the moment you sniff it — you don't even have to touch it. This kural says guests are even more delicate than that. You don't have to say anything unkind; if your face just goes cold when you see them, they wilt.

University

The chapter closes on its most celebrated image. The அனிச்சம் (anicham) was proverbial in Sangam-era Tamil for extreme delicacy — Valluvar returns to it at Kural 1111 and 1120 in the love poems — and the point of the comparison is a fortiori: the flower at least requires contact with the nostrils, whereas the guest is wilted by nothing more than a look. Parimelalagar's commentary makes the structural argument explicit, listing the three indispensable things a host owes a guest in order: a welcoming face when the guest is still at a distance, kind words as he draws near, and material aid once he is received. Since the face operates first and at a distance, its failure destroys the encounter before hospitality can even begin — which is why Valluvar ends the chapter here rather than with any point about provisions. The couplet is thus the culmination of an argument that has been narrowing steadily from the household's economy (81) to its resources (85) to its liturgy (87) to, finally, a single expression. Note that the received manuscript tradition transmits the second line's verb in a corrupted form in several digital editions; the correct reading, confirmed by Parimelalagar's own gloss, is குழையும், repeating the verb from the first line.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Grass, room (for resting), water, and fourthly a kind word; these (things) never fail in the houses of good men.”
— Manusmriti 3.101 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East) [4]
Manu lists the irreducible minimum of hospitality that even the poorest household can always supply, and makes a kind word one of the four — placed alongside physical necessities as equally indispensable.
Both texts insist that the immaterial part of hospitality — the word, the face — is not an optional refinement but part of the minimum.
Christianity
“Use hospitality one to another without grudging.”
— 1 Peter 4:9 (King James Version) [5]
Peter's terse instruction to scattered Christian households addresses not whether to host but how — the Greek aneu gongusmou means literally 'without muttering,' targeting the grudging manner rather than the act.
Identifies exactly what the Kural identifies: hospitality can be technically performed and still be destroyed by the host's demeanour.
Islam
“Don't consider anything insignificant out of good things even if it is that you meet your brother with a cheerful countenance.”
— Sahih Muslim 2626, Book of Virtue and Good Manners [6]
Abu Dharr reports the Prophet elevating a facial expression to the status of a genuine good deed, on the grounds that its effect on the other person is real.
Both sayings insist that the host's countenance is not a trivial accompaniment to hospitality but an act in its own right.
Judaism
“Shammai says, ‘Make your Torah [study] fixed, say little and do much, and receive every person with a pleasant countenance.’”
— Pirkei Avot (Mishnah, Tractate Avot) 1:15 (Sefaria Community Translation) [7]
The maxim is preserved in Pirkei Avot under the name of Shammai, whose own temperament was proverbially severe — which the tradition notes made the instruction all the more pointed.
Nearly identical instruction to the Kural's: the reception is made or broken by the face that meets the arriving person.
Buddhism
In the Sappurisadāna Sutta the Buddha describes the five gifts of a person of integrity: he gives with conviction, gives attentively and with respect, gives at the proper time, gives with a sympathetic heart, and gives without injuring himself or others — the manner of the giving being specified in every case alongside the gift.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.148, Sappurisadāna Sutta (A Person of Integrity's Gifts) [8]
The sutta goes on to say that the results of a gift correspond to the way it was given, so that a gift given respectfully produces a very different fruit from the same object given carelessly.
Makes the Kural's point systematically: the object handed over is not what determines the act, the manner of handing it over is.
Jainism
“And the right manner for giving of gift consists in: respectful welcome, a high seat, washing the feet, worshipping, bowing, purity of mind, speech and body, and purity of food.”
— Amṛtacandra, Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya v. 168 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2012), as quoted in the explanatory note to Ratnakaraṇḍaka-śrāvakācāra v. 113 on wisdomlib.org; cf. Sarvārthasiddhi on Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.39 [9]
Jain rules for hosting a visiting monk put the manner of welcome first: respectful greeting, a seat, and purity of mind and speech, before the food itself. The Sarvarthasiddhi likewise ranks a gift by whether the guest is received 'with reverence or without reverence'.
Valluvar says a guest wilts at a face that turns aside even before the meal begins, and Jainism likewise makes the respectful welcome the first and essential part of the gift.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all. Do not break anyone's heart; these are all priceless jewels.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 129) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid teaches that every heart is a priceless jewel in which God dwells, so even one harsh word or slight can shatter it.
It shares the Kural's sense of how fragile the guest is: like the anicham flower, a heart can be wounded by a cold word or look alone.
Greek Mythology
Telemachus, seeing a stranger standing at the gate while the suitors feast, is ashamed that a guest should be kept waiting at the door; he goes straight to Athena in her disguise as Mentes, takes her right hand, relieves her of her spear and greets her before asking a single question about who she is.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.113–124 [11]
Homer devotes several lines to the immediacy of the greeting, and the poem's whole moral architecture depends on the contrast between this instant welcome and the suitors' indifference.
Dramatises the Kural's principle that the guest is won or lost in the first look, before any food appears.
Philosophy
Levinas argues that ethics begins not in reasoning but in the face-to-face encounter: the face of the other person makes a claim on me before I have decided anything, and he describes the fundamental ethical relation as a welcome extended to the other — subjectivity itself, he writes, is structured as hospitality.
— Emmanuel Levinas, Totality and Infinity (1961), Section I and Section III [12]
For Levinas the face is not a physical surface to be read but the point at which another person's claim becomes unavoidable, which is why his entire ethics is framed in terms of reception and welcome.
Supplies the philosophical case for the Kural's final image: the face is the exact site where hospitality succeeds or fails.

Real Life Example

Marian Anderson is refused Constitution Hall and sings at the Lincoln Memorial
9 April 1939 · Washington, D.C., United States
In 1939 the Daughters of the American Revolution, owners of Constitution Hall in Washington, refused to let the celebrated contralto Marian Anderson perform there because she was Black. First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from the organisation in protest, and with the help of Interior Secretary Harold Ickes an open-air concert was arranged on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. On Easter Sunday, 9 April 1939, Anderson sang there to an integrated crowd of about 75,000, with millions more listening on the radio.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
A great artist was turned away by the very hosts who should have welcomed her, and the snub became a landmark of the civil rights movement.
The kural says a guest withers at a host's averted face, and the hall's cold refusal of Marian Anderson shows how wounding an unwelcoming reception is.
