Kural 72

The Possession of Love · Anbudaimai · அன்புடைமை

அன்பிலார் எல்லாம் தமக்குரியர் அன்புடையார் என்பும் உரியர் பிறர்க்கு.
Anbilaar ellaam thamakkuriyar anbudaiyaar, Enbum uriyar pirarkku.

Meaning

People without love keep everything for themselves. People with love feel that even their own bones belong to others.

Elementary

Think about two kids with a bag of sweets. One says, "These are mine, nobody touch them." The other hands them around until the bag is empty, and is happier for it. This kural says a loving person gives so completely that they would give away their very bones if it helped someone else.

University

The couplet is built on a sharp antithesis of possession: எல்லாம் தமக்கு உரியர் ("all things belong to them") versus என்பும் உரியர் பிறர்க்கு ("even bones belong to others"). The word உரிமை/உரியர் is a legal term of ownership, so Valluvar is deliberately using the vocabulary of property law to describe self-giving. என்பு (enbu, bone) is the harshest, least giveable part of the body — the opposite of the money or grain one might expect a charity verse to mention — and its choice pushes generosity past the limit of comfort. Note also the chapter's internal sound-play: அன்பு (love) and என்பு (bone) are near-rhymes that Valluvar will exploit again in Kurals 73, 77 and 80, binding the ethical argument to the anatomy of the body. Many commentators, including Manakkudavar and Parimelalhagar, read the second line as covering not only the corpse but the whole living self placed at the disposal of others.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The sage Dadhīci, asked by the gods for a weapon that could destroy the demon Vṛtra, voluntarily gives up his own body so that his spine can be forged into the thunderbolt (vajra) that saves the worlds.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa (Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam) 6.10 — the story of Dadhīci [3]
Dadhīci's asceticism had made his bones uniquely hard, and rather than refuse, he yields his skeleton to the common good and abandons the body by yogic will.
This is Kural 72's second line taken absolutely literally: a loving man whose very bones belonged to others.
Christianity
“Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends.”
— Gospel of John 15:13, King James Version [4]
Jesus names self-surrender to the point of death as the definitive measure of love, spoken on the night before his own execution.
Matches the Kural's scale: love is measured not by what one gives away but by whether one's own body is still one's own.
Islam
“They give ˹the emigrants˺ preference over themselves even though they may be in need. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful.”
— Qurʾān 59:9 (Sūrat al-Ḥashr), The Clear Quran, trans. Dr. Mustafa Khattab [5]
The Qurʾān praises the Anṣār of Medina, who housed and fed the refugees from Mecca while themselves in poverty — the virtue called īthār, preferring others over oneself.
The verse draws the same line Valluvar draws: the loveless hoard for themselves, the loving treat what is theirs as belonging to others.
Judaism
“If I am not for myself, who is for me? But if I am for my own self only, what am I? And if not now, when?”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 1:14 [6]
Hillel the Elder's three-part aphorism refuses both self-neglect and self-enclosure, insisting that a life lived only for oneself forfeits its claim to be a human life at all.
Hillel's middle clause is Valluvar's first line: the person who is only for himself has become something less than a person.
Buddhism
In the Sasa Jātaka, the Bodhisatta born as a hare has nothing to offer a hungry traveller, so he shakes the insects from his fur and throws himself into the fire so that his own body may serve as the guest's meal.
— Sasa Jātaka (Jātaka no. 316), Pāli Jātaka collection [7]
The tale is the canonical Buddhist illustration of dāna-pāramī, the perfection of giving, where the gift is the giver's own flesh because nothing else is available.
The hare's body is not his own to keep — precisely the Kural's claim that the loving hold even their bones on behalf of others.
Jainism
“I will give you my own flesh, weighing it with the dove. Be satisfied. Do not die.”
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book V (Shântinâthacaritra), ch. IV, part 7, 'Story of the dove and hawk' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS) [8]
When a hawk demanded a dove that had fled to his lap, King Megharatha (an earlier birth of the Tîrthankara Shântinâtha) cut off his own flesh to balance the dove on the scales and finally climbed onto the scales himself.
Megharatha treats his own body as belonging to others, which is precisely Valluvar's picture of the loving, who count even their bones as others' property.
Sikhism
“Why do you say, "Mine, mine"? Look to God, who has given it to you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 50, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan diagnoses the possessive cry of mine, mine as a symptom of moh (attachment), one of the five thieves, and reminds the listener that everything held was given and is not truly owned.
It answers the first half of the Kural exactly: the loveless claim everything as their own, and Gurbani names that claim as the false root to be uprooted.
Greek Mythology
When the Fates allow Admetus to escape death if someone will die in his place, his parents refuse, and only his wife Alcestis consents; she dies for him and is later brought back from the underworld by Heracles.
— Euripides, Alcestis (438 BCE) [10]
Euripides' play turns on the fact that everyone in Admetus' household regards their own life as their own — except Alcestis, whose love makes hers transferable.
The play stages the Kural's two lines as two characters: the loveless keeping what is theirs, the loving surrendering even the body.
Philosophy
“It is true of the good man too that he does many acts for the sake of his friends and his country, and if necessary dies for them; for he will throw away both wealth and honours and in general the goods that are objects of competition.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.8, 1169a, trans. W. D. Ross [11]
Aristotle argues that proper self-love is not hoarding but spending — the good person secures what is truly noble by giving away the goods everyone else competes for.
Aristotle inverts ownership the way Valluvar does: the loving person's goods, and finally his life, are held on others' account.

Real Life Example

Maximilian Kolbe offers his life for a fellow prisoner at Auschwitz
29 July 1941 · Auschwitz, German-occupied Poland
After a prisoner escaped from Auschwitz in July 1941, ten men were selected to be starved to death in reprisal. When one of them, Franciszek Gajowniczek, pleaded for his wife and children, the Polish Franciscan priest Maximilian Kolbe stepped forward and asked to take his place, and the SS accepted. Kolbe died in the cellar of Block 11 on 14 August 1941; Gajowniczek survived the war, and Kolbe was canonised in 1982.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Kolbe gave up his own body and life for a stranger, the furthest extent to which a person can give themselves to another.
The kural says the loving regard even their own bones as belonging to others, and Kolbe literally gave his body in exchange for another man's life.
