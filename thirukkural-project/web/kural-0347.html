Kural 347

Renunciation · Thuravu · துறவு

பற்றி விடாஅ இடும்பைகள் பற்றினைப் பற்றி விடாஅ தவர்க்கு.
Patri vidaaa idumbaigal patrinaip Patri vidaaa thavarkku.

Meaning

Sorrows grab hold and never let go of people who hold tightly to the things they are attached to and will not let them go.

Elementary

If you grab a cactus and refuse to let go, it keeps hurting you. This kural says when you hang on tightly to things, troubles hang on tightly to you.

University

One of the Kural's most celebrated pieces of wordplay: the phrase patri vidaaa (“seizing, not releasing”) appears twice, first describing sorrows (idumbaigal) and then describing people who hold on to patru (attachment). The mirror structure enacts the moral — the grip we exert is the grip exerted upon us. Mu. Varadarasan specifies the attachments as the twofold 'I' and 'mine' of the previous couplet, making this verse the causal explanation of Kural 346. The same self-reinforcing loop is analysed in Buddhist accounts of craving, in the Gita's chain from dwelling on objects to attachment to desire, and in Spinoza's description of human bondage to the passions.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna traces a chain reaction: dwelling on objects breeds attachment, attachment breeds desire, and desire breeds anger (and, in the next verse, ruin).
Both show attachment generating its own suffering in a chain that tightens around the one who clings.
Christianity
“But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”
— 1 Timothy 6:9-10 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that clinging to wealth traps people in a snare and ends with them piercing themselves with many sorrows.
'Pierced themselves through with many sorrows' is the Kural's image exactly: the one who clings is held fast by grief of his own making.
Islam
“Competition in [worldly] increase diverts you Until you visit the graveyards.”
— Quran 102:1-2 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran describes people so gripped by the race for more that it holds them until death.
The grip of acquisitiveness never lets go until the grave — the Kural's picture of attachment holding its holder fast.
Judaism
“He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver; nor he that loveth abundance with increase: this is also vanity.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:10 (KJV) [4]
Qoheleth observes that loving wealth produces endless dissatisfaction rather than contentment.
Clinging to wealth produces a restless dissatisfaction that never releases its grip.
Buddhism
“Whoever is overcome by this wretched and sticky craving, his sorrows grow like grass after the rains.”
— Dhammapada 335 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha calls craving 'sticky' and says the sorrows of one it overcomes multiply like grass after rain.
'Sticky' craving and multiplying sorrows mirror the Kural's image of attachment and grief each refusing to let go.
Jainism
“As the crane is produced from an egg, and the egg is produced from a crane, so they call desire the origin of delusion, and delusion the origin of desire.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 185) [7]
Mahāvīra describes a closed loop: desire produces delusion, and delusion produces desire again, like crane and egg. Elsewhere in the same lecture this cycle is shown to generate karma and misery.
Valluvar's mirrored phrase, sorrows that seize and never let go of those who seize and never let go, enacts the same self-feeding cycle that the crane-and-egg image describes.
Sikhism
“In that pool, people have made their homes, but the water there is as hot as fire! In the swamp of emotional attachment, their feet cannot move. I have seen them drowning there.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa (in the So Purakh section of Rehras), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak pictures the world as a burning pool and emotional attachment (moh) as a swamp that holds the feet fast until people drown.
The Kural says sorrows grip and never release those who grip their attachments; Guru Nanak shows the same grip from the other side, a swamp of attachment that will not let the feet go.
Greek Mythology
Granted his wish that everything he touched should turn to gold, King Midas finds that his food, drink and everything he holds turns to metal, and he begs to be freed from the gift he craved.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 [9]
In Ovid's telling, Midas's greed becomes a curse that clings to his very hands until the god releases him.
Midas grasps at gold, and the thing he grasps grasps back — the Kural's 'clinging clings to you' in mythic form.
Philosophy
Spinoza names human weakness in moderating and restraining the emotions 'bondage', explaining that a person ruled by passions is not his own master but is subject to fortune.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part IV, Preface (tr. R. H. M. Elwes) [10]
In Part IV of the Ethics, Spinoza analyses how passive affects — attachments to external things — hold people captive.
Like the Kural, Spinoza sees our attachments as the very chains by which we are held.

Real Life Example

Qin Shi Huang's search for an elixir of immortality
219-210 BCE · Qin China
Qin Shi Huang, who unified China in 221 BCE and became its first emperor, became preoccupied with escaping death. In 219 BCE he sent an expedition to the islands of the Eastern Sea to find an elixir of immortality, and when it failed he repeatedly summoned magicians to his court. He died in 210 BCE while on an inspection tour, and his dynasty collapsed within a few years.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The most powerful man in China could not loosen his grip on life and power, and his fear of losing them drove years of anxious and fruitless searching.
Valluvar warns that sorrows cling to those who cling to their attachments, and the First Emperor's grasping after endless life brought him restless anxiety rather than peace.
