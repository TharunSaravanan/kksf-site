Kural 1225

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

காலைக்குச் செய்தநன்று என்கொல் எவன்கொல்யான் மாலைக்குச் செய்த பகை.
Kaalaikkuch cheydhanandru enkol evankolyaan maalaikkuch cheydha pakai.

Meaning

What good did I ever do for the morning, that it is gentle with me? And what wrong did I do to the evening, that it hurts me so much?

Elementary

Have you ever felt that one part of the day is friendly and another part is mean to you? The wife feels mornings are kind and evenings are cruel, and she asks, puzzled, what she did to deserve it.

University

The couplet is a pair of rhetorical questions built on the parallel kālaikku / mālaikku (‘to the morning / to the evening’) and naṉṟu / pakai (‘a good turn / enmity’): the wife treats the two times of day as persons who repay her for imagined past conduct. Varadarasanar glosses pakai as ‘a hostile evil’ done to the evening, and Kalaignar adds the implied reason: love-pain grows sharpest when evening comes. The playful logic of reciprocity echoes the Kural’s ethical chapters on returning good for good, now applied absurdly to the hours. Pope: ‘O morn, how have I won thy grace? thou bring’st relief / O eve, why art thou foe! thou dost renew my grief.’ The text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons. They arise from sense perception, O scion of Bharata, and one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.14 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami, As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that pleasure and pain come and go with the turning of times and must be borne with equanimity.
The wife feels the morning as friend and the evening as foe; the Gita answers that such alternations arise from perception and pass in their season.
Christianity
“It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.”
— Lamentations 3:22-23 (KJV) [4]
In the midst of lament, the poet finds that mercy returns fresh with each morning.
The Kural’s wife likewise feels the morning as a kindly presence set against a cruel evening.
Islam
“By the morning brightness, And [by] the night when it covers with darkness, Your Lord has not taken leave of you, [O Muhammad], nor has He detested [you].”
— Qur’an 93:1-3 (Saheeh International) [5]
Revealed when the Prophet feared he had been abandoned, Surat ad-Duha sets morning against night and reassures him he has not been forsaken.
The wife sets morning against evening and feels herself abandoned; the Qur’an uses the same pair of times to deny abandonment.
Judaism
“For His anger is but for a moment, His favour is for a life-time; weeping may tarry for the night, but joy cometh in the morning.”
— Psalms 30:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria; 30:5 in KJV numbering) [6]
The Psalmist sets the weeping of the night against the joy of the morning.
This is the Kural’s very contrast: night brings tears and the morning brings relief.
Buddhism
The opening verses of the Dhammapada teach that the mind goes before all mental states: if one acts or speaks with a corrupted mind, suffering follows as the wheel follows the ox’s foot, and with a pure mind, happiness follows like a shadow.
— Dhammapada 1-2 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhism locates the source of pain and joy in the mind, not in outside conditions.
The wife asks what she did to the evening; the Dhammapada would answer that the hour is neutral and the suffering is shaped within.
Jainism
“He who passionately hates (a colour), will at the same moment suffer pain. It is the fault of an undisciplined man that he is annoyed (by a colour); it is not the ‘colour’ itself that annoys him.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira teaches that the objects of the senses do not themselves cause pain; our passion toward them does.
The wife asks what she did to make the evening her enemy; Jain teaching answers that the evening has done nothing, the pain lies in the mind’s attachment and aversion.
Sikhism
“When the sun rises, she sees her beloved with her eyes; she bows and touches his feet.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1273, Raag Malaar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak completes the image of the chakvi: parted from her mate all night, she is reunited with him when the sun comes up.
The Kural's wife asks what kindness she did the morning that it treats her gently, and what wrong she did the evening that it torments her; the chakvi's lot shows the same pattern, with nightfall bringing separation and sunrise bringing reunion.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus is favoured throughout his wanderings by Athena yet pursued by the unrelenting anger of Poseidon, so that one god helps and another harms him, and he endures years of suffering for a grudge he only half understands.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.19-21, 1.68-79 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer’s hero lives between a friendly power and a hostile one.
The Kural’s wife feels the same split, with a kindly morning and a hostile evening, and asks what she has done to earn each.
Philosophy
Epictetus teaches that people are disturbed not by things themselves but by their judgements about things, so that one should blame neither others nor circumstances for one’s distress.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (paraphrase; cf. tr. George Long) [11]
The Stoic locates the source of suffering in our opinions rather than in events.
The evening is not truly the wife’s enemy; on Epictetus’s view it is her judgement of the evening, coloured by absence, that wounds her.

Real Life Example

Michitsuna's Mother records lonely nights in the Kagerō Diary
954-974 · Heian-kyō (Kyoto), Japan
The Heian noblewoman known as Michitsuna's Mother recorded twenty years of her life, from 954 to 974, in the Kagerō Diary, centred on her marriage to the powerful courtier Fujiwara no Kaneie, which ended in divorce. One of her poems, later chosen as poem 53 of the anthology Hyakunin Isshu, describes lying alone through the hours of the night until daylight comes and asks whether anyone can understand how empty such a night is.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The diary is one of the earliest autobiographies by a woman and gives a frank picture of a wife's long waits for a husband who seldom came.
The kural's wife finds the morning kind and the evening cruel, and this poet's most famous poem dwells on a night of lonely waiting that ends only when daylight comes.
