Kural 1114

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

காணின் குவளை கவிழ்ந்து நிலன்நோக்கும் மாணிழை கண்ணொவ்வேம் என்று.
Kaanin kuvalai kavizhndhu nilannoakkum maanizhai kannovvaem endru.

Meaning

If the blue water lilies could see her, they would hang their heads and stare at the ground, saying, 'We cannot match the eyes of this girl with fine jewels.'

Elementary

Picture the prettiest flowers in a garden suddenly able to see. They look at a girl’s lovely eyes, feel shy, and droop their heads, as if saying, 'We can’t compete with that!'

University

The conceit (a form of what later poetics calls tharkurippetram, attributing a feeling to something that has none) gives the kuvalai (குவளை, blue water-lily, the stock comparison for eyes) the power of sight and shame: it would kavizhndhu nilan nokkum (கவிழ்ந்து நிலன் நோக்கும், 'bend down and look at the ground'), the gesture of a defeated or abashed person. The botanical observation behind it is that the kuvalai flower droops on its stalk. Maanizhai (மாணிழை, 'she of excellent jewels') is a standard epithet for the heroine. Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah agree on the sense; Pappaiah adds 'out of shame' (நாணத்தால்). In the Parimelazhagar tradition this kural follows 1112 as its answer: the flower itself knows it cannot match her eyes. Pope (who translates kuvalai as 'lotus'): 'The lotus, seeing her, with head demiss, the ground would eye.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The meanest vesture glows / On beauty that enchants: / The lotus lovelier shows / Amid dull water-plants;”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
The king, seeing Shakuntala in her plain bark dress, says her beauty outshines its setting as a lotus outshines the dull water-plants around it.
Both poets measure the beloved against the water-flowers, and in both the flowers come off second.
Christianity
“How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! how much better is thy love than wine! and the smell of thine ointments than all spices!”
— Song of Solomon 4:10 (KJV) [4]
The lover says his beloved surpasses the finest wine and every spice.
Like the Kural, the Song sets the beloved above the best things of nature, which lose by the comparison.
Islam
“And when they saw him, they greatly admired him and cut their hands and said, ‘Perfect is Allāh! This is not a man; this is none but a noble angel.’”
— Qur’an 12:31 (Sahih International) [5]
The women of the city, seeing Joseph, are so overcome by his beauty that they cut their hands and declare him beyond any human standard.
As the Kural’s flowers admit defeat before her eyes, the onlookers here admit no human comparison can hold.
Judaism
“all people compared to Sarah are like a monkey compared to a human, as Sarah was exceedingly beautiful”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 58a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
The Talmud expresses Sarah’s beauty by saying that everyone else, set beside her, looks like a monkey beside a person.
Both texts praise beauty by showing how badly anything else fares in the comparison.
Buddhism
“Radiant, brilliant like jewels, my eyes: With age, they’re no longer splendid. The truth of the Truth-speaker’s words doesn’t change.”
— Therigatha 13.1, Ambapali (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Ambapali remembers that her eyes once shone like jewels and notes that age has dimmed them.
The Kural’s eyes outshine the water-lilies; Ambapali’s jewel-bright eyes remind the reader that such splendour too will fade.
Jainism
“If you owned the beauty of Vaisramana, the pleasing manners of Nalakubara, if you were like Purandara himself, I should have no desire for you.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.41 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The nun Rajimati tells the tempted monk that even the beauty of the gods would not move her from her vows.
The Kural sets beauty above every rival; the Jain heroine sets her vow above every beauty.
Sikhism
“In the Fear of God, the sun shines, and in the Fear of God, the moon reflects.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 464, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak describes the whole of nature, sun, moon, wind and water, as moving in awe of God.
The Kural imagines the water-lilies bowing their heads in shame before the beloved's eyes; Guru Nanak pictures all of nature humbled in awe before God.
Greek Mythology
“then is Leto proud at seeing her daughter stand a full head taller than the others, and eclipse the loveliest amid a whole bevy of beauties), even so did the girl outshine her handmaids.”
— Homer, Odyssey 6 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer compares Nausicaa among her maids to Artemis among the nymphs, outshining even the loveliest.
Like the Kural’s flowers that bow their heads, the other beauties here are eclipsed by the one.
Philosophy
In the Hippias Major Socrates cites Heraclitus: the most beautiful ape is ugly compared with the human race, and the most beautiful girl would look plain beside a god.
— Plato, Hippias Major 289a-b (paraphrase) [11]
Plato uses the example to show that beauty judged by comparison always depends on what it is compared with.
The Kural plays the same comparative game in reverse: the lovely kuvalai becomes plain beside her eyes.

Real Life Example

The bust of Nefertiti is found at Amarna
6 December 1912 · Amarna, Egypt; now in the Neues Museum, Berlin, Germany
On 6 December 1912 a German excavation led by the archaeologist Ludwig Borchardt found a painted limestone bust of Queen Nefertiti in the ruins of the workshop of the court sculptor Thutmose at Amarna, Egypt. The bust is about 3,300 years old, and Nefertiti's name is usually translated as the beautiful one has come. Borchardt wrote in his diary of the find's liveliness, and the bust is now displayed in the Neues Museum in Berlin.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The bust became one of the most famous images of beauty from the ancient world.
The kural imagines that the blue water-lilies, if they could see the beloved's eyes, would hang their heads in defeat; Nefertiti's face is a real-world case of beauty that, more than three thousand years later, still makes admirers feel that nothing else compares.
