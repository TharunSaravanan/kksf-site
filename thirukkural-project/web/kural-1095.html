Kural 1095

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

குறிக்கொண்டு நோக்காமை அல்லால் ஒருகண் சிறக்கணித்தாள் போல நகும்.
Kurikkondu nokkaamai allaal orukan sirakkaniththaal poala nagum.

Meaning

She does not look straight at me. Yet she glances sideways, as if narrowing one eye, and smiles to herself.

Elementary

Sometimes a friend pretends not to watch you, but you catch them looking out of the corner of their eye with a little grin. They are not really ignoring you; they are just being shy and playful.

University

Kuṟikkoṇṭu nōkkāmai (குறிக்கொண்டு நோக்காமை) is ‘not looking with a fixed aim’, that is, avoiding a direct gaze. The difficult word ciṟakkaṇittāḷ (சிறக்கணித்தாள்) is glossed by Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar as ‘narrowing one eye’ (oru kaṇṇaic curukki), while Puliyur Kesikan takes it as tilting one eye to one side; Pope’s ‘smiling side-long glance’ follows the second sense, and the explanatory gloss on ytamizh renders it as looking ‘with a half-closed eye’. Either way the commentators read the sidelong smile as a kurippu that she returns his love while seeming not to. The Tamil is the same on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Her glance was loving–but ’twas not for me; / Her step was slow–’twas grace, not coquetry.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act II (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
The king tries to talk himself out of reading Shakuntala’s side glances as love, then admits that a lover reads every sign in his own favor.
The puzzle of whether an indirect glance means love is exactly the puzzle the Kural answers.
Christianity
“There be three things which are too wonderful for me, yea, four which I know not: The way of an eagle in the air; the way of a serpent upon a rock; the way of a ship in the midst of the sea; and the way of a man with a maid.”
— Proverbs 30:18-19 (KJV) [4]
The sage confesses that the hidden ways of love between a man and a woman are beyond his understanding.
The Kural’s half-closed-eye smile is one of those mysterious ‘ways’ that only lovers can read.
Islam
“In them are women limiting [their] glances.”
— Qur’an 55:56 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the companions of paradise as women who keep their gaze restrained.
The Kural’s beloved likewise does not look straight at her lover, though her restraint hides a smile.
Judaism
“Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair; Thine eyes are as doves behind thy veil.”
— Song of Songs 4:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover praises the beloved’s eyes glimpsed half-hidden behind her veil.
Eyes that look out half-hidden recall the Kural’s narrowed, sidelong glance.
Buddhism
In the Zen tradition’s Flower Sermon, the Buddha silently holds up a flower; only Mahakashyapa understands and answers with a smile, and the Buddha says the true teaching has passed to him.
— Wumenguan (The Gateless Gate), case 6, ‘The Buddha Holds Up a Flower’ [7]
A wordless smile is shown as the sign of perfect understanding between two people.
As in the Kural, a quiet smile carries a meaning that words do not.
Jainism
“A Sramana, engaged in penance, should not allow himself to watch the shape, beauty, coquetry, laughter, prattle, gestures, and glances of women, nor retain a recollection of them in his mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain monk is told not to dwell on the playful glances and laughter that the Kural celebrates.
Jain scripture names the same playful glance and laughter as the Kural, but as things a renunciant must not watch.
Sikhism
“The Truth does not remain hidden, even though one may try to hide it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 232, Raag Gauri, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gauri, Guru Amar Das says that the divine light within a person shows itself even when he tries to keep it concealed.
The girl in the Kural will not look straight at her lover, yet her sidelong smile gives her away; Guru Amar Das says what is truly within cannot stay hidden.
Greek Mythology
“This honour she has from the beginning, and this is the portion allotted to her amongst men and undying gods, – the whisperings of maidens and smiles and deceits with sweet delight and love and graciousness.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 203-206 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod assigns to Aphrodite the realm of maidens’ whispers, smiles and playful deceits.
The Kural’s sly half-smile, pretending not to look, belongs to exactly this province of Aphrodite.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Diotima describes Eros as bold and resourceful, a clever hunter always weaving some scheme to win what is beautiful.
— Plato, Symposium 203d (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Love, for Diotima, works by stratagem and indirection.
The beloved’s pretended indifference with a sidelong smile is one of love’s small stratagems.

Real Life Example

Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa and her famous smile
about 1503–1519 · Florence, Italy (now in the Louvre, Paris)
Leonardo da Vinci began the portrait known as the Mona Lisa in Florence around 1503 and worked on it for years. The sitter is traditionally identified as Lisa Gherardini, wife of the merchant Francesco del Giocondo. Her slight, enigmatic smile, painted with soft sfumato shading, has fascinated viewers for centuries.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The quiet, half-hidden smile has made it probably the most famous painting in the world.
The kural describes a girl who does not look straight at her lover yet smiles quietly to herself; the Mona Lisa's restrained, private smile is the best-known image of such a look.
