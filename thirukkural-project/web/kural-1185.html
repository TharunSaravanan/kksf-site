Kural 1185

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

உவக்காண்எம் காதலர் செல்வார் இவக்காண்என் மேனி பசப்பூர் வது.
uvakkaan-em kaadhalar selvaar ivakkaan-en maeni pasappoor vadhu

Meaning

Look there, my lover is walking away. And look here, the paleness is already spreading over my body.

Elementary

Picture an ice cream on a hot day: the moment you take it out of the freezer, it starts to melt. The wife says that the moment her husband started walking away, before he was even out of sight, her face started going pale.

University

The couplet’s force lies in two deictic phrases: uvakkāṇ (உவக்காண், ‘see, over there’, using the mid-distance demonstrative u-) and ivakkāṇ (இவக்காண், ‘see, right here’), setting the departing husband and the arriving pallor side by side as simultaneous events; pasappu ūrvatu (பசப்பு ஊர்வது) is ‘the creeping of pallor’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan keep the present tense (‘there he goes; here it spreads’), whereas Solomon Pappaiah reads it as a memory of how, even in earlier partings, the pallor came the instant he left, so that the present absence must be worse. Pope’s version, ‘My lover there went forth to roam; / This pallor of my frame usurps his place at home’, adds the idea of the pallor taking his place. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Akrura takes Krishna away to Mathura, the gopis of Vraja stand watching his chariot as it goes, gazing after it until the banner and even the dust it raised can no longer be seen, and are left stricken and lamenting.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.39 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata dwells on the exact moment of the beloved’s departure and the grief that begins as he recedes from sight.
The Kural catches the same instant: ‘there he goes’ and, at once, ‘here comes the pallor’.
Christianity
“And when he had spoken these things, while they beheld, he was taken up; and a cloud received him out of their sight.”
— Acts 1:9 (KJV) [4]
The disciples watch their beloved master depart before their eyes until he is hidden from them.
The Kural’s ‘look there, he goes’ freezes the same moment of watching the loved one leave.
Islam
Rumi opens the Masnavi with the reed-flute, which complains of separation and says that ever since it was cut from the reed-bed its lament has made men and women weep.
— Rumi, Masnavi I.1-2 (paraphrase of R.A. Nicholson’s translation) [5]
For the Sufi poet, the moment of being cut off from the beloved source is where all longing and lament begins.
The Kural places the onset of the pallor at that same moment of severance, as the beloved walks away.
Judaism
“I opened to my beloved; But my beloved had turned away, and was gone. My soul failed me when he spoke. I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Songs 5:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The bride finds her beloved gone and at once her soul fails within her.
In both texts the beloved’s going and the lover’s collapse happen in the same breath.
Buddhism
On his wedding day the Buddha’s half-brother Nanda follows the Buddha to the monastery carrying his bowl, while his bride Janapada Kalyani calls after him to come back quickly; he is ordained and afterwards pines for her.
— Nanda Sutta, Udana 3.2, and Dhammapada commentary on verses 13-14 (paraphrase) [7]
The canonical story of Nanda captures a bride watching her groom walk away on the very day of their union.
The scene is the Kural’s ‘there he goes’ seen from the bride’s doorway, though the Buddhist story follows the monk rather than her grief.
Jainism
Riding in his wedding procession to marry Rajimati, Arishtanemi hears the cries of animals penned for the wedding feast, gives his ornaments to his charioteer and turns away to renounce the world, leaving the bride whose wedding was about to begin.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.13-23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [8]
In the Jain account the groom turns back at the very threshold of marriage out of compassion for living beings.
Rajimati’s sorrow begins the moment her bridegroom turns away, the same instant the Kural captures.
Sikhism
“If her Husband Lord does not return home, how can the soul-bride find peace? Her body is wasting away with the sorrow of separation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1108, Raag Tukhaari, Barah Maha, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha in Raag Tukhaari, Guru Nanak describes the soul-bride whose body visibly wastes away while her Husband Lord stays away.
The Kural's wife watches her husband walk off and sees the pallor creep over her body at once; Guru Nanak likewise shows the body of the separated bride giving way the moment the Beloved is absent.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s Heroides Laodamia writes to Protesilaus that as his ship sailed out of Aulis she watched it while she could, and when it vanished she fell senseless and turned pale, lifeless as the dead.
— Ovid, Heroides 13 (Laodamia to Protesilaus) (paraphrase) [10]
Laodamia describes her body failing and paling at the very moment her husband’s ship disappeared.
Her letter is almost the Kural verbatim in narrative form: there goes the husband, here comes the pallor.
Philosophy
Plato describes the lover’s soul as warmed and growing its wings while the beloved is present, but when they are parted the passages dry up and close, and the soul is stung all over and in anguish until it sees the beloved again.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-e (paraphrase) [11]
In the Phaedrus the lover’s pain begins as soon as the beloved is absent, as an almost physical change.
The Kural records the same immediacy: absence begins and the bodily change, the pallor, begins with it.

Real Life Example

Ovid's last night in Rome before exile
8 CE · Rome, Roman Empire (modern Italy)
In 8 CE the emperor Augustus banished the poet Ovid to Tomis on the Black Sea (modern Constanta, Romania). In his Tristia, poems largely addressed to his wife, Ovid describes his last night in Rome: his wife weeping in his arms and praying at the household shrine, his own repeated delays at the threshold, and his wife clinging to him and asking to follow him into exile. When he refused and left, she collapsed in grief.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ovid's account is one of the most vivid surviving descriptions from the ancient world of the moment of parting between husband and wife.
The kural sets side by side the husband walking away and the pallor already spreading over the wife, and Ovid records his wife falling in grief at the very moment he went out of the door.
