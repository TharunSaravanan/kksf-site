Kural 364

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

தூஉய்மை என்பது அவாவின்மை மற்றது வாஅய்மை வேண்ட வரும்.
Thoouymai enbadhu avaavinmai matradhu Vaaaimai venda varum.

Meaning

Being pure means nothing other than being free from desire, and that freedom comes from longing for the truth.

Elementary

A glass of water looks clear when nothing is stirring it up. Your heart is “clean” when wanting isn’t stirring it. The way to keep it calm is to love what is true more than what is shiny.

University

Valluvar defines thooymai (purity) psychologically rather than ritually, not by baths, diet or caste observances but as avaavinmai, the absence of craving. The second line adds a cause: freedom from craving arises from desiring vaaymai. Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasan gloss vaaymai as meipporul, ultimate Reality, while Karunanidhi reads it as truthfulness. Either way the chapter keeps its strategy of redirecting desire, letting one longing, for truth, push out all lesser ones. The lengthened forms thoouymai and vaaaymai (alapedai, a metrical lengthening) bind the two key terms by sound as well as sense.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As fire is covered by smoke, as a mirror is covered by dust, or as the embryo is covered by the womb, the living entity is similarly covered by different degrees of this lust.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.38 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna compares desire to smoke over a fire or dust on a mirror, something that clouds the soul’s natural clarity.
Both treat purity as what remains when desire is removed. Take away the dust of craving and the mirror is clean.
Christianity
“Seeing ye have purified your souls in obeying the truth through the Spirit unto unfeigned love of the brethren, see that ye love one another with a pure heart fervently:”
— 1 Peter 1:22 (KJV) [4]
Peter tells believers that their souls have been purified by obeying the truth.
This matches the Kural’s causal chain very closely: purity comes by way of truth.
Islam
“He has succeeded who purifies it, And he has failed who instills it [with corruption].”
— Qur’an 91:9-10 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says success belongs to the one who purifies the soul (tazkiyat al-nafs) of its base inclinations.
Both make purity an inner cleansing of the self, not an outward ritual.
Judaism
“Who shall ascend into the hill of the LORD? or who shall stand in his holy place? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart; who hath not lifted up his soul unto vanity, nor sworn deceitfully.”
— Psalm 24:3-4 (KJV) [4]
The psalm defines the pure person as one who has not set his soul on empty things and has not sworn falsely.
Both tie purity to not craving empty things and to truthfulness, which are the two halves of the Kural.
Buddhism
“‘All conditioned things are impermanent’ — when one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering. This is the path to purification.”
— Dhammapada 277 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada teaches that seeing the truth about impermanence makes a person turn away from craving, and that this is purification.
This has the same logic as the Kural: seeing truth leads to the end of craving, and that is purity.
Jainism
“Purity–śauca–is freedom from greed (lobha).”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 9.6, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Among the ten supreme virtues (daśa-dharma) in Tattvārtha Sūtra 9.6, Pūjyapāda's classic commentary defines purity not as ritual cleanness but simply as the absence of greed. Supreme truthfulness (uttama satya) is listed as a companion virtue in the same sūtra.
Valluvar defines purity as freedom from desire rather than as ritual washing. The Jain commentary gives almost the same definition, making śauca identical with freedom from lobha (greed).
Sikhism
“They are not called pure, who sit down after merely washing their bodies. Only they are pure, O Nanak, within whose minds the Lord abides.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 472, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak rejects ritual bathing as the test of purity and says only those in whose minds God dwells are pure.
The Kural defines purity as freedom from desire rather than bathing, diet or caste rule; Guru Nanak likewise moves purity from the washed body to the inner mind.
Greek Mythology
In Socrates’ myth of the winged soul, souls follow the gods to gaze on true Being in the ‘plain of truth’, and their wings are nourished by it. Souls weighed down by base appetite lose their wings and fall.
— Plato, Phaedrus 246a-248c (paraphrase) [9]
The soul stays pure and winged by feeding on truth, and it falls when appetite drags it down.
Like the Kural, it links a pure soul to longing for truth and a fallen soul to craving.
Philosophy
In the Phaedo, Socrates describes philosophy as a “purification” (katharsis). The lover of wisdom separates the soul from bodily pleasures and desires, which ‘nail’ the soul to the body, so that it can grasp truth itself.
— Plato, Phaedo 67a-c, 82d-83d (paraphrase) [10]
For Plato, purity means freedom from bodily cravings, and it comes through the love of wisdom and truth.
This is almost the Kural’s formula: purity is freedom from desire, and it is reached by desiring truth.

Real Life Example

Ludwig Wittgenstein gives away his inherited fortune
1919-1920 · Vienna and Lower Austria, Austria
Ludwig Wittgenstein, son of one of the richest industrial families in Austria-Hungary, inherited a fortune when his father died in 1913. After serving in the First World War, during which he completed the work later published as the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, he gave away his share of the family fortune. He then trained as a teacher and worked in village schools in Lower Austria in the early 1920s, living very simply.
— Sources: [11] [12]
One of the great philosophers of the twentieth century chose plain living over wealth as he devoted himself to questions of truth and meaning.
The kural says purity is freedom from desire, and that this freedom comes from longing for truth; Wittgenstein's giving up of riches alongside his single-minded search for clarity offers a modern parallel.
