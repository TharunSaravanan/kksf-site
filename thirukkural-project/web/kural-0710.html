Kural 710

The Knowledge of Signals · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

நுண்ணியம் என்பார் அளக்குங்கோல் காணுங்கால் கண்ணல்லது இல்லை பிற.
Nunniyam enpaar alakkungol kaanungaal Kannalladhu illai pira.

Meaning

If you look closely at people who say they can read others well, you will find their only measuring stick is the eyes. They use nothing else.

Elementary

A builder uses a ruler to measure wood. A person who is good at understanding others uses a different ruler: their eyes. By watching carefully, they measure what someone is feeling.

University

Nunniyam enpaar are ‘those who say, we are subtle (nunmai)’, i.e., who claim fine discernment. Alakkungol is ‘the rod with which they measure’ (alakkum kol), and kaanungaal ‘when one examines it’. Parimelazhagar, as usual, applies it to ministers: the measuring rod of those who claim to know others’ minds turns out, on inspection, to be nothing but their eyes. There is a double sense that commentators note: the minister measures by his own eye, and what he measures is the other person’s eye (continuing 709). The closing kan alladhu illai pira (‘there is nothing other than the eye’) sums up the chapter, which moves from praise of the perceptive (701-704) to the face (706-707) and finally the eye (705, 709-710) as the decisive sign. Sandhi text: நுண்ணிய மென்பா ரளக்குங்கோல் காணுங்காற் / கண்ணல்ல தில்லை பிற.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A well-known verse of the Panchatantra (also in the Hitopadesha) says that even an animal understands what is spoken, but the wise infer what is unspoken, for the fruit of intelligence is knowing the gestures of others (parengita-jnana-phala hi buddhayah).
— Panchatantra, Book I (Mitrabheda); Hitopadesha [3]
Classical Sanskrit niti literature defines intelligence itself as the ability to read others’ unspoken signs.
Both texts make reading signs the real measure of a subtle mind.
Christianity
“The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light.”
— Matthew 6:22 (KJV) [4]
Jesus calls the eye the lamp of the body, the organ through which the whole person is lit or darkened.
Both texts give the eye a unique, measuring role over the whole person.
Islam
“Indeed in that are signs for those who discern.”
— Qur’an 15:75 (Saheeh International) [5]
The word translated ‘those who discern’ (al-mutawassimin) means those who read marks, and classical exegetes connected it to firasa, the believer’s insight into people.
The Qur’an honours those who read signs, the same people the Kural says measure by the eye.
Judaism
The Mishnah says that the disciples of Abraham have a good eye, while the disciples of Balaam have an evil eye, making the eye the index of a person’s character.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 5:19 [6]
In rabbinic idiom the eye (ayin tovah, ayin ra‘ah) reveals whether a person is generous or grudging.
Like the Kural, the rabbis treat the eye as the clearest measure of what lies inside.
Buddhism
The Buddha speaks of three eyes: the eye of flesh, the divine eye, and the unsurpassed eye of discernment (pañña-cakkhu).
— Itivuttaka 61, Cakkhu Sutta [7]
Real seeing, for the Buddha, is the eye of wisdom that sees things as they are.
Both texts make ‘the eye’, rightly trained, the true instrument of subtle knowledge.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra classes knowledge gained through the senses and mind (mati) and through testimony (śruta) as parokṣa, indirect, and keeps the name pratyakṣa, direct, for knowledge the soul has without the senses: clairvoyance, knowledge of other minds, and omniscience.
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 1.11–12 (cf. tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [8]
For Jainism, any judgment that relies on the eye is a mediated and limited instrument. Only the purified soul knows another being directly.
The Kural says the eye is the only measuring rod even the subtle have, while Jainism agrees that ordinary judges rely on the senses but holds that a higher, eye-free knowing exists.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, those eyes are different, which behold my Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 577, Raag Vadhans, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that truly seeing eyes are of another kind altogether: the eyes that see the Beloved.
The Kural says the only measuring rod of the truly discerning is the eye; Gurbani also makes the quality of one's seeing the real measure, though of the divine rather than of other people.
Greek Mythology
“Achilles turned in amaze, and by the fire that flashed from her eyes at once knew that she was Minerva.”
— Homer, Iliad 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Achilles recognises the disguised goddess Athena at once by the look of her eyes alone.
For Achilles, as for Valluvar’s discerning minister, the eye is the one sure test.
Philosophy
The ancient Physiognomonics attributed to Aristotle holds that character can be inferred from bodily signs, and that the region around the eyes, forehead, head and face is the most reliable place to look.
— Pseudo-Aristotle, Physiognomonica 814b [11]
Greek physiognomy made the eyes the chief evidence for reading a person.
This is a systematic version of the Kural’s claim that the eye is the discerner’s only measuring rod.

Real Life Example

Bernard Berenson sets out a connoisseurship of the trained eye
1895 · Florence, Italy
The Harvard-educated art critic Bernard Berenson, who lived most of his life near Florence, became one of the most famous connoisseurs of Italian Renaissance painting, known for a discriminating eye and exceptional memory. In 1895 he published his Lorenzo Lotto and the essay The Rudiments of Connoisseurship, setting out his method of attributing pictures through close visual study in the manner of Giovanni Morelli. His opinions were widely sought by collectors, and many masterpieces now in American museums were bought on his recommendation.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Berenson built a career on the claim that the trained eye, rather than documents alone, could tell one master's hand from another's.
The kural says that for those who claim fine discernment the only true measuring rod is the eye, and Berenson made the eye the measure of artistic judgement.
