History of the Thirukkural

Who was Thiruvalluvar?

Very little about Thiruvalluvar's life can actually be verified. Even basic details, such as where he lived, what work he did, what faith he followed, and when he lived, are debated by historians, and different traditions and scholars say different things. Because the evidence is so thin, this project does not pick a side. What we can say is that the Thirukkural itself never argues for one religion, and that people of many backgrounds have claimed it as their own.[1] Popular stories about his life, including the one about his wife Vasuki and the handful of sand she turned into rice, are loved legends passed down over centuries. They are not documented history.[2]

When was it written?

Dating the Thirukkural is a genuinely open scholarly question, and estimates vary widely depending on the method used. Tamil Nadu's government officially adopted 31 BCE as Valluvar's year of birth in January 1935, and built an entire calendar system — the Valluvar Year, currently 31 years ahead of the Gregorian calendar — around that date.[3] Scholars who work from the text's internal linguistic and cultural evidence tend to land elsewhere: Kamil Zvelebil, a Czech scholar of Tamil literature, considered dates earlier than 1 BCE unsupported by the text itself; a commonly cited scholarly window is roughly 200 BCE–200 CE; and Stuart Blackburn has described the present scholarly consensus as centering closer to 500 CE.[4] In short: the traditional/official date and the range favored by textual scholars don't agree, and neither is settled beyond dispute.

How the text is organized

The Thirukkural's 1,330 couplets (kurals) are grouped into 133 chapters (adhikarams) of exactly 10 couplets each, and those 133 chapters are in turn organized into three books: Aram (virtue, chapters 1–38), Porul (wealth, statecraft, and worldly life, chapters 39–108), and Inbam (love, chapters 109–133).[5] Each kural itself is written in a tight, fixed couplet form — traditionally described as seven metrical feet in total, four in the first line and three in the second — which is part of why the text reads so much like proverb or aphorism even in translation.[5]

The commentary tradition

Ten medieval commentators are traditionally credited with writing authoritative commentaries on the Kural; of these, five have survived and reached the modern era: Manakkudavar, Pari Perumal, Kaalingar, Paridhi, and Parimelalhagar.[6] Parimelalhagar, writing around the 13th century CE, is generally regarded by scholars as having produced the finest of these commentaries, both textually and as literature, and his reading of the text remains the most influential traditional interpretation used today.[6]

Reaching the rest of the world

The Thirukkural's best-known bridge into English came in 1886, when the missionary-scholar G.U. Pope published The Sacred Kurral of Tiruvalluva Nayanar in London — the translation still most widely cross-referenced today, including throughout this project.[7] Since then the text has been rendered into roughly 30 English versions on its own, and into more than 100 versions across around 30 languages worldwide.[7]

A living text in Tamil Nadu

The Thirukkural remains a genuinely public, civic text in Tamil Nadu rather than a purely academic or purely religious one. Thiruvalluvar Day is an official state holiday, observed as part of the Pongal harvest festival in mid-January.[8] And at Kanyakumari, a 133-foot statue of Thiruvalluvar — dedicated on January 1, 2000 — encodes the text's own structure into its architecture: the statue and pedestal together stand 133 feet tall for the text's 133 chapters, with the 95-foot figure of Valluvar resting on a 38-foot pedestal representing the 38 chapters of the Aram (Virtue) book alone.[9]
