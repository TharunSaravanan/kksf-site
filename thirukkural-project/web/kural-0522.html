Kural 522

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

விருப்பறாச் சுற்றம் இயையின் அருப்பறா ஆக்கம் பலவும் தரும்.
ViruppaRaach chutram iyaiyin aruppaRaa Aakkam palavum tharum.

Meaning

If a person has relatives whose love never fails, they will bring that person many kinds of good fortune that keep on growing.

Elementary

Think of a garden where lots of people help water the plants. The plants keep growing new branches and flowers. A family that loves you all the time works like those helpers: with them around, good things keep sprouting in your life.

University

Parimelazhagar notes that விருப்பறா (‘whose affection never ceases’) is there to exclude kin who are secret enemies (உட்பகை), and that அருப்பறா qualifies ஆக்கம் to mean wealth that never stops putting out new shoots (அருப்பு, ‘sprouting’, which he glosses as கிளைத்தல், ‘branching’). This separates it from wealth that grows only by its owner’s own effort. He explains the conditional இயையின் (‘if one should obtain’) as a sign of how rare such kin are, and reads பலவும் (‘all the many’) as pointing to the six limbs of the state (army, subjects, treasury, ministers, allies and fortress), which keep growing because many hands tend them together. The couplet sets out the economic logic of the chapter: kin are the root of expanding prosperity. The text is stable, and the Tamil commentators agree on the meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Relatives, again, in consequence of mutual dependence and mutual aid, grow together, like lotus-stalks in a lake.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 36 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura warns Dhritarashtra that relatives who support one another flourish together.
Loving kin make prosperity grow together, which is the Kural’s அருப்பறா ஆக்கம், wealth that keeps sprouting.
Christianity
“Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up.”
— Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher praises working together because it multiplies what each person gains and protects each of them.
Bonds that never fail bring greater returns, as the Kural says loving kin bring wealth of many kinds.
Islam
“Whoever loves that he be granted more wealth and that his lease of life be prolonged then he should keep good relations with his Kith and kin.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5986 (Book of Adab) [5]
The Prophet ties silat al-rahim, keeping up the bonds of kinship, directly to more provision and a longer life.
This is almost the Kural’s own claim: loving kin bring ever-increasing prosperity.
Judaism
“Behold, how good and how pleasant it is For brethren to dwell together in unity!”
— Psalms 133:1 (JPS 1917) [6]
The psalm goes on to compare brothers living in harmony to fragrant oil and to the dew of Hermon, on which God commands blessing.
Brothers united in affection bring down blessing, much as the Kural’s loving kin bring abundance.
Buddhism
In the Sammodamana Jataka, a flock of quails caught in a hunter’s net rises together at their leader’s signal, lifts the net and escapes. They thrive while they stay in harmony, but once they start quarrelling they are all caught.
— Sammodamana Jataka (Jataka No. 33), trans. E. B. Cowell et al. [7]
The Bodhisatta’s fable teaches that a kin group in harmony prospers and is safe, and that discord ruins it.
Kin whose love never fails keep the whole group flourishing, which is the Kural’s point.
Jainism
Samantabhadra counts vatsalya, loving and guileless affection toward one's fellow believers, among the eight limbs of right faith; the Jain community treats this mutual affection as what keeps the fellowship strong and flourishing.
— Ratnakaranda Shravakachara, verses on the eight limbs of right faith (vatsalya-anga) (tr. Champat Rai Jain) [8]
Vatsalya is the Jain virtue of steady, sincere affection within the community of the faithful, and it is a required limb of samyak darshana.
The Kural praises kin whose love never fails; vatsalya is the nearest Jain ideal of unfailing affection among one's own, though its circle is the faith community, not blood kin.
Sikhism
“You are my Relative, and You are my Brother. You are my Protector everywhere; why should I feel any fear or anxiety?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 103, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan addresses God as the kinsman and brother whose care never fails, and finds in that bond freedom from all fear.
The Kural prizes kin whose love never fails; for Guru Arjan the one kinsman whose love truly never fails is God, and the gift is fearlessness rather than ever-growing wealth.
Greek Mythology
When Castor is killed, his brother Polydeuces (Pollux) begs Zeus to let him share his own immortality with him, and the twins end up alternating their days between Olympus and the underworld.
— Pindar, Nemean Ode 10 [10]
A brother’s unfailing love wins the twins a lasting shared blessing.
Love between kin that never fails brings lasting gain, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
Plutarch argues that nature made brothers the way it made pairs of hands, feet and eyes, so that they could help each other, and that a man who treats his brother as a partner doubles his strength in every undertaking.
— Plutarch, Moralia, De Fraterno Amore (On Brotherly Love) [11]
For Plutarch, a strong bond between brothers is a practical asset that makes each brother more capable.
Affectionate kin multiply one’s resources, as the Kural teaches.

Real Life Example

Mayer Amschel Rothschild's five sons build a banking house across Europe
1798-1822 · Frankfurt, London, Paris, Vienna and Naples
Mayer Amschel Rothschild, a Frankfurt dealer and banker, had five sons, all of whom entered the family business. The eldest, Amschel, stayed in Frankfurt, while Nathan established himself in England and founded N M Rothschild in London in 1809, James set up in Paris, Salomon in Vienna and Carl in Naples, and the brothers kept in close touch and worked together on major government loans. In 1810 the father made the Frankfurt firm a partnership with his sons, and in 1822 the Austrian emperor ennobled all five brothers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Historians describe the five linked houses as working in effect like a single multinational bank, a strength that no one brother could have built on his own.
The kural says kin whose affection never fails bring prosperity that keeps putting out new shoots, and the Rothschild brothers' cooperation across five cities made their family business grow far beyond its Frankfurt beginnings.
