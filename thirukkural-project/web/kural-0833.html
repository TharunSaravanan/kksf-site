Kural 833

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

நாணாமை நாடாமை நாரின்மை யாதொன்றும் பேணாமை பேதை தொழில்.
Naanaamai naadaamai naarinmai yaadhondrum penaamai pedhai thozhil.

Meaning

Feeling no shame, not looking for what is worth having, having no love, and caring about nothing: these are the habits of a fool.

Elementary

Think of a kid who breaks a window and doesn’t feel sorry, never tries to learn anything, isn’t kind to friends, and leaves their toys out in the rain. Each of those things on its own is a warning sign; together they are what the poet calls a fool.

University

The couplet is a fourfold catalogue built on negative nouns ending in -aamai/-inmai: naanaamai (நாணாமை, shamelessness at what deserves shame), naadaamai (நாடாமை, not seeking out what deserves seeking), naarinmai (நாரின்மை, lack of naar, ‘fibre’, a metaphor for the affection that binds people), and penaamai (பேணாமை, not cherishing or protecting anything). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar gloss naar as anbu (love), and the commentators agree the list describes a whole disposition rather than isolated faults; Pope renders it ‘Ashamed of nothing, searching nothing out, of loveless heart, / Nought cherishing’. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads naarinmai as lacking sweetness of manner, a minor variation. The text is identical in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who are demoniac do not know what is to be done and what is not to be done. Neither cleanliness nor proper behavior nor truth is found in them.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.7 (As It Is) [3]
The Gita’s portrait of the demoniac nature is a list of absences: no sense of right action, no purity, no truth.
Valluvar likewise defines the fool by what is missing in him: shame, seeking, love and care.
Christianity
“Whose end is destruction, whose God is their belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who mind earthly things.”
— Philippians 3:19 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes people who boast of what ought to embarrass them and care only for appetite.
It matches the Kural’s first mark of the fool, shamelessness at what deserves shame.
Islam
“One of the sayings of the early Prophets which the people have got is: If you don't feel ashamed … do whatever you like.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6120 (sunnah.com; translator’s parenthetical gloss elided) [5]
The hadith treats haya’ (a sense of shame) as the brake on bad conduct: once it is gone, anything goes.
Valluvar puts naanaamai, absence of shame, first among the fool’s traits for the same reason.
Judaism
“Were they ashamed when they had committed abomination? nay, they were not at all ashamed, neither could they blush: therefore they shall fall among them that fall.”
— Jeremiah 6:15 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Jeremiah condemns a people who have lost the ability to blush at their own wrongdoing.
Both texts see the loss of shame as a sign of ruin.
Buddhism
“Those who are ashamed of what they should not be ashamed of, and are not ashamed of what they should be ashamed of — upholding false views, they go to states of woe.”
— Dhammapada 316 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada says misplaced shame, especially no shame where it is due, leads to suffering.
Valluvar’s naanaamai is exactly shamelessness at what should shame us.
Jainism
“If he is frequently angry; if he perseveres in his wrath; if he spurns friendly advice; if he is proud of his learning; if he finds fault with others; if he is angry even with friends; if he speaks evil even of a good friend behind his back; if he is positive in his assertions; if he is malicious, egoistical, greedy, without self-discipline; if he does not share with others; if he is always unkind: then he is called ill-behaved.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.7-9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana lists the marks of an 'ill-behaved' monk, a whole disposition of anger, ingratitude, greed and unkindness that keeps him from Nirvāṇa.
Valluvar sketches the fool through a catalogue of lacks (no shame, no seeking, no affection, no care); the Uttarādhyayana similarly defines the ill-behaved by a list of traits, including lacking kindness and not sharing with others.
Sikhism
“Those who are totally blind in their minds, do not have the integrity to keep their word. … Some do not know the Sound-current of the Naad, spiritual wisdom or the joy of song. They do not even understand good and bad.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1411, Saloks Vaaran Te Vadheek (salok 15), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak lists the marks of the inwardly blind: no integrity in keeping their word, no taste for wisdom or song, and no grasp of good and bad.
The Kural catalogues the habits of the fool, shamelessness, not seeking, lacking affection and cherishing nothing; Guru Nanak gives a similar list of inner lacks.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod foresees a degenerate age in which Aidos (Shame) and Nemesis (Righteous Indignation) wrap themselves in white robes and leave the earth, abandoning humankind to grief.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 197-201 [9]
In Greek myth shame is a goddess, and her departure marks the moral collapse of a whole generation.
The Kural likewise treats the absence of shame as the root mark of the fool.
Philosophy
Aristotle says shame (aidos) befits the young, who live by feeling and make mistakes, because it holds them back; and he calls the person who would feel no shame at disgraceful acts base.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.9 [10]
Aristotle treats a sense of shame as a safeguard for those not yet fully virtuous.
Valluvar’s fool lacks exactly this safeguard.

Real Life Example

The six-month reign of Tsar Peter III
January - July 1762 · St. Petersburg, Russia
Peter III became emperor of Russia in January 1762, while Russia was fighting Prussia in the Seven Years' War, and in May he made peace with Frederick II of Prussia and withdrew Russia from the war, giving up its gains. He planned a war against Denmark on behalf of his German duchy of Holstein, offended the Orthodox Church by pressing changes in its practices, and alienated the imperial guards by threatening to disband them. In July 1762, about six months after his accession, his wife Catherine and the guards overthrew him, and she took the throne as Catherine II.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Peter III showed little regard for his subjects' feelings or his country's interests, and in a few months he lost the support of every group that mattered.
The kural lists the fool's habits as shamelessness, not seeking what is worth seeking, lack of affection and caring for nothing, and Peter III's brief reign showed indifference to the Russia he had been given to rule.
