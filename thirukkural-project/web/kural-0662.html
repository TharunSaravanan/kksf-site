Kural 662

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

ஊறொரால் உற்றபின் ஒல்காமை இவ்விரண்டின் ஆறென்பர் ஆய்ந்தவர் கோள்.
OoRoraal utrapin olkaamai ivviraNdin AaRenpar aaindhavar koaL.

Meaning

People who have studied this say firm action follows two rules: stay away from harm before it comes, and do not give up when it comes anyway.

Elementary

Before riding your bike, you check the tyres and wear a helmet so you don’t get hurt. But if you still fall, you don’t quit biking forever; you get up and keep riding. Being careful first, and being brave after, are the two secrets of finishing things.

University

The couplet sets out a two-part rule: ūṟu orāl, keeping clear of harm (ūṟu, injury or obstacle; orāl, avoidance), and uṟṟa piṉ olkāmai, not flagging (olku, to shrink or slacken) once harm has struck. Parimelazhagar reads the first clause as not undertaking an act likely to come to grief, and the ‘āyntavar’ (those who have investigated) as the authors of the political treatises (nīti nūl), so that the couplet cites a consensus of statecraft; Solomon Pappaiah follows him. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar instead read the first clause as removing an obstacle before it arrives, which puts more weight on foresight than on choice of task. Either way, firmness is shown to include prudence, not mere stubbornness, which links this chapter back to terintu ceyal vakai (ch. 47). The three web texts agree on the wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not disturbed in mind even amidst the threefold miseries or elated when there is happiness, and who is free from attachment, fear and anger, is called a sage of steady mind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [4]
Krishna describes the sthita-prajña, the person whose mind stays level when suffering arrives.
This matches the Kural’s second rule, not shrinking once harm has come.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?”
— Luke 14:28 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells his hearers to count the cost of an undertaking beforehand, lest they begin and be mocked for failing to finish.
This is the Kural’s first rule: foresee the trouble before it comes, so the work can be carried through.
Islam
“And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient,”
— Quran 2:155 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran says trials will come regardless and praises ṣabr, patient steadfastness, in meeting them.
Like the Kural’s second rule, it assumes setbacks will come and makes not buckling under them the virtue.
Judaism
“Seven times a righteous person falls and gets up, While the wicked are tripped by one misfortune.”
— Proverbs 24:16 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, via Sefaria); cf. Proverbs 22:3 [7]
Proverbs contrasts the righteous, who rise again after repeated falls, with the wicked, whom one blow undoes; elsewhere (22:3) it praises the shrewd person who sees trouble coming and takes cover.
Taken together, the two proverbs give both halves of the Kural’s rule, foresight and refusing to stay down.
Buddhism
In the Sabbāsava Sutta the Buddha lists different ways of abandoning the mental effluents, among them some to be abandoned by avoiding (keeping away from a wild elephant, a thorny place, bad company) and some to be abandoned by enduring (bearing cold, heat, hunger and harsh words).
— Majjhima Nikaya 2, Sabbasava Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The discourse treats avoiding and enduring as two distinct, complementary skills in dealing with harm.
Almost exactly the Kural’s pair: ūṟu orāl (avoid) and olkāmai (endure without giving way).
Jainism
“twenty-two troubles which a monk must learn and know, bear and conquer, in order not to be vanquished by them when he lives the life of a wandering mendicant.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra, Lecture 2, prose introduction (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira's teaching on the parisahas (troubles) asks the monk to know the hardships in advance and to bear them without being overcome when they arrive.
This mirrors the kural's two rules of firmness: prepare against harm before it comes, and do not give way once it has come.
Sikhism
After Guru Arjan's martyrdom in 1606, the sixth Guru, Hargobind, took up his guruship girded with two swords, following his father's advice to maintain an army; he trained a force, moved his base to the Punjab hills, and later fought several battles with the Mughals.
— The Pluralism Project, Harvard University, The Development of the Sikh Community [10]
Guru Hargobind both prepared against further harm and refused to let the community be broken once persecution had struck.
The Kural gives two rules of firmness: guard against harm before it comes, and do not give way when it has come; Guru Hargobind's response to his father's martyrdom shows both.
Greek Mythology
Forewarned by Circe, Odysseus stops his crew’s ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast so the Sirens cannot lure the ship to ruin; soon afterwards, when Scylla seizes six of his men, he grieves but sails on.
— Homer, Odyssey 12 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
The episode shows both preventing a known danger before it strikes and pressing on after an unavoidable loss.
Odysseus follows the Kural’s two rules in a single voyage.
Philosophy
Writing after a fire destroyed the city of Lugdunum (Lyon) overnight, Seneca urges Lucilius to rehearse possible misfortunes in advance, so that nothing strikes the mind as unexpected and it is not crushed when disaster comes.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 91 (trans. R. M. Gummere) [12]
Stoic praemeditatio malorum prepares the mind beforehand so that it stays firm afterwards.
Seneca, like the Kural, joins foresight before harm with steadiness after it.

Real Life Example

Cook's crew saves the Endeavour after it strikes the Great Barrier Reef
11 June 1770 · Endeavour Reef, off Queensland, Australia
Around midnight on 10-11 June 1770 James Cook's ship HMS Endeavour struck a coral reef off the coast of what is now Queensland and began taking on water. Cook had the crew lighten the ship by throwing overboard around 40 to 50 tons of material, including ballast and six of its cannons, and the ship was hauled off the reef on a rising tide. The damaged ship was then taken to a river mouth, now the Endeavour River, where repairs took about six to seven weeks before the voyage continued.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Faced with the loss of the ship far from any help, Cook and his crew neither panicked nor gave up, and the expedition survived to complete its voyage.
The kural's second rule of firmness is not to give way once harm has struck, and the Endeavour's crew kept working methodically after the grounding until the ship was saved.
