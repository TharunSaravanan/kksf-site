Kural 1053

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

கரப்பிலா நெஞ்சின் கடனறிவார் முன்நின்று இரப்புமோ ரேஎர் உடைத்து.
Karappilaa nenjin kadanarivaar munnindru irappumor eer udaiththu.

Meaning

Asking can even have a kind of beauty, when you stand before people who hide nothing in their hearts and know what they should do.

Elementary

Asking your grandmother, who loves to share, for a slice of cake doesn't feel bad at all. It can even feel nice, because she is happy you asked.

University

Karappilaa nenju (கரப்பிலா நெஞ்சு, 'a heart without concealment') and kadan arivaar (கடனறிவார், 'those who know their duty') describe the ideal giver. Kadan is read as duty (Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah: 'this is my duty'), while Puliyur Kesikan glosses the givers as people who understand honour (மானம் அறிபவர்). The striking word is eer (ஏஎர், with alapedai lengthening, 'beauty, grace'): standing before such givers, even begging has beauty. The commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar reads the -um in irappum ('even begging') as conceding that begging is normally without grace. Pope: 'To stand as suppliants affords especial grace.' Texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree, both printing the sandhi form இரப்புமோ ரேஎர்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Charity given out of duty, without expectation of return, at the proper time and place, and to a worthy person is considered to be in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.20 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna calls sattvic, the highest kind, the gift given simply because it ought to be given (dātavyam iti), with no thought of return.
The Kural's kadan arivaar, givers who know their duty, are the Gita's sattvic givers.
Christianity
“Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away.”
— Matthew 5:42 (KJV) [4]
In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus tells his followers simply to give to whoever asks.
Givers who never turn away a request are the open-hearted givers before whom the Kural says even asking has grace.
Islam
“And from their properties was [given] the right of the [needy] petitioner and the deprived.”
— Qur'an 51:19 (Sahih International) [5]
Describing the righteous, the Qur'an says that the asker and the deprived had a right (haqq) in their wealth.
If the asker has a right to a share, then asking such people is not shameful, which is close to the Kural's 'grace'.
Judaism
“do not harden your heart and shut your hand against your needy kindred. Rather, you must open your hand and lend whatever is sufficient to meet the need.”
— Deuteronomy 15:7-8 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Torah commands an open hand and an unhardened heart toward a needy kinsman, and lending enough to meet the need.
The open hand and unhardened heart are the Kural's karappilaa nenju, the heart that hides nothing.
Buddhism
“I am a brahman, responsive to requests, open-handed, bearing my last body, an unsurpassed doctor & surgeon.”
— Itivuttaka 100 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes himself as one who answers requests with an open hand, and then says that the gift of Dhamma is the highest gift.
'Responsive to requests, open-handed' describes the kind of giver before whom, the Kural says, asking has grace.
Jainism
Atithi-samvibhaga, 'sharing with the guest', is one of the vows of the Jain householder: a duty to share pure food and requisites with mendicants who arrive unannounced.
— Tattvartha Sutra 7 (list of householder vows, incl. atithi-samvibhaga; verse numbering differs between Svetambara and Digambara recensions) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain householders treat sharing with an arriving ascetic as a vow, part of their duty, rather than as an optional favour.
A giver for whom sharing is a vow is the Kural's kadan arivaar, someone who knows giving as a duty.
Sikhism
“Balwand says that Khivi, the Guru's wife, is a noble woman, who gives soothing, leafy shade to all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 967, Raag Ramkali, Ramkali ki Vaar, Satta and Balwand (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Ramkali ki Vaar of Satta and Balwand, Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, is praised for running the Guru's langar and sheltering everyone who came. She is remembered as the model of open-hearted, dutiful giving.
The Kural says there is grace even in asking from those whose hearts hide nothing and who know their duty; Mata Khivi is the Sikh example of such a giver, whose shelter covered all who came.
Greek Mythology
“Take this to the stranger, and tell him to go the round of the suitors, and beg from them; a beggar must not be shamefaced.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Telemachus sends the disguised Odysseus a whole loaf and tells him that a beggar need not be ashamed to ask.
In a house where the host gives freely, asking carries no shame, which is the Kural's point.
Philosophy
Aristotle says the liberal (generous) person gives for the sake of what is noble and gives rightly: to the right people, in the right amounts, at the right time, and with pleasure rather than pain.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, liberality is a settled disposition to give well, not an occasional act.
The Kural's ideal giver, open-hearted and knowing his duty, is close to Aristotle's liberal man.

Real Life Example

Joseph Pulitzer's appeal pays for the Statue of Liberty's pedestal
March - August 1885 · New York City, United States
When the American committee raising money for the Statue of Liberty's pedestal ran about 100,000 dollars short and construction stopped, the publisher Joseph Pulitzer used his newspaper, the New York World, to ask ordinary readers for small contributions and promised to print the name of every donor. By August 1885 the World had collected more than 100,000 dollars from some 120,000 people, most giving a dollar or less, and the pedestal was completed the following year.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The appeal turned a stalled project into a shared civic act, with ordinary people proud to see their names in print beside their gift.
The kural says that asking of people whose hearts hide nothing and who know their duty has a grace of its own, and Pulitzer's public appeal drew an open-handed response from a whole city.
