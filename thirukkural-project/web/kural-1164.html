Kural 1164

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

காமக் கடல்மன்னும் உண்டே அதுநீந்தும் ஏமப் புணைமன்னும் இல்.
Kaamak kadalmannum undae adhuneendhum aemap punaimannum il.

Meaning

There is a sea of love in front of me, but there is no safe raft to cross it.

Elementary

Picture standing on a beach looking at a huge ocean you need to cross, but there is no boat, not even a little raft. That is how the wife feels about her love while her husband is away: it is as big as the sea, and the one who could carry her across is not here.

University

The couplet balances two clauses with the emphatic particle maṉṉum (மன்னும், ‘indeed’): there is (uṇṭē) a sea of kāmam, and there is no (il) ēmap puṇai (ஏமப் புணை), a ‘raft of safety’. Puṇai (புணை) is a float or raft, and ēmam (ஏமம்) means protection or security. Puliyur Kesikan identifies the missing raft as the husband himself, who alone could carry her across and is now absent, while Solomon Pappaiah reads the sea as love-pain that has outgrown the shame of the previous couplet. The sea image recurs at 1166 and 1167, and readers have long noted its echo of the Kural’s opening chapter (Kural 10), where the ‘great sea of births’ is crossed only by those who cling to the feet of God: here the ocean is love and no such crossing is available. Pope: ‘A sea of love, ’tis true, I see stretched out before, / But not the trusty bark that wafts to yonder shore.’ The text is identical on both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna tells Arjuna that even the worst of sinners, once in the boat of knowledge, will cross over the whole ocean of wrongdoing and misery.
— Bhagavad Gita 4.36 (paraphrase) [3]
The Gita offers knowledge as the boat that carries a person across an otherwise impassable sea.
The Kural’s heroine faces a sea of love and finds no such boat; the Gita supplies the boat she lacks, but for a different ocean.
Christianity
“But when he saw the wind boisterous, he was afraid; and beginning to sink, he cried, saying, Lord, save me.”
— Matthew 14:30 (KJV) [4]
Peter, trying to cross the water to Jesus, begins to sink and cries out for rescue.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Peter is in water too great for him and without anything to hold him up but the one he is trying to reach.
Islam
“[But] he said, ‘I will take refuge on a mountain to protect me from the water.’ [Noah] said, ‘There is no protector today from the decree of Allāh, except for whom He gives mercy.’ And the waves came between them, and he was among the drowned.”
— Qur’an 11:43 (Sahih International) [5]
Noah’s son refuses the ark and looks for his own refuge, but there is none, and the waves separate him from his father.
The Kural’s heroine also faces a flood with no means of safety, and waves of distance lie between her and the one she loves.
Judaism
“Save me, O God; For the waters are come in even unto the soul. I am sunk in deep mire, where there is no standing; I am come into deep waters, and the flood overwhelmeth me.”
— Psalms 69:2-3 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist describes distress as deep water with no footing and no escape.
The heroine’s sea of love is just such water: deep, overwhelming, and with nothing to keep her afloat.
Buddhism
In the Simile of the Raft, the Buddha describes a man facing a great expanse of water with no ferry or bridge, who gathers grass and branches, binds them into a raft, and crosses safely to the far shore.
— Majjhima Nikaya 22, Alagaddupama Sutta (Simile of the Raft) (paraphrase) [7]
The Dhamma is compared to a raft built for crossing a flood and then set down once the crossing is made.
The Kural’s heroine sees the same flood but has no raft; the Buddha’s simile supplies exactly the vessel she says is missing.
Jainism
“The body is the boat, life is the sailor, and the Circle of Births is the ocean which is crossed by the great sages.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.73 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Gautama explains to Kesi that only a boat that does not leak reaches the far shore, and names the boat, the sailor and the ocean.
The heroine has a sea before her and no boat; the Jain answer is that the disciplined body itself must become the vessel, though for the ocean of births, not of love.
Sikhism
“The terrifying world-ocean is difficult and dreadful; there is no shore on this side or the one beyond. There is no boat, no raft, no oars and no boatman.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 59, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes the world as a fearful ocean with no visible shore and no boat or raft to cross it, a crossing made possible only by the Guru.
The Kural's heroine says a sea of love lies before her but there is no safe raft to cross it; Guru Nanak uses the same image of a vast sea with no raft.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Poseidon wrecks the raft Odysseus built to leave Calypso, and he is left swimming for days in the open sea until the sea-goddess Ino gives him her veil to keep him afloat.
— Homer, Odyssey 5 (paraphrase) [10]
Odysseus, trying to get home to his wife, loses his only means of crossing and is left at the mercy of the sea.
The Kural’s heroine is in the same plight from the other side: a sea separates the couple and the raft is gone.
Philosophy
Spinoza calls the human lack of power to govern the emotions ‘bondage’, explaining that a person under their sway is not his own master but lies at the mercy of fortune.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part IV, Preface (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, someone swept by passion has no secure footing of his own.
The heroine’s sea without a raft is a picture of exactly this helplessness before a passion.

Real Life Example

Lord Byron swims the Hellespont in imitation of Leander
May 1810 · Dardanelles (Hellespont), between Sestos and Abydos, Ottoman Empire (now Turkiye)
While his ship lay at anchor in the Dardanelles in 1810, the poet Lord Byron swam across the strait from Sestos to Abydos. In a letter to his mother dated 18 May 1810 he described the swim as done in imitation of Leander. In Greek legend the Hellespont was the scene of the love of Hero and Leander, in which Leander swam the strait at night to reach Hero.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Byron's swim recreated, in real life, the most famous image of a lover who has no boat and must cross a sea of water by strength alone.
The kural says there is a sea of love before the heroine but no safe raft to carry her across, the same picture of love as a strait to be crossed without a boat that Byron's swim brought to life.
