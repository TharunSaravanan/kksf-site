Kural 121

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

அடக்கம் அமரருள் உய்க்கும் அடங்காமை ஆரிருள் உய்த்து விடும்.
Adakkam amararul uykkum adangaamai Aarirul uyththu vidum.

Meaning

Self-control lifts a person up to live among the gods. A lack of it pushes them into a deep darkness they cannot escape.

Elementary

Picture two staircases. One climbs up to a bright room full of wise, kind people; the other goes down into a dark basement with no lights. Every time you hold yourself back from doing something wrong, you climb one step up. Every time you just do whatever you feel like, you slip one step down.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter by setting the stakes in the starkest possible terms: amarar (the immortals, the devas) versus aar-irul (a darkness that is ‘hard to bear’ or ‘dense’), which the medieval commentator Parimelazhagar glosses as a particular hell. Parimelazhagar defines adakkam as the condition in which body, speech and mind (mey, mozhi, manam) do not stray into wrong paths, and explains that the chapter follows ‘Impartiality’ because one who sees his own faults as clearly as others’ will naturally restrain himself. Modern commentators such as Karunanidhi read the couplet in this-worldly terms — restraint earns undying renown, indiscipline darkens life itself — showing how the couplet supports both a religious and a secular reading. Its two-path structure, one road upward and one downward, is among the most widespread forms in world ethical literature, from the Dhammapada’s ‘path to the Deathless’ to Paul’s sowing to the flesh or the Spirit.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 [4]
Krishna names the unrestrained passions as the literal doorways into hell and tells Arjuna that a sensible person closes them.
Like the Kural, the Gita makes unrestraint the road into darkness, so restraint is what keeps a person out of it.
Christianity
“For he that soweth to his flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption; but he that soweth to the Spirit shall of the Spirit reap life everlasting.”
— Galatians 6:8 (KJV) [5]
Paul describes two harvests: indulging the flesh yields decay, while living by the Spirit yields eternal life.
Same two destinations as the Kural: giving in to impulse leads down, disciplined living leads up to everlasting life.
Islam
“But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge.”
— Qur’an 79:40–41 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an promises Paradise to the one who restrains his soul from its unlawful desires out of awe of God.
This is nearly the Kural’s first line: holding back the self is what brings a person to the heavenly realm.
Judaism
“A man’s pride shall bring him low; but he that is of a lowly spirit shall attain to honour.”
— Proverbs 29:23 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs teaches that pride drags a person down while humility lifts him to honour.
The same up-or-down outcome as the Kural, with humility (a core sense of adakkam) as the thing that raises a person.
Buddhism
“Heedfulness is the path to the Deathless. Heedlessness is the path to death. The heedful die not. The heedless are as if dead already.”
— Dhammapada 21 [8]
The Buddha presents watchful self-guarding as the road to the Deathless (Nibbana) and careless living as the road to death.
Same two-road teaching: guarding oneself leads to the highest state, letting oneself go leads to ruin and darkness.
Jainism
“My own Self is the river Vaitaraṇî, my own Self the Śâlmalî tree; my own Self is the miraculous cow Kâmaduh, my own Self the park Nandana. My own Self is the doer and undoer of misery and happiness; my own Self, friend and foe, according as I act well or badly.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.36–37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The monk Anāthi tells King Śreṇika that the soul itself becomes the hell-river and thorn-tree of the damned or the wish-cow and paradise-garden of the gods, depending on how it governs its own conduct.
Like the Kural, it makes self-command the only thing that decides whether a person rises to the realm of the gods or sinks into the darkness of hell.
Sikhism
“They conquer the five thieves, and enshrine the True Lord in the heart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 223, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak teaches that subduing the five thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment and ego) opens the heart to God.
The Kural says self-restraint lifts a person to the company of the immortals; in Sikh terms, conquering the five thieves leads to union with God.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus warns his son Icarus to fly a middle course on their wax-and-feather wings. Icarus, thrilled by flight, soars too near the sun; the wax melts and he plunges into the sea.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.183–235 (paraphrase) [11]
The myth shows the fate of a youth who could not hold himself back from excess even when warned.
Icarus shows the Kural’s second line: lack of restraint doesn’t just fail, it sends a person plunging down.
Philosophy
Socrates pictures the soul as a charioteer driving a noble horse and an unruly one. Souls whose charioteer masters the unruly horse follow the gods up to the heavens and see true reality; those dragged about by it lose their wings and sink to earth.
— Plato, Phaedrus 246a–248c (paraphrase) [12]
For Plato, the soul’s rise or fall depends on whether reason can govern appetite.
Plato’s myth has the same two directions as the Kural: self-mastery carries the soul up among the gods, unruliness drags it down.

Real Life Example

Stanislav Petrov judges a missile alert to be false
26 September 1983 · Early-warning command bunker near Moscow, Soviet Union (now Russia)
Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov was on duty at a Soviet early-warning bunker outside Moscow when the satellite system reported that the United States had launched five intercontinental ballistic missiles at the Soviet Union. Reasoning that a real American first strike would use far more than five missiles, and with ground radars not confirming the launches, he reported the alert as a false alarm rather than treating it as an attack; the satellites had mistaken sunlight reflecting off clouds for missile launches. He was reprimanded over paperwork rather than rewarded, the incident stayed secret until after the Soviet Union collapsed, and he later received international honours including the 2013 Dresden Peace Prize, although Russia's UN mission argued that no single officer could have started or prevented a nuclear war.
— Sources: [13] [14]
In a period of high tension, one officer's refusal to act on alarm rather than evidence is widely credited with avoiding a possible catastrophe.
The kural sets restraint, which raises a person among the immortals, against its absence, which leads into thick darkness; Petrov's restraint is remembered worldwide, while the alternative could have meant darkness for millions.
