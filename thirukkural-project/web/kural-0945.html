Kural 945

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

மாறுபாடு இல்லாத உண்டி மறுத்துண்ணின் ஊறுபாடு இல்லை உயிர்க்கு.
Maarupaadu illaadha undi maruththunnin oorupaadu illai uyirkku.

Meaning

If you eat food that suits your body and stop before you eat too much, no harm will come to your life.

Elementary

Even your favourite ice cream will give you a tummy ache if you eat the whole tub. Valluvar says that even good food must be eaten with a 'no, that's enough' at the right moment.

University

Maarupaadu illaadha undi (மாறுபாடு இல்லாத உண்டி) is food that does not clash with the body, the same category as maaralla in 944. The key word is maruththu (மறுத்து, 'refusing'): Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it as refusing more once the measure is reached, and Kesikan as eating not as the mind desires but only as much as causes no illness. Solomon Pappaiah takes a different line, reading maruththu as rejecting unsuitable foods, so that the couplet becomes about selection rather than quantity; this is a genuine interpretive divergence. Oorupaadu (ஊறுபாடு) is injury or harm, and uyirkku (உயிர்க்கு) 'to life' or 'to the soul dwelling in the body'. Parimelazhagar's reading, followed by most modern commentators, stresses restraint even with good food. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Foods that are too bitter, too sour, salty, pungent, dry and hot, are liked by people in the modes of passion. Such foods cause pain, distress, and disease.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.9 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, As It Is, 1972) [3]
The Gita warns that foods chosen for intense taste rather than wholesomeness bring disease.
The Kural's 'food without contrariety' is the opposite of these disease-causing rajasic foods.
Christianity
Proverbs advises that if you find honey you should eat only as much as is sufficient for you, lest you be filled with it and vomit it.
— Proverbs 25:16 (KJV), paraphrase [4]
Even the sweetest good thing must be taken only up to sufficiency.
This is the Kural's lesson that even agreeable food must be eaten with refusal of excess.
Islam
“The human does not fill any container that is worse than his stomach. It is sufficient for the son of Adam to eat what will support his back. If this is not possible, then a third for food, a third for drink, and third for his breath.”
— Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2380 (graded sahih by Darussalam) [5]
The Prophet sets the limit of eating at what keeps one upright, or at most a third of the stomach for food.
Like the Kural, the hadith asks for deliberate restraint even with lawful, wholesome food.
Judaism
“A man who is wise, overcomes his desires, is not drawn by his appetites and eats nothing of the aforementioned unless he needs them for a medical reason, is [indeed] heroic.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:10 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides calls the one who masters his appetite at table a hero.
The Kural's maruththu, refusing at the table, is exactly this heroic self-mastery.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares right eating to parents crossing a desert who must eat the flesh of their dead child to survive: they eat only to get across, without any relish or excess.
— Puttamamsa Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 12.63, paraphrase [7]
The stark simile teaches that food should be taken only for sustenance, never for indulgence.
It pushes to an extreme the Kural's counsel to eat even good food with refusal of excess.
Jainism
“Abstention from dainty food means abstention from such highly nourishing food and drink as milk, curds, ghee, &c.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rasaparityaga, giving up rich and tasty foods, is one of the six external Jain austerities.
Jainism, like the Kural, asks the eater to refuse what appetite wants, though it renounces even wholesome rich foods for the soul's sake.
Sikhism
“Sleep little, and eat little; O Nanak, this is the essence of wisdom.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 939, Raag Ramkali, Sidh Gosht (pauri 8), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sidh Gosht, Guru Nanak's dialogue with the yogis, this line ends a stanza describing the disciplined seeker who lives in the world without being ruled by appetite.
The Kural says no harm comes to one who holds back from eating too much even of wholesome food; the verse likewise makes eating little part of wisdom.
Greek Mythology
Stranded on Thrinacia, Odysseus' starving companions slaughter the forbidden cattle of the Sun despite his warnings; Zeus destroys their ship, and only Odysseus, who refused the meat, survives.
— Homer, Odyssey 12 (tr. Samuel Butler), paraphrase [10]
The men who could not refuse food perished, while the one who held back lived.
The Kural's promise, that the one who refuses excess suffers no harm to life, is played out in mythic terms.
Philosophy
Epicurus writes to Menoeceus that plain fare gives as much pleasure as a costly diet once the pain of want is removed, and that becoming used to simple food is a full supply of health.
— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus (Diogenes Laertius X.130-131, tr. R. D. Hicks), paraphrase [11]
The philosopher of pleasure himself argues that restraint and simple food are the true road to health and lasting pleasure.
This supports the Kural's claim that wholesome food eaten with restraint keeps life free from harm.

Real Life Example

Finland launches the North Karelia Project
1972 · North Karelia, Finland
In the early 1970s the Finnish province of North Karelia had one of the highest rates of death from heart disease in the world, and in 1972 local authorities, working with the World Health Organization, launched a community-wide programme to change how people lived. It encouraged residents to replace butter and whole milk with lower-fat alternatives, eat more vegetables and less salt, and stop smoking, and worked with schools and local food producers to change what was on offer. Over the following decades heart-disease deaths among working-age people in the region fell by roughly 80 percent, though the exact figure varies with the period measured.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The project became a world model for preventing illness by changing everyday diet across a whole community rather than treating disease after it appears.
The kural promises that no harm comes to the life of one who eats wholesome food and holds back from excess, and North Karelia showed that promise borne out across a whole population.
