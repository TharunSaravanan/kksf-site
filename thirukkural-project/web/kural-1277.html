Kural 1277

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

தண்ணந் துறைவன் தணந்தமை நம்மினும் முன்னம் உணர்ந்த வளை.
Thannan thuraivan thanandhamai namminum munnam unarndha valai.

Meaning

My bangles knew before I did that my husband, the lord of the cool seashore, had left me.

Elementary

Sometimes your body knows you are sad before your mind does, like when you lose your appetite. The heroine says her arms grew so thin from missing her husband that her bangles slipped off, even before she understood he had left her in his heart.

University

The husband is called taṇṇam tuṟaivaṉ, ‘lord of the cool shore’, the conventional epithet of the hero of the neytal (seashore) landscape, whose mood in classical Tamil poetics is anxious waiting. His taṇantamai (having parted) was sensed by her vaḷai (bangles) even before (muṉṉam) by herself. The image rests on a classical convention: grief at separation makes the wife’s arms grow thin, so her bangles slip. Varadarasanar and Kesikan read it as the bangles loosening at his departure; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar sharpen it, saying he had parted in mind while still with her in body, and her bangles knew it first. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa’s exiled Yaksha, pining for his wife, has grown so thin that his golden bracelet has slipped from his wasted forearm.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Purvamegha 2 (paraphrase) [3]
The slipping bracelet is a classical Indian sign of a body wasted by separation from the beloved.
The Kural uses the same sign, bangles loosened by grief, to show separation felt in the body.
Christianity
“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh”
— Song of Solomon 5:2 (KJV) [4]
The bride’s heart registers her beloved’s presence even while she sleeps.
The Kural similarly has something other than the conscious mind, her bangles, register the beloved’s absence first.
Islam
“And when the caravan departed [from Egypt], their father said, ‘Indeed, I find the smell of Joseph [and would say that he was alive] if you did not think me weakened in mind.’”
— Qur’an 12:94 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob senses Joseph before any news of him arrives.
Like the Kural’s bangles, Jacob perceives the truth about his beloved before anyone tells him.
Judaism
“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had turned away, and was gone. My soul failed me when he spoke.”
— Song of Songs 5:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The bride discovers too late that her beloved has already withdrawn.
The Kural’s wife, too, realises only after the fact that her beloved had already turned away.
Buddhism
“When a deva is about to pass away from the company of devas, five omens appear: his garlands wither, his clothes get soiled, sweat comes out of his armpits, a dullness descends on his body, he no longer delights in his own deva-seat.”
— Itivuttaka 83 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Itivuttaka lists bodily omens that announce a coming departure before it happens.
The Kural’s slipping bangles are such an omen: the body’s adornments signal a parting before the mind admits it.
Jainism
“Then the famous man presented the charioteer with his pair of earrings, his neck-chain, and all his ornaments.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Arishtanemi’s giving away his ornaments is the first outward sign that he is about to leave his bride for the ascetic life.
In the Kural the slipping bangles mark a separation; in the Jain story the removed ornaments mark a groom’s departure from his bride.
Sikhism
“The soul-bride has grown weak, in the pain of separation from her Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 242, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes the soul-bride's body wasting with the pain of being apart from the Lord.
In the Kural the wife's bangles slip from her thinned wrists, showing the parting before she admits it; Guru Nanak likewise shows separation written on the body of the soul-bride.
Greek Mythology
Dido senses Aeneas’s secret preparations to sail before he tells her, for, as Virgil asks, who can deceive a lover?
— Virgil, Aeneid 4.296 (paraphrase) [10]
Virgil shows a lover perceiving her beloved’s intent to leave before any word is spoken.
The Kural’s wife, like Dido, knows of the parting before being told, through her bangles.
Philosophy
Pascal writes that the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées, no. 277 (Brunschvicg numbering; paraphrase; cf. tr. W.F. Trotter) [11]
Pascal holds that the heart perceives some truths before and apart from reasoning.
The Kural gives the same priority to a non-rational knowing, voiced through her bangles before her mind.

Real Life Example

Doctors in Japan describe broken heart syndrome
1990 · Japan
In 1990 doctors in Japan first identified takotsubo cardiomyopathy, now also called broken heart syndrome. It is a temporary weakening of the heart's main pumping chamber, usually triggered by intense emotional or physical stress such as the death of a loved one, and its symptoms can resemble a heart attack. Most patients recover fully within weeks to a few months.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The discovery showed that grief and loss can register in the body itself, sometimes before a person has fully taken them in.
The kural's wife says her bangles, slipping from her thinning arms, knew of her husband's parting before she did; broken heart syndrome is medical evidence that the body can react to loss on its own.
