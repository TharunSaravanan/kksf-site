Kural 4

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

வேண்டுதல் வேண்டாமை இலானடி சேர்ந்தார்க்கு யாண்டும் இடும்பை இல.
Vendudhal vendaamai ilaan adi serndhaarkku, yaandum idumbai ila.

Meaning

No suffering will ever touch those who take refuge in the feet of the One who is beyond all craving and all aversion.

Elementary

Some people get super excited when they get what they want, and really upset when they don’t. This kural says if you can stay as calm as God — not too excited, not too upset — bad things can’t really shake you.

University

The verse names two categories — raga (craving/attachment) and dvesha (aversion) — as the very things the Divine transcends. This dyad is the central diagnostic pair in most Indian soteriologies for why beings suffer. The claim is that participation in a consciousness beyond craving/aversion is itself what removes vulnerability to affliction — functionally identical to the Stoic and Buddhist claims that suffering arises from the mind’s reactive patterns rather than external events themselves.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not disturbed by sorrows, who has no craving for joy, who is free from attachment, fear, and anger — is called a sage of steady wisdom.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 [3]
Krishna describes the ideal person as someone whose inner peace doesn’t rise and fall with good or bad news.
This is the Kural’s 'beyond craving and aversion' standard almost word for word.
Christianity
“Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee.”
— Isaiah 26:3 (KJV) [4]
The prophet promises complete inner peace specifically to the person whose attention stays fixed on God rather than on circumstances.
Matches the Kural’s claim that peace/protection from suffering comes from where your mind is anchored.
Islam
“Unquestionably, [for] the allies of Allah there will be no fear concerning them, nor will they grieve.”
— Qur'an 10:62, Yunus [5]
This verse promises God’s close friends a life free of the two things that usually plague people: fear about the future and grief about the past.
Nearly identical structure to the Kural’s promise of a life free from affliction.
Judaism
“Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me.”
— Psalm 23:4 (Tanakh, Ketuvim / KJV) [4]
Even walking through the most frightening place imaginable, the psalmist says fear has no grip on him because God is present.
Direct parallel to the Kural’s 'no suffering touches them' promise.
Buddhism
“The fever of passion exists not for him who has completed the journey, who is sorrowless and wholly set free, and has broken all ties.”
— Dhammapada, verse 90 [6]
The Buddha describes the fully awakened person as someone who has completely stopped being agitated by craving — their inner 'fever' has simply gone out.
Reaches the same end state as the Kural — total freedom from affliction — through spiritual completion rather than devotion to a personal God.
Greek Mythology
As a young man, Hercules is approached by two women personifying Virtue and Vice; Vice offers an easy life free of pain, Virtue offers only what is earned through effort — and Hercules chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus, 'The Choice of Hercules', in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-33 [7]
The fable frames maturity as choosing to build a life on hard-won virtue rather than chasing momentary pleasure and avoiding pain.
Echoes the Kural’s contrast between craving/aversion (Vice’s offer) and a settled, unshakeable good (Virtue’s path).
Philosophy
“Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion, Chapter 5 [8]
Epictetus argues that outward events never directly cause distress — our judgments about them do, which means the disturbance is always something we can let go of.
Gives the psychological mechanism behind the Kural’s claim: freedom from craving/aversion is what actually removes vulnerability to suffering.
