Kural 492

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

முரண்சேர்ந்த மொய்ம்பி னவர்க்கும் அரண்சேர்ந்தாம் ஆக்கம் பலவுந் தரும்.
Muranserntha moimbi navarkkum aransernthaam Aakkam palavun tharum.

Meaning

Even for people who are already strong in battle, fighting from a safe, well-protected place gives them many extra advantages.

Elementary

Even the strongest kid in a snowball fight does better behind a snow wall. Being strong is good, but being strong and in a safe spot is much better.

University

‘Muran’ means hostility or opposition and ‘moimbu’ means strength, so the first line describes people strong enough to take on an enemy. ‘Aran’ is the fortress, one of the six limbs of the state that later gets its own chapter (74, அரண்), and ‘aakkam’ is gain or prosperity. The ‘-um’ (even) in ‘navarkkum’ is the point: position adds to strength and is no substitute for the lack of it, which is why Parimelazhagar reads the verse as saying that even the mighty gain from a secure base. Modern glosses widen ‘aran’ to any safe place of operation, not only a walled fort (thirukkural-thamizh.org: ‘the place of action is safe’). Older printings show the sandhi ‘மொய்ம்பி னவர்க்கு மரண்சேர்ந்தா மாக்கம்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As enemies do not hurt these (beings, when they are) sheltered by (their) fortresses, even so foes (can) not injure a king who has taken refuge in his fort.”
— Manusmriti 7.73 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu lists the kinds of forts (desert, water, forest, earthwork, armed camp, hill) and compares a king in his fort to animals that are safe in their own shelters.
Like the Kural, this makes the aran (fortified position) a source of power beyond the king’s own strength.
Christianity
“Upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.”
— Matthew 16:18 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says the community he is founding will stand on a rock that no hostile power can overcome.
The image is a secure foundation that makes its occupants hard to overcome, which is the Kural’s aran.
Islam
At the Battle of the Trench (al-Khandaq, 627 CE), the outnumbered Muslims of Medina dug a trench across the open northern approach on Salman al-Farisi’s advice, and the confederate army could not force its way through.
— Ibn Ishaq, Sirat Rasul Allah (tr. A. Guillaume), account of the Trench; cf. Quran 33:9–25 [5]
A prepared defensive line let a much smaller force hold off a large coalition until the siege fell apart.
The Kural says a secure position adds advantages even to fighters who are already strong, and the trench did this for Medina.
Judaism
“The conies are but a feeble folk, yet make they their houses in the rocks.”
— Proverbs 30:26 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs counts the rock-hyrax among creatures that are small but very wise, because it makes up for weakness by living among rocks.
The hyrax is a picture of the Kural’s aran: safety comes from where you are.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares a disciple to a king’s frontier fortress with a deep foundation post, a moat, a patrol road, many weapons, troops, a wise gatekeeper, high walls and ample stores. Such a fortress cannot be taken by enemies from outside.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 7.63 (7.67 in some editions), Nagara Sutta: The Fortress [6]
Each part of the fortress stands for a spiritual quality that keeps the practitioner safe from Mara.
The Kural’s point that a fortified position multiplies security is the literal basis of this simile.
Jainism
“Making Faith his fortress, Penance and Self-control the bolt (of its gate), Patience its strong wall, so that guarded in three ways it is impregnable; … (in this way) a sage will be the victor in battle and get rid of the Saṃsâra.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.20-22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
When Indra urges King Nami to build walls, gates and a moat, Nami replies with a fortress made of virtues, guarded threefold and impregnable.
The kural says even the strong gain greatly by fighting from a fortress; Nami agrees that a fortress brings victory, but his fortress is faith and patience and his battle is against karma.
Sikhism
“The Sanctuary of the Lord's Feet is my fortress and battlement.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 742, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan names God's protection as his fort and rampart, the secure position from which he faces every threat.
The Kural says even the strong gain much by fighting from a fortified position; Guru Arjan names the one fortress Gurbani trusts above all others.
Greek Mythology
When Athens asked Delphi about the Persian invasion, the oracle said that a ‘wooden wall’ alone would stay unconquered. Themistocles took this to mean the fleet and persuaded the Athenians to place their hope in the ships and the waters around Salamis.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.141–143 (tr. A. D. Godley) [9]
Athens survived by picking the defensive position that suited it best, which was the sea, rather than trusting its city walls.
The Kural says the right place of refuge multiplies a people’s strength, and the Athenians found theirs in the ships.
Philosophy
“The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat, and then waited for an opportunity of defeating the enemy.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War IV.1 (tr. Lionel Giles) [10]
Sun Tzu says securing yourself comes before attacking, and is something within your own control.
The Kural’s secured base (aran) is exactly this first step of making yourself impossible to defeat.

Real Life Example

Constantinople's walls withstand the Arab siege of 717-718
717-718 · Constantinople (Istanbul), Byzantine Empire
Soon after Leo III became Byzantine emperor in 717, a large Umayyad army and fleet laid siege to Constantinople, which was protected by the massive Theodosian land walls and by sea walls. Behind these fortifications, and using the incendiary weapon known as Greek fire against the enemy fleet, the defenders held out through a harsh winter while the besiegers suffered from hunger and disease. With a Bulgar force also attacking them, the Arab army withdrew in August 718.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The victory checked the Arab advance into southeastern Europe and showed how a fortified capital multiplied the strength of its defenders.
The kural says that even those already strong in war gain many advantages from a secure fortified position, and Leo's forces held on because they fought from behind Constantinople's walls.
