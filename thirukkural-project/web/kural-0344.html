Kural 344

Renunciation · Thuravu · துறவு

இயல்பாகும் நோன்பிற்கொன்று இன்மை உடைமை மயலாகும் மற்றும் பெயர்த்து.
Iyalbaagum nonbirkondru inmai udaimai Mayalaagum matrum peyarththu.

Meaning

Owning nothing at all is natural for someone who has truly given up the world. Owning even one thing pulls them back into the false thinking they had left behind.

Elementary

If you are trying to stop eating sweets, keeping 'just one piece' in your pocket makes it very hard to stop thinking about sweets. This kural says someone who has given things up should keep nothing, because even one thing can pull them back.

University

Inmai (having nothing) is called the iyalbu — the natural, defining character — of nonbu (austerity, tapas). The second line's peyarththu (“turning back, reversing”) suggests that possession does not merely distract but undoes progress, returning the renunciant to mayal, bewilderment; Karunanidhi glosses that even one attachment multiplies into many. This is a stricter standard than Kural 341's gradualism, and it applies specifically to one who has already taken up the ascetic life. Comparable rules shaped the Buddhist monk's short list of requisites, the Jain ideal of aparigraha, and the apostolic mission charge in Matthew 10; the Cynic Diogenes made the same point theatrically.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Such a man of understanding acts with mind and intelligence perfectly controlled, gives up all sense of proprietorship over his possessions and acts only for the bare necessities of life. Thus working, he is not affected by sinful reactions.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.21 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes the sage who has abandoned all possessiveness (tyakta-sarva-parigraha) and keeps only what bare survival needs.
Both identify non-possession as the defining mark of the renunciate.
Christianity
“Provide neither gold, nor silver, nor brass in your purses, Nor scrip for your journey, neither two coats, neither shoes, nor yet staves: for the workman is worthy of his meat.”
— Matthew 10:9-10 (KJV) [4]
Sending out the twelve apostles, Jesus forbids them to carry money, spare clothing or supplies.
The missionary is to own nothing, like the Kural's ascetic, depending entirely on God and on others' hospitality.
Islam
“Your wealth and your children are but a trial, and Allah has with Him a great reward.”
— Quran 64:15 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran describes possessions and family as a test that can distract the believer from God.
Like the Kural's warning that possession brings back delusion, the Quran treats wealth as a trial that can lead the heart astray.
Judaism
“The more property, the more anxiety”
— Pirkei Avot 2:7 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Hillel's saying lists the hidden costs that come with each kind of increase — including more worry with more possessions.
Possession breeds a disturbed mind — a Jewish echo of the Kural's 'possession brings bewilderment'.
Buddhism
“Happy indeed we live, we who possess nothing. Feeders on joy we shall be, like the Radiant Gods.”
— Dhammapada 200 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes the monks' happiness as flowing from owning nothing at all.
Owning nothing is not a hardship but the natural condition of the joyful renunciate — the Kural's 'inmai' as 'iyalbu'.
Jainism
“He who owns even a small property in living or lifeless things, or consents to others holding it, will not be delivered from misery.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.1.1.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 235) [8]
In the opening answer of the Sūtrakṛtāṅga, Sudharman gives Mahāvīra's account of what binds the soul. Possessing even a little property, or approving of others holding it, is named first.
The Kural says owning even one thing drags the ascetic back into delusion. The Jain canon makes the same uncompromising claim: even a small property blocks deliverance. This is the root of the monastic vow of aparigraha.
Sikhism
“As the lotus flower floats unaffected in the water, so does he remain detached in his own household.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 949, Raag Raamkalee (Ramkali ki Vaar), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das gives the classic Sikh image of detachment: the lotus lives in water without being wetted, and the Gurmukh lives in the family home without being bound by it.
The Kural makes owning nothing the very nature of austerity; Sikh teaching answers that detachment lies in the mind, so one may keep a household and possessions and still be free of them.
Greek Mythology
“A child has beaten me in plainness of living.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 6.37 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [10]
Seeing a child drink water from his cupped hands, Diogenes threw away the cup from his bag, saying this.
Even one small possession was one too many for Diogenes — the Kural's point that the ascetic should keep nothing.
Philosophy
“For to that which once exceeds a due measure, there is no bound.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 39 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus warns that once one goes beyond what the body actually needs, desire has no natural stopping point — the shoe becomes gilded, then purple, then jewelled.
Like the Kural's 'possession brings delusion again', even one step beyond need restarts an endless cycle of wanting.

Real Life Example

Clare of Assisi wins papal approval for a Rule of absolute poverty
1228 and 9 August 1253 · San Damiano, Assisi, Italy
Clare of Assisi, founder of the order of Poor Clares, insisted that her community own nothing, either individually or in common, and in 1228 Pope Gregory IX granted her community the privilege of poverty. When later popes pressed the sisters to accept property, Clare wrote her own Rule enjoining absolute poverty. Pope Innocent IV approved her definitive Rule on 9 August 1253, two days before she died.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Clare spent decades defending the right of her sisters to hold no property at all, against well-meaning pressure from church authorities. She treated even shared ownership as a step away from the life she had chosen.
Valluvar says owning nothing is the very nature of austerity and that possessing even one thing pulls the renunciant back into delusion, which is exactly why Clare refused even communal property.
