Kural 597

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

சிதைவிடத்து ஒல்கார் உரவோர் புதையம்பிற் பட்டுப்பா டூன்றுங் களிறு.
Sidhaividaththu olkaar uravor pudhaiyambir Pattuppaadu oondrum kaliru.

Meaning

Strong-minded people do not give up even when they are ruined, just as a war elephant stands firm with pride even when arrows are stuck deep in its body.

Elementary

Think of a brave firefighter who keeps going even when they are tired and hurt. The Kural compares strong-hearted people to a huge elephant that stays standing even when arrows hit it. Tough times don’t make them quit.

University

‘Sidhaividaththu’ means at the place or time of ruin (sidhaivu), and ‘olkaar’ means they do not bend or droop; ‘uravor’ are those with uram, firmness of mind. The simile is from war: ‘pudhai ambu’ are arrows buried in the flesh, and ‘pattup paadu oondrum kaliru’ is the bull elephant that, though struck, plants itself (oondrum) and upholds its paadu, glossed by Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar as its perumai (greatness, dignity). The image comes from Sangam battle poetry, where the wounded tusker holding its ground is a stock emblem of valour. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads ‘pudhai’ as arrows that cover the body. The spelling ‘kaliru’ (களிறு) is the standard word for a male elephant; the texts in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhishma, pierced by so many of Arjuna’s arrows that when he falls from his chariot his body does not touch the ground, lies on a bed of arrows for many days, calm and lucid, and teaches the Pandavas until his chosen time of death.
— Mahabharata, Bhishma Parva (fall of Bhishma), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
The great warrior, riddled with arrows, keeps his composure and his dignity and continues to act as a teacher.
He is the human counterpart of the Kural’s elephant that stands firm though pierced by arrows.
Christianity
“We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair; Persecuted, but not forsaken; cast down, but not destroyed;”
— 2 Corinthians 4:8-9 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes suffering attacks from every side without being crushed in spirit.
Like the Kural’s strong-minded, he is struck but does not falter.
Islam
“So do not weaken and do not grieve, and you will be superior if you are [true] believers.”
— Qur’an 3:139 (Saheeh International) [5]
After the setback at Uhud, the Qur’an tells the wounded believers not to lose heart.
The Kural too says the strong do not droop even in the hour of ruin.
Judaism
“Be strong and of good courage; be not affrighted, neither be thou dismayed: for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.”
— Joshua 1:9 (JPS 1917) [6]
God tells Joshua, facing hard battles, to stay strong and not be shaken.
This is the same firmness in danger the Kural praises.
Buddhism
“As an elephant in the battlefield withstands arrows shot from bows all around, even so shall I endure abuse.”
— Dhammapada 320 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares his patient endurance of insults to a war elephant standing firm under a hail of arrows.
Both texts use the identical image of the arrow-struck war elephant that does not give way.
Jainism
“As an elephant at the head of the battle, so was Mahâvîra there victorious. … As a hero at the head of the battle is surrounded on all sides, so was there Mahâvîra. Bearing all hardships, the Venerable One, undisturbed, proceeded (on the road to Nirvâna).”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra 1.8.3.8 and 1.8.3.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Describing Mahavira's harsh wanderings in Lāḍha, where he was beaten, cut and set upon by dogs, the Acaranga compares him to a war elephant at the front of battle who stays unshaken.
This is almost the Kural's own image: the strong-minded do not falter in ruin, just as a war elephant pierced by arrows stands firm.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir, whose hymns the Gurus included in the Guru Granth Sahib, defines the true hero (soora) as one who fights for what is right and holds his ground even as he is cut down.
The Kural's war elephant, pierced deep with arrows, still stands firm; Kabir's hero, cut piece by piece yet never leaving the field, gives the same picture of steadfastness in ruin.
Greek Mythology
As the Trojans storm the Greek ships, Ajax is battered by missiles until his shoulder aches under his shield and he can barely breathe, yet he keeps his position and does not give ground.
— Homer, Iliad 16.102-111 [10]
The great Greek defender stays at his post even while being hammered from all sides.
Ajax holding firm under the barrage mirrors the elephant standing fast though pierced by arrows.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius tells himself to be like the headland against which the waves keep breaking, which stands firm while the water calms around it.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.49 [11]
The emperor-philosopher pictures the steady mind as a rock that stays put while trouble crashes against it.
His headland and the Kural’s elephant are both images of the strong mind that does not yield in disaster.

Real Life Example

Rana Pratap fights on after the defeat at Haldighati
1576-1597 · Mewar, Rajasthan, India
In June 1576 Rana Pratap Singh of Mewar was defeated by a Mughal army of the emperor Akbar at the Battle of Haldighati near Gogunda. Instead of submitting, he withdrew to the hills and continued resistance from mountain strongholds, later making Chavand his capital. He kept raiding until his death in 1597, and Mewar did not formally acknowledge Mughal overlordship until 1614.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Defeat in open battle did not break Pratap's resolve, and he spent the rest of his life standing his ground in the hills.
The kural says the strong-minded do not falter even in ruin, like a war elephant that stands firm though pierced by arrows, and Pratap held firm for two decades after Haldighati.
