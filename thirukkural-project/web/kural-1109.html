Kural 1109

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

ஊடல் உணர்தல் புணர்தல் இவைகாமம் கூடியார் பெற்ற பயன்.
Oodal unardhal punardhal ivaikaamam koodiyaar petra payan.

Meaning

A small lovers' quarrel, making up, and coming close again: these are the rewards enjoyed by people whose love has come true.

Elementary

Best friends sometimes have a small grumpy fight, then say sorry, and then feel even closer than before. The verse says that this little circle of a pretend sulk, making up and being together again is one of the sweet parts of love.

University

The couplet names the three-part movement that structures the closing chapters of the Kamattuppal: ūṭal (ஊடல், the lover’s feigned or playful sulking), uṇartal (உணர்தல், its resolution, the partner understanding and appeasing it) and puṇartal (புணர்தல், reunion). Kāmam kūṭiyār (‘those in whom love has come together’, i.e. lovers whose love is fulfilled) obtain these as payaṉ, ‘fruit’. Solomon Pappaiah places it explicitly in married life (a small tiff, reconciliation, then reunion), Puliyur Kesikan adds that the sulk should be kept within measure (aḷavōṭu), and Kalaignar gives a slightly different reading of uṇartal. The triad anticipates chapters 131-133 (Pulavi, Pulavi Nuṇukkam, Ūṭaluvakai), where the delight of the lovers’ quarrel becomes the main theme. Pope: ‘The jealous variance, the healing of the strife, reunion gained: / These are the fruits from wedded love obtained.’ Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana Krishna teases his wife Rukmini by suggesting she would have done better to marry another king; she is so distressed that she faints, and he lifts her up, comforts her and assures her of his love, after which they are happier than before.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.60 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
A playful provocation leads to hurt, then to tender reconciliation and renewed closeness.
This is the Kural’s sequence of quarrel, making up and reunion between married lovers.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath.”
— Ephesians 4:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul allows that anger will come but insists that it be resolved before the day ends.
Like the Kural, Paul expects quarrels to be followed quickly by reconciliation.
Islam
Ali and Fatima had a quarrel and Ali went off to sleep in the mosque; the Prophet found him lying covered in dust, brushed it off him and playfully called him ‘Abu Turab’ (father of dust), and the matter was smoothed over.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 441 [5]
A small marital quarrel is gently eased by the Prophet’s humour and affection.
Like the Kural’s ūṭal and uṇartal, this is a tiff between loving spouses that ends in reconciliation.
Judaism
“I opened to my beloved; But my beloved had turned away, and was gone. My soul failed me when he spoke. I sought him, but I could not find him.”
— Song of Songs 5:6, with 6:3 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
In the Song of Songs the bride delays opening to her beloved, he turns away, and she searches for him until they are reunited and she says ‘I am my beloved’s’.
The Song traces the Kural’s arc of estrangement, seeking and reunion between lovers.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that hatred is never ended by hatred in this world, but only by non-hatred; this, he says, is an eternal law.
— Dhammapada 5 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Quarrels end not by answering anger with anger but by letting it go.
The Kural’s uṇartal, the healing of the quarrel, works by the same giving-up of resentment.
Jainism
“Love and hatred are caused by Karman, and they say that Karman has its origin in delusion; Karman is the root of birth and death, and birth and death they call misery.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira teaches that attachment and aversion alike come from karma and lead to further suffering.
The Kural delights in love’s swing between sulking and union; Jainism sees that very swing of attraction and aversion as binding karma.
Sikhism
“O foolish and ignorant soul-bride, why are you so proud?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 722, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak gently scolds the bride-soul for her maan (sulky pride), which keeps her from her Husband Lord, and teaches that humility brings reunion.
The Kural names the lovers' quarrel, its making up and their coming together again as love's reward; Gurbani knows the same pattern of the bride's pride, reconciliation and reunion.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus returns, Penelope tests him by ordering their bed moved; he angrily protests that he built it around a living olive trunk, and this proof melts her doubt, so that they embrace in tears and go together to their old bed.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.173-296 [10]
A moment of testing and anger between the spouses turns into recognition and joyful reunion.
The scene follows the Kural’s order exactly: a quarrel, its resolution, and coming together.
Philosophy
“Amantium irae amoris integratio est” (The quarrels of lovers are the renewal of love).”
— Terence, Andria 555 (Act III, scene 3) [11]
In Terence’s comedy a father consoles himself that the lovers’ quarrel he hears of will only renew their love.
This is the Kural’s claim in one line: the lovers’ sulk leads to a stronger reunion.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton remarry
10 October 1975 · Chobe, Botswana
The film stars Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton first married in Montreal in 1964 and divorced in 1974. About sixteen months later, on 10 October 1975, they quietly remarried beside the Chobe River in Botswana while on holiday there, and the news came out a few days afterwards. The second marriage lasted less than a year.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Their partings and reunions made them one of the most talked-about couples of the twentieth century.
The kural names quarrel, reconciliation and reunion as the fruits of love, and Taylor and Burton's return to each other after their divorce is a famous real-life case of falling out and coming back together.
