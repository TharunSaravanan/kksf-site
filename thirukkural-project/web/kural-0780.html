Kural 780

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

புரந்தார்கண் நீர்மல்கச் சாகிற்பின் சாக்காடு இரந்துகோள் தக்கது உடைத்து.
Purandhaarkan neermalgach chaagirpin saakkaadu Irandhukol thakkadhu udaiththu.

Meaning

If a soldier can die in a way that brings tears to the eyes of the king who cared for him, that death is worth begging for.

Elementary

When someone gives everything to protect others, the people who loved and led them are deeply moved and never forget them. The verse says the greatest honour is to be so loyal and good that your leader is moved to tears for you.

University

Purandhaar are “those who nurtured or protected” the warrior, chiefly the king who maintained him; the condition is that their kan (eyes) neer malga, brim with tears; such a saakkaadu (death) has a worth that makes it irandhu kol thakkadhu, fit to be obtained even by begging (iravu, the act the Kural elsewhere treats as shameful). The paradox is deliberate: Valluvar, who devotes chapters 106-107 to the shame of begging, says this one thing is worth begging for. The couplet closes the chapter on the bond between ruler and soldier rather than on slaughter: the measure of the death is the grief of the one who knew its worth. Solomon Pappaiah renders the king’s eyes as those that weigh the heroes’ deeds; the other commentators take the tears literally.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Pārtha, happy are the kṣatriyas to whom such fighting opportunities come unsought, opening for them the doors of the heavenly planets.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.32 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna describes a righteous battle as a rare opening to heaven for a warrior.
The Kural similarly treats an honourable death as a prize worth seeking, though it measures the honour by the king’s tears rather than by heaven.
Christianity
“Jesus wept. Then said the Jews, Behold how he loved him!”
— John 11:35-36 (KJV) [4]
At the tomb of his friend Lazarus, Jesus weeps, and onlookers see in his tears the depth of his love.
The tears of the leader as a sign of how much the departed was valued is the heart of the Kural’s image.
Islam
“Zaid took over the flag and was martyred. Then it was taken by Jafar who was martyred as well. Then `Abdullah bin Rawaha took the flag but he too was martyred and at that time the eyes of Allah's Messenger (ﷺ) were full of tears.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1246 (Sunnah.com) [5]
Announcing the deaths of three commanders at the battle of Mu’ta, the Prophet wept for them.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s scene: the leader’s eyes filling with tears for faithful fighters who fell.
Judaism
“How have the mighty fallen In the thick of battle—Jonathan, slain on your heights! I grieve for you, My brother Jonathan, You were most dear to me.”
— 2 Samuel 1:25-26 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
David laments Saul and Jonathan, who died fighting the Philistines on Mount Gilboa.
David’s lament is the grief of a future king for warriors he loved, the very tears the Kural says make such a death precious.
Buddhism
Asked whether warriors slain in battle are reborn among the devas, the Buddha at last answers that a warrior’s mind in battle is set on others’ destruction, so such a death leads to a woeful rebirth, not heaven; the warrior-headman weeps at having been misled.
— Yodhajiva Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 42.3 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu; paraphrase) [7]
The Yodhajiva Sutta directly rejects the ancient warrior teaching that death in battle is a path to heaven.
This is the sharpest divergence from the Kural: the death the Kural calls worth begging for, the Buddha calls a grave danger.
Jainism
“A wise man having weighed (both kinds of death) and chosen the better one (taught in) the Law of Compassion, will become calm through patience, with an undisturbed mind (at the time of death).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The wise man deliberately chooses the right kind of death and meets it calmly.
The Kural calls a hero's death that brings tears to his king's eyes worth begging for, and the Jain text also treats a good death as something to be sought and chosen, though it measures that death by inner calm and compassion rather than by a patron's grief.
Sikhism
“The death of brave heroes is blessed, if it is approved by God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 579, Raag Vadhans, Alahanian, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Alahanian (dirges), Guru Nanak says death need not be called bad, and that the hero's death is worthy when it is accepted in God's court.
The Kural says a death that brings tears to the eyes of the king who cared for the warrior is worth begging for; Guru Nanak likewise honours the hero's death, measuring its worth by the approval of the true Master.
Greek Mythology
When Achilles learns that Patroclus has fallen, he pours dust on his head and weeps, and later leads the Myrmidons in mourning and holds funeral games in his honour.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 18 and 23 (paraphrase) [10]
The Iliad gives some of its most moving scenes to a commander’s grief for his fallen companion.
Patroclus’ death is honoured by the tears of the leader who loved him, as the Kural envisions.
Philosophy
In the Funeral Oration, Pericles honours the Athenian war dead, saying that their memory lives on in the hearts of others and urging the living to imitate them.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 2.35-46 (paraphrase) [11]
Thucydides presents public mourning as the city’s way of declaring what the fallen were worth.
The Kural measures an honourable death by the grief of the one who sustained the warrior; Pericles measures it by the city’s remembrance.

Real Life Example

Lincoln weeps for Colonel Elmer Ellsworth
24-25 May 1861 · Alexandria, Virginia, and the White House, Washington, D.C., United States
Colonel Elmer Ellsworth, a young officer and close friend of President Abraham Lincoln, was shot and killed in Alexandria, Virginia, after removing a Confederate flag from the roof of the Marshall House inn, becoming one of the first Union officers to die in the Civil War. Lincoln wept at the news. At his order Ellsworth's body lay in state in the East Room of the White House, where both the President and Mary Lincoln wept during the funeral.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The death of one young officer moved the nation's leader to open grief and made Ellsworth a national symbol in the war's first weeks.
The kural says a warrior's death is worth begging for if it brings tears to the eyes of the leader who cared for him, and Ellsworth's death drew exactly such tears from Lincoln.
