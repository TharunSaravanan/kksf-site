Kural 1009

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

அன்பொரீஇத் தற்செற்று அறநோக்காது ஈட்டிய ஒண்பொருள் கொள்வார் பிறர்.
Anporeeith Tharsetru Aranokkaadhu Eettiya Onporul Kolvaar Pirar

Meaning

Shining riches gathered by giving up love, making yourself go without, and ignoring what is right will be taken by others in the end.

Elementary

A boy saves all his pocket money, never shares, never buys himself a treat, and even cheats a little to get more. One day he moves away and leaves the jar behind, and other kids spend it. Valluvar says money gathered in such a selfish, unhappy way ends up with someone else.

University

The couplet lists three things sacrificed in the getting: anbu oreei (அன்பு ஒரீஇ, 'having forsaken love', the -īi lengthening marking the verbal participle), thaR setRu (தற்செற்று, 'having afflicted oneself', i.e. denying oneself comfort), and aRam nokkaadhu (அறம் நோக்காது, 'without regard for virtue'). The resulting oNporuL (ஒண்பொருள், 'bright, glittering wealth') is taken by piRar (பிறர், 'others'). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Sivayogi Sivakumar read 'others will enjoy it', Kalaignar sharpens it to 'others will plunder it', and Pope's explanation has 'strangers will inherit'; Pope glosses the three omissions as friendship, comfort and charity. Pappaiah reads anbu as love shown to others through giving, which ties the couplet to the chapter's theme rather than to domestic affection. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Isha Upanishad opens by saying that all this world is pervaded by the Lord; enjoy it through renunciation, and do not covet, for whose is wealth?
— Isha Upanishad 1 [3]
Wealth never truly belongs to anyone, so grasping at it is futile.
The Kural's hoarder discovers this when the wealth he amassed passes to others.
Christianity
“There is one alone, and there is not a second; yea, he hath neither child nor brother: yet is there no end of all his labour; neither is his eye satisfied with riches; neither saith he, For whom do I labour, and bereave my soul of good? This is also vanity, yea, it is a sore travail.”
— Ecclesiastes 4:8 (KJV, kingjamesbibleonline.org) [4]
The Preacher portrays a solitary man who toils endlessly and denies himself, never asking who will benefit.
It mirrors the Kural's three omissions, love, self-care and wisdom, and the question of who will finally have the wealth.
Islam
“Who among you considers the wealth of his heirs dearer to him than his own wealth?” They replied, “O Allah's Messenger! There is none among us but loves his own wealth more.” The Prophet said, “So his wealth is whatever he spends (in Allah's Cause) during his life (on good deeds) while the wealth of his heirs is whatever he leaves after his death.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6442 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that what one does not spend on good belongs, in truth, to one's heirs.
This is the Kural's point that wealth amassed without virtue or generosity is really gathered for others.
Judaism
When King Monobaz gave away his treasures in a famine, his relatives protested; he replied that his fathers had stored treasures below and for others, while he was storing them above and for himself.
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 11a [6]
Giving converts wealth into a treasure one truly keeps; hoarding merely saves it for others.
The Kural likewise warns that wealth hoarded without virtue goes to others.
Buddhism
A deva tells the Buddha that when a house is on fire, the vessel carried out is of use, not what burns inside; so, since the world is ablaze with aging and death, one should take out wealth by giving, for what is given is well saved.
— Samyutta Nikaya 1.41, Aditta Sutta [7]
Only wealth given away is truly rescued; what is kept is lost.
The Kural's hoarder, who never gave, loses all his amassed wealth to others.
Jainism
“Leaving behind bipeds and quadrupeds, his fields, his house, his wealth, his corn, and everything; against his will, and accompanied only by his Karman, he enters a new existence, either a good or a bad one. When they have burned with fire on the funeral pile his forlorn, helpless corpse, his wife and sons and kinsfolk will choose another man to provide for them.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 13.24–25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The monk Citra warns King Brahmadatta that at death all property is left behind and others carry on, while only one's karma goes with the soul.
Like the Kural, it shows that wealth gathered without regard for dharma passes to others, leaving its gatherer only the karma of how he got it.
Sikhism
“It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Ashtpadi (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Reflecting on the wealth of rulers ruined in Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak says that such riches are never amassed without wrongdoing and are left behind at death.
The Kural says wealth gathered by abandoning love and virtue will be taken by others; Guru Nanak says it is gathered through sin and stays behind when its owner dies.
Greek Mythology
When Cyrus's troops sack Sardis, the captured Croesus tells Cyrus that the soldiers are not plundering his city or his treasures, for nothing is his any longer, but are carrying off what now belongs to Cyrus.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.88 [10]
The richest king of his age sees his hoard pass in a single day to another.
Croesus's fate illustrates the Kural's warning that amassed wealth is finally taken by others.
Philosophy
Horace reminds Postumus that the years slip away and death comes for all, and that a worthier heir will drink the Caecuban wine he guarded behind a hundred locks.
— Horace, Odes 2.14.25–28 [11]
What one jealously hoards will be enjoyed by an heir after one's death.
This is the Kural's verdict that the hoarder's shining wealth will be taken by others.

Real Life Example

Louis XIV seizes Fouquet's wealth and takes his artists for Versailles
1661 · Vaux-le-Vicomte and Nantes, France
Nicolas Fouquet, Louis XIV's superintendent of finances, amassed a vast fortune and built the magnificent chateau of Vaux-le-Vicomte, where he entertained the king at a lavish fete on 17 August 1661. Less than three weeks later, on 5 September 1661, he was arrested at Nantes and charged over his handling of state finances; his assets were seized, and many works of art from Vaux passed into the royal collections. The king took into his own service Fouquet's architect Louis Le Vau, painter Charles Le Brun and garden designer Andre Le Notre, with the team of craftsmen they had assembled, and set them to work on Versailles, while Fouquet spent the rest of his life in prison.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The splendour Fouquet had built up passed almost overnight to the king, who used it to create something even grander for himself.
The kural says that shining wealth gathered without regard for virtue will in the end be taken by others, and Fouquet's fortune, artists and treasures were taken over by the king.
