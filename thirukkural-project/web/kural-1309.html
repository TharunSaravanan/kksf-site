Kural 1309

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

நீரும் நிழலது இனிதே புலவியும் வீழுநர் கண்ணே இனிது.
Neerum nizhaladhu inidhae pulaviyum veezhunar kannae inidhu.

Meaning

Water is sweet only when it is in the shade. In the same way, sulking is sweet only with those who truly love you.

Elementary

Water in a sunny puddle gets hot and yucky, but water in a cool shady pond tastes lovely. A little sulk is only nice between people who love each other; with someone who does not care, it is just unpleasant.

University

Nīrum niḻalatu iṉitē, ‘even water is sweet only when in the shade,’ and pulaviyum vīḻunar kaṇṇē iṉitu, ‘sulking too is sweet only with (vīḻunar) those who desire/love.’ The emphatic -ē particles stress exclusivity: pique has its charm only inside a secure, mutual love. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss vīḻunar as those who show love; Solomon Pappaiah contrasts water under the sun with water under the shade; Kalaignar notes that water by the shade is cool. Pope renders ‘Water is pleasant in the cooling shade; / So coolness for a time with those we love.’ ytamizh.com divides நிழலது இனிதே, while thirukkural.net prints the metrical division நிழல தினிதே, the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Please tolerate the wrongs that I may have done to You and bear with me as a father with his son, or a friend with his friend, or a lover with his beloved.”
— Bhagavad Gita 11.44 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Arjuna appeals to the special forbearance that exists within loving relationships.
The Kural likewise says that friction is sweet only within such relationships, where love bears it gladly.
Christianity
“Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (KJV) [4]
The proverb holds that hurt from someone who truly loves you is trustworthy, while sweetness from an enemy is not.
The Kural similarly says that a sulk is sweet only from those who love; its value depends on the love behind it.
Islam
“I know when you are pleased with me or angry with me.” … “When you are pleased with me, you say, ‘No, by the Lord of Muhammad,’ but when you are angry with me, then you say, ‘No, by the Lord of Abraham.’” Thereupon I said, “Yes (you are right), but by Allah, O Allah’s Messenger, I leave nothing but your name.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5228 [5]
Aisha’s sulks with the Prophet are gentle and affectionate: even when cross she leaves out only his name, never him.
This is the Kural’s sweet pique between those who love, where the anger is only skin deep and the love remains whole.
Judaism
“For whom the LORD loveth He correcteth, Even as a father the son in whom he delighteth.”
— Proverbs 3:12 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Correction is presented as a sign of love, not of rejection.
The Kural similarly treats a sulk as sweet because it comes from love; between strangers it would be only bitter.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure.”
— Dhammapada 76 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Reproof from the right person, a wise friend, is welcomed as treasure.
The Kural too says a reproach is sweet when it comes from the right person, one who loves.
Jainism
“Quitting your former connections place your affection on nothing; a monk who loves not even those who love him, will be freed from sin and hatred.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 8.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Kapila’s verses tell the monk to give up affection even for those who love him.
The Kural finds sweetness in pique only among those who love; Jain monastic teaching would set aside even that loving bond.
Sikhism
“Those brides who enjoy their Husband Lord are very blessed, resting in the shade of the mango tree.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 762, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak pictures the soul-brides united with the Lord as resting at ease in cool shade.
The Kural says water is sweet only in the shade, as sulking is sweet only with one who truly loves; Guru Nanak likewise pictures the shade of a secure, mutual love as where sweetness lives.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls on Aphrodite, who comes down smiling and gently asks what troubles her this time and whom she should persuade to love her.
— Sappho, fragment 1, Hymn to Aphrodite (paraphrase) [10]
The goddess meets Sappho’s complaint with amused tenderness rather than annoyance.
Complaint received with such loving indulgence is the Kural’s sweet pique: it is pleasant only where love already exists.
Philosophy
Plutarch argues that the true friend is known by his frank speech, which stings but heals, while the flatterer only pleases.
— Plutarch, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend, Moralia (paraphrase) [11]
For Plutarch, sharpness is welcome when it comes from genuine goodwill.
The Kural similarly says a lover’s sharpness is sweet only because it comes from real love.

Real Life Example

Abigail Adams tells John to Remember the Ladies
31 March – 7 May 1776 · Braintree, Massachusetts, and Philadelphia, United States
On 31 March 1776, while John Adams was at the Continental Congress in Philadelphia, Abigail Adams wrote to him from Braintree asking that in the new code of laws he would "Remember the Ladies" and be more generous to women than earlier lawmakers, warning playfully that women might start a rebellion of their own. John replied on 14 April that he could not help laughing at her extraordinary code of laws and teasingly called her saucy. Abigail answered on 7 May that she did not think him very generous to the ladies, since men proclaiming liberty for all still insisted on keeping absolute power over their wives.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14] [15]
The exchange mixes a serious argument for women’s rights with the affectionate teasing of a couple who were completely secure in each other.
The kural says that just as water is sweet only in the shade, a sulk is sweet only between those who truly love each other, and the Adamses’ spirited, mock-indignant letters show pique that delights because the love beneath it is never in doubt.
