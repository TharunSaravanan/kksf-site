Kural 862

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

அன்பிலன் ஆன்ற துணையிலன் தான்துவ்வான் என்பரியும் ஏதிலான் துப்பு.
Anbilan aandra thunaiyilan thaanthuvvaan enpariyum edhilaan thuppu.

Meaning

A person who has no love for their own people, no strong friends to help, and no strength of their own, how could they ever beat a strong enemy?

Elementary

Think of a team captain who is mean to his teammates, has no friends on other teams to help, and is not a strong player himself. When a tough team shows up, how can he possibly win? Kindness, friends and your own strength are what keep you safe.

University

The couplet lists three deficits: anbilan (அன்பிலன், without love, which Pappaiah and Varadarasanar read as lacking affection for kin and subjects), aandra thunaiyilan (ஆன்ற துணையிலன், without worthy or powerful allies) and thaan thuvvaan (தான் துவ்வான், himself lacking strength). The closing phrase enpariyum edhilaan thuppu (என் பரியும் ஏதிலான் துப்பு) asks how such a man could destroy (pari) the strength (thuppu) of a stranger-foe (edhilaan). Parimelazhagar reads the verse as the converse of 861: such a person is himself the ‘weak’ party whom others may safely oppose. The trio of affection, alliance and own strength mirrors the Arthashastra’s concern with internal loyalty and allies (mitra) as elements of royal power. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Arthashastra names the seven constituent elements of a state (the king, ministers, country, fort, treasury, army and ally) and treats a ruler lacking loyal subjects and allies as easily overthrown.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 6.1 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya sees power as a combination of personal capacity, popular loyalty and alliances.
The Kural’s three deficits (no love, no allies, no strength) are the same missing elements.
Christianity
Paul writes that even if he had prophecy, all knowledge and faith to move mountains, without charity (love) he would be nothing.
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
For Paul, love is not an extra but the thing that gives every other gift its worth.
Valluvar likewise puts love (anbu) first among the things without which a person is powerless.
Islam
“And obey Allāh and His Messenger, and do not dispute and [thus] lose courage and [then] your strength would depart; and be patient. Indeed, Allāh is with the patient.”
— Qur’an 8:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns a community facing enemies that internal quarrelling drains away both courage and strength.
Both texts tie a group’s power against foes to the bonds within it.
Judaism
Kohelet observes that two are better than one, that woe comes to the one who falls alone with no one to lift him, and that a threefold cord is not quickly broken.
— Ecclesiastes 4:9-12 (Tanakh) [6]
The sage notes, in simple human terms, that a person without companions is vulnerable.
It echoes Valluvar’s friendless man who cannot withstand an enemy.
Buddhism
When Ananda suggests that admirable friendship is half the holy life, the Buddha corrects him: it is the whole of the holy life.
— Upaddha Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 45.2 [7]
The Buddha treats good companionship as essential, not optional, for spiritual progress.
Valluvar too treats worthy companions as indispensable, though for worldly survival.
Jainism
“Mother, father, daughter-in-law, brother, wife, and sons will not be able to help me, when I suffer for my own deeds.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana teaches that no kinsman or ally can shield a soul from the fruit of its own karma, so the seeker must rely on his own conduct.
The Kural says a man without loving kin, strong allies or strength of his own cannot break an enemy. The Jain verse agrees that relying on others fails, but it moves the struggle inward, where only one's own strength counts.
Sikhism
In 1699 Guru Gobind Singh instituted the Khalsa, a purified and reconstituted Sikh community bound together as a disciplined order of saint-soldiers committed to courage and to Sikh ideals.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, articles Khalsa and Guru Gobind Singh (paraphrase) [9]
By founding the Khalsa the tenth Guru joined his followers in one loyal brotherhood and gave it the strength to defend itself.
The Kural says a man without love for his own people, without allies and without strength cannot break an enemy; Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa built exactly those three things, a devoted people, a brotherhood and armed strength.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the father and his quarrelling sons, he shows them that a bundle of sticks cannot be broken while single sticks snap easily.
— Aesop’s Fables, ‘The Father and His Sons’ [10]
The fable teaches that unity makes a family strong and division makes each member easy to defeat.
Valluvar’s loveless, ally-less man is the single stick.
Philosophy
Machiavelli advises that the best fortress a prince can have is not to be hated by his people, since walls will not save a ruler whose subjects turn against him.
— Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 20 (tr. W. K. Marriott) [11]
For Machiavelli, the loyalty of one’s own people is a more reliable defence than any fortification.
Valluvar likewise makes affection for one’s own people the first line of defence.

Real Life Example

Maximilian's Mexican empire falls once French troops leave
1866-1867 · Querétaro, Mexico
Maximilian, an Austrian archduke, became emperor of Mexico in 1864 with the backing of French troops sent by Napoleon III. Under diplomatic pressure from the United States and rising costs at home, Napoleon withdrew the French army, which had fully embarked by March 1867, leaving Maximilian with a small force of foreign volunteers and largely untrained Mexican draftees. Besieged by republican forces at Querétaro, his army surrendered in May 1867, and he was executed in June.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A regime that lacked broad support among its own subjects, lost its only powerful ally and had little strength of its own collapsed within months.
The kural asks how one without the affection of his people, without strong allies and without strength of his own could ever overcome his enemy, and Maximilian's fall answers it.
