Kural 292

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

பொய்மையும் வாய்மை யிடத்த புரைதீர்ந்த நன்மை பயக்கும் எனின்.
Poymaiyum vaaymai yidaththa puraitheerndha Nanmai payakkum enin.

Meaning

Even a lie can count as the truth if it brings about pure good with no harm in it.

Elementary

Imagine a friend is scared before a big test and asks if they look nervous. Saying “You look calm and ready!” — to help them, not to trick them for your own gain — is the kind of “kind untruth” this kural talks about. But it only counts if it does real good and hurts no one.

University

This is one of the most debated couplets in the Kural. Having defined truth by harmlessness in 291, Valluvar draws the logical consequence: a statement that is factually false but yields ‘faultless good’ (puraitheerndha nanmai) occupies ‘the place of truth’ (vaaymai idaththa). The qualifier purai theerndha — ‘with the defect removed’ — is strict: the good must be free of any taint, not merely useful to the speaker. Commentators diverge on scope: Varadarasan says such words ‘obtain the place’ of truth; Pappaiah reads it as permission to speak falsely where truth would otherwise be required. The verse places the Kural in a consequentialist line of Indian ethics (compare Krishna’s teaching in the Mahabharata) rather than the absolutism of Kant or of Buddhist precept.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Truth may be unutterable, and even falsehood may be utterable where falsehood would become truth and truth would become falsehood.”
— Mahabharata, Karna Parva, Section 69 (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna the story of the ascetic Kausika, who had vowed always to speak the truth. When robbers asked him which way their fleeing victims had gone, he told them truthfully; the victims were killed, and Kausika fell into hell for the harm his ‘truth’ caused.
Exactly the Kural’s logic: a harmful ‘truth’ ceases to be truth, and a harmless untruth can take truth’s place.
Christianity
“Likewise also was not Rahab the harlot justified by works, when she had received the messengers, and had sent them out another way?”
— James 2:25 (KJV) [4]
Rahab hid Israel’s spies and misled the king’s men to save their lives (Joshua 2); the New Testament praises her as justified by her deed.
A deception that saved innocent lives is honoured — close to the Kural’s ‘faultless good.’
Islam
“He who makes peace between the people by inventing good information or saying good things, is not a liar.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2692 [5]
The Prophet taught that someone who carries good (even embellished) words between quarrelling people to reconcile them is not counted a liar.
Like the Kural, Islam allows a narrowly bounded untruth when its fruit is pure good — here, peace.
Judaism
“It is permitted for a person to depart from the truth in a matter that will bring peace.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 65b (William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz tr.) [6]
The Talmud cites Joseph’s brothers, and even God’s softened report of Sarah’s words to Abraham, as examples of altering the truth to preserve peace. Rabbi Natan calls it a mitzvah.
Jewish law, like the Kural, lets a peace-making untruth stand in truth’s place.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be unfactual, untrue, unbeneficial, but endearing & agreeable to others, he does not say them.”
— Abhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 58 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha lists which words he will speak: only what is true and beneficial, with a sense of the proper time. As the translator notes, the possibility of saying something untrue but beneficial is not even considered.
An honest contrast: early Buddhism shares the Kural’s concern for benefit, but will never allow falsehood to take truth’s place.
Jainism
“The mendicant should not tell it, nor show it, he should not comply with their request, but look on silently, or, though knowing it, he should say that he did not know.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.3.3.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884, pp. 146-147) [8]
If travellers ask a wandering monk whether he has seen a man or an animal on the road, the Ācārāṅga tells him not to give them away. He should stay silent or, though he knows, say that he does not know, so that no living being comes to harm.
Valluvar allows that even a falsehood stands in place of truth when it produces faultless good. The Jain monk is likewise permitted to say 'I do not know', against the literal facts, in order to protect life.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, where there is spiritual wisdom, there is righteousness and Dharma. Where there is falsehood, there is sin.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks Bhagat Kabir sets falsehood plainly on the side of sin. Gurbani as a whole treats kooR (falsehood) as the wall between the soul and God.
The Kural allows that a falsehood can stand in the place of truth if it produces faultless good. Gurbani offers no such exception, which makes this verse a point of contrast rather than agreement.
Greek Mythology
“We know how to speak many false things as though they were true; but we know, when we will, to utter true things.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 27-28 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
The Muses tell Hesiod that they can speak convincing falsehoods as well as truth — the divine source of poetry itself deals in both.
Greek myth, like the Kural, recognises that falsehood can wear truth’s clothing — though the Muses do not limit it to ‘faultless good.’
Philosophy
In the Republic (389b), Socrates says falsehood is useless to the gods but may be useful to humans ‘as a medicine,’ and that only the rulers may use it, for the good of the city.
— Plato, Republic 389b (paraphrase of Jowett tr.) [11]
Plato accepts a ‘medicinal lie’ for the common good, while condemning the ‘true lie’ of self-deception in the soul.
Both Plato and Valluvar allow falsehood when it heals rather than harms — though Plato restricts it to rulers.

Real Life Example

Doctors at Rome's Fatebenefratelli Hospital invent K Syndrome
October 1943 - June 1944 · Fatebenefratelli Hospital, Tiber Island, Rome, Italy
After the Nazi round-up of Jews in Rome on 16 October 1943, doctors at the Fatebenefratelli Hospital, led by head physician Giovanni Borromeo, admitted Jewish and anti-fascist refugees as patients and listed them in official records as suffering from K Syndrome, an invented and supposedly contagious disease. Occupying soldiers who searched the hospital avoided the wards said to hold these patients, and the hospital continued sheltering refugees until the Allies entered Rome in June 1944. In 2004 Yad Vashem recognised Borromeo as Righteous Among the Nations.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The doctors deliberately told a falsehood whose only purpose and effect was to save innocent lives, at serious risk to themselves.
The kural says even a falsehood takes the place of truth when it yields good wholly free of fault, and the invented disease that protected refugees is about as clear a case of such a blameless lie as history offers.
