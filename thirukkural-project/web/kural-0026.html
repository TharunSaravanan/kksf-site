Kural 26

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

செயற்கரிய செய்வார் பெரியர் சிறியர் செயற்கரிய செய்கலா தார்.
Seyarkariya seyvaar periyar siriyar, Seyarkariya seykalaa thaar.

Meaning

Great people do things that are hard to do. Small people cannot do what is hard to do.

Elementary

Two people stand in front of the same steep hill. One starts climbing even though it hurts; the other says “too hard” and walks away. This kural says that is the whole difference between a big person and a small one — not how they look, just which one starts climbing.

University

The couplet is built as a chiasmus, with “seyarkariya” (things difficult to do) standing at the head of both halves so that the same object divides humanity into two classes. What is striking is the definition's austerity: Valluvar offers no content for greatness at all, no list of virtues, only a functional test. “Periyar” and “siriyar” — the great and the small — are terms he uses throughout the Kural as a moral rather than a social ranking, and here he strips them of every birth-based or wealth-based criterion. In context the difficult things are the ascetic disciplines named in the surrounding verses: quenching the senses, discerning the two worlds, holding anger. Later Tamil tradition, reading the chapter alongside yogic literature, glossed the “hard things” as the eight limbs of yoga from yama through samadhi. The verse's blunt symmetry has made it one of the most-quoted couplets in Tamil, routinely cited well outside any ascetic context.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Rise, awake! having obtained your boons, understand them! The sharp edge of a razor is difficult to pass over; thus the wise say the path (to the Self) is hard.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.14 (trans. Max Müller, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15) [4]
The Katha Upanishad refuses to soften the demand: the road to self-knowledge is compared to walking a razor's edge, and the hearer is roused rather than comforted.
It supplies exactly what the Kural leaves implicit — that the thing which separates the great from the small is a path most people will not attempt because it is hard.
Christianity
“But Jesus beheld them, and said unto them, With men this is impossible; but with God all things are possible.”
— Matthew 19:21-26 (KJV) [5]
The disciples have just watched a wealthy man refuse the demand to sell everything and follow, and they ask in astonishment who then can be saved; Jesus concedes the difficulty outright before answering it.
The passage stages the Kural's division exactly — one man finds the hard thing undoable and walks away, and the difficulty is named as the whole issue.
Islam
“The Paradise is surrounded by hardships and the Hell-Fire is surrounded by temptations.”
— Sahih Muslim 2822 (Book of Paradise) [6]
The hadith pictures the two destinations as walled in by what stands in front of them: to reach Paradise one must push through what is unpleasant, while what is pleasant leads the other way.
It gives the Kural's criterion a topography — doing the hard thing is literally the only route, and failing to do it is what defines the lesser path.
Judaism
Ben Hei Hei's maxim in Pirkei Avot, l'fum tza'ara agra, teaches that the reward is in proportion to the difficulty — the harder the exertion a mitzvah demands, the greater what is earned by it.
— Pirkei Avot 5:23 [7]
The rabbinic principle turns difficulty into a measure of value rather than a reason for exemption, so effort itself becomes the unit of account.
Both sayings make the capacity to shoulder what is hard the index of a person's standing.
Buddhism
“Though one may conquer a thousand times a thousand men in battle, yet he indeed is the noblest victor who conquers himself.”
— Dhammapada 103, Sahassavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada sets the hardest available achievement — self-conquest — above the most spectacular external one, and awards greatness accordingly.
Same verdict as the Kural: the title of 'great' goes to whoever can do the thing that is genuinely difficult to do.
Jainism
“As it is difficult to cross the heavenly Ganges, or to swim against the current, or to swim with one's arms over the sea, so it is difficult to get over the ocean of duties.” … ‘but in this world nothing is difficult for one who is free from desire.’”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 19.24-44, esp. 19.36 and 19.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
Mrigaputra's parents list at length how hard a monk's life is, from lifelong non-injury to swallowing 'iron grains', to talk him out of it. He answers that for one free of desire nothing is too hard, then takes the vows and reaches perfection.
The Kural defines the great as those who do what is hard to do. The Uttaradhyayana spells out exactly how hard renunciation is, then shows a great soul doing it anyway.
Sikhism
“If you desire to play this game of love with Me, then step onto My Path with your head in hand. When you place your feet on this Path, give Me your head, and do not pay any attention to public opinion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaran te Vadheek, Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak calls the spiritual path the hardest commitment there is: one must be ready to give up one's head and ignore what others think.
The Kural separates the great from the small by whether they do what is hard. Guru Nanak's path is open only to those willing to do the hardest thing.
Greek Mythology
Heracles is set twelve labours that no other man could survive — strangling the Nemean lion whose hide no weapon can pierce, fetching Cerberus out of the underworld — and his standing in Greek myth rests entirely on his having completed tasks designed to be impossible.
— Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.5.1-12 [11]
The labours are explicitly chosen for their undoability; the hero's greatness is constituted by the doing of them rather than demonstrated by anything else.
Greek myth's central definition of a great man is the Kural's definition: the one who does what cannot be done.
Philosophy
“…it is no easy task to be good. For in everything it is no easy task to find the middle… to do this to the right person, to the right extent, at the right time, with the right motive, and in the right way, that is not for every one, nor is it easy; wherefore goodness is both rare and laudable and noble.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.9, 1109a24-30 (trans. W.D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle argues that virtue consists in hitting a mean that is difficult to locate and harder to hit reliably, and concludes that this difficulty is exactly why goodness is rare and admirable.
He reaches the Kural's conclusion by analysis: it is the difficulty of the thing that makes those who manage it great, and its rarity that makes most people small.

Real Life Example

Hillary and Tenzing make the first ascent of Everest
29 May 1953 · Mount Everest, Nepal
On 29 May 1953 Edmund Hillary of New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa mountaineer, reached the summit of Mount Everest, becoming the first people known to stand on the world's highest mountain. Tenzing had taken part in six earlier attempts on the mountain going back to 1935. The two men spent about fifteen minutes on top before descending.
— Sources: [13] [14]
After decades of failed attempts, the climb became a worldwide symbol of doing what had seemed impossible.
The kural defines the great as those who do what is hard to do, and the first ascent of Everest is one of history's clearest cases of people achieving what others could not.
