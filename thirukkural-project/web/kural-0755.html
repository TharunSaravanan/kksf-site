Kural 755

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

அருளொடும் அன்பொடும் வாராப் பொருளாக்கம் புல்லார் புரள விடல்.
Arulodum anbodum vaaraap porulaakkam pullaar purala vidal.

Meaning

Do not hold on to money that comes without kindness and love. Let it go.

Elementary

Suppose you could win a big prize, but only by being mean to a friend. Valluvar says to let that prize go. Money that makes you unkind is like a shiny apple that is rotten inside.

University

The first line names two qualities the earning must carry: arul (அருள், compassion toward all beings, the subject of chapter 25) and anbu (அன்பு, affection toward one’s own, the subject of chapter 8). The second line is vivid: pullaar, ‘not embracing’ (from pullu, to hug or cling), and purala vidal, ‘letting it roll off’, as one lets go of something that slips from the arms. Parimelazhagar applies the verse to the ruler, who should refuse revenue gathered by cruelty to his subjects, and Pope and Drew’s English accordingly begins with ‘(Kings) should rather avoid’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah generalise it to anyone who earns. The sandhi-joined text reads அருளொடு மன்பொடும், which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The first verse of the Isha Upanishad teaches that all this is pervaded by the Lord, that one should enjoy by renunciation, and that one should not covet anyone’s wealth (ma gridhah kasya svid dhanam).
— Isha Upanishad 1 [3]
The Upanishad teaches that wealth is to be enjoyed with detachment and never grasped from others.
The Upanishad’s refusal to grasp at others’ wealth matches the Kural’s advice to let wrongly gained wealth roll away.
Christianity
“We are. Thirdly, to gain all we can without hurting our neighbour. But this we may not, cannot do, if we love our neighbour as ourselves.”
— John Wesley, Sermon 50, The Use of Money, I.3 [4]
Wesley tells Methodists to gain all they can, but never by harming a neighbour’s body, soul or livelihood, since that breaks the law of love.
Wesley’s rule that gain must never break love for one’s neighbour is the Kural’s rule that gain must come with anbu and arul.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly but only [in lawful] business by mutual consent.”
— Qur’an 4:29 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an forbids taking others’ wealth unjustly and allows only fair trade that both sides agree to.
Both texts reject wealth that comes at the cost of justice and goodwill toward others.
Judaism
“Ill-gotten wealth is of no avail, But righteousness saves from death.”
— Proverbs 10:2 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs teaches that treasure gained by wickedness brings no real benefit.
Wealth gained without righteousness is worthless, which is why the Kural says to let it go.
Buddhism
“Discerning clearly, a clever person knows both kinds of happiness: the other kind is not worth a sixteenth part of the happiness of blamelessness.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.62, Anana Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha ranks the happiness of owning and using wealth far below the happiness of a blameless life.
Wealth that costs one’s blamelessness is not worth keeping, which is the Kural’s point in letting it roll away.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that wealth gained by wrongful acts will be lost and leaves its owner bound by passion and hatred, binding further karma.
The Kural tells us to let go of wealth that comes without compassion and love, and the Jain verse gives the reason: wealth gained by evil deeds does not stay and drags its owner into bondage.
Sikhism
“For the sake of this wealth, so many were ruined; because of this wealth, so many have been disgraced. It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa (Ashtapadi, Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Reflecting on the fall of the Lodi nobility before Babur, Guru Nanak says that their hoarded wealth brought ruin and disgrace, because it had been gathered through wrongdoing.
The Kural tells us to let wealth gained without compassion and love roll away rather than embrace it; Guru Nanak shows what such wealth does to those who cling to it.
Greek Mythology
“Wealth should not be seized: god-given wealth is much better; for if a man take great wealth violently and perforce, or if he steal it through his tongue, as often happens when gain deceives men’s sense and dishonour tramples down honour, the gods soon blot him out and make that man’s house low, and wealth attends him only for a little time.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 320-326 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod warns that wealth taken by force or deceit is soon lost and brings the gods’ punishment.
Hesiod, like Valluvar, tells us to refuse wealth gained without justice or kindness.
Philosophy
“There are two sorts of wealth-getting, as I have said; one is a part of household management, the other is retail trade: the former necessary and honorable, while that which consists in exchange is justly censured; for it is unnatural, and a mode by which men gain from one another.”
— Aristotle, Politics, Book I, Part X (trans. B. Jowett) [11]
Aristotle separates honourable wealth-getting that serves the household from money-making that profits at other people’s expense.
Aristotle, like the Kural, rejects gain made at the cost of others.

Real Life Example

The Kangxi Emperor freezes China's tax quota permanently
1712 · Beijing, China
In 1712 the Kangxi Emperor of the Qing dynasty decreed that the empire's direct tax quota, tied to the count of adult males liable to tax, would be fixed at its existing level and never increased, even as the population grew. The policy is summed up in the phrase yongbu jiafu, never to add taxes. Economic historians note that the cap was largely observed for more than a century, so the tax burden per person fell as China's population and farmland grew; some scholarly accounts date the measure to 1713.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The emperor gave up revenue he could have raised, choosing light taxation of a growing population over a fuller treasury.
The kural tells rulers to let go of wealth that does not come with compassion for the people, and Kangxi's freeze declined the extra revenue that heavier taxing of his subjects would have brought.
