Kural 1168

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

மன்னுயிர் எல்லாம் துயிற்றி அளித்திரா என்னல்லது இல்லை துணை.
Mannuyir ellaam thuyitri aliththiraa ennalladhu illai thunai.

Meaning

Poor night! It has put every living thing to sleep, and now it has no friend left but me.

Elementary

Have you ever been the only one awake in the house at night? Everyone is asleep, it is very quiet, and you feel like it is just you and the dark. The wife feels so lonely that she starts feeling sorry for the night itself, because the only friend the night has left is her.

University

The couplet personifies irā (இரா, night): it has put to sleep (tuyiṟṟi, துயிற்றி) all ‘abiding lives’ (maṉṉuyir, மன்னுயிர்), and now has no tuṇai (துணை, companion) except (allatu) me. Aḷittu (அளித்து) is read in two ways: Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah take it as ‘pitiable’ (‘this night is to be pitied’), while Pope’s commentary-based prose takes it as ‘graciously’ (‘the night which graciously lulls to sleep all living creatures’). The irony is turned inward: by pitying the night’s solitude the heroine reveals her own. Kalaignar makes it an address, ‘O Night!’. Pope’s verse: ‘All living souls in slumber soft she steeps; / But me alone kind night for her companing keeps!’ The text is identical on both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Searching Lanka by night while the city sleeps, Hanuman at last finds Sita in the Ashoka grove, worn with grief, keeping a lonely watch among sleeping guards.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (paraphrase) [3]
Sita, separated from Rama, is the one wakeful figure in a slumbering city.
Like the Kural’s heroine, she keeps company with the night while all others sleep.
Christianity
“I watch, and am as a sparrow alone upon the house top.”
— Psalms 102:7 (KJV) [4]
The afflicted psalmist lies awake, solitary as a lone bird on a roof.
The Kural’s heroine also watches through the night with no companion but the dark.
Islam
“And made your sleep [a means for] rest.”
— Qur’an 78:9 (Sahih International) [5]
Among the signs of God’s care, the Qur’an names sleep, given to all creatures for their rest.
The night gives this rest to every living being except the Kural’s heroine, who is left awake.
Judaism
“She weepeth sore in the night, And her tears are on her cheeks; She hath none to comfort her Among all her lovers.”
— Lamentations 1:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Lamentations pictures Jerusalem as a woman weeping alone through the night with no one to comfort her.
This is the Kural’s heroine at night: weeping, wakeful, and without any companion.
Buddhism
On the night of his renunciation, Prince Siddhartha wakes in the palace to find everyone around him asleep, and leaves while Yasodhara and their newborn son sleep on, so that she wakes to find herself without him.
— Jataka Nidanakatha, the Great Renunciation (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddhist narrative of the Great Departure is set in a sleeping palace with one figure awake.
The Kural’s scene is the same night seen from Yasodhara’s side: all others sleep, and the one left behind keeps watch alone.
Jainism
In the dialogue of Kesi and Gautama, a frightful fire is said to be blazing up and burning embodied beings, and Gautama explains that the fire is the passions, quenched only by knowledge, virtue and penance.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.50-53 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain teaching describes the passions as a fire that keeps burning those who have not mastered them.
While the night quiets every other being, the heroine alone is kept awake by the burning of her longing.
Sikhism
“Like a patient nearing his death, whom should I tell this state that has resulted due to the fire of separation? All these things (moon, stars, dresses etc.) are becoming uncomfortable and painful for me, whereas all these are highly peace-giving and source of comfort for the whole world.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabitt Savaiye 668, English translation as given in the BaniDB database [9]
Bhai Gurdas has the separated lover say that the moon, stars and other things that comfort everyone else only torment her. His Kabitt Savaiye are early exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural's heroine pities the night, which has lulled every creature to sleep and is left with her alone for company; Bhai Gurdas's lover likewise finds that what soothes the whole world leaves her alone in her pain.
Greek Mythology
A short poem traditionally attributed to Sappho says that the moon has set and the Pleiades, it is midnight, the hour goes by, and the speaker lies alone.
— Sappho (attributed), fragment 168B Voigt, ‘The moon has set’ (paraphrase) [10]
The lines catch the precise moment of midnight solitude in a few words.
Like the Kural’s heroine, the speaker is awake at midnight with no companion but the night.
Philosophy
Epictetus distinguishes being alone from being forlorn: a forlorn person is one who has lost those he depended on and feels himself abandoned even in a crowd.
— Epictetus, Discourses 3.13 (What solitude is) (paraphrase) [11]
Epictetus analyses loneliness as the feeling of being bereft of a needed companion.
The heroine’s loneliness is of this kind, so complete that she imagines the night itself as her only companion.

Real Life Example

Pablo Neruda publishes Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair
1924 · Russia
In 1924 the young Chilean poet Pablo Neruda, then about twenty, published Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair. Its Poem 20, often called Tonight I Can Write, is spoken on a starry night by a lover who remembers holding a woman he once loved on nights like this one and grieves that she is no longer with him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters record years of wartime separation borne largely through writing.
The kural pictures the night, after it has put every creature to sleep, keeping company only with the heroine, and Neruda's speaker likewise sits alone with the night, remembering the one who is gone.
