Kural 305

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

தன்னைத்தான் காக்கின் சினங்காக்க காவாக்கால் தன்னையே கொல்லுஞ் சினம்.
Thannaiththaan kaakkin sinangaakka kaavaakkaal Thannaiye kollum sinam.

Meaning

If you want to protect yourself, keep your anger in check. If you don’t, your own anger will destroy you.

Elementary

Anger is like a fire you carry in your own pocket. If you don't put it out, it burns you first. So the best way to keep yourself safe is to keep anger away.

University

The couplet turns on the reflexive thannai ('oneself'), which frames it: the whole argument is about self-preservation, not the protection of others. Valluvar's point is strategic as much as moral: anger, left unguarded, turns back on its owner, destroying health, judgment, reputation, and eventually life. The repetition of kaa ('guard') across both lines makes guarding anger identical with guarding the self. This self-interested argument against anger complements the altruistic ones elsewhere in the chapter and matches Stoic and Buddhist analyses that anger harms its owner before its target.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.63 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna traces a chain of self-destruction: anger leads to delusion, loss of memory, and loss of intelligence, ending in ruin.
The Gita's final step, 'praṇaśyati' ('one perishes'), is the kural's 'anger will kill you'.
Christianity
“For wrath killeth the foolish man, and envy slayeth the silly one.”
— Job 5:2 (KJV) [4]
Eliphaz, speaking in Job, states a proverb that wrath is fatal to the one who harbours it.
Almost a restatement of the kural's second line: anger kills its own owner.
Islam
“The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength, but the strong is the one who controls himself while in anger.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 [5]
The Prophet redefines strength as self-control at the moment of anger.
Like the kural, the emphasis is on guarding oneself (yamliku nafsahu, 'controls himself') against one's own anger.
Judaism
At Meribah, Moses, angered by the people, addressed them as 'rebels' and struck the rock instead of speaking to it; as a result God decreed that he would not bring Israel into the Promised Land.
— Numbers 20:7-12; Maimonides, Shemonah Perakim ch. 4 [6]
Jewish tradition (for example Maimonides in his Eight Chapters) identifies Moses' failing here as anger, which cost the greatest prophet his life's goal.
Even for Moses, failing to guard against anger turned it against himself, as the kural warns.
Buddhism
“Like oneself, all beings hold themselves most dear, yet an angry person, deranged, can kill himself in many ways: with a sword, taking poison, hanging himself by a rope in a mountain glen.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.60, Kodhana Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The verses of the Kodhana Sutta describe how anger can drive a person to destroy even himself.
Almost literally the kural's 'anger will kill you yourself'.
Jainism
“The passions are the fire; knowledge, a virtuous life, and penances are the water; sprinkled with the drops of knowledge the fire of the passions is extinguished and does not burn me.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23.50–53 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Keśi asks how Gautama puts out the frightful fire that burns embodied beings. Gautama explains that the fire is the passions (Jacobi notes that the plural 'fires' stands for the four kaṣāyas), and that he protects himself by constantly dousing them with knowledge and discipline.
Valluvar tells anyone who wants to protect himself to guard against anger, or it will consume him. Gautama describes exactly that kind of guarding: he keeps the fire of passion from burning him by constantly dousing it.
Sikhism
“Unfulfilled sexual desire and unresolved anger waste the body away,”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 932, Raag Raamkalee, Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Dakhni Oankar Guru Nanak compares anger and lust to borax that melts gold: they consume the person who harbours them.
The Kural warns that if you do not guard against anger, it will destroy you. Guru Nanak likewise describes anger wearing away the very one who keeps it.
Greek Mythology
Denied the armour of the dead Achilles, Ajax sets out in rage to kill the Greek commanders; Athena drives him mad so that he slaughters livestock instead, and on waking in shame he falls on his own sword.
— Sophocles, Ajax [10]
Sophocles' tragedy shows unchecked wrath ending in the self-destruction of the greatest warrior after Achilles.
A literal enactment of 'if you do not guard against anger, it will kill you'.
Philosophy
“...careless of itself provided it hurts another, rushing upon the very point of the sword, and greedy for revenge even when it drags the avenger to ruin with itself.”
— Seneca, On Anger (De Ira), Book I.1 (trans. Aubrey Stewart, 1900) [11]
Seneca opens De Ira by describing anger as a passion that will wreck its owner so long as it can harm someone else.
Seneca's 'drags the avenger to ruin with itself' is the kural's point exactly.

Real Life Example

Surgeon John Hunter collapses during a heated hospital board meeting
16 October 1793 · St George's Hospital, London, England
John Hunter, the leading surgeon and anatomist of his day, had long suffered from angina pectoris. On 16 October 1793, during a meeting of the board of governors of St George's Hospital, he was engaged in a heated discussion over the admission of students when he collapsed and died. His collections later passed to the Royal College of Surgeons, and he was reburied in Westminster Abbey in 1859.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Hunter's death in the middle of a heated quarrel has long been cited as an example of how anger can endanger the person who gives way to it.
The kural says that if you do not guard yourself against anger it will destroy you yourself, and Hunter's life ended in the very moment of a bitter dispute.
