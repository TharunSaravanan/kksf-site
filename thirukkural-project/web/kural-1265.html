Kural 1265

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

காண்கமன் கொண்கனைக் கண்ணாரக் கண்டபின் நீங்கும்என் மென்தோள் பசப்பு.
Kaankaman konkanaik kannaarak kandapin neengum-en men-dhoal pasappu.

Meaning

Let me just see my husband until my eyes are full. Once I have seen him, the paleness on my soft arms will go away by itself.

Elementary

When you are missing someone, you can feel pale and tired. But the moment you see them, you feel better right away. The lady in this verse says: “I just need to see him properly, and all my paleness will disappear like magic.”

University

Kāṇka maṉ is an optative: ‘may I see’, with maṉ marking an intense wish. Koṇkaṉ, ‘husband’ (originally ‘lord of the seashore’ in the neytal landscape), is to be seen kaṇṇāra, ‘to the eyes’ full’. Pacappu is pacalai, the sallow pallor that in Tamil love convention spreads over a woman’s body in separation; chapter 119 is devoted to it. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar say it will vanish ‘of itself’ (தானே) once she sees him, so the cure is sight alone. The commentary tradition associated with Parimelazhagar treats this as her answer to the friend who urges patience. Text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Krishna reappears among the gopis after vanishing from the rasa dance, they rise up all at once, their eyes wide with love, as if life had returned to their bodies.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.32.2-3 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada; paraphrase) [3]
In the Bhagavata, the mere sight of the returning beloved revives those who were wasting in separation.
The heroine expects exactly this: one full look at him and her pallor will vanish.
Christianity
“And ye now therefore have sorrow: but I will see you again, and your heart shall rejoice, and your joy no man taketh from you.”
— John 16:22 (KJV) [4]
Jesus promises his grieving disciples that seeing him again will turn their sorrow into lasting joy.
The Kural’s heroine makes the same promise to herself: seeing him will cure her sorrow.
Islam
“And when the bearer of good tidings arrived, he cast it over his face, and he returned [once again] seeing. He said, ‘Did I not tell you that I know from Allah that which you do not know?’”
— Qur’an 12:96 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s sight, lost through grief for Joseph, returns when Joseph’s shirt is laid on his face.
As in the Kural, the bodily mark of separation is healed by contact with the beloved’s return.
Judaism
“O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock, in the covert of the cliff, let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.”
— Song of Songs 2:14 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The lover asks only to see the beloved’s face and hear her voice.
The heroine’s whole wish is the same: let me see him, and that will be enough.
Buddhism
The introduction to the Canda-Kinnara Jataka tells how, when the Buddha first returned to Kapilavatthu, Yasodhara stayed in her chamber, saying he would come to her if she had any worth; he came, and she held his feet and wept.
— Canda-Kinnara Jataka (Jataka No. 485), introductory story (tr. E.B. Cowell et al.; paraphrase) [7]
The commentarial story gives Yasodhara a long, faithful waiting rewarded by the sight of her former husband.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Yasodhara’s long sorrow finds release the moment she sees him again.
Jainism
“After practising severe austerities both of them became Kêvalins, and having completely annihilated their Karman, they reached the highest perfection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati and Rathanemi end not in reunion with a beloved but in liberation, each through their own discipline.
The Kural’s heroine expects healing from seeing her husband; the Jain story places final healing in liberation rather than in any human reunion.
Sikhism
“Gazing upon my Beloved with my eyes, I have obtained all treasures;”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 372, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that simply seeing the Beloved Lord with one's own eyes gives everything a person could want.
The wife says that once she sees her husband to her eyes' content, her paleness will vanish of itself; Guru Arjan likewise finds that the sight of the Beloved fulfils every lack.
Greek Mythology
Heracles brings a veiled woman back from the dead to Admetus, who at first refuses her; when the veil is lifted he sees his wife Alcestis and his grief turns to joy.
— Euripides, Alcestis (final scene) (paraphrase) [10]
Euripides stages a reunion in which the mere sight of the lost wife ends the mourning.
Admetus’ grief, like the heroine’s pallor, vanishes the moment he sees the one he lost.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates says that when the soul is parted from the beloved it is stung and in pain, but when it looks on the beloved again it is refreshed and its pangs cease.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251c-e (tr. Benjamin Jowett; paraphrase) [11]
Plato describes the lover’s suffering in absence and the relief that comes from sight of the beloved.
This is the Kural’s point in philosophical form: separation makes the lover ill, and seeing the beloved heals.

Real Life Example

Natan and Avital Sharansky are reunited after twelve years
1974-1986 · Soviet Union and Israel
Natan (Anatoly) Sharansky married Natalya Stiglitz in 1974, and she was allowed to emigrate to Israel the day after the wedding, expecting him to follow. He was arrested in March 1977 and sentenced to 13 years in prison and labour camps, while she, taking the name Avital, campaigned for his release from Jerusalem and abroad. He was freed in an East-West prisoner exchange on 11 February 1986 and arrived in Israel that day, where the couple were together again.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Avital's long campaign was driven by a single wish: to see her husband again, after a marriage that had lasted only one day before their separation.
The kural's heroine asks only to see her husband until her eyes are full, sure that her pallor will then fade; Avital spent twelve years working toward exactly that sight.
