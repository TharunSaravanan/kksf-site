Kural 622

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

வெள்ளத் தனைய இடும்பை அறிவுடையான் உள்ளத்தின் உள்ளக் கெடும்.
Vellath thanaiya itumpai arivutaiyaan Ullaththin ullak ketum.

Meaning

Even trouble as huge as a flood goes away once a wise person thinks about it with a steady mind.

Elementary

Think of a huge wave rushing at a beach. It looks scary, but when it hits a strong rock it just breaks into foam. A wise, calm mind is like that rock: even a flood of problems falls apart when it meets clear, brave thinking.

University

The flood simile (veḷḷattaṉaiya, ‘flood-like’) stresses both the size and the sudden, overwhelming onrush of trouble (iṭumpai). The second line plays on uḷḷam (mind) and uḷḷa (to think, to resolve): the trouble perishes (keṭum) merely by the wise man’s mind thinking upon it, which Mu. Varadarasanar explains as reflecting on the nature of the trouble, and Solomon Pappaiah as the mind refusing to slacken. Parimelazhagar and later commentators read uḷḷam here as close to ūkkam, the mental elation or resolve praised in chapter 60, so that one act of firm resolve dissolves a whole flood of difficulty. The text agrees in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net, and Pope’s rendering (‘When wise men’s mind regards it, it is gone’) follows the same construal.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Having gained that state, one does not consider any attainment to be greater. Being thus established, one is not shaken even in the midst of the greatest calamity.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.22 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna describes the yogi whose mind is settled in the Self as one whom even the heaviest sorrow (duḥkhena guruṇāpi) cannot move.
The Gita and the Kural both place victory over the greatest affliction in the state of a steady, wise mind.
Christianity
“And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock.”
— Matthew 7:25 (KJV) [4]
Jesus compares the person who hears and practises his teaching to a house on rock that survives the flood.
Both use the flood as the picture of overwhelming trouble and say it breaks harmlessly on a well-founded mind.
Islam
“Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity.”
— Qur’an 2:286 (Saheeh International) [5]
The closing verses of al-Baqarah assure believers that no burden laid on them exceeds what they are able to bear.
The Kural says even a flood of sorrow can be undone by the wise mind; the Qur’an assures that no trial is truly beyond a person’s capacity.
Judaism
“When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee, And through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee; When thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned, Neither shall the flame kindle upon thee.”
— Isaiah 43:2 (JPS 1917) [6]
Isaiah promises Israel that floods and fire will not overwhelm it, because God goes with it through every ordeal.
Isaiah and the Kural share the image of a flood that cannot drown the one who meets it rightly.
Buddhism
“By effort and heedfulness, discipline and self-mastery, let the wise one make for himself an island which no flood can overwhelm.”
— Dhammapada 25 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha urges the wise to build an inner island of discipline and mindfulness that no rising flood of passion or sorrow can cover.
Both texts picture trouble as a flood and the wise person’s trained mind as the thing that survives it.
Jainism
“Is there a shelter, a refuge, a firm ground for the beings carried away by the great flood of water? do you know the island, O Gautama?” … “The flood is old age and death, which carry away living beings; Law is the island, the firm ground, the refuge, the most excellent shelter.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.65 and 23.68 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the dialogue of Kesi and Gautama, the flood that sweeps beings away is answered by the Law (dharma), an island that the flood cannot cover.
The Kural pictures sorrow as a flood that the wise mind overcomes; the Jain text uses the same flood image and places firm ground against it, though that ground is the Law rather than the mind's own resolve.
Sikhism
“Remembering God, one is not touched by fear. Remembering God, one does not suffer sorrow.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 262, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani Sahib), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan lists the fruits of simran, among them freedom from fear and from sorrow.
The Kural says a flood of sorrow vanishes once the wise turn a steady mind on it; Guru Arjan says sorrow cannot afflict a mind turned steadily towards God.
Greek Mythology
When Zeus drowned the world in a great flood, only the pious Deucalion and Pyrrha survived; left alone on a devastated earth, they puzzled out the oracle’s riddle to throw ‘the bones of their mother’ behind them, understood it meant the stones of Mother Earth, and so repeopled the world.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.313-415 [10]
Ovid’s survivors of the flood overcome total catastrophe by keeping their heads and thinking their way to a solution.
As in the Kural, a literal flood of disaster is finally undone by a clear, thoughtful mind.
Philosophy
“Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.49 (tr. George Long) [11]
Marcus Aurelius tells himself to meet misfortune like a headland that absorbs wave after wave without moving.
The Kural’s wise mind that dissolves a flood of sorrow is the same as Marcus’s promontory that calms the raging water.

Real Life Example

Pombal directs the relief and rebuilding of Lisbon after the 1755 earthquake
1755-1758 · Lisbon, Portugal
On the morning of 1 November 1755 a series of earthquakes, followed by a tsunami and fires lasting about six days, devastated Lisbon and killed an estimated 60,000 people in the city. The king's chief minister, Sebastiao de Carvalho, later Marquis of Pombal, organised the aid and reconstruction effort. Rebuilding of the downtown began in 1756 on a new orthogonal street plan drawn up by Manuel da Maia and carried out by the military engineers Eugenio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel, with earthquake-resistant and fire-resistant buildings.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Instead of being overwhelmed by one of the worst disasters in European history, the government turned to orderly relief and a rational plan, and the rebuilt Baixa district still stands as a model of planned recovery.
The kural says even a flood-like sorrow perishes once a wise person turns a steady mind upon it, and Lisbon's catastrophe was met by deliberate planning that turned ruin into a new city.
