Kural 1241

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

நினைத்தொன்று சொல்லாயோ நெஞ்சே எனைத்தொன்றும் எவ்வநோய் தீர்க்கும் மருந்து.
Ninaiththondru sollaayo nenje enaiththondrum evvanoai theerkkum marundhu.

Meaning

My heart, won't you think hard and tell me some medicine, any kind at all, that can cure this sickness of sorrow?

Elementary

Imagine you miss someone so much that it feels like a tummy ache that no medicine can fix. So you talk to yourself: “Come on, heart, you are clever, think of something that will make me feel better!”

University

Chapter 125, neñcoṭu kiḷattal (‘speaking with one’s heart’), belongs to the kaṟpiyal (wedded-love) section: the wife, alone in separation (pirivu), turns her own neñcu (heart/mind) into a confidant and argues with it, a device Pope renders ‘Soliloquy’. The key compound evvanōy joins evvam (‘distress, sorrow’) with nōy (‘disease’), and enaittonṟum (‘whatever it may be, of any kind’) stresses that she will accept any remedy at all; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses the sickness as grown out of love (காதலால் வளர்ந்த), and Kalaignar as kātal nōy ‘incurable by any medicine’. The commentary tradition reads the opening ninaittu onṟu collāyō as a plea to the heart, the one faculty still ‘with’ her, to think on her behalf. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the chapter title is printed as one word (நெஞ்சொடுகிளத்தல்) on ytamizh and as நெஞ்சோடு கிளத்தல் (long ō) on thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I can find no means to drive away this grief which is drying up my senses.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.8 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Arjuna, overwhelmed by sorrow, confesses that nothing he can think of will remove the grief that is withering him, and turns to Krishna for a remedy.
The Kural’s heroine likewise admits her grief is beyond ordinary cures and begs her own heart to find the medicine.
Christianity
“Why is my pain perpetual, and my wound incurable, which refuseth to be healed?”
— Jeremiah 15:18 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah cries out that his inner wound will not heal no matter what is tried.
Like the Kural’s ‘sickness no medicine cures’, Jeremiah’s pain is described as incurable, prompting a desperate question.
Islam
“And when I am ill, it is He who cures me”
— Qur’an 26:80 (Saheeh International) [5]
Abraham names God as the one true healer of every illness.
Where the Kural’s heroine searches her own heart for a cure, the Qur’an points the sufferer to God as the source of healing.
Judaism
“Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there? Why then is not the health of the daughter of my people recovered?”
— Jeremiah 8:22 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Jeremiah asks, in grief, why no remedy or healer seems able to cure his people’s wound.
The Kural’s plea for ‘any medicine at all’ echoes this famous lament asking whether any balm exists for an aching heart.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha diagnoses grief as springing from attachment to the dear, and names freedom from attachment as its only real cure.
The Kural asks for a medicine for love-grief; the Dhammapada answers that the cure lies in loosening the endearment itself.
Jainism
“The mind of those who always live in unfrequented lodgings, who eat low food, and who subdue their senses, will not be attacked by the foe, Love, who is vanquished as disease is by medicine.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira’s teaching treats Love as an enemy-sickness and prescribes the discipline of the senses as its medicine.
The Kural asks what medicine can cure love-sickness; the Uttaradhyayana names one, though it is the monk’s cure of renunciation, not the lover’s cure of reunion.
Sikhism
“O mind, there is only the One medicine, mantra and healing herb - center your consciousness firmly on the One Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 156, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak speaks directly to his own mind and tells it that there is a single remedy for its sickness: steady remembrance of the One Lord.
The Kural's wife begs her heart to think of some medicine, any medicine, for a grief nothing else can cure; Guru Nanak, also addressing the mind, names the one medicine that heals the longing soul.
Greek Mythology
The poet Theocritus tells of the Cyclops Polyphemus, hopelessly in love with the sea-nymph Galatea, and opens by saying there is no other remedy for love, neither ointment nor salve, except the Muses; Polyphemus eased his love by sitting on a rock and singing to her.
— Theocritus, Idyll 11 (‘The Cyclops’), lines 1-18 (paraphrase) [10]
The Idyll treats love as an illness with almost no cure, and finds relief only in song, a kind of talking to oneself.
The Kural’s heroine likewise looks for a medicine for love and, finding none, pours her heart out in words to herself.
Philosophy
Cicero, discussing the passions, sets out the ‘cure’ for love: show the lover how trivial the object of desire is, turn his mind to other cares and interests, and sometimes treat it like other illnesses by change of place.
— Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 4.74-75 (paraphrase) [11]
Cicero treats erotic love as a disturbance of the mind that philosophy can treat like a disease.
Where the Kural’s heroine begs for any medicine for love-sickness, Cicero offers a philosopher’s prescription.

Real Life Example

Ancient Egyptian love songs on lovesickness no physician can cure
13th-12th centuries BCE · Deir el-Medina, Egypt
Love songs from Egypt's Ramesside period, linked with the craftsmen's village of Deir el-Medina, survive on papyri including Papyrus Chester Beatty I, now in Dublin. In one stanza the young man says that his sickness cannot be relieved by the remedies of physicians and that only his beloved's presence can cure him; the songs also describe the beloved herself as a medicine whose sight brings health.
— Sources: [12] [13]
These are among the oldest surviving love poems in the world, and they already treat love-longing as an illness beyond ordinary medicine.
The kural's wife begs her heart to name any medicine at all for a sickness nothing else can cure, and the Egyptian lover, more than three thousand years ago, found that no physician's remedy could touch his.
