Kural 1052

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

இன்பம் ஒருவற்கு இரத்தல் இரந்தவை துன்பம் உறாஅ வரின்.
Inbam oruvarku iraththal irandhavai thunbam uraaa varin.

Meaning

Even asking for help can be a pleasure, if what you ask for comes to you without pain for either person.

Elementary

If you ask a friend to share a crayon and they hand it over with a smile, asking feels nice, not embarrassing. Asking only hurts when the other person sighs or makes you feel small.

University

Valluvar makes the surprising claim that iraththal (இரத்தல், 'begging') can itself be inbam (இன்பம், 'pleasure'), on one condition: that what is begged comes thunbam uraaa (துன்பம் உறாஅ, 'without suffering'), where the lengthened uraaa is an alapedai (metrical lengthening). Commentators differ on whose suffering is meant: Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as the asker's (the gift comes without hardship), Solomon Pappaiah as the giver's reluctance, and Kalaignar as both minds, giver's and receiver's, being free of pain. The commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar stresses that the pleasure depends on the giver's goodwill (Puliyur Kesikan: ஈவாரது நல்லுணர்வால்). Pope: 'Even to ask an alms may pleasure give, / If what you ask without annoyance you receive.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhagavad Gita 17.21 classes as rajasic (passionate) the charity given grudgingly, or with an eye to some return, setting it below the sattvic gift given freely out of duty.
— Bhagavad Gita 17.21 (paraphrase; cf. Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna grades gifts by the giver's state of mind, and a grudging gift falls to the lower, passionate grade.
The Kural's condition that the gift come 'without pain' matches the Gita's preference for gifts given without grudging.
Christianity
“Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks the Corinthians to give willingly and without reluctance, because a cheerful gift is what pleases God.
A cheerful gift is exactly what makes asking a pleasure in the Kural.
Islam
“And as for the petitioner, do not repel [him].”
— Qur'an 93:10 (Sahih International) [5]
God tells the Prophet not to drive away or rebuff someone who comes asking.
Not being repelled is what keeps asking free of pain, which is the Kural's condition for it being a pleasure.
Judaism
Maimonides ranks charity in eight ascending degrees, and the higher degrees are those that spare the poor person shame, for example when giver and receiver do not know each other, or when the poor person is helped to become self-supporting.
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:7-14 (paraphrase; tr. Eliyahu Touger available on Sefaria) [6]
Maimonides judges a gift partly by how much it spares the recipient embarrassment.
His concern that receiving should not hurt the poor matches the Kural's condition that the gift come without pain.
Buddhism
“As a bee gathers honey from the flower without injuring its color or fragrance, even so the sage goes on his alms-round in the village.”
— Dhammapada 49 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares the monk on his alms round to a bee that takes nectar without harming the flower.
Asking that harms no one is exactly the painless begging the Kural calls a pleasure.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra opens (ch. 1, 'Drumapushpika') with the image of the bee that drinks from flowers without harming them, and teaches that monks should take alms from householders in the same way, accepting only what is given freely and causing no distress.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra, ch. 1 (paraphrase) [8]
The image underlies the Jain term madhukari (bee-like) for the mendicant's alms round, in which the monk takes a little from many houses so that no family is burdened.
The Jain rule that alms must cost the giver no hardship is the Kural's 'without pain' made into monastic discipline.
Sikhism
“When I am hungry, I ask for food; when I am full, I am totally at peace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1214, Raag Sarang, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan speaks as a child to its parent: asking for food is natural and carries no shame, and being fed brings complete contentment.
The Kural says asking can itself be a pleasure if what is asked for comes without pain to either side; Guru Arjan pictures exactly such easy asking between a child and a loving parent.
Greek Mythology
“Why should you want to see this stranger turned out of the house? Heaven forbid; take' something and give it him yourself; I do not grudge it; I bid you take it.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Telemachus tells Antinous to give the beggar something from the house's stores, saying he does not grudge it.
A gift offered without grudging is what makes asking pleasant, which is the Kural's point.
Philosophy
“He was begging of a miserly man who was slow to respond; so he said, ‘My friend, it's for food that I'm asking, not for funeral expenses.’”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.56 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
Diogenes mocks a miser whose slow, pained giving made a small request feel like a great burden.
The joke assumes the Kural's point: asking turns sour when the gift comes with pain.

Real Life Example

Beethoven's patrons grant him a lifelong annuity to stay in Vienna
1 March 1809 · Vienna, Austria
When Beethoven was offered the post of music director at the court of Jerome Bonaparte in Kassel, he negotiated with three Viennese patrons, Archduke Rudolph, Prince Lobkowitz and Prince Kinsky, for support that would allow him to remain in Vienna. Their contract, signed on 1 March 1809, guaranteed him 4,000 florins a year, requiring only that he live in Vienna and compose. Archduke Rudolph, Beethoven's pupil, remained his most dependable patron, although the princes' payments were later disrupted by war, death and the devaluation of 1811.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Beethoven's request was met readily by admirers who considered it an honour to keep him in their city, and for years afterward he was provided for.
The kural says that asking itself can be a pleasure when what is asked comes without pain to either side, and Beethoven's patrons gave willingly to keep him as a composer in Vienna.
