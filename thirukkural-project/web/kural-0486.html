Kural 486

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

ஊக்க முடையான் ஒடுக்கம் பொருதகர் தாக்கற்குப் பேருந் தகைத்து.
Ookka Mutaiyaan Otukkam Porudhakar Thaakkarkup Perun Thakaiththu.

Meaning

When a strong, active person holds back for a while, it is like a fighting ram stepping back so it can charge harder.

Elementary

When a ram wants to butt, it first walks backward a few steps, then rushes forward with great force. A strong person who waits quietly for a while is not giving up. They are getting ready to charge.

University

Ookkam means energy or zeal, and otukkam means drawing in, withdrawal or restraint. Porudhakar is a fighting ram (poru, ‘fight’, + thakar, ‘ram’), and thaakkal means butting or striking. Perum thakaiththu means ‘is of the nature of drawing back’, which Parimelazhagar reads as backing up to strike harder. The image reassures anyone who might misread the patient man’s restraint as weakness: his pause is how he gathers force. The same logic appears in the feigned retreats of military history and in Sun Tzu’s ‘coyness of a maiden’ followed by the rush of a hare. The printed ‘perun thakaiththu’ shows the nasal sandhi of perum before th-.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Jarasandha besieged Mathura again and again, Krishna and Balarama finally withdrew. They moved the Yadavas to the island fortress of Dwaraka, so that Krishna came to be called Ranchhod, ‘the one who left the battlefield’. From that secure base Krishna later arranged Jarasandha’s death through Bhima.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa, Canto 10, chapters 50–52 and 72 [3]
The withdrawal looked like flight but was a strategic step back that made a decisive later blow possible.
This is the ram stepping back before the charge: an energetic hero’s retreat that sets up the strike.
Christianity
When the Pharisees plotted to destroy Jesus, Matthew says he knew of it and withdrew from there. Great crowds followed him and he healed them, and he told them not to make him known, since his hour had not yet come.
— Matthew 12:14–16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus repeatedly avoided confrontation that would have come too early, and kept his mission going until the appointed time.
His withdrawal is restraint in the service of a later and greater purpose, like the ram’s step back.
Islam
Facing persecution in Mecca, the Prophet and his followers emigrated to Medina in 622 CE (the Hijra). There they built a community and strength. Eight years later they returned to Mecca in triumph.
— Qur’an 9:40; sira accounts of the Hijra [5]
The Hijra was a withdrawal that became the base for later success. Islam dates its calendar from it.
Like the ram’s backward step, the retreat to Medina gathered the force for the return.
Judaism
At Ai, Joshua set an ambush behind the city and led the main army in a feigned flight. When the men of Ai left their walls to pursue, the ambush took the city, and Israel turned back and struck.
— Joshua 8:1–22 [4]
The pretended retreat drew the enemy out of his strong position so that Israel’s counter-attack would be decisive.
This is the fighting ram’s step back turned into a battle plan.
Buddhism
In the Kumma Sutta, a tortoise draws its limbs and head into its shell when a jackal comes near. The jackal waits for it to put something out, but the tortoise keeps still until the jackal gives up and leaves. The Buddha tells monks to guard their senses in the same way.
— Saṃyutta Nikāya 35.199 (Kumma Sutta), tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [6]
Drawing in at the dangerous moment protects one from a waiting predator.
Otukkam, drawing in, is shown as strength rather than weakness, though here it protects and does not strike.
Jainism
When Bahubali, after defeating his elder brother Bharata in the contests that settled their war, raised his fist for a final blow, he stopped, judged that striking his brother would be wrong, and turned the same raised hand on himself, pulling out his hair in the ascetic's renunciation. Jain tradition then shows him standing in unmoving meditation until he gained omniscience.
— Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra of Hemacandra, Book 1, Bharata and Bahubali (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; paraphrase) [7]
The story turns a blow held back into a greater victory: the energy withdrawn from striking an outward foe is redirected against the passions within.
The kural's ram draws back so that it can strike harder; Bahubali's drawn-back fist also becomes a stronger blow, aimed at his own karma rather than at an enemy.
Sikhism
“The sinner, like the deer hunter, bows down twice as much.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa Ki Vaar Guru Nanak notes that a hunter stalking deer bends low only to strike, so outward bowing is no proof of humility.
The Kural compares the energetic man's holding back to a ram stepping back before it charges; Guru Nanak sees the same pattern in the hunter's crouch before the kill.
Greek Mythology
After ten years of fighting, the Greeks burned their camp and sailed away to hide behind the island of Tenedos, leaving the wooden horse on the shore. When the Trojans had brought it inside, the fleet came back by night and the men in the horse opened the gates.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.492–520 (Demodocus’ song); fuller in Virgil, Aeneid II [9]
The apparent withdrawal was the preparation for the blow that ended the war.
The ram steps back and then charges. The Greeks retreat and then take Troy.
Philosophy
“At first, then, exhibit the coyness of a maiden, until the enemy gives you an opening; afterwards emulate the rapidity of a running hare, and it will be too late for the enemy to oppose you.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War XI.68 (tr. Lionel Giles) [10]
Sun Tzu advises apparent passivity until the enemy exposes himself, then sudden, overwhelming speed.
Restraint first and then a rush is the ram’s backward step and forward charge.

Real Life Example

Napoleon gives up the Pratzen Heights before striking at Austerlitz
2 December 1805 · Near Brno, Moravia, Czech Republic
Before the Battle of Austerlitz, Napoleon withdrew his troops from the Pratzen Heights and kept his right flank weak, and the Russian and Austrian allies occupied the heights and launched their main attack against that flank. As the morning mist cleared, Napoleon saw that the allies had moved troops away from the heights and weakened their centre, and he sent Marshal Soult's corps up the slope to take it, cutting the allied army in two. His 68,000 troops defeated almost 90,000 Russians and Austrians, and Austria made peace soon afterwards.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Giving up the high ground at first helped draw the allies into the move that exposed them to Napoleon's decisive attack.
The kural compares a strong man's holding back to a fighting ram stepping back to strike harder, and Napoleon's withdrawal from the heights was followed by the blow that won the battle.
