Kural 213

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

புத்தே ளுலகத்தும் ஈண்டும் பெறலரிதே ஒப்புரவின் நல்ல பிற.
Puththe lulagaththum eendum peralaridhe Oppuravin nalla pira.

Meaning

Neither in the world of the gods nor here on earth is it easy to find anything as good as helping others.

Elementary

If you searched every place — here and even up in heaven — for the very best thing a person can do, you would find nothing better than helping others. It is the top prize of all good deeds.

University

Parimelazhagar gives an ingenious reason for why oppuravu is rare even in heaven: in the world of the gods all are equal, so there are neither givers nor receivers and the virtue cannot be practised; on earth nothing else benefits everyone so equally. He also records a variant reading, peRaRkaridhu, which some commentators took to mean ‘the means to obtain it is rare.’ With 211 and 212, he notes, this couplet completes the chapter's praise of oppuravu before Valluvar turns to its practice. The comparison of heaven and earth is a recurring Tamil device (cf. Kural 23) for asserting a value's absolute primacy, here placing social beneficence above ritual or ascetic merit.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who are beyond the dualities that arise from doubts, whose minds are engaged within, who are always busy working for the welfare of all living beings and who are free from all sins achieve liberation in the Supreme.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.25 [4]
Krishna names working for the good of all beings (sarva-bhuta-hite ratah) as a mark of those who reach the highest spiritual goal.
Like the Kural, it ranks devotion to the welfare of all among the highest goods, reaching beyond this world.
Christianity
“And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:13 (KJV) [5]
Paul ranks love (charity) above even faith and hope as the greatest virtue.
Same move of ranking love-in-action above all other goods, as the Kural ranks oppuravu.
Islam
“Never will you attain the good [reward] until you spend [in the way of Allah] from that which you love. And whatever you spend - indeed, Allah is Knowing of it.”
— Qur'an 3:92 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an says true righteousness (al-birr) is not reached until one gives away what one values.
Makes generous giving the gateway to the highest good, as the Kural does.
Judaism
“the world stands upon three things: the Torah, the Temple service, and the practice of acts of piety.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:2 (Sefaria, trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Simeon the Just names gemilut chasadim — acts of loving-kindness — as one of the three pillars holding up the world.
Like the Kural, it treats kindness to others as foundational to the world, not optional.
Buddhism
“Monks, if beings knew, as I know, the results of giving & sharing, they would not eat without having given, nor would the stain of selfishness overcome their minds.”
— Itivuttaka 26 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha says that if people understood how great the fruit of sharing is, they would never eat without sharing first, even their last bite.
Shares the Kural's claim that the good of sharing is unmatched, in this world and after.
Jainism
“Those who are ten miles distant can hear the sound of the wide drum beaten with the stick; those a yojana distant, can hear the hoarse thunder; but all who live in the three worlds piled up will hear the report that some of the excellent have given alms.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 10 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Nālaṭiyār ends its chapter on liberality by saying the fame of generous giving reaches the whole three-tiered Jain universe, further than any drum or thunder.
Valluvar says nothing in the world of the gods or on earth equals beneficence; the Nālaṭiyār likewise gives charity a renown that spans all three worlds.
Sikhism
“In the midst of this world, do seva, and you shall be given a place of honor in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sri Raag, Guru Nanak teaches that service done here in the world is what earns honour in the divine Court.
The Kural says no good in the world of the gods or on earth equals helping others; Guru Nanak likewise places worldly seva at the centre of what is honoured both here and hereafter.
Greek Mythology
“Stranger, though a still poorer man should come here, it would not be right for me to insult him, for all strangers and beggars are from Jove.”
— Homer, Odyssey 14 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Eumaeus the swineherd explains that care for strangers and beggars is sacred because Zeus himself protects them.
Places helping the needy under divine sanction, echoing the Kural's heaven-and-earth esteem for oppuravu.
Philosophy
“we are not born for ourselves alone, but our country claims a share of our being, and our friends a share; and since, as the Stoics hold, everything that the earth produces is created for man's use; and as men, too, are born for the sake of men, that they may be able mutually to help one another”
— Cicero, De Officiis I.22 (trans. Walter Miller) [12]
Cicero, citing Plato and the Stoics, argues that humans exist partly for one another and should strengthen society through exchanging kindnesses.
Articulates the same civic-reciprocity ideal the Kural calls oppuravu and places at the summit of virtue.

Real Life Example

Edward Jenner's free vaccinations in the Temple of Vaccinia
From 1796 · Berkeley, Gloucestershire, England, United Kingdom
After his first vaccination experiment against smallpox on 14 May 1796, the country doctor Edward Jenner used a small rustic hut in the garden of his home at Berkeley, which he named the Temple of Vaccinia, to vaccinate the poor of the district free of charge. He did not patent his discovery, and he shared cowpox material and his findings widely with other doctors. The Wellcome Collection describes the hut as the world's first free vaccination clinic, and vaccination eventually led to the global eradication of smallpox, declared in 1980.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Jenner's discovery and his free clinic began a chain of benefit that reached every part of the world.
The kural says no good on earth or in heaven equals helping others as the world rightly expects, and few acts of help have spread as widely as vaccination freely given.
