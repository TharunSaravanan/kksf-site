Kural 66

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

குழல்இனிது யாழ்இனிது என்பதம் மக்கள் மழலைச்சொல் கேளா தவர்.
Kuzhalinidhu Yaazhinidhu Enpadham Makkal, Mazhalaichchol Kelaadhavar.

Meaning

‘The flute is sweet, the harp is sweet,’ say only those who have never heard the baby talk of their own children.

Elementary

Some people say the most beautiful sound in the world is music. This kural teases them a little: they only think that because they have never sat and listened to a toddler talking nonsense. If they had, they would know which sound really wins.

University

The couplet is the Kural's most famous piece of wit, and its structure is a rhetorical trap. Valluvar never asserts that a child's babble is sweeter than music; he simply reports who it is that ranks music first — namely, those who have not heard மழலைச்சொல் (maḻalai-c-col), the half-formed lisping speech of a small child. Parimēlaḻakar identifies the remainder as a குறிப்பெச்சம், a deliberately unspoken implication that the reader is left to supply, and Maṇakkuṭavar makes it blunt: "those who have heard do not say it." The two instruments named are not decorative: the குழல் (kuḻal, the transverse flute) and the யாழ் (yāḻ, the ancient Tamil harp-lute) were the paired standards of vocal-and-instrumental excellence in the Caṅkam musical system, so Valluvar is measuring the child's voice against the recognised summit of the art. The word maḻalai has since passed into ordinary Tamil as an affectionate term for toddler-speech largely on the strength of this verse.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kṛṣṇa's flute is treated across Vaiṣṇava poetry as the most irresistible sound in creation, drawing cows, rivers and gopīs out of themselves; yet in the same literature Yaśodā's happiness comes not from the flute at all but from her child's broken half-words and the noise of his anklets.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.8–10.21 (the flute of Kṛṣṇa and Yaśodā's vātsalya) [5]
The devotional tradition holds both claims at once — the flute as supreme music, and the child's voice as what a mother actually treasures.
The two sounds the Kural sets against each other, flute and toddler-speech, are the same two that Hindu devotion places side by side in the Kṛṣṇa cycle.
Christianity
“Yea; have ye never read, Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings thou hast perfected praise?”
— Matthew 21:16 (King James Version) [6]
The chief priests are indignant at children shouting in the temple; Jesus answers by citing scripture to the effect that infant voices are the medium through which praise reaches its completed form.
Both texts rank the untrained sound of small children above the accomplished performances of adults.
Islam
“[Jesus] said, 'Indeed, I am the servant of Allah. He has given me the Scripture and made me a prophet.'”
— Qur'an 19:30, Sūrat Maryam (Sahih International) [7]
Mary, challenged over her newborn, points to the infant; and the child in the cradle speaks — the Qur'an's most striking instance of a baby's voice silencing a crowd of adults.
It shares the Kural's premise that an infant's speech carries a weight no adult performance can match, though for the Qur'an the weight is miraculous rather than affectionate.
Judaism
“Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength because of thine enemies, that thou mightest still the enemy and the avenger.”
— Psalm 8:2 (King James Version) [6]
The psalm places the voices of the very youngest at the centre of a poem about cosmic majesty, making infant sound a source of strength rather than mere noise.
Like the Kural, it deliberately elevates the least trained human voice above everything more impressive.
Buddhism
When the Buddha returned to Kapilavatthu, Yasodharā sent their young son Rāhula to him saying that he should ask his father for his inheritance; the boy followed the Buddha through the city repeating the request, and the Buddha's answer was to have him ordained, giving him the Dhamma instead.
— Vinaya, Mahāvagga I.54 (the ordination of Rāhula) [8]
The scene is one of very few in the early canon that lingers on a small child's persistent, artless speech to a parent.
It offers the Kural's situation — a father hearing his small son's voice — but the tradition treats the moment as an occasion for renunciation rather than for delight.
Jainism
When Trisalâ believed her unborn child had died, ‘in the palace of king Siddhârtha the music of drums and stringed instruments, the clapping of hands, the dramatical performances, and the amusements of the people ceased, and mournful dejection reigned there.’
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahâvîra §92 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
Once the child seemed lost, the royal musicians and players fell silent, because without the child their music had nothing to celebrate.
Valluvar ranks a child's prattle above flute and lute, and here music loses all its sweetness when the child seems gone, which places the child above the instruments.
Sikhism
“As the child places so much faith in his father, I speak as it pleases the Guru to have me speak.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 396, Raag Asa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a hymn celebrating the birth of his son, Guru Arjan likens his own speech before the Guru to a small child talking with complete trust to its father.
Both texts linger on the voice of a child before a loving parent, though only the Kural ranks that voice above the finest music.
Greek Mythology
The satyr Marsyas, having taken up the aulos (the reed pipe), challenged Apollo and his lyre to decide which instrument was sweeter; Apollo won and flayed him alive for the presumption.
— Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 1.4.2 (Apollo and Marsyas) [11]
Greek myth stages the question of pipe versus lyre as a literal contest, and treats the answer as so serious that the loser is destroyed for it.
Valluvar names the same two categories of instrument, pipe and stringed lute, and then dismisses the whole contest — neither wins against a toddler talking.
Philosophy
Lucretius, describing how early humans first softened, writes that it was the coming of children and the winning ways of their affection that broke down the fierce, proud temper of primitive men and made settled life possible.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 5.1014–1018 [12]
For the Epicurean poet, the charm of small children is a causal force in human history, not a sentimental aside — it is what taught the species gentleness.
It gives a naturalistic account of why the Kural's claim is true: children's artless voices and gestures really do disarm adults in a way art cannot.

Real Life Example

Charles Darwin records his infant son's first expressions and sounds
1839 to 1877 · London, England, United Kingdom
From the birth of his first child, William Erasmus, on 27 December 1839, Charles Darwin kept a notebook recording the baby's movements, cries, smiles and responses to sounds, and he continued the observations for several years. The notes later fed into his 1872 book on the expression of emotions. In 1877 he drew on them for the article A biographical sketch of an infant, published in the journal Mind.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The great naturalist turned the same patient attention he gave to finches and barnacles on his own baby, and the Darwin Correspondence Project notes the obvious pride and joy that shows through the notes.
The kural says those who rank the flute and lute sweetest have never heard their own child's babble, and Darwin, among the most observant minds of his age, found his infant's first sounds worth recording for decades.
