Kural 85

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

வித்தும் இடல்வேண்டும் கொல்லோ விருந்தோம்பி மிச்சில் மிசைவான் புலம்.
Viththum idal vendum kollo virundhombi, Michchil misaivaan pulam.

Meaning

Does a person who feeds guests first and eats only what is left even need to sow seeds in their field?

Elementary

Think of someone who always lets visitors eat first and takes the leftovers for himself. This kural asks, with a smile: does his field even need seeds? It's saying his land will be so full of grain that it seems to grow all by itself. Sharing plants a crop you never had to sow.

University

The verse is a rhetorical question — கொல்லோ is a Tamil particle of wondering interrogation — and its force is hyperbolic rather than agronomic. மிச்சில் ('the remainder, the leftover') is the pivot: the householder who eats the remnant after his guests have eaten is participating in a pan-Indian sacrificial logic in which the leftover of an offering, far from being inferior, is the sanctified portion. Valluvar's innovation is to transpose that logic from the ritual fire to the ordinary dinner and from the priest to the guest. Manakkudavar and later commentators read the question as asserting that such a person's field yields without sowing; modern readers often take it as saying that his generosity is itself the sowing. The agricultural metaphor for giving — seed, field, harvest — is one of the most widely distributed in world religious literature, appearing independently in Vedic, Buddhist, Qur'anic and Pauline texts, and the Kural's compressed version is among the most elegant.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The devotees of the Lord are released from all kinds of sins because they eat food which is offered first for sacrifice. Others, who prepare food for personal sense enjoyment, verily eat only sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.13 (A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [4]
Krishna sets out a strict binary: food consumed as the remnant of an offering purifies, while food cooked and eaten for oneself alone is itself a transgression.
The Kural's householder who eats only the மிச்சில், the leftover after the guests, is precisely the Gita's eater of the sacrificial remnant.
Christianity
“But this I say, He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; and he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:6 (King James Version) [5]
Paul makes the farming metaphor explicit in a passage about a charitable collection, treating gifts to others as literal seed whose yield is proportional to what was scattered.
Same metaphor, same claim: generosity is a form of sowing, and the harvest answers to it.
Islam
“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is like a seed [of grain] which grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains. And Allah multiplies [His reward] for whom He wills.”
— Qur'an 2:261 (Sahih International) [6]
The parable puts a precise multiplier on generosity — seven hundredfold — using the same grain imagery as the Kural to say the return vastly exceeds the outlay.
Both texts convert the act of giving food into an image of a field yielding far beyond what was put into it.
Judaism
“Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days.”
— Ecclesiastes 11:1 (King James Version) [5]
Qohelet's famously enigmatic counsel is read in most Jewish commentary as advocating generosity that appears wasteful at the moment of giving but returns to the giver in time.
Both verses claim that food given away is not lost but constitutes an investment whose return is unaccountably large.
Buddhism
In the Khetta Sutta the Buddha compares giving to planting: seed sown in a field endowed with good qualities yields abundantly, and in just the same way a gift given to recipients endowed with good qualities yields great fruit.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 8.34, Khetta Sutta (The Field) [7]
The sutta is built entirely on agricultural imagery, treating the worthy recipient as the fertile soil into which the donor's seed is cast.
Shares the Kural's exact figure of speech — hospitality as an act of sowing whose harvest returns to the household.
Jainism
“The husbandmen throw the corn on high ground and on low ground, hoping (for a return). For the like motive give unto me; I may be the field which may produce merit (as the return for your benevolence).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Speaking for the begging monk Harikeśa, the yaksha describes giving to a worthy guest as sowing seed in a field that yields merit. A few verses earlier he asks that the mendicant receive what is left over.
Valluvar asks whether a man who feeds his guests first even needs to sow his field, and the Jain verse uses the same farming image: the guest is the field and the gift is the seed.
Sikhism
“Make good deeds the soil, and let the Word of the Shabad be the seed; irrigate it continually with the water of Truth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 24, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak turns farming into a picture of the moral life, in which good conduct is the field that yields the true harvest.
Like Valluvar's field that needs no sowing for the man who feeds guests first, it treats right action itself as the sowing that brings the crop.
Greek Mythology
Demeter, disguised as an old woman and grieving for Persephone, is taken in and cared for by the household of King Celeus at Eleusis; in return the goddess of grain teaches Celeus's son Triptolemus the art of agriculture and her sacred rites, and through him the growing of crops spreads to the rest of Greece.
— Homeric Hymn 2, To Demeter (lines 96–495) [10]
The Homeric Hymn makes the gift of cultivated grain itself the repayment for one household's kindness to a stranger at the well.
Nowhere else is the Kural's linkage of hospitality with the fertility of fields so literal: a family fed a guest, and received the knowledge of how to sow.
Philosophy
Seneca's treatise on benefits argues that a benefit is not a transaction to be balanced in a ledger; the giver should give as the earth gives and count the good already done as the whole of the return, expecting nothing and yet finding that benefits circulate back.
— Seneca, De Beneficiis I.1–2 [11]
Seneca is deliberately attacking the accountant's view of generosity, insisting that the person who gives with an eye on the repayment has not really given.
Touches the Kural's theme of generosity that yields without being sown, but from the opposite direction — denying rather than promising a harvest.

Real Life Example

The Choctaw Nation sends famine relief to Ireland
1847 · Choctaw Nation, Indian Territory (now Oklahoma), United States
In 1847, less than two decades after their forced removal from their homelands on the Trail of Tears, Choctaw people gathered and raised 170 dollars for the relief of the Irish famine. The money was sent through relief committees in the United States to help buy food for the starving in Ireland. The gift is still commemorated in both nations, including by the Kindred Spirits sculpture in County Cork and a Choctaw-Ireland scholarship.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A people who had themselves suffered dispossession gave from their own scarcity to strangers an ocean away, and the gift is still remembered as a bond between the two nations.
The kural praises the one who feeds others first and lives on what remains, and the Choctaw gave to the hungry before attending to their own hardships.
