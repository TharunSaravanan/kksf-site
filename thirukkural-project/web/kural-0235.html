Kural 235

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

நத்தம்போல் கேடும் உளதாகும் சாக்காடும் வித்தகர்க் கல்லால் அரிது.
Naththampol kedum uladhaakum saakkaadum Viththakarkku allaal aridhu.

Meaning

A loss that is really a gain, and a death that lets you live on in people's memory, are hard for anyone except the truly wise to achieve.

Elementary

A candle gets smaller as it burns, but it lights up the whole room. Some very wise people are like that. When they give away their time, money, or even their life for others, they seem to lose, but their goodness grows and people remember them forever.

University

This is the chapter’s most paradoxical and textually difficult couplet. நத்தம் is glossed by Parimelazhagan as ஆக்கம் (increase), giving ‘a ruin that is like gain’: the loss suffered by the physical body (poverty through giving, hardship) that enlarges the ‘body of fame’ (புகழுடம்பு). சாக்காடும் உளதாகும், ‘death that becomes existence’, refers to dying in such a way that one’s renown is fixed for ever. Only the வித்தகர் (the skilled, the wise) manage this conversion of loss into gain. Commentators including Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah explain it through the Tamil idea of two bodies, the perishing body of flesh and the enduring body of renown. Cross-culturally it matches the logic of heroic or sacrificial death: Achilles’ choice at Troy, Diotima’s account of those who die ‘for the sake of leaving behind them a name’, and the grain of wheat that must die to bear fruit.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, either you will be killed on the battlefield and attain the heavenly planets, or you will conquer and enjoy the earthly kingdom.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.37 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that a righteous warrior cannot lose: death in duty brings heaven, and victory brings the kingdom.
Like the Kural, it treats a rightly met death not as loss but as a form of gain.
Christianity
“Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit.”
— John 12:24 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses the image of a seed that must die in the soil in order to produce a harvest, pointing to his own death.
The same paradox as the Kural: an apparent loss or death that is actually increase.
Islam
“And never think of those who have been killed in the cause of Allāh as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision,”
— Qur’an 3:169 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an declares that those who die in God’s cause are not truly dead but living and provided for.
Both describe a death that is actually a continued life, ‘death that becomes existence’.
Judaism
“A good name is better than precious ointment; and the day of death than the day of one’s birth.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:1 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
Qoheleth pairs the value of a good name with the claim that the day of death surpasses the day of birth, since only at death is a life’s name complete.
Death that seals a good name is better than a beginning: close to the Kural’s death that establishes lasting renown.
Buddhism
In the Sasa Jataka the Bodhisatta, born as a hare with nothing else to give, leaps into a fire to offer his own body as food to a brahmin, who is Sakka in disguise. The fire turns icy and does not burn him, and Sakka marks the sign of a hare on the moon so that the hare’s virtue will be known throughout the aeon.
— Jataka No. 316, Sasa-Jataka (ed. E.B. Cowell, Vol. III) [6]
The hare’s willingness to give up his life is converted into an everlasting sign in the sky.
A self-sacrifice that should have been pure loss becomes lasting renown, the very conversion the Kural credits to the wise.
Jainism
“These two ways of life ending with death have been declared: death with one's will, and death against one's will. Death against one's will is that of ignorant men, and it happens (to the same individual) many times. Death with one's will is that of wise men, and at best it happens but once.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.2–3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Mahāvīra's lecture on death contrasts the fool's repeated, unwilling deaths with the wise person's single, willing death, which can bring the cycle of rebirth to an end.
Valluvar says a loss that is really gain, and a death that is really living on, are within reach only of the wise. The Jain text likewise reserves a death that is a victory rather than a loss for the wise alone.
Sikhism
“The death of brave heroes is blessed, if it is approved by God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 579, Raag Vadhans, Alahanian, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Alahanian, hymns sung in mourning, Guru Nanak says that the death of the truly brave is blessed when it is accepted by God.
The Kural speaks of a death that is really a way of living on, achieved only by the wise; Guru Nanak likewise calls a death approved by God not a loss but a blessing.
Greek Mythology
“If I stay here and fight, I shall not return alive but my name will live for ever: whereas if I go home my name will die, but it will be long ere death shall take me.”
— Homer, Iliad 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Achilles explains his two possible fates: early death at Troy with undying fame, or a long, obscure life at home.
The classic statement of trading the body’s life for a name that lives forever, the Kural’s ‘death that becomes existence’.
Philosophy
“They are ready to run all risks greater far than they would have for their children, and to spend money and undergo any sort of toil, and even to die, for the sake of leaving behind them a name which shall be eternal.”
— Plato, Symposium 208c-d (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Diotima tells Socrates that the love of immortal fame drives people to spend, toil and even die, citing Alcestis, Achilles and Codrus.
Both identify the paradoxical exchange of wealth and even life for an undying name.

Real Life Example

Joan of Arc is executed, rehabilitated and canonised
1431-1920 · Rouen, France
Joan of Arc, the peasant girl who led French forces to relieve Orleans during the Hundred Years' War, was tried by an ecclesiastical court and executed at Rouen on 30 May 1431. On the order of Pope Callixtus III, proceedings in 1455-56 annulled her conviction, and in 1920 she was canonised by Pope Benedict XV, while the French parliament decreed a yearly national festival in her honour.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A death meant to discredit her became the foundation of a renown that has grown for nearly six centuries; she is honoured as France's national heroine and patron saint.
The kural speaks of a death that is really a way of living on, and Joan's execution became the beginning of her enduring life in the memory of her nation.
