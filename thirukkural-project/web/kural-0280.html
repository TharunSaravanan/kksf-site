Kural 280

Inconsistent Conduct · Koodaavozhukkam · கூடாவொழுக்கம்

மழித்தலும் நீட்டலும் வேண்டா உலகம் பழித்தது ஒழித்து விடின்.
Mazhiththalum neettalum vendaa ulagam Pazhiththadhu ozhiththu vidin.

Meaning

There is no need to shave your head or to grow your hair long, if you simply give up what the world says is wrong.

Elementary

Some holy people shave their heads and some grow their hair really long. Valluvar says: your haircut doesn’t make you good! If you stop doing bad things, you’re already on the right path — hair or no hair.

University

The chapter closes with its most famous and most radical line. மழித்தல் (shaving the head, as Jain and Buddhist monks did) and நீட்டல் (growing matted locks, as Shaiva and Vedic ascetics did) were the defining visual signs of rival renunciant orders; Valluvar dismisses both in a single breath, declaring them unnecessary (வேண்டா). The only real requirement is ethical: உலகம் பழித்தது ஒழித்தல் — abandoning what the wise of the world condemn. This non-sectarian move, placing conduct above every order’s insignia, is characteristic of the Kural as a whole and parallels the Dhammapada’s dismissal of tonsure and matted hair, Manu’s ‘the external mark is not the cause of merit’, and Seneca’s warning against the philosopher’s unkempt beard.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…let him, though blemished (by a want of the external marks), fulfil his duty, equal-minded towards all creatures; (for) the external mark (of the order) is not the cause of (the acquisition of) merit.”
— Manusmriti 6.66 (Bühler translation) [3]
Manu tells the renunciant that the outward signs of his order do not produce spiritual merit; right conduct does.
Nearly the Kural’s own claim: ascetic insignia are unnecessary; ethical conduct is what counts.
Christianity
“Moreover when ye fast, be not, as the hypocrites, of a sad countenance: for they disfigure their faces, that they may appear unto men to fast. Verily I say unto you, They have their reward.”
— Matthew 6:16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus criticises people who make their fasting visible on their faces so others will admire them.
Like the Kural’s shaved or matted heads, visible ascetic signs are rejected as mere display.
Islam
“Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but [true] righteousness is [in] one who believes in Allah… and gives wealth… [and who] establishes prayer and gives zakah; [those who] fulfill their promise…”
— Qur’an 2:177 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says righteousness isn’t about outward ritual forms like the direction you face, but about faith and good conduct.
Same move as the Kural: outward religious form is secondary; ethical life is what matters.
Judaism
“And rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the LORD your God…”
— Joel 2:13 (KJV) [4]
Joel calls people to real inner repentance instead of the outward sign of tearing their clothes.
Replaces an outward religious gesture with inner change — as the Kural replaces tonsure and matted locks with giving up wrongdoing.
Buddhism
“Not by shaven head does a man who is indisciplined and untruthful become a monk.” / “Not by matted hair, nor by lineage, nor by birth does one become a holy man. But he in whom truth and righteousness exist — he is pure, he is a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 264 and 393 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha rejects both tonsure and matted hair as marks of holiness; only truth and righteousness count.
Names the same two hairstyles as the Kural (shaving and long locks) and reaches the same verdict.
Jainism
“One does not become a Sramana by the tonsure, nor a Brâhmana by the sacred syllable ôm, nor a Muni by living in the woods, nor a Tâpasa by wearing (clothes of) Kusa-grass and bark. One becomes a Sramana by equanimity, a Brâhmana by chastity, a Muni by knowledge, and a Tâpasa by penance.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.31-32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Jayaghosha says that no outward sign, whether a shaven head, a sacred word or forest dress, makes someone holy; only inner virtue does.
Valluvar says there is no need to shave the head or grow matted hair if one gives up what the world condemns; the Jain verse likewise denies that tonsure or ascetic dress make a renunciant.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, you have not shaved your mind, so why do you shave your head?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir tells the shaven-headed ascetic that unless the mind itself has been cleared of vice, shaving the head means nothing.
Valluvar says neither shaving the head nor growing matted hair is needed if one gives up what the world condemns; Kabir likewise says the outward mark is useless without a changed mind.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads the young Heracles meets two women: Vice, dressed and made up to show off her charms, and Virtue, plainly and modestly clothed; Heracles chooses Virtue’s hard road of good deeds.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21–34 (Prodicus’ ‘Choice of Heracles’) [9]
Prodicus’ allegory contrasts glamorous outward appearance with genuine virtue shown in action.
Like the Kural, it tells us that real goodness is chosen in conduct, not in how one is dressed or adorned.
Philosophy
“Repellent attire, unkempt hair, slovenly beard, open scorn of silver dishes, a couch on the bare earth, and any other perverted forms of self-display, are to be avoided.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 5.2 (tr. Gummere) [10]
Seneca tells Lucilius not to advertise his philosophy through scruffy hair and ostentatious poverty; the real difference should be inward.
Very close to the Kural: long hair and showy austerity are unnecessary; inner reform is what matters.

Real Life Example

Erasmus publishes The Praise of Folly
1511 · Western Europe (work of the Dutch humanist Erasmus)
The Dutch humanist Desiderius Erasmus published The Praise of Folly, a satire in which Folly herself mocks theologians, monks and rulers, including the monks' pride in their habits, rules and ceremonies. In his earlier Enchiridion of 1503 he had argued that true piety consists not in outward ceremonies but in inward conversion. The satire provoked strong resentment among theologians, and in 1564 it was among his works placed on the Church's index of prohibited books.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Erasmus became the most famous voice of his age for the view that outward religious forms count for nothing without an inward change of life.
The kural says there is no need to shave the head or grow long hair if one simply gives up what the world condemns, and Erasmus mocked the monks' outward marks while urging inner reform.
