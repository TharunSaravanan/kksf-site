Kural 22

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

துறந்தார் பெருமை துணைக்கூறின் வையத்து இறந்தாரை எண்ணிக்கொண் டற்று.
Thurandhaar perumai thunaikkoorin vaiyaththu, Irandhaarai ennikkon datru.

Meaning

Trying to measure the greatness of those who have given up worldly life is like trying to count all the people who have ever died on earth.

Elementary

Imagine someone asked you to count every leaf that has ever fallen from every tree in the world. You could start, but you could never finish. This kural says that asking “how great are people who have let go of wanting things?” is that same kind of question — too big to ever finish counting.

University

The force of the verse is in “thunaik-koorin” — “if one were to say the measure (thunai) of it.” Thunai here is quantity, extent, an amount; the couplet is not saying the renunciant's greatness is hard to describe but that it is the wrong sort of thing to put a number on. The chosen comparison is deliberately brutal: “vaiyaththu irandhaarai ennikkondu,” tallying up everyone who has ever died in the world, an arithmetic that is not merely large but principled endless, since the dead accumulate faster than any count. Commentators from Parimelazhagar onward read the simile as one of category error rather than mere magnitude. Placed second in the chapter, it functions as a rhetorical guard-rail: having just claimed in Kural 21 that the books rank renunciants highest, Valluvar immediately blocks the reader from imagining that this ranking could be cashed out in any scale or quantity.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Taittiriya Upanishad measures out a ladder of bliss, multiplying each rung a hundredfold from human joy up to the bliss of Brahman, and then breaks off its own arithmetic by saying that from that bliss words turn back, together with the mind, unable to reach it.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 2.8-2.9 (Ananda Valli) [4]
The text deliberately starts an enumeration in order to demonstrate that enumeration fails: the highest state is reached by abandoning the measuring apparatus, not by extending it.
The same structure as the Kural — begin to count, then show that counting is the wrong instrument for this object.
Christianity
“And what shall I more say? for the time would fail me to tell of Gedeon, and of Barak, and of Samson, and of Jephthae; of David also, and Samuel, and of the prophets:”
— Hebrews 11:32 (KJV) [5]
The writer of Hebrews abandons his own list of the faithful mid-sentence, conceding that the catalogue of the righteous outruns the time available to recite it.
Like the Kural, the text stages its own failure to enumerate as the strongest possible tribute to those it is praising.
Islam
“Indeed, the patient will be given their reward without account [i.e., limit].”
— Qur'an 39:10, Az-Zumar (Sahih International) [6]
The closing clause of the verse promises the ṣābirūn — those who endure and hold back — a reward given bi-ghayri ḥisāb, without reckoning, outside the ordinary ledger of deeds and payments.
The Qur'an makes the same point about the self-restrained that the Kural makes about the renounced: their worth is explicitly taken off the accounting scale.
Judaism
“These are the things that have no definite quantity: The corners [of the field]. First-fruits; [The offerings brought] on appearing [at the Temple on the three pilgrimage festivals]. The performance of righteous deeds; And the study of the torah.”
— Mishnah Peah 1:1 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp) [7]
The Mishnah opens the tractate on gleanings by listing the obligations that Jewish law deliberately leaves without a fixed measure, so that no ceiling can be placed on them.
A legal text formally declaring certain goods to be unquantifiable, which is exactly the move the Kural makes about the greatness of renunciants.
Buddhism
“He who reveres those worthy of reverence, the Buddhas and their disciples, who have transcended all obstacles and passed beyond the reach of sorrow and lamentation — he who reveres such peaceful and fearless ones, his merit none can compute by any measure.”
— Dhammapada 195-196, Buddhavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada says that even the by-product of honouring the perfected — the merit accruing to the one who honours them — defeats every attempt at calculation.
A near-verbatim parallel: the greatness attaching to the renounced is declared, in as many words, incomputable by any measure.
Jainism
“‘All sounds recoil thence, where speculation has no room,’ nor does the mind penetrate there.” … “he perceives, he knows, but there is no analogy (whereby to know the nature of the liberated soul).”
— Acaranga Sutra I.5.6.3-4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
At the close of its fifth lecture the Acaranga Sutra describes the state of the one who has left the round of birth and death, and says that words, reasoning and even comparison cannot reach or measure it.
The Kural says that trying to put a measure on the greatness of renunciants is like counting all the dead, a category error rather than a hard sum; the Acaranga likewise says the renouncer's final state has no measure in words or analogy.
Sikhism
“The God-conscious being cannot be appraised... Who can describe the limits of the God-conscious being? Only the God-conscious being can know the state of the God-conscious being. The God-conscious being has no end or limitation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 273, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 8, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan says again and again that the greatness of the God-conscious person cannot be valued, measured or put into words.
This is the Kural's own point: trying to measure such greatness is like counting all the dead.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Odysseus summons the dead at the edge of the world and they come up in such numbers, with such a cry, that he loses his nerve and flees back to his ship rather than face the countless tribes of the perished.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.630-640 [11]
Homer uses the sheer unmanageable multitude of the dead as an image of something a living person cannot confront or take stock of.
It supplies the Kural's own comparison in narrative form — the dead of the world are the standing Greek image of a number no one can take.
Philosophy
“Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.”
— Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Proposition 7 (trans. C.K. Ogden, 1922) [12]
The final proposition of the Tractatus marks off a domain — value, ethics, the sense of the world — which Wittgenstein holds can be shown but never stated in propositions that measure facts.
It formalises the Kural's instinct that some kinds of greatness are not the sort of thing about which a measurement can be pronounced at all.

Real Life Example

Mother Teresa receives the Nobel Peace Prize
1979 · Oslo, Norway
Mother Teresa, who founded the Missionaries of Charity in Calcutta (Kolkata), India, in 1950 after taking religious vows, was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1979. The Norwegian Nobel Committee cited her work for bringing help to suffering humanity. She received the prize in Oslo in December 1979.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The world's most prominent peace award was given to a nun who owned almost nothing, recognising a kind of greatness that cannot be measured in wealth or power.
The kural says measuring the greatness of renunciants is like counting all the dead, and the Nobel honour for a woman who had given up everything shows the world struggling to put a measure on such greatness.
