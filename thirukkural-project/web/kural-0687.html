Kural 687

The Envoy · Thoodhu · தூது

கடனறிந்து காலங் கருதி இடனறிந்து எண்ணி உரைப்பான் தலை.
Kadanarindhu kaalang karudhi idanarindhu Enni uraippaan thalai.

Meaning

The best envoy knows their duty, waits for the right time, chooses the right place, and thinks carefully before speaking.

Elementary

If you want to ask your parents for a puppy, the smartest way is to wait until they are relaxed, not in the middle of a busy morning. Pick a good spot, and practise what you will say and what they might answer. That is how the best messengers do it.

University

Parimelazhagar glosses kaṭaṉ as the proper conduct toward foreign kings, which comes from weighing their standing, one’s own king’s and one’s own. Kālam is their cevvi, the mood in which they are receptive. Iṭaṉ is a place where one’s supporters are at hand. Eṇṇi means rehearsing in advance one’s own words, the other side’s likely replies, and one’s counter-replies. He says Valluvar chose ‘talai’ (the foremost) because the Sanskrit treatises (vaṭanūlār) rank envoys as first, middle and last, the last being a mere letter-carrier. With this couplet ends the section (681-687) on the envoy who frames his own words (tāṉ vakuttuk kūṟuvāṉ). The following three couplets deal with the envoy who repeats a given message. Manakkudavar likewise calls this the mark of the foremost envoy. On the text: the sandhi-joined text reads கருதி யிடனறிந் / தெண்ணி யுரைப்பான் றலை, the same wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya ranks envoys in three grades: one with full ministerial qualifications who may negotiate on his own judgement (nisṛṣṭārtha), one lacking a quarter of those qualities who is limited to a set commission (parimitārtha), and one lacking half who only carries messages (śāsanahara).
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.16 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [5]
The Arthashastra grades envoys by how much they may frame the message themselves.
This is the Sanskrit three-grade scheme (talai, iṭai, kaṭai) that Parimelazhagar says Valluvar alludes to with ‘talai’.
Christianity
“Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.”
— Matthew 10:16 (KJV) [6]
Sending out his disciples, Jesus tells them to combine shrewd judgement with innocence among hostile hearers.
The Kural’s foremost envoy is also shrewd about time and place while doing no harm.
Islam
“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best.”
— Qur’an 16:125 (Sahih International) [7]
The Qur’an tells the Prophet to deliver his message with wisdom and to argue in the most fitting manner.
‘Wisdom’ and ‘the way that is best’ correspond to the Kural’s careful weighing of duty, moment and manner.
Judaism
“And there was set meat before him to eat: but he said, I will not eat, until I have told mine errand. And he said, Speak on.”
— Genesis 24:33 (KJV) [6]
Abraham’s servant, sent to find a wife for Isaac, will not eat until he has delivered his mission. He then tells it in a carefully arranged way.
He knows his duty (kaṭaṉ aṟintu) and chooses his moment and words, like the Kural’s foremost envoy.
Buddhism
When Devadatta split the Sangha and took 500 monks to Gayāsīsa, the Buddha sent Sāriputta and Moggallāna after them. Devadatta invited Sāriputta to teach while he rested and then fell asleep. Sāriputta and Moggallāna took that moment to teach the monks and lead them back.
— Vinaya Piṭaka, Cullavagga VII.4 [8]
The Buddha’s two chief disciples, sent as envoys into a hostile assembly, waited for the right time and place before speaking.
This is the Kural’s kālam karuti, iṭaṉ aṟintu: choosing the moment and setting before delivering the message.
Jainism
“At the right time a monk should sally forth, and he should return at the right time; avoiding to do anything out of time, he should do what is appropriate for each period of the day.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The discipline lecture asks the monk to do each thing at its proper time and nothing out of season.
The Kural's best envoy knows protocol and waits for the right time and place; the Jain rule shares the stress on fitting action to the proper moment.
Sikhism
“Those words are acceptable, which, when spoken, bring honor. Harsh words bring only grief. Listen, O foolish and ignorant mind!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak teaches that speech must be weighed by its effect, since honourable words are accepted and careless or harsh words bring ruin.
The Kural's foremost envoy rehearses his words, the other side's replies and his own answers before speaking; Guru Nanak likewise asks that words be judged by what they will bring before they are said.
Greek Mythology
Shipwrecked and naked, Odysseus wonders whether to clasp the princess Nausicaa’s knees or to plead from a distance with gentle words. He decides to stand apart and speak, so as not to anger her.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.141-147 [11]
Homer shows Odysseus weighing place, manner and words before speaking to someone whose help he needs.
This is the Kural’s eṇṇi uraippāṉ: the speaker who thinks through where and how to speak before he opens his mouth.
Philosophy
Socrates tells Phaedrus that a true speaker must first learn the different kinds of soul and which kinds of speech persuade each. He must then be able to recognise them quickly in real life, and must add to this a knowledge of the right times for speaking and for keeping silent before his art is complete.
— Plato, Phaedrus, 271d–272a (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
In the closing discussion of rhetoric, Plato makes knowledge of the listener, together with a sense of the right moment (kairos) for each kind of speech, a condition of any real art of persuasion.
Plato's speaker, like the Kural's ideal envoy, must judge the audience and the moment before choosing his words.

Real Life Example

John Adams presents his credentials to King George III
1 June 1785 · St James's Palace, London, England
John Adams, the first United States minister to Britain, presented his credentials to King George III less than a week after arriving in London, following eight years of war between the two countries. The audience followed the formal ceremony of the royal court, and Adams delivered a short address he had rehearsed and committed to memory, assuring the King of his country's wish for friendly relations. He reported the exchange, including the King's reply, to John Jay the next day.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Adams treated a delicate first meeting between former enemies with careful attention to court protocol and prepared words, and the King answered in kind.
The kural describes the best envoy as one who knows the proper protocol, chooses the right moment and place, and thinks his words through before speaking, as Adams did before George III.
