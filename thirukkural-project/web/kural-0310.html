Kural 310

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

இறந்தார் இறந்தார் அனையர் சினத்தைத் துறந்தார் துறந்தார் துணை.
Irandhaar irandhaar anaiyar sinaththaith Thurandhaar thurandhaar thunai.

Meaning

People who let their anger go beyond all limits are as good as dead. People who have given up anger completely are as great as holy people who have left the world behind.

Elementary

A person who is always furious is like someone who isn't really alive: there's no joy, kindness, or peace inside. But a person who has given up anger is like a wise holy person. Letting go of anger makes you truly alive.

University

The closing couplet is a celebrated piece of Tamil wordplay built on two repeated verbs. Irandhaar can mean 'those who have exceeded (all limits)' and 'those who have died'; thurandhaar can mean 'those who have renounced (anger)' and 'those who have renounced (the world)', that is, ascetics. Hence: the excessively angry are like the dead; those who give up anger equal the renunciants. Solomon Pappaiah's gloss offers an alternative reading, that those free of anger are like those who have escaped death, which neatly mirrors the first line. Placed in the Thuravaraviyal (the section on ascetic virtue), the couplet makes giving up anger itself a form of renunciation available to everyone.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who neither hates nor desires the fruits of his activities is known to be always renounced. Such a person, free from all dualities, easily overcomes material bondage and is completely liberated, O mighty-armed Arjuna.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.3 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that true renunciation (nitya-sannyasa) is an inner condition, being free of hatred and craving, not an outward vow.
Exactly the kural's second line: the person who gives up hostility is equal to the renunciant.
Christianity
“Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill ... But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment:”
— Matthew 5:21-22 (KJV) [4]
Jesus places anger in the same moral category as killing, linking wrath to the realm of death and judgment.
Like the kural's first line, anger is associated with death itself.
Islam
“And those who avoid the major sins and immoralities, and when they are angry, they forgive,”
— Quran 42:37 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran lists forgiving while angry among the defining qualities of believers devoted to God.
Like the kural, letting go of anger is counted among the marks of the spiritually committed.
Judaism
“The Sages taught: There are three types of people whose lives are not lives, due to their constant suffering: The compassionate, the hot tempered, and the delicate.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 113b (William Davidson/Koren-Steinsaltz trans., via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud counts the hot-tempered among those whose lives are 'not lives'.
Almost exactly the kural's first line: the excessively angry are as good as dead.
Buddhism
“He who is free from anger, is devout, virtuous, without craving, self-subdued and bears his final body — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 400 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
In the chapter on the true brahmin, freedom from anger is listed first among the marks of the holy person.
Like the kural's second line, being free of anger places one among the renunciant saints.
Jainism
“Bravo! you have conquered anger; bravo! you have vanquished pride; bravo! you have banished delusion; bravo! you have subdued greed. … Here (on earth) you are the highest man, Reverend sir, and hereafter you will be the highest; exempt from all blemishes you will reach Perfection.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.56–58 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Indra drops his disguise and praises the renouncing king Nami, first of all for having conquered anger, and declares him the highest of men, bound for Perfection.
Valluvar says those who have wholly given up anger are equal to renunciants. Here the renunciant is praised precisely for having conquered anger, so the conquest of anger and renunciation are presented together.
Sikhism
“who is not touched by sexual desire or anger - within his heart, God dwells.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 633, Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the person who stays even-minded in pleasure and pain, honour and dishonour. Because anger does not touch such a person, God abides in their heart.
The Kural says those who have wholly given up anger are equal to those who have renounced the world. Guru Tegh Bahadur finds the true saint not in renunciation but in the one whom anger cannot touch.
Greek Mythology
At the close of Aeschylus' Oresteia, the Furies, ancient spirits of vengeful wrath, are persuaded by Athena to give up their rage against Athens and are transformed into honoured guardians, the Eumenides ('Kindly Ones').
— Aeschylus, Eumenides [10]
The trilogy ends with wrath itself being renounced and turned into a blessing.
Renouncing anger raises the Furies to a sacred, honoured status, echoing the kural's claim that those who give up anger equal the holy.
Philosophy
“For which reason also, I am not angry with my accusers, or my condemners; they have done me no harm...”
— Plato, Apology 41d (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Sentenced to death, Socrates declares that he holds no anger toward those who condemned him.
Socrates facing death without anger embodies the kural's anger-renouncer, who stands above both anger and death.

Real Life Example

Tolstoy makes Do not be angry the first commandment of his faith
1884 · Russia
In What I Believe, written in 1884, Leo Tolstoy set out the teaching he drew from the Sermon on the Mount, naming as Jesus' first commandment that one must live in peace with all and never regard anger as justified. His later Christianity stressed five tenets, beginning with be not angry and including do not resist evil and love your enemies, and his doctrine of nonviolent nonresistance went on to inspire Gandhi. Tolstoy renounced the church's rituals and was excommunicated by the Russian Orthodox Church in 1901.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One of the world's great novelists placed the complete renunciation of anger at the head of a moral teaching that he presented as a renunciant's way of life.
The kural says those who have wholly renounced anger are equal to those who have renounced the world, and Tolstoy built a renouncer's creed whose very first rule was to give up anger.
