Kural 626

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

அற்றேமென்று அல்லற் படுபவோ பெற்றேமென்று ஓம்புதல் தேற்றா தவர்.
Atremendru allar patupavo petremendru Ompudhal thetraa thavar.

Meaning

People who never held on tightly to their riches, saying 'we have it,' will not cry 'we have lost it all' when it is gone.

Elementary

If you share your toys freely and don’t get too attached to them, you won’t cry much when one breaks. The Kural says people who didn’t cling to their riches don’t fall apart when the riches are gone.

University

The couplet sets two first-person plural verbs against each other: aṟṟēm, ‘we are bereft’, and peṟṟēm, ‘we have obtained’, the first the cry of the ruined man, the second the self-congratulation of the prosperous. Ōmputal tēṟṟātavar, ‘those who do not know (or care) to hoard and guard’, is read by Mu. Varadarasanar as those who did not greedily cling to wealth when they had it, while Solomon Pappaiah takes it as those who freely gave it away to others, and Kalaignar as those who were not elated by it; the three glosses share the idea that detachment in prosperity prevents despair in loss. The rhetorical question (paṭupavō, ‘would they suffer?’) expects the answer no, and the text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net. The couplet shifts the chapter from enduring trouble to preventing it inwardly, preparing for the equanimity teaching of 627-629.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A person who has given up all desires for sense gratification, who lives free from desires, who has given up all sense of proprietorship and is devoid of false ego – he alone can attain real peace.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.71 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that the one free of possessiveness (nirmama) and ego finds a peace that no loss can take away.
The Kural’s person who never said ‘we have got it’ is the Gita’s nirmama, and that lack of possessiveness is why loss cannot make them wail.
Christianity
“As sorrowful, yet alway rejoicing; as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing all things.”
— 2 Corinthians 6:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes apostles who, holding nothing tightly, remain joyful even in poverty and grief.
The Kural’s unclinging person who never cries ‘we are ruined’ matches Paul’s ‘having nothing, and yet possessing all things’.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you.”
— Qur’an 57:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches believers to hold gains and losses lightly, neither boasting over the one nor despairing over the other.
This is almost the Kural’s couplet in reverse order: no exulting ‘we have it’, and therefore no despairing ‘we have lost it’.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines wealth as contentment with what one has rather than the amount one holds.
A person who rejoices in his lot has nothing to wail over when fortune changes, just as the Kural says.
Buddhism
“Happy indeed we live, we who possess nothing. Feeders on joy we shall be, like the Radiant Gods.”
— Dhammapada 200 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha, living without possessions, says his community is happy because it has nothing to lose.
Those who do not cling to what they own cannot be driven to despair by losing it; the Buddha’s joyful non-possession is the Kural’s point at its extreme.
Jainism
“Happy are we, happy live we who call nothing our own; when Mithilâ is on fire, nothing is burned that belongs to me.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
When Indra tells King Nami, newly turned monk, that his palace and city are on fire, Nami answers that nothing of his is burning, because he calls nothing his own.
The Kural asks whether those who never clung to wealth as 'ours' will wail 'we have lost everything'; Nami's reply as Mithila burns is that exact attitude in story form.
Sikhism
“If some of this capital is later taken back by the Banker, the ignorant person shows his anger. He himself destroys his own credibility, and he shall not again be trusted.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 268, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani Sahib, ashtpadi 5), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan compares all we possess to capital lent by God the Banker; the ignorant person who clings to it as his own grows angry when part of it is taken back.
The Kural asks whether those who never clung to wealth as their own would wail at its loss; Guru Arjan shows that only the one who treats God's loan as his own rages when it is recalled.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, an old Phrygian couple, lived in a humble cottage, making their poverty light by owning it openly and bearing it contentedly; when the disguised gods Zeus and Hermes came, they gladly gave them their best, and were rewarded.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 [10]
Ovid’s old couple are the model of people who neither clung to goods nor were embittered by lacking them.
Their calm, generous poverty shows the Kural’s point: those who do not hoard do not despair when they have little.
Philosophy
“Never say of anything, ‘I have lost it’; but, ‘I have returned it.’ Is your child dead? It is returned. Is your wife dead? She is returned. Is your estate taken away? Well, and is not that likewise returned?”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 11 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus teaches that nothing external is truly ours, so its loss should be seen as giving back a loan.
Epictetus forbids the very cry the Kural mocks, ‘I have lost it’, by forbidding the possessive ‘I have it’ that comes before.

Real Life Example

Chuck Feeney gives away his fortune and lives modestly
1984 · United States and Bermuda
In 1984 Chuck Feeney, co-founder of the airport retailer Duty Free Shoppers, secretly transferred his entire 38.75 per cent stake in the company to his foundation, later known as The Atlantic Philanthropies, leaving himself with only a few million dollars. For the rest of his life he lived modestly in rented apartments, owned no car and flew economy. By the time the foundation closed in 2016 it had given away more than eight billion dollars.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Feeney treated his wealth as something to give rather than to hold, and he lived without the possessions that usually define a billionaire.
The kural asks whether those who never clung to their wealth would ever wail at losing it, and Feeney, who chose to part with almost everything he owned, shows the detachment the couplet praises.
