Kural 350

Renunciation · Thuravu · துறவு

பற்றுக பற்றற்றான் பற்றினை அப்பற்றைப் பற்றுக பற்று விடற்கு.
Patruga patratraan patrinai appatraip Patruga patru vidarku.

Meaning

Hold on to the One who is free of all attachment. Hold on to that one bond alone, so that you can let go of all others.

Elementary

When you're learning to swim, you hold on to the edge of the pool so you can stop being scared of the water. This kural says hold on to God — the one who clings to nothing — so you can let go of everything else.

University

The chapter's closing couplet repeats forms of patru (“attachment, clinging”) six times, a virtuoso climax. It resolves a paradox the chapter has built up: desire cannot simply be willed away, so Valluvar prescribes a single counter-attachment — to patratraan, 'the One without attachment', whom Parimelazhagar and most commentators identify as God, whose feet are to be held — as the instrument for dropping all other attachments. Karunanidhi, in keeping with his secular reading, takes patratraan to mean any truly detached person, making the verse a counsel to cling to the company of the detached. The strategy of replacing a lower attachment with a higher one parallels the Gita's 'higher taste', while the Buddha's raft simile adds the caution that even the means is finally to be let go.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The embodied soul may be restricted from sense enjoyment, though the taste for sense objects remains. But, ceasing such engagements by experiencing a higher taste, he is fixed in consciousness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.59 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna notes that forced abstinence leaves the craving intact; only the experience of something higher makes the lower taste fall away.
The Gita's 'higher taste' is the Kural's strategy exactly: cling to the higher attachment in order to release the lower ones.
Christianity
“Yea doubtless, and I count all things but loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things, and do count them but dung, that I may win Christ,”
— Philippians 3:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that holding on to Christ has made everything else he once valued worthless by comparison.
Clinging to Christ is what enables Paul to let go of everything else — the Kural's single, liberating attachment.
Islam
“And remember the name of your Lord and devote yourself to Him with [complete] devotion.”
— Quran 73:8 (Sahih International) [5]
The Arabic 'tabattal ilayhi tabtila' literally suggests cutting oneself off from all else in order to devote oneself wholly to God.
Tabattul — severing from everything to cleave to God alone — is the Kural's formula of one attachment used to cut all others.
Judaism
“Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever.”
— Psalm 73:25-26 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist declares that God is his only desire and his lasting portion, even as everything else fails.
A single, all-absorbing attachment to God that renders every other desire unnecessary — the heart of the Kural.
Buddhism
“In the same way, monks, I have taught the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 22, Alagaddupama Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha compares his teaching to a raft: something to hold on to while crossing the flood, and then to leave behind.
Like the Kural's single permitted attachment, the Dhamma is grasped as a means to let go — though the Buddha adds that even it must finally be released.
Jainism
Samantabhadra's hymn to the twelfth Tīrthaṅkara, Vāsupūjya, says that worship serves no purpose for the Jina, who is free from attachment, and that blame means nothing to one who has cast off enmity. Yet remembering his pure qualities cleanses the devotee's mind of the stains of sin.
— Svayambhūstotra of Samantabhadra, verse 57 (Vāsupūjya-stuti), Sanskrit text at sanskritdocuments.org [7]
Jain devotion is directed to the vītarāga, 'one free of attachment'. Since the Jina grants nothing, the benefit of devotion comes entirely from fixing the mind on detachment itself.
Valluvar tells us to cling to the One who is without attachment so that every other attachment falls away. Jain worship of the passionless Jina works by exactly this method, although the Jina is a liberated soul, not a creator God.
Sikhism
“I am joined with You, and I have broken with all others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 659, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Ravidas tells God that by joining himself to the Lord in love he has broken his bond with everything else.
The Kural resolves its chapter by prescribing one attachment, to the One without attachment, so that every other may be let go; Ravidas states exactly that exchange of one bond for all others.
Greek Mythology
When his raft is sucked into Charybdis, Odysseus leaps up and clings to a fig tree growing over the whirlpool, holding on until the wreckage is thrown back up and he can escape.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.426-446 [9]
Everything else is swallowed by the whirlpool; holding one fixed point is what saves him.
A loose image of the Kural's counsel: amid the whirlpool, cling to the one stable thing in order to be freed from everything else.
Philosophy
Plotinus ends the Enneads by describing the soul's ascent as a withdrawal from all things of this world toward the One, famously characterised as the flight of the alone to the Alone.
— Plotinus, Enneads 6.9.11 [10]
For Plotinus, the soul must turn away from multiplicity and cleave to the One, which is itself beyond need and attachment.
Clinging to the One that clings to nothing, in order to shed every lower tie, is close to the Kural's paradox.

Real Life Example

Ignatius of Loyola lays down his sword at Montserrat
March 1522 · Montserrat, Catalonia, Spain
Ignatius of Loyola, a Basque soldier who had been seriously wounded at Pamplona and turned to religion during his recovery, went to the Benedictine abbey of Montserrat in March 1522. He hung up his sword and dagger before the shrine of the Virgin, kept an all-night vigil, gave away his fine clothes to a poor man and put on rough pilgrim's garb. He went on to found the Society of Jesus.
— Sources: [11] [12]
By binding himself wholly to God, Ignatius found the strength to drop the military ambitions and worldly honour that had ruled his early life. The moment at Montserrat became a turning point in the history of the Jesuits.
The kural advises holding fast to the One who is free of attachment so as to let go of every other attachment, and Ignatius gave up his sword and finery by committing himself entirely to God.
