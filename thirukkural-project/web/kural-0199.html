Kural 199

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

பொருள்தீர்ந்த பொச்சாந்துஞ் சொல்லார் மருள்தீர்ந்த மாசறு காட்சி யவர்.
Poruldheerndha pochchaandhum sollaar maruldheerndha, Maasaru kaatchi yavar.

Meaning

People who see clearly, with no confusion or fault in their thinking, will not speak meaningless words, not even by mistake.

Elementary

Some people are so careful and clear-headed that even when they are tired or distracted, they don’t blurt out silly things. This kural says that people whose minds are truly clean and clear don’t say empty words even by accident.

University

The couplet balances two phrases built on தீர்ந்த (theerndha, ‘ended, freed of’). The speech is பொருள்தீர்ந்த (‘emptied of meaning’), and the speakers are மருள்தீர்ந்த (‘freed of delusion’). The symmetry suggests that meaningless words can only come from a mind that still holds some confusion. பொச்சாந்தும் (pochchaandhum, ‘even through forgetfulness/heedlessness’) raises the standard from deliberate restraint to spontaneous purity: the மாசறு காட்சியவர் (maasaru kaatchiyavar, ‘those of flawless insight’) do not need to police their tongues, because nothing empty is left in them to come out. Pochchaappu (heedlessness) later gets a chapter of its own (ch. 54, பொச்சாவாமை), which shows how central vigilance is to Valluvar’s ethics. There is a close parallel in the Buddhist portrait of the freed person whose ‘speech is calm’ and in James’s ‘perfect man’ who ‘offends not in word’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Serenity of thought, gentleness, silence, self-control, and purity of purpose—all these are declared as austerity of the mind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.16 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna places mauna (silence) among the disciplines of a purified mind, not just of the tongue.
Both texts trace restrained speech back to a purified, clear mind. Where the mind is clear, silence comes naturally.
Christianity
“For in many things we offend all. If any man offend not in word, the same is a perfect man, and able also to bridle the whole body.”
— James 3:2 (KJV) [4]
James makes perfect control of speech the mark of spiritual maturity.
Both texts describe a rare perfection in which one does not slip in speech at all.
Islam
“A slave of Allah may utter a word without thinking whether it is right or wrong, he may slip down in the Fire as far away a distance equal to that between the east.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6477 (trans. Muhsin Khan, sunnah.com) [5]
The hadith warns about the unconsidered word spoken carelessly.
The Kural praises the clear-minded, who do not slip even through heedlessness, and the hadith shows the danger of the heedless word they avoid.
Judaism
“One who wakes up at night, or walks on the way alone and turns his heart to idle matters, behold, this man is mortally guilty.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:4 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Hananiah ben Hakinai holds that even unguarded, solitary moments must not drift into idleness.
Both texts require vigilance even in unguarded moments; the Kural says the clear-minded do not lapse even in forgetfulness.
Buddhism
“Calm is his thought, calm his speech, and calm his deed, who, truly knowing, is wholly freed, perfectly tranquil and wise.”
— Dhammapada 96 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada describes the arahant, freed by true knowledge, whose speech has become calm.
Both texts describe a mind freed of delusion, from which calm, meaningful speech follows without effort.
Jainism
“Possessing perfect wisdom, a sage always vanquishes (his passions); he correctly expounds the Law; he never neglects even the smallest (duty); he is neither angry nor proud.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.2.2.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga describes the sage of perfect wisdom as one whose teaching is always correct and who never slips even in the smallest duty.
Valluvar says those whose vision is free of delusion never speak meaningless words, even by a slip, and the Jain sage of perfect wisdom likewise speaks the Law correctly and never neglects even the smallest duty.
Sikhism
“Without the Name, all other praise and speech is insipid and tasteless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 301, Raag Gauree, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gauree, Guru Ram Das teaches that to one who knows the Naam, every other kind of talk loses its taste.
The Kural says those whose vision is free of delusion will not speak meaningless words even by a slip; Guru Ram Das likewise says the awakened find all speech without the Name empty.
Greek Mythology
Pythagorean tradition held that new disciples kept a strict silence (echemythia) for up to five years before they could speak in the school, training the mind before the tongue.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives 8.10 (Pythagoras); cf. Iamblichus, Life of Pythagoras [10]
The semi-legendary Pythagorean discipline treated long silence as purification that prepared a person for wise speech.
Both traditions make disciplined speech the product of long mental purification, so that careless words no longer arise.
Philosophy
“The world would be much happier, if men were as fully able to keep silence as they are to speak. Experience abundantly shows that men can govern anything more easily than their tongues.”
— Baruch Spinoza, Ethics III, Prop. 2, Scholium (trans. R. H. M. Elwes) [11]
Spinoza notes how rarely people control their speech, as evidence of how far they are from real freedom.
Spinoza says ordinary minds cannot govern their tongues, and the Kural says the delusion-free do so effortlessly. Both link command of speech to clarity of mind.

Real Life Example

Gandhi's weekly day of silence
Weekly, over many years of his public life (died 1948) · India
For many years Mohandas Gandhi kept every Monday as a day of silence, during which he did not speak and, when he had to communicate, wrote notes instead, even when a meeting with an official could not be avoided. He kept the practice through the busiest years of the independence struggle and used the day for writing and other work. Gandhi himself allowed only rare exceptions, such as severe pain or comforting someone in distress.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The weekly silence was a deliberate discipline of speech, kept by one of the busiest public figures of his age.
The kural describes people of clear vision who never utter meaningless words even through heedlessness, and Gandhi's silent Mondays were a regular training in saying nothing that was not needed.
