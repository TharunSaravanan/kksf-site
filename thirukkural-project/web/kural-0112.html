Kural 112

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

செப்பம் உடையவன் ஆக்கஞ் சிதைவின்றி எச்சத்திற் கேமாப்பு உடைத்து.
Seppam udaiyavan aakkanj sidhaivindri Echchaththirku emaappu udaiththu.

Meaning

The riches of a fair-minded person do not fall apart. They last and support even their children and grandchildren.

Elementary

Think of a house built on strong, straight foundations. It doesn’t fall down; the grandchildren can still live in it. Wealth earned fairly is like that house, and it keeps protecting the family.

University

செப்பம் (seppam, “straightness, rectitude”) is used here as a synonym for naduvu nilaimai, and the couplet makes a claim about consequences: the ஆக்கம் (aakkam, “increase, prosperity”) of the just person is சிதைவின்றி (“without disintegration”) and furnishes ஏமாப்பு (emaappu, “security, protection”) to the எச்சம் (echcham, “what remains after one”), which the commentators read as descendants or posterity. The point is intergenerational: unjust gain may be large but brittle, while justly earned wealth is durable. The idea that righteousness is an inheritance for children’s children is widespread, found in Proverbs, the Psalms, the Qur’anic story of the orphans’ buried treasure, and Hesiod’s claim that the truthful man’s “generation” prospers. The word echcham reappears in Kural 114, where its meaning (offspring or legacy) becomes a matter of commentarial debate.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: Son of Pṛthā, a transcendentalist engaged in auspicious activities does not meet with destruction either in this world or in the spiritual world; one who does good, My friend, is never overcome by evil.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.40 [4]
Krishna reassures Arjuna that a person who does good is never destroyed, in this life or the next.
Shares the Kural’s promise that right conduct leads to lasting security rather than ruin.
Christianity
“A good man leaveth an inheritance to his children’s children: and the wealth of the sinner is laid up for the just.”
— Proverbs 13:22 (KJV) [5]
This proverb, part of the Christian Old Testament, says a good person’s legacy reaches even grandchildren, while the wicked person’s wealth ends up with others.
Almost the Kural’s exact claim: the upright person’s wealth lasts and benefits their descendants.
Islam
“And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and there was beneath it a treasure for them, and their father had been righteous. So your Lord intended that they reach maturity and extract their treasure, as a mercy from your Lord.”
— Qur’an 18:82 (Saheeh International) [6]
In the story of Moses and al-Khidr, a wall is repaired so that two orphans’ hidden treasure stays safe, explicitly because their father had been righteous.
The righteous father’s goodness protects his children’s wealth after him, which is the Kural’s “security to posterity.”
Judaism
“His seed shall be mighty upon earth: the generation of the upright shall be blessed. Wealth and riches shall be in his house: and his righteousness endureth for ever.”
— Psalm 112:2–3 (KJV) [5]
The psalm describes the upright person: their descendants are blessed, their house has wealth, and their righteousness lasts forever.
Same three elements as the Kural: uprightness, lasting prosperity, and blessing to descendants.
Buddhism
“He who acquires his wealth in harmless ways like to a bee that honey gathers, riches mount up for him like ant hill’s rapid growth.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha tells the householder Sigala that wealth gathered without harming anyone grows steadily, the way an anthill rises.
Both say that wealth gained righteously grows and endures.
Jainism
“Nyayasampannavibhavah : Possessed of honestly earned wealth.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of 35 qualities of the ideal śrāvaka, no. 1, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, 'Prescription of Ethical Code' (jainworld.com) [8]
The first of Hemacandra's thirty-five qualities of the ideal Jain householder is wealth acquired by just means (nyāya).
Valluvar promises that the prosperity of the upright endures, and Jain householder ethics likewise puts justly earned wealth first, though it makes no explicit promise about its lasting for descendants.
Sikhism
“It does not run short, and it does not run out.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 375, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes the wealth of the Lord's Name as a treasure that never diminishes however much it is used.
The Kural promises that the upright person's prosperity does not crumble; Gurbani makes the same claim, but for the spiritual wealth of the righteous rather than material gain.
Greek Mythology
“For whoever knows the right and is ready to speak it, far-seeing Zeus gives him prosperity; but whoever deliberately lies in his witness and forswears himself, and so hurts Justice and sins beyond repair, that man’s generation is left obscure thereafter. But the generation of the man who swears truly is better thenceforward.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 280–285 (trans. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod says Zeus gives prosperity to the one who speaks what is right, and that this person’s descendants fare better, while the perjurer’s line fades into obscurity.
An exact counterpart: justice brings prosperity that carries over to one’s descendants (echcham).
Philosophy
“Then this must be our notion of the just man, that even when he is in poverty or sickness, or any other seeming misfortune, all things will in the end work together for good to him in life and death”
— Plato, Republic X, 613a (trans. Jowett) [11]
At the end of the Republic, Socrates concludes that the just person is ultimately secure: whatever happens, things work out for his good.
Shares the Kural’s conviction that justice yields lasting security rather than eventual collapse.

Real Life Example

Ernst Abbe creates the Carl Zeiss Foundation
1889; statute 1896 · Jena, Germany
Physicist Ernst Abbe, who became head of the Zeiss optical works after Carl Zeiss's death in 1888, was concerned that future owners might enrich themselves at the expense of the companies and their employees. In 1889 he set up and endowed the Carl Zeiss Foundation, which became sole owner of the Zeiss works in 1891, and his 1896 foundation statute gave employees rights such as profit-sharing, paid holidays, sick pay and pensions, with an eight-hour day introduced at Zeiss in 1900. The ZEISS company credits Abbe with a decisive role in the continued existence of ZEISS and SCHOTT, both of which still operate today.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Abbe chose to build fairness to workers into the ownership of the firm itself, so that it could not easily be undone by later owners.
The kural says the prosperity of the just does not crumble but supports those who come after; Abbe's fairly organised enterprise has outlasted him by more than a century.
