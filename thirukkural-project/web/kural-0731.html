Kural 731

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

தள்ளா விளையுளும் தக்காரும் தாழ்விலாச் செல்வரும் சேர்வது நாடு.
Thallaa vilaiyulum thakkaarum thaazhvilaach chelvarum servadhu naadu.

Meaning

A true country has three things together: crops that never fail, good and worthy people, and rich people whose riches do not shrink.

Elementary

Think of a good neighbourhood. The gardens always grow food, there are wise grown-ups you can ask for help, and the shopkeepers do well enough to keep their shops open. When all three are there together, it feels like a real home. Valluvar says a country is the same.

University

The chapter opens the Kural’s treatment of naadu (the realm or territory), the third of the six angas (limbs) of kingship listed in kural 381, and it begins with a definition by constituents. Thallaa vilaiyul is literally ‘produce that does not fail’ (thal, to reject or fall off), thakkaar are ‘the worthy’, whom Parimelazhagar glosses as men of virtue and learning, and thaazhvilaa chelvar are ‘the wealthy without decline’, whom he identifies as merchants whose stock never runs out; Pope’s prose gloss follows him in naming merchants. Modern commentators widen the reading: Solomon Pappaiah turns the first term into the labourers who produce the harvest and the third into the unselfish rich, while Kalaignar reads the wealthy as those who do not squander riches on evil ends. The verb servadhu (that in which they gather together) makes the point that a land is defined by the coexistence of these groups, not by any one of them. The sandhi-joined text reads ‘விளையுளுந் தக்காருந்’ and ‘செல்வருஞ்’, which divide into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Valmiki’s opening description of Ayodhya under King Dasaratha paints a city whose people were learned, virtuous and content with their own wealth, where no one was poor, miserly or uneducated, and whose granaries and treasuries were full.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda, Sarga 6 (description of Ayodhya) [3]
The Ramayana begins its story by showing the ideal kingdom: a place that is rich in goods and also rich in good and learned people.
Ayodhya brings together the same three things as the Kural: plenty, worthy people and wealth that does not run out.
Christianity
“Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy; That they do good, that they be rich in good works, ready to distribute, willing to communicate.”
— 1 Timothy 6:17-18 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Timothy to teach wealthy believers not to be proud or rely on riches, but to use them generously for others.
Modern commentators read the Kural’s ‘wealthy without decline’ as the rich who spend well, which is exactly Paul’s ideal of the rich.
Islam
“There was for [the tribe of] Saba’ in their dwelling place a sign: two [fields of] gardens on the right and on the left. [They were told], ‘Eat from the provisions of your Lord and be grateful to Him. A good land [have you], and a forgiving Lord.’”
— Qur’an 34:15 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an recalls the people of Sheba, who lived between two great gardens and were told they had been given a good land.
Saba’s gardens on either side are a picture of the Kural’s unfailing harvest, the first mark of a real country.
Judaism
“For the LORD thy God bringeth thee into a good land, a land of brooks of water, of fountains and depths that spring out of valleys and hills; A land of wheat, and barley, and vines, and fig trees, and pomegranates; a land of oil olive, and honey; A land wherein thou shalt eat bread without scarceness, thou shalt not lack any thing in it.”
— Deuteronomy 8:7-9 (KJV) [4]
Moses describes the promised land to Israel as a place of water, grain and fruit where bread will never be scarce.
‘Bread without scarceness’ is the Torah’s version of the Kural’s ‘harvest that never fails’.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta, the Buddha foretells a future age when India will be rich and prosperous, its villages, towns and royal cities so close together that a cockerel could fly from one to the next, under a righteous wheel-turning king.
— Digha Nikaya 26, Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta [6]
After a long decline, people return to good conduct and the land becomes crowded, fertile and wealthy again.
The Buddhist ideal realm, like the Kural’s, is a land where plenty and good people exist together.
Jainism
“In the night in which the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra was brought into the family of the Gñâtris their silver increased, their gold increased; their riches, corn, majesty, and kingdom increased; their army, train, treasure, storehouse, town, seraglio, subjects, and glory increased”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §90 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Kalpa Sutra describes King Siddhartha's realm flourishing in grain, riches, stores and subjects from the moment the future Tirthankara entered the family, a sign of the merit attached to his presence.
The Kural defines a true country by unfailing harvests, worthy people and lasting wealth; the Jain text presents the same marks of a flourishing realm as the result of a virtuous presence within it.
Sikhism
When Guru Arjan completed the pool at Amritsar and built the Harimandar in its midst, he kept a community kitchen open to all castes and creeds, invited traders from different regions to settle and open their businesses around the temple, and had rest houses built for pilgrims, so that a city grew up with the Harimandar as its focus.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entry Arjan Dev, Guru [8]
The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism describes how Guru Arjan deliberately built a living town: a shared kitchen that fed everyone, merchants drawn in to trade, and a community of devotees gathered around the shrine.
The Kural defines a true country as the place where steady food, worthy people and prospering merchants come together, and Guru Arjan's Amritsar was planned around exactly that combination.
Greek Mythology
“The fruits never rot nor fail all the year round, neither winter nor summer, for the air is so soft that a new crop ripens before the old has dropped.”
— Homer, Odyssey 7 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Odysseus reaches the land of the Phaeacians and sees King Alcinous’ orchard, where pears, apples, figs and grapes are always in season.
Homer’s orchard where fruit ‘never fails’ is the mythic picture of the Kural’s thallaa vilaiyul, the harvest that never fails.
Philosophy
“First, there must be food; secondly, arts, for life requires many instruments; thirdly, there must be arms ... fourthly, there must be a certain amount of revenue, both for internal needs, and for the purposes of war.”
— Aristotle, Politics VII.8 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Aristotle lists the functions a city cannot do without, starting with food and going on to crafts, arms, revenue, worship and justice.
Like Valluvar, Aristotle defines a state by the things and people it must contain, and food comes first on both lists.

Real Life Example

Emperor Zhenzong distributes drought-resistant Champa rice
1012 · Lower Yangtze and Huai river regions, Song China
After a severe drought struck the lower Yangtze and Huai river valleys in 1012, the Song emperor Zhenzong had thirty thousand bushels of early-ripening, drought-resistant rice seed from Champa, in present-day central Vietnam, distributed to farmers in the stricken areas. He also had instructions on how to grow the new rice circulated to the farmers. The fast-maturing variety could be grown on higher ground and, in many places, allowed two harvests a year.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The new rice made harvests far less vulnerable to drought, and historians link its spread to the growth of population in southern China.
The kural names harvests that never fail as the first mark of a true country, and Zhenzong's distribution of Champa rice was a deliberate effort to make his land's harvests failure-proof.
