Kural 429

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

எதிரதாக் காக்கும் அறிவினார்க் கில்லை அதிர வருவதோர் நோய்.
Ethirathaak kaakkum arivinaark killai Athira varuvathor noi.

Meaning

Wise people who prepare ahead for what is coming will never be hit by a disaster that makes them shake with fear.

Elementary

If you know a storm is coming and you close the windows and bring in your toys, the storm can't really hurt you. Wise people prepare ahead of time, so when trouble comes it doesn't shake them.

University

Building on 427 (knowing what will come), this couplet adds action: எதிரதாக் காக்கும் means 'guarding against it as something ahead', that is, protecting oneself before a danger arrives. The result: இல்லை அதிர வருவதோர் நோய் ('there is no affliction that comes so as to make them shudder'). நோய் (noi) literally means disease but is used broadly for suffering or calamity, and அதிர (to shake or tremble) vividly describes the shock of being unprepared. The claim is not that the wise escape every misfortune but that nothing catches them off guard. This parallels the Stoic premeditatio malorum, the Panchatantra fable of the three fish, and Jesus's parable of the house built on rock. It links back to 421's image of arivu as a protective weapon.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Panchatantra, three fish in a pond overhear fishermen planning to return. Anagatavidhata ('Provider-for-the-future') leaves at once for another pond. Pratyutpannamati ('Ready-wit') escapes by playing dead. Yadbhavishya ('What-will-be-will-be') does nothing and is caught.
— Panchatantra, Book 1, 'The Three Fish' [4]
The fable ranks prudence by foresight. The fish who guards against danger before it arrives survives with no distress at all.
This dramatises the Kural: the one who guards against what is coming is untouched by calamity.
Christianity
“I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock: And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not.”
— Matthew 7:24-25 (KJV) [5]
Jesus contrasts the wise builder, who prepared a foundation before the storm, with the foolish builder whose house collapsed.
Both texts teach that the wise prepare beforehand, so the storm does not bring them down.
Islam
“O you who have believed, fear Allah. And let every soul look to what it has put forth for tomorrow — and fear Allah. Indeed, Allah is Acquainted with what you do.”
— Qur'an 59:18 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an calls believers to look ahead to 'tomorrow' (the Hereafter) and prepare for it now.
Both texts urge guarding in advance against what is coming.
Judaism
“A prudent man seeth the evil, and hideth himself; but the thoughtless pass on, and are punished.”
— Proverbs 22:3 (JPS 1917; repeated at 27:12) [7]
Proverbs contrasts the prudent person, who sees trouble coming and takes cover, with the naive, who walk straight into it.
This is nearly the same thought as the Kural: foresight plus precaution means calamity does not strike.
Buddhism
“Just as a border city is closely guarded both within and without, even so, guard yourself. Do not let slip this opportunity (for spiritual growth). For those who let slip this opportunity grieve when consigned to hell.”
— Dhammapada 315 [8]
The Buddha uses the image of a frontier city that guards itself before an attack comes. Those who fail to prepare later grieve.
Both texts teach that guarding in advance prevents later suffering.
Jainism
“He who is careless in all respects, is in danger; he who is not careless in all respects, is free from danger.” … “Faithful according to the commandment (of the Tîrthakaras), wise, and understanding the world according to the commandment--such a man is without danger from anywhere.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.3.4.1, I.3.4.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [9]
The Acaranga teaches that constant vigilance, together with wisdom and a true understanding of the world, leaves a person without danger from any quarter; carelessness is what exposes one to danger.
Valluvar promises that no calamity will shake those who guard in advance against what is coming; the Acaranga likewise says the vigilant and wise man is 'without danger from anywhere.'
Sikhism
“The hot wind does not even touch one who is under the Protection of the Supreme Lord God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 819, Raag Bilaaval, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Raag Bilaaval Guru Arjan says that one who takes refuge in God is not even touched by the scorching wind of affliction.
The Kural says no calamity can make the wise tremble, because they guard against it in advance; Guru Arjan says affliction cannot even touch one sheltered in God.
Greek Mythology
Forewarned by Circe about the Sirens, Odysseus prepares before reaching them. He seals his crew's ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast, so the ship passes unharmed where others have perished.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.37-200 [11]
Odysseus turns foreknowledge into precaution and faces a lethal danger without being destroyed.
This is exactly the Kural's claim: guard against what is ahead, and it will not strike you down.
Philosophy
Seneca urges his friend Lucilius to anticipate every misfortune (exile, loss, disaster) in advance. Nothing should be unexpected, and what has been foreseen strikes more lightly.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 91 (and 24, 76) [12]
The Stoic practice of premeditatio malorum rehearses possible calamities so that, when they come, they do not overwhelm.
This matches the Kural's 'no affliction comes to make them tremble': prepared minds are not shaken.

Real Life Example

Amundsen's carefully prepared journey to the South Pole
1911-1912 · Framheim, Ross Ice Shelf, and the South Pole, Antarctica
Roald Amundsen's Norwegian expedition prepared every detail at its base, Framheim, on the Ross Ice Shelf: before winter it laid about 3,000 kilograms of supplies in depots at 80, 81 and 82 degrees south, and over the winter it lightened its sledges and readied equipment, relying on Greenland dogs and expert skiers. After a first start in September 1911 was abandoned as too early in the season, Amundsen and four companions set out in October, reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911, and returned to Framheim in late January 1912 after about 99 days and some 3,000 kilometres, with all five men safe.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The expedition is a classic example of how meticulous preparation can make one of the most dangerous journeys on earth succeed without loss of life.
The kural says no calamity comes to make the wise tremble when they guard in advance against what lies ahead, and Amundsen's depots, equipment and planning meant the polar journey never caught his party unprepared.
