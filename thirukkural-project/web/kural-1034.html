Kural 1034

Agriculture · Uzhavu · உழவு

பலகுடை நீழலும் தங்குடைக்கீழ்க் காண்பர் அலகுடை நீழ லவர்.
Palakudai neezhalum thangutaikkeezhk kaaNpar alakutai neezha lavar.

Meaning

Farmers whose fields are shaded by ripe grain will see many kings' umbrellas (signs of royal power) come under their own king's umbrella.

Elementary

In old Tamil kingdoms a king's royal umbrella stood for his rule. Valluvar says that a land with plenty of grain becomes so strong and attractive that other lands come under its protection. Good farms make a strong country.

University

The couplet plays on kudai (குடை, the royal parasol, emblem of sovereignty) and neezhal (நீழல், 'shade'). alakudai neezhalavar (அலகுடை நீழலவர்) are those who have the shade of alaku (அலகு, ears of grain), that is, prosperous farmers; Puliyur Kesikan, following Parimelazhagar, glosses them as nel udaiyavar, 'owners of paddy', and also as karunaiyaalar, 'the compassionate', taking neezhal as the cool shade of grace (thannali), a reading Mu. Varadarasanar keeps. Such farmers 'see' the umbrellas of many kings come under their own king's umbrella, because grain surplus makes a realm strong. Solomon Pappaiah takes a softer line: people of other lands come to desire the farmers' ruler. Pope's 'Whose fields are shaded by the waving grain' brings out the pun. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; only word spacing differs.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“They therefore constructed places of refuge … and formed villages and cities … Having thus provided security against the weather, men next began to employ themselves in manual labour, as a means of livelihood, (and cultivated) the seventeen kinds of useful grain”
— Vishnu Purana 1.6 (tr. H.H. Wilson, 1840, sacred-texts.com) [3]
The Vishnu Purana describes organised settlements and grain cultivation arising together as the basis of ordered human society.
Both texts make grain-growing the foundation of a secure, ordered polity, though the Purana does not speak of one kingdom drawing in others.
Christianity
“And all countries came into Egypt to Joseph for to buy corn; because that the famine was so sore in all lands.”
— Genesis 41:57 (KJV) [4]
Because Egypt stored grain in the years of plenty, every land had to turn to Pharaoh's Egypt in the famine.
A land rich in grain draws other nations under its influence, which is the Kural's 'many umbrellas under one'.
Islam
“There was for [the tribe of] Saba' in their dwelling place a sign: two [fields of] gardens on the right and on the left. [They were told], ‘Eat from the provisions of your Lord and be grateful to Him. A good land [have you], and a forgiving Lord.’”
— Qur'an 34:15 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an recalls the kingdom of Sheba, made prosperous by the gardens on either side of its land.
Sheba shows the Kural's link between abundant fields and a flourishing realm.
Judaism
“All the days of Solomon, Judah and Israel from Dan to Beer-sheba dwelt in safety, every family under its own vine and fig tree.”
— 1 Kings 5:5 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition 2023, via Sefaria; 4:25 in Christian numbering) [6]
Solomon's reign is remembered for secure, settled farm life spread across the whole land.
Every family under its own vine within one secure kingdom echoes the Kural's shade of grain and shade of the king's umbrella.
Buddhism
In the Kutadanta Sutta, a king facing banditry is advised to give food and seed-corn to those who farm and keep cattle, capital to traders, and wages to officials. Once the people are prosperous and content, the realm becomes peaceful, so that people can live with open doors.
— Kutadanta Sutta, DN 5 [7]
The Buddha retells this advice to show that a realm made prosperous through its farmers is stronger than one held down by force.
As in the Kural, the farmers' prosperity becomes the strength of the king's rule.
Jainism
Jain universal history tells how Bharata, Rishabhanatha's eldest son, became the first cakravartin of this age and brought all six divisions of Bharatavarsha under one rule; the same tradition credits his father with teaching the arts of cultivation.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 1 (tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
In the Jain account, a realm built on the arts Rishabha taught, farming among them, grows into a universal kingdom under Bharata.
Many umbrellas under one, the Kural's image, matches Bharata's unification, though the Jain story does not credit the farmers for it.
Sikhism
“Dhanna has obtained the Lord, the Sustainer of the World, as his wealth; meeting the humble Saints, he merges in the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 487, Raag Asa, Bhagat Dhanna (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Dhanna, a Jat farmer whose hymns are in the Guru Granth Sahib, plays on his own name (dhan, wealth) to say that the humble cultivator has gained the greatest wealth of all. On the following page (Ang 488) Guru Arjan notes that the Lord met this Jat in person.
The Kural says farmers rich in ripening grain will see many kings' parasols bow under their own king's; Dhanna's verse shows a poor farmer whose true riches outshine any royal treasure.
Greek Mythology
Demeter entrusts the Eleusinian prince Triptolemus with wheat and a chariot drawn by winged dragons, and he flies over the inhabited earth sowing it.
— Apollodorus, Library 1.5.2 [10]
In the myth, the gift of grain spreads outward from one small kingdom to all lands.
Grain from one realm reaches every land, a mythic version of the Kural's farmer whose king's shade covers many others.
Philosophy
“it is from the farming class that the bravest men and the sturdiest soldiers come, their calling is most highly respected, their livelihood is most assured and is looked on with the least hostility”
— Cato the Elder, De Agricultura, Preface 4 (tr. W.D. Hooper and H.B. Ash, Loeb 1934, LacusCurtius) [11]
Cato argues that a farming population gives the state its strongest soldiers and its most loyal citizens.
For Cato, as for the Kural, a strong farming base is what lets a ruler's power stand and spread.

Real Life Example

Li Bing builds the Dujiangyan irrigation system
c. 256 BCE · Dujiangyan, Sichuan, China
Around 256 BCE Li Bing, an official of the state of Qin governing the region of Shu, built the Dujiangyan system, which divides the Min River without a dam and distributes its water across the Chengdu Plain. It turned flood-prone land into fertile farmland, and it still irrigates more than 600,000 hectares today; it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2000.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The farmland it watered became a source of grain for the Qin state, which went on to unify China in 221 BCE, and Sichuan has been known ever since as a land of abundance.
The kural says a realm rich in grain will see other kings' parasols bow beneath its own king's, and the abundance of the Chengdu Plain belonged to the state that later absorbed all its rivals.
