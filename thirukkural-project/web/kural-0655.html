Kural 655

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

எற்றென்று இரங்குவ செய்யற்க செய்வானேல் மற்றன்ன செய்யாமை நன்று.
Etrendru iranguva seyyarka seyvaanel Matranna seyyaamai nandru.

Meaning

Do not do anything that will later make you cry, 'What have I done!' And if you have already done such a thing, it is best not to do it again.

Elementary

If you ever do something that makes you think ‘Oh no, why did I do that?’, the best thing is to learn from it and never do it again. Even better is to stop and think first, so you don’t have to feel that way at all.

University

Eṟṟu eṉṟu is an exclamation of dismay (‘what is this!’), and iraṅkuva are deeds one will afterwards lament. The first line forbids such deeds, and the second deals with the case where one has already done one. Here the commentaries split. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar read maṟṟu aṉṉa ceyyāmai as ‘not doing such things again’, so repentance means not repeating the fault. The older reading, kept in the Drew-Lazarus prose explanation printed with Pope (‘it were good that he grieved not’) and in Puliyur Kesikan, takes aṉṉa ceyyāmai as ‘not doing that (grieving) again’: a minister should not waste himself in useless regret. This reading is usually traced to Parimelazhagar. The text also varies: ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net read செய்வானேல் (‘if he does’), while thirukkural-thamizh.org prints செய்வானேன், probably a typographical variant, since the conditional -ēl is needed for the sense.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu teaches that a person who has done a blameworthy deed, knowingly or not, and wishes to be freed from it, must not commit it a second time, and that the resolve ‘I will not do so again’ purifies him.
— Manusmriti 11.231-232 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
In the Laws of Manu, the heart of expiation is sincere regret together with a firm resolve never to repeat the deed.
This matches the majority reading of the second line: if one has erred, the good course is never to repeat it.
Christianity
“Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more.”
— John 8:11 (KJV) [5]
Jesus sends away the woman taken in adultery without condemning her, but tells her not to repeat the sin.
This is the same two-step logic as the Kural’s second line: once a wrong is done, the good is never to do it again.
Islam
“And those who, when they commit an immorality or wrong themselves [by transgression], remember Allah and seek forgiveness for their sins - and who can forgive sins except Allah? - and [who] do not persist in what they have done while they know.”
— Quran 3:135 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran praises those who, after doing wrong, turn back to God and do not persist in the wrong.
‘Do not persist’ is exactly the Kural’s counsel after a regrettable deed.
Judaism
Maimonides defines complete repentance as the sinner abandoning the sin, removing it from his thoughts and resolving never to do it again, so firmly that God can testify he will not return to it.
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Teshuvah 2:1-2 [7]
In the Laws of Repentance, the proof of teshuvah is facing the same situation and not repeating the sin.
Maimonides spells out the Kural’s second line as a full theory of repentance.
Buddhism
“Should a person commit evil, let him not do it again and again. Let him not find pleasure therein, for painful is the accumulation of evil.”
— Dhammapada 117 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada accepts that people may fall into wrong but insists they must not repeat it or take pleasure in it.
This is almost a word-for-word match for the second line of the Kural in its common reading.
Jainism
“He should not be angry with them nor do them any harm, nor say a single hard word to them, but he should promise not to commit the same sin again; for this is better than to do wrong.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
When a monk is corrected for a fault, the Sutrakritanga tells him to accept it calmly and promise not to commit the same sin again.
The kural says to avoid deeds one will regret and, if one has done one, not to do the like again; the Jain verse makes exactly that promise of non-repetition the right response to a fault.
Sikhism
“Acting in egotism, so very many have died. After making mistakes, they later repent and regret.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 109, Raag Maajh, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak observes that deeds done from haumai (ego) end in regret and repentance afterwards.
The Kural warns against doing anything that will later make one cry out in regret; Guru Nanak traces such regret to deeds done in egotism.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ play, Ajax, maddened by Athena, slaughters cattle believing them to be the Greek chiefs; on waking and seeing what he has done, he is overwhelmed with shame and despair.
— Sophocles, Ajax [11]
The tragedy dwells on the horror of recognising a deed that cannot be undone.
Ajax’s awakening is the ‘what have I done!’ moment that the Kural tells us to avoid by never acting that way to begin with.
Philosophy
Spinoza holds that repentance is not a virtue and does not arise from reason; one who repents of a deed is twice unhappy or weak, first in the deed and then in the sorrow.
— Spinoza, Ethics IV, Proposition 54 [12]
For Spinoza, useless grief over past actions only adds a second weakness to the first.
This supports the older commentarial reading of the second line, that a minister who has erred should not waste himself in grieving.

Real Life Example

Canada apologises to Japanese Canadians and pledges 'never again'
22 September 1988 · Ottawa, Canada
During and after the Second World War, about 22,000 Japanese Canadians were uprooted from their homes, interned or sent to work camps, and had their property confiscated and sold, though not one was charged with disloyalty. On 22 September 1988 Prime Minister Brian Mulroney and Art Miki of the National Association of Japanese Canadians signed the Redress Agreement, and Mulroney apologised in the House of Commons, pledging that the government would ensure, to the limits of its powers, that such injustice would never occur again. The settlement included 21,000 dollars for each survivor and funds to create the Canadian Race Relations Foundation.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The government acknowledged a wrong that its predecessors had committed and created an institution meant to keep such discrimination from happening again.
The kural says to avoid deeds that one will later lament, and if such a deed has been done, not to do the like again, and Canada's apology joined that admission of regret to a formal pledge against repetition.
