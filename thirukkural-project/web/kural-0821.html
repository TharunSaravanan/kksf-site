Kural 821

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

சீரிடம் காணின் எறிதற்குப் பட்டடை நேரா நிரந்தவர் நட்பு.
Seeridam kaanin erithar̲kup pattadai neraa niranthavar natpu.

Meaning

The friendship of people who spend time with you but are not with you in heart is like an anvil, the iron block a blacksmith hammers on. When they get the chance, they will strike you down on it.

Elementary

A blacksmith’s anvil looks like a strong, steady table, but its real job is to hold the metal still so the hammer can hit it. A fake friend can feel like solid support, but they are only holding you in place until it suits them to let you get hurt.

University

The chapter title kooḍaa naṭpu (கூடா நட்பு) means ‘friendship that does not truly join’, and this opening couplet defines it: those who are neraa (நேரா, not inwardly agreeing) yet niranthavar (நிரந்தவர், outwardly close). Paṭṭaḍai (பட்டடை) is the smith’s anvil, the block on which metal is laid to be cut; Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read the simile this way, Pappaiah adding that it looks like support while really serving the cutting. Seer-idam (சீரிடம்) is ‘the fitting place or occasion’, so the danger lies in timing: the false friend waits for an opening. Parimelazhagar places this chapter after Thee Natpu (82, evil friendship) as a treatment of friendship that is enmity in disguise, a theme he reads in a royal-political frame. The couplet is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After the war, the blind king Dhritarashtra asks to embrace Bhima. Krishna, sensing his grief-driven rage, pushes forward an iron statue of Bhima instead, and the old king crushes it to pieces in what was meant to look like a loving embrace.
— Mahabharata, Stri Parva, Section 12 (Ganguli translation) [3]
An embrace, the most intimate sign of affection, is turned into the very means of destruction once the chance appears.
Like Valluvar’s anvil, the embrace looks like support but is the place where the blow is meant to fall.
Christianity
“And from that time he sought opportunity to betray him.”
— Matthew 26:16 (KJV) [4]
Judas remains among the twelve, sharing their life, while secretly waiting for the right moment to hand Jesus over.
This is exactly the Kural’s ‘when they find the fitting moment’: closeness kept up while waiting for the chance to strike.
Islam
“How [can there be a treaty] while, if they gain dominance over you, they do not observe concerning you any pact of kinship or covenant of protection? They satisfy you with their mouths, but their hearts refuse [compliance], and most of them are defiantly disobedient.”
— Qur’an 9:8 (Saheeh International) [5]
The verse describes allies whose words please while their hearts refuse, and who will honour no bond once they gain the upper hand.
Pleasing outwardly, hostile inwardly, and waiting for the advantage: the same anatomy as the Kural’s false friend.
Judaism
“Their tongue is a sharpened arrow, / They use their mouths to deceive. / They speak to their fellows in friendship, / But lay an ambush for them in their hearts.”
— Jeremiah 9:7 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria; KJV 9:8) [6]
Jeremiah describes a society where friendly speech covers a heart that is lying in wait.
Friendly speech outside, an ambush inside: Valluvar’s anvil waiting for its moment.
Buddhism
“These four, young householder, should be understood as foes in the guise of friends: (1) he who appropriates a friend's possessions, (2) he who renders lip-service, (3) he who flatters, (4) he who brings ruin.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha teaches the layman Sigala to recognise four kinds of people who look like friends but act as enemies.
‘Foes in the guise of friends’ is almost a definition of the Kural’s kooḍaa naṭpu.
Jainism
“Avoiding the friendship of those who resemble elephants, seek the friendship of those who resemble dogs; for an elephant will kill his driver whom he has known for a long time, but a dog will wag his tail while the spear thrown at him is still in his body.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 22 ('The choice of friends'), st. 3 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain didactic anthology warns that some companions, like a war elephant, stay docile for years and then turn on the very person who has handled them longest.
Valluvar's anvil, which seems to hold you up until the moment comes to strike, is the same danger as the Nālaṭiyār's elephant that kills the driver it has long known.
Sikhism
“False is friendship with the false and greedy. False is its foundation. O Moollah, no one knows where death shall strike.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Saloks Vaaran Te Vadheek (salok 21), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok added after the vaars, Guru Nanak warns that a bond formed with the false and greedy rests on a false foundation, and sets it beside the unpredictability of death's blow.
The Kural pictures false friendship as an anvil that looks like support but is the base for a blow; Guru Nanak likewise calls such friendship false at its very foundation.
Greek Mythology
“He asked me to his house, feasted me, and then butchered me most miserably as though I were a fat beast in a slaughter house”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 11 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Agamemnon’s ghost tells Odysseus how Aegisthus welcomed him home with a feast, the very act of hospitality becoming the trap.
The feast is the anvil: a show of welcome that is really the place prepared for the blow.
Philosophy
“when he has been deceived by the pretences of the other person, it is just that he should complain against his deceiver; he will complain with more justice than one does against people who counterfeit the currency, inasmuch as the wrongdoing is concerned with something more valuable.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle treats pretended friendship as worse than forging money, because what is counterfeited is more precious.
Both thinkers see feigned friendship as a counterfeit that does its harm precisely because it is trusted.

Real Life Example

Germany breaks the Nazi-Soviet Pact and invades the Soviet Union
23 August 1939 - 22 June 1941 · Moscow and the western Soviet Union
On 23 August 1939 Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union signed a ten-year non-aggression pact in Moscow, negotiated by their foreign ministers Joachim von Ribbentrop and Vyacheslav Molotov, with a secret protocol dividing eastern Europe into spheres of influence. Hitler regarded the pact as a temporary tactical move, and in July 1940 he decided to attack the Soviet Union within the following year. On 22 June 1941 more than three million German soldiers invaded the Soviet Union, ending the pact after less than two years.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The pact gave each side what it wanted for a time, but for Germany it was only a platform from which to prepare the attack it had always intended.
The kural compares the friendship of those who are outwardly close but not one at heart to an anvil, a seeming support that is really the base on which you will be struck when the moment comes, and the pact served Germany in exactly that way.
