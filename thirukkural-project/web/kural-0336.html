Kural 336

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

நெருநல் உளனொருவன் இன்றில்லை என்னும் பெருமை உடைத்துஇவ் வுலகு.
Nerunal ulan oruvan indru illai ennum Perumai udaiththu ivvulagu.

Meaning

This world has a strange kind of greatness: someone who was here yesterday is gone today.

Elementary

Sometimes we hear, ‘I just saw him yesterday, and now he's gone.’ This kural says that is simply how the world works: people can be here one day and gone the next.

University

The couplet is famous for its irony. “Perumai” (greatness, glory) is normally praise, but Valluvar awards it to the world for the most unsettling of distinctions, that a man alive “nerunal” (yesterday) is not there “indru” (today). Commentators such as the thirukkural-thamizh gloss explicitly read perumai here as a mocking word for the world's instability. The unit of time is only a single day, which heightens the sense of suddenness. The world's one reliable ‘achievement’ is the constant disappearance of its inhabitants. The observation echoes the Yaksha's question in the Mahabharata about the wonder of human denial of death, and Marcus Aurelius' remark about yesterday's mucus becoming tomorrow's ashes.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Asked by the Yaksha what is the most wonderful thing in the world, Yudhishthira answers that every day countless beings go to the abode of Yama, yet those who remain act as though they will live forever.
— Mahabharata, Aranyaka (Vana) Parva, Yaksha Prashna (ch. 313 in Ganguli's numbering) [3]
In the Mahabharata, the wisest of the Pandavas names humanity's denial of death, in the face of daily deaths, as the greatest wonder.
The Kural's ‘greatness’ of a world where yesterday's man is gone today is the same ironic wonder Yudhishthira points to.
Christianity
“For all flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass. The grass withereth, and the flower thereof falleth away:”
— 1 Peter 1:24 (KJV) [4]
Peter, quoting Isaiah, says human life and glory wither like grass and flowers.
Peter speaks of the ‘glory of man’ withering; the Kural ironically calls the world's constant loss its ‘glory’.
Islam
“Every soul will taste death, and you will only be given your [full] compensation on the Day of Resurrection. ... And what is the life of this world except the enjoyment of delusion.”
— Quran 3:185 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran states that every living soul will die and that worldly life is a deceptive enjoyment.
Both texts present death as the world's universal and reliable feature, which exposes the world's promise of permanence as a delusion.
Judaism
“As for man, his days are as grass: as a flower of the field, so he flourisheth. For the wind passeth over it, and it is gone; and the place thereof shall know it no more.”
— Psalm 103:15-16 (KJV) [4]
The Psalm says a person flourishes like a wildflower, then a wind passes and they are gone, and even their place forgets them.
‘It is gone, and the place thereof shall know it no more’ is the Kural's ‘here yesterday, not here today’.
Buddhism
“As a mighty flood sweeps away the sleeping village, so death carries away the person of distracted mind who only plucks the flowers (of pleasure).”
— Dhammapada 47 [6]
The Buddha compares death to a sudden flood that sweeps away a sleeping village overnight.
Overnight disappearance is the Kural's image: those present yesterday are swept away by today.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 10.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
Mahāvīra tells Gautama that human life ends as suddenly as a withered leaf drops from its tree.
The Kural's ironic 'greatness' of the world, where one alive yesterday is gone today, matches Mahāvīra's image of lives falling like leaves.
Sikhism
“Whatever has been created shall be destroyed; everyone shall perish, today or tomorrow.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1429, Salok Mahala 9, Salok 52, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Tegh Bahadur states the universal law that all that is born must pass, the only question being whether it is today or tomorrow.
The Kural ironically praises the world's 'greatness', that one who was here yesterday is gone today; Guru Tegh Bahadur states the same sudden passing as a plain law.
Greek Mythology
“Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees. Those of autumn the wind sheds upon the ground, but when spring returns the forest buds forth with fresh ones. Even so is it with the generations of mankind, the new spring up as the old are passing away.”
— Homer, Iliad 6.146-149 (Butler translation) [9]
On the battlefield, Glaucus tells Diomedes that human generations fall and replace one another like leaves.
The world's constant coming and going of people, which the Kural wryly calls its ‘greatness’, is Homer's falling of leaves.
Philosophy
“Always observe how ephemeral and worthless human things are, and what was yesterday a little mucus to-morrow will be a mummy or ashes.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.48 (George Long translation) [10]
Marcus Aurelius reminds himself how quickly human life passes from beginning to end.
Marcus' ‘yesterday ... to-morrow’ matches the Kural's ‘yesterday ... today’, both stressing how short the interval is.

Real Life Example

The eruption of Vesuvius buries Pompeii
79 CE · Pompeii, Campania, Italy
In 79 CE Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the thriving Roman town of Pompeii under a deep layer of volcanic ash and pumice, while nearby Herculaneum disappeared under volcanic flows. Busy towns full of people, shops and homes were lost in a single eruption. The buried ruins were rediscovered centuries later and now give a detailed picture of Roman daily life frozen at one moment.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Pompeii has become the classic image of how suddenly ordinary life can vanish. Its preserved streets show a world that was going about its business one day and gone the next.
The kural's ironic 'greatness' of the world is that one who was here yesterday is gone today, and Pompeii shows a whole town undergoing that change in a single day.
