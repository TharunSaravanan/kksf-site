Kural 734

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

உறுபசியும் ஓவாப் பிணியும் செறுபகையும் சேரா தியல்வது நாடு.
Urupasiyum ovaap piniyum serupakaiyum seraa thiyalvadhu naadu.

Meaning

A true country carries on free from terrible hunger, diseases that never end, and enemies that destroy.

Elementary

A happy home is one where nobody goes to bed hungry, nobody is sick all the time, and nobody is afraid of bullies breaking in. Valluvar says a good country is a whole land like that.

University

The kural names the classic triad of public calamity: urupasi (severe hunger, famine), ovaap pini (disease that does not cease, that is, epidemic) and serupakai (enemies who destroy). Parimelazhagar notes that the third means enemies who come from outside and attack, since internal enmity is treated separately in the next kural. The verb iyalvadhu (that which goes on, continues) suggests a lasting condition rather than a single lucky year. The same trio of famine, pestilence and war recurs in Sanskrit, biblical and Greek accounts of disaster, which makes this one of the most widely shared ideas in the chapter. The sandhi text reads ‘உறுபசியு மோவாப் பிணியுஞ் செறுபகையுஞ்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the close of the Yuddha Kanda, Valmiki describes Rama’s reign: no widows mourned, there was no fear of wild beasts or disease, no robbers, the rains came in season, trees were always in fruit, and people lived long lives free from sickness.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda, Sarga 128 (Rama’s reign) [3]
This description of Rama Rajya became the classic Indian picture of a perfect realm.
Rama Rajya is a land with no hunger, no sickness and no danger, the three absences the Kural makes the test of a country.
Christianity
“Nation shall rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom: And great earthquakes shall be in divers places, and famines, and pestilences.”
— Luke 21:10-11 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns his disciples of the calamities to come: war, earthquake, famine and plague.
Luke lists the very evils the Kural’s good country is free from: enemies, famine and pestilence.
Islam
“Let them worship the Lord of this House, Who has fed them, [saving them] from hunger and made them safe, [saving them] from fear.”
— Qur’an 106:3-4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an reminds the Quraysh of Mecca that God protected their city from hunger and from fear.
Freedom from hunger and from the fear of enemies are two of the Kural’s three marks of a good land.
Judaism
“I will grant your rains in their season, so that the earth shall yield its produce and the trees of the field their fruit ... you shall eat your fill of bread and dwell securely in your land. I will grant peace in the land ... and no sword shall cross your land.”
— Leviticus 26:4-6 (Revised JPS 2023, Sefaria) [6]
God promises Israel that if they keep the covenant, their land will have rain, full harvests, security and peace from invasion.
‘Eat your fill’ and ‘no sword shall cross your land’ are the Torah’s version of a land free from hunger and enemies.
Buddhism
The commentarial tradition says the Ratana Sutta was first taught when the city of Vesali was struck by drought and famine, then by disease, then by harmful spirits drawn by the dead. The Buddha came, the sutta was recited around the city, and the three dangers passed.
— Sutta Nipata 2.1, Ratana Sutta, with its commentary (Piyadassi’s introduction) [7]
The Ratana Sutta is still chanted as a protection because of this story of a city freed from famine, plague and fear.
Vesali’s three afflictions closely mirror the Kural’s triad of hunger, disease and hostile forces.
Jainism
“Bhadrabahu, the last Srutakevali, predicted at Ujjain a twelve years drought and famine in the north, where upon the Jaina community migrated to the south under his leadership.”
— Jain Heritage Centres, 'Chandragiri' (Shravanabelagola), jainheritagecentres.com, summarising the Bhadrabahu tradition and Shravanabelagola inscriptions [8]
Jain tradition, supported by Shravanabelagola inscriptions, holds that a twelve-year famine made the north unlivable for the monastic community, which moved south with Bhadrabahu (traditionally joined by Chandragupta Maurya).
The Kural says a true country is untouched by crushing hunger; the Bhadrabahu story shows from the Jain side how famine strips a land of its worth, so that even the religious community has to leave it.
Sikhism
As Guru Arjan moved from village to village he helped people sink wells and undertake other works of public welfare, especially to relieve the hardship of the famine then gripping the Punjab; a baoli (step-well) he built still stands in Lahore, and at Tarn Taran lepers were treated by the Guru with loving care.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entry Arjan Dev, Guru [9]
The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism records the fifth Guru's response to famine and disease as practical seva: water works, relief and care for the sick.
The Kural names crushing hunger and endless disease among the evils a true land must be free of; Guru Arjan's wells, famine relief and leper care were Sikh work against exactly those evils.
Greek Mythology
“Peace, the nurse of children, is abroad in their land, and all-seeing Zeus never decrees cruel war against them. Neither famine nor disaster ever haunt men who do true justice.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 225-230 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod describes the city whose people judge fairly: it is spared war, famine and disaster, and the earth feeds it richly.
Hesiod’s just city is free of war, famine and disaster, which is almost the Kural’s list of hunger, disease and enemies.
Philosophy
Kautilya devotes a chapter to ‘remedies against national calamities’, naming fire, floods, pestilence, famine, rats, wild beasts, snakes and demons, and prescribing what the state must do against each.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 4.3 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [11]
For Kautilya, protecting the land from these disasters is a basic duty of government.
Kautilya’s list includes the Kural’s famine and pestilence, and treats their absence as a matter of good statecraft.

Real Life Example

War, crowding and plague strike Athens
430 to 429 BCE · Athens and Attica, Greece
Early in the Peloponnesian War, Pericles allowed the people of the Attic countryside to take refuge inside the walls of Athens while the Spartans raided the land, and the city became severely overcrowded. In 430 BCE a plague broke out in the crowded city and over the following years killed a large part of its population, including both of Pericles' legitimate sons. When the Spartans again invaded and laid waste to the fields in 429 BCE, crops could not be planted, and Pericles himself died of the plague that autumn.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Athens, the richest city in Greece, was brought low when a destructive enemy, hunger and an unrelenting epidemic struck together.
The kural says a true country is one untouched by crushing hunger, endless disease and destructive enemies, and Athens in 430 to 429 BCE shows what befalls a land when all three arrive at once.
