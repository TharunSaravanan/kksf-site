Kural 603

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

மடிமடிக் கொண்டொழுகும் பேதை பிறந்த குடிமடியும் தன்னினும் முந்து.
Madimadik kondozhugum pedhai pirandha Kudimadiyum thanninum mundhu.

Meaning

When a foolish person holds on to laziness, which destroys everything, the family they were born into is ruined even before they die.

Elementary

Imagine a boy who never helps and never learns, and lets his family’s shop fall apart. The sad part is that the shop can close long before he grows old. His laziness hurts his family first.

University

Valluvar makes மடி ring three times: மடி (sloth) … மடி … மடியும் (perishes). The phrase மடிமடிக் கொண்டு is read in two ways. Varadarasanar and Parimelazhagar take the second மடி as an adjective, ‘sloth that destroys’ (அழிக்கும் இயல்புடைய சோம்பல்). Others hear மடி in its everyday sense of ‘lap’, ‘holding sloth in his lap’, cherishing it; Pappaiah’s ‘the sloth that ought to be cast off’ is close to this. பேதை, ‘fool’, is a strong word in the Kural (see ch. 84, பேதைமை): the fault is presented as a failure of understanding, not just of energy. The paradox that the குடி dies ‘before himself’ (தன்னினும் முந்து) means that the family’s standing, its reputation and wealth, can die while its lazy member is still alive to see it. Both source texts agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi forms குடிமடியுந் தன்னினு முந்து.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That happiness which covers the nature of the self from beginning to end, and which is derived from sleep, indolence, and negligence, is said to be in the mode of ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.39 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna classifies the pleasure people get from sleep, laziness and carelessness as tamasic: it deludes a person from start to finish.
The Kural’s ‘fool’ (பேதை) who holds on to sloth is exactly the person deluded by tamasic pleasure.
Christianity
“For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat.”
— 2 Thessalonians 3:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul sets out a blunt rule for the Thessalonian community: whoever refuses to work has no claim on others’ support.
Paul, like Valluvar, treats the person who clings to idleness as someone who has cut himself off from the life of the household that feeds him.
Islam
“There are two blessings that many people are deceived into losing: health and free time.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6412 [5]
The Prophet warns that people commonly squander health and leisure, not realising what they have until it is gone.
The deceived squanderer of free time is the Kural’s பேதை, who wastes his chances until his family’s fortune is gone.
Judaism
“I passed by the field of a lazy man, / By the vineyard of someone lacking sense. / It was all overgrown with thorns; / Its surface was covered with chickweed, / And its stone fence lay in ruins.”
— Proverbs 24:30-31 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The sage describes the estate of a lazy man ‘lacking sense’, now overgrown and with its walls fallen, as a lesson in how sloth ruins what was inherited.
‘Lazy’ and ‘lacking sense’ appear together here, as in the Kural’s மடி and பேதை, and the ruined estate stands for the family that dies before its owner.
Buddhism
“The idler who does not exert himself when he should, who though young and strong is full of sloth, with a mind full of vain thoughts — such an indolent man does not find the path to wisdom.”
— Dhammapada 280 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes a man who has youth and strength but wastes them in sloth and idle thinking, and so never reaches wisdom.
Both texts pair sloth with folly: the idler never finds wisdom, and the Kural’s பேதை loses his family.
Jainism
“You cannot prolong your life, therefore be not careless; you are past help when old age approaches. Consider this: what (protection) will careless people get, who kill living beings and do not exert themselves?”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The fourth lecture warns that the careless, who will not exert themselves, have no protection when their end comes.
The Kural's fool who clings to destructive sloth and perishes is the Jain 'careless' person who does not exert himself and finds no protection.
Sikhism
“O my foolish and silly soul, why are you too lazy to serve?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1095, Raag Maaroo (Maaroo ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan calls the lazy soul foolish for neglecting service (seva) to the Lord.
The Kural calls the man who clings to laziness a fool whose family perishes even before he does; Guru Arjan likewise links laziness with folly, though the loss he warns of is spiritual.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the Ant and the Grasshopper, the grasshopper sings away the summer while the ant stores grain, and when winter comes the grasshopper starves.
— Aesop, The Ants and the Grasshopper (trans. G. F. Townsend) [10]
The idler enjoys the present and ignores the season for work, then finds himself with nothing when need arrives.
Like the Kural’s fool, the grasshopper pays for his idleness before he expects to.
Philosophy
In Book VIII of the Republic, Socrates describes the idle spendthrift as a ‘drone’ in a household, a plague to the house just as a drone is to the hive, who uses up its wealth while contributing nothing.
— Plato, Republic VIII, 552c (trans. B. Jowett) [11]
Plato uses the drone to picture the member of a family or city who consumes without working and so brings ruin on the whole.
Plato’s drone that ruins the house matches the Kural’s idle fool whose family perishes before he does.

Real Life Example

Ahmad Shah, the last Qajar shah, is deposed while living in Europe
1923-1925 · Tehran, Iran, and France
Ahmad Shah, who had come to the throne of Iran as a boy in 1909, left for Europe in 1923 on a journey of indefinite length and never returned. While he was absent, Iran's national assembly, the Majles, formally deposed him in October 1925, ending the Qajar dynasty, and the crown was conferred on Reza Khan. Ahmad Shah settled permanently in France and died about four years later.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A ruling house that had governed Iran since the late eighteenth century came to an end while its last ruler, absent from his country, was still a young man.
The kural says the family of one who clings to idleness dies before he does, and the Qajar dynasty ended while its absent shah lived on in exile.
