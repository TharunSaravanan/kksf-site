Kural 1187

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

புல்லிக் கிடந்தேன் புடைபெயர்ந்தேன் அவ்வளவில் அள்ளிக்கொள் வற்றே பசப்பு.
pullik kidandhen pudaipeyarndhaen avvalavil allikkol vatre pasappu

Meaning

I lay held close in his arms. I turned away just a little, and in that instant the paleness rushed over me as if scooping me up.

Elementary

You know how a snowman starts melting the second the sun comes out? The wife says she was cuddled up safe and happy, turned over for just a moment, and in that tiny moment the sad pale colour grabbed her, like a big scoop.

University

The couplet heightens the previous one by shrinking the interval to almost nothing: pullik kiṭantēṉ (புல்லிக் கிடந்தேன், ‘I lay embracing’), puṭai peyarntēṉ (புடைபெயர்ந்தேன், ‘I shifted to one side’), avvaḷavil (‘in just that much’), the pallor aḷḷik koḷvaṟṟē (அள்ளிக்கொள்வற்றே, ‘was like something scooped up in handfuls’, i.e. it came in a rush). Solomon Pappaiah frames it as a recollection of an earlier occasion, making the point a fortiori for the present long absence, while Kalaignar and Puliyur Kesikan keep it as a vivid present. Pope: ‘I lay in his embrace, I turned unwittingly; / Forthwith this hue, as you might grasp it, came on me.’ The treatment is tasteful and hyperbolic, the conventional rhetoric of the heroine’s extreme sensitivity. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata’s story of Nala and Damayanti, Nala, ruined and wandering in the forest with his wife, leaves her while she sleeps; Damayanti wakes to find him gone and searches the forest crying out for him, wasting with grief.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase) [3]
The Nalopakhyana gives the classic Indian scene of a wife who was beside her husband one moment and abandoned the next.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Damayanti passes from closeness to desolation in the space of a single turning in sleep.
Christianity
“But the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken away from them, and then shall they fast in those days.”
— Mark 2:20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says the joy of the bridegroom’s presence gives way to fasting and mourning as soon as he is taken away.
The Kural shows the same switch in miniature: closeness, a moment’s parting, and grief at once.
Islam
“They are a clothing for you and you are a clothing for them.”
— Qur’an 2:187 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes husband and wife as each other’s garment, giving closeness, covering and protection.
In the Kural, the instant that ‘garment’ of closeness slips away, the wife is left exposed to pallor.
Judaism
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth; I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Songs 3:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The bride reaches for her beloved in the night and finds he is not there.
The Kural’s heroine also turns in the night and finds the closeness gone, with grief rushing in.
Buddhism
“Don’t ever — regardless — be conjoined with what’s dear or undear. It’s painful not to see what’s dear or to see what’s not.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha teaches that being bound to a dear one makes even a moment of not seeing them painful.
The Kural’s heroine shows how sharp that pain can be: a moment of not holding her husband is enough.
Jainism
“Pleasures bring only a moment’s happiness, but suffering for a very long time, intense suffering, but slight happiness; they are an obstacle to the liberation from existence, and are a very mine of evils.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The sons of Bhrigu tell their father that the happiness of pleasure is brief while the suffering it brings is long.
The Kural shows that imbalance in a single night: a moment’s closeness, and at the slightest parting a flood of pallor.
Sikhism
“This night, I did not sleep with my Husband Lord, and now my body is suffering in pain.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 30) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are in the Guru Granth Sahib, says that being apart from the Husband Lord for just one night leaves the body aching.
The Kural's wife says that she merely turned aside in his arms and the pallor swept over her at once; Farid likewise feels a single night without the Beloved at once in the body.
Greek Mythology
Psyche, forbidden to look on her unseen husband, raises a lamp over him as he sleeps; a drop of hot oil wakes Cupid, who at once flies away, leaving her alone and grieving.
— Apuleius, Metamorphoses 5.22-24 (Cupid and Psyche) (paraphrase) [10]
In Apuleius’s tale a single small movement in the bridal chamber ends the lovers’ closeness in an instant.
The Kural’s heroine also turns for a moment and finds closeness gone and grief already upon her.
Philosophy
In Aristophanes’ speech, humans are halves of an original whole who, once divided, long for each other, throw their arms around each other, and pine away when apart, wishing never to be separated even for a moment.
— Plato, Symposium 191a-192e (paraphrase) [11]
Plato’s Aristophanes explains love as the desire of two halves to be whole, so that any separation is felt as a wound.
The Kural’s heroine feels exactly that: the slightest parting from her other half brings immediate suffering.

Real Life Example

Ida Straus stays with her husband on the Titanic
15 April 1912 · North Atlantic Ocean
Isidor Straus, co-owner of Macy's department store, and his wife Ida, aged 63, were passengers on the RMS Titanic. Isidor declined a place in a lifeboat under the rule of women and children first, and Ida, who had nearly entered a lifeboat, turned back to stay with him despite friends urging her to go. Both were lost in the sinking.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ida Straus's choice became one of the most remembered acts of devotion of the disaster.
The kural's wife says that even turning aside a little from her husband's embrace let the pallor rush over her, and Ida Straus refused to be parted from her husband even for the safety of a lifeboat.
