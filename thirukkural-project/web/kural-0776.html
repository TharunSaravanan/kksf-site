Kural 776

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

விழுப்புண் படாதநாள் எல்லாம் வழுக்கினுள் வைக்கும்தன் நாளை எடுத்து.
Vizhuppun padaadhanaal ellaam vazhukkinul Vaikkumdhan naalai eduththu.

Meaning

A hero counts his days and calls every day wasted on which he did not get a wound in an honorable fight.

Elementary

Some athletes feel a day without practice is a day wasted. The warrior here feels that way about days when he did not stand up bravely for his people. The real lesson is about not letting days slip by without doing something that matters.

University

Vizhuppun is a technical term of puram ethics: a wound of honour, received on the chest or face while facing the enemy, as opposed to a wound in the back (a sign of flight). The warrior “takes up” (eduththu) his days and places (vaikkum) every day without such a wound among vazhukku, lapses or wasted days. Solomon Pappaiah explicitly specifies wounds on face and chest, following the traditional reading. The idea echoes Purananuru poems in which a mother checks that her son’s wounds are on his chest. Modern readers may take the hyperbole as a vivid figure for a life measured by service and risk rather than comfort.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In a Purananuru poem attributed to Kakkaipadiniyar Naccellaiyar, an aged mother, told her son fled the battle, vows to cut off the breast that fed him; searching the field, she finds he fell facing the enemy and rejoices more than at his birth.
— Purananuru 278 (paraphrase; text at Project Madurai) [3]
Sangam heroic poetry treats a wound received facing the foe as the mark of honour for a warrior and his family.
This is exactly the Kural’s vizhuppun, the wound that makes a day count; note this is Tamil puram literature rather than Hindu scripture.
Christianity
“From henceforth let no man trouble me: for I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus.”
— Galatians 6:17 (KJV) [4]
Paul points to the scars of his beatings and hardships as proof of his faithful service.
Like the Kural’s warrior, Paul treats wounds borne in service as badges of honour, though his are suffered, not inflicted in battle.
Islam
“Fighting has been enjoined upon you while it is hateful to you. But perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you.”
— Qur’an 2:216 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an frankly acknowledges that fighting is naturally disliked, even when it is a duty.
Unlike the Kural’s warrior who longs for wounds, the Qur’an treats combat as a burden accepted, not desired, an honest divergence.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines the gibbor, the mighty hero, as the one who masters himself, citing Proverbs 16:32.
The Kural measures a day by its battle wounds; the rabbis measure might by self-mastery, a meaningful divergence.
Buddhism
“The wise show no elation or depression when touched by happiness or sorrow.”
— Dhammapada 83 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada counsels equanimity toward whatever each day brings.
Where the warrior grades his days by wounds, the Buddhist ideal is to stop grading days by such marks at all, a gentle divergence.
Jainism
“Acquire perfect knowledge of the Law! why do you not study it? It is difficult to obtain instruction in it after this life. The days (that are gone by) will never return, nor is it easy a second time to obtain human birth.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.2.1.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Rishabha warns his sons that each day not used for the Law is lost for good, because human birth is rare.
The Kural's hero counts every day without an honourable wound as wasted, and the Jain teaching likewise reckons each day by whether it was spent in hard spiritual effort.
Sikhism
“You fill your belly, and sleep like an animal; you have wasted and lost this human life.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir rebukes a life given to eating and sleeping, saying it squanders the human birth meant for remembering God.
The Kural's hero counts as wasted every day without an honourable wound; Kabir likewise counts as wasted a life spent in comfort rather than in its true purpose.
Greek Mythology
When the disguised Odysseus returns home, his old nurse Eurycleia recognises him by the scar on his leg from a boar-hunt of his youth.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 19 (paraphrase) [10]
In the Odyssey a scar earned in a test of courage becomes the sign of the hero’s true identity.
For the Kural’s warrior too, honourable wounds are what mark him as who he is.
Philosophy
Nietzsche’s maxim holds that what does not kill a person makes them stronger.
— Friedrich Nietzsche, Twilight of the Idols, “Maxims and Arrows” 8 (paraphrase) [11]
The saying treats hardship survived as a source of growth.
The Kural’s warrior counts days without trial as lost, sharing Nietzsche’s view that struggle is formative.

Real Life Example

Adrian Carton de Wiart returns to the front again and again
1914-1918 · Somaliland and the Western Front (France and Belgium)
Adrian Carton de Wiart, a British Army officer, lost an eye in Somaliland early in the First World War and later lost his left hand on the Western Front. He kept returning to active service and was wounded many times in that war, by one count eight times. In July 1916 he was awarded the Victoria Cross for his leadership of the 8th Battalion, Gloucestershire Regiment, at La Boisselle during the Battle of the Somme.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rather than treating each wound as a reason to step back, he treated it as an interruption before returning to the fighting.
The kural's hero counts as wasted every day without an honourable wound, and Carton de Wiart's repeated return to the front shows a man who measured his days by service under fire.
