Kural 190

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

ஏதிலார் குற்றம்போல் தங்குற்றங் காண்கிற்பின் தீதுண்டோ மன்னும் உயிர்க்கு.
Ethilaar kutrampol thankutrang kaankirpin, Theedhundo mannum uyirkku.

Meaning

If people could see their own faults the way they see the faults of others, could any harm ever come to anyone?

Elementary

It’s easy to notice when a friend makes a mistake, but hard to notice our own. If everyone checked their own mistakes as carefully as they check others’, there would be much less gossip — and much less trouble for everyone.

University

Valluvar closes the chapter by moving from prohibition to cure. The comparison ētilār kuṟṟampōl (“like the faults of strangers”) captures the ease and even pleasure with which we see others’ failings; if that same clear sight were turned on taṉ kuṟṟam, one’s own faults, the rhetorical question asks, would any harm (tītu) remain for mannum uyir — beings who endure, or, in some commentaries, the soul that persists? The logic is psychological: backbiting feeds on an asymmetry of attention, and self-examination removes both the motive and the moral standing for it. Mu. Karunanidhi’s gloss draws out the practical point — the habit of slander disappears and life becomes peaceful. The ending thus turns the chapter’s condemnation of the slanderer into a discipline for the reader, echoing a strikingly wide range of traditions that contrast the tiny flaw in others with the large one in oneself.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Thou seest, O king, the fault of others, even though they be as small as a mustard seed. But seeing, thou noticest not thy own faults even though they be as large as the Vilwa fruit.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Sambhava Parva §74 (K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Shakuntala rebukes King Dushyanta, who slanders her while blind to his own wrong in denying her.
Nearly the Kural’s exact image: others’ tiny faults seen, one’s own huge faults ignored.
Christianity
“And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?”
— Matthew 7:3 (KJV) [4]
Jesus mocks the person who picks at a speck in someone else’s eye while ignoring a plank in his own.
The same lesson as the Kural: look at your own faults as sharply as you look at others’.
Islam
“One of you looks at the mote in his brother's eye while forgetting the stump in his own eye.”
— Al-Adab Al-Mufrad 592 (saying of Abu Hurayra) [5]
Abu Hurayra warns against seeing a speck in a brother’s eye while forgetting the stump in one’s own.
Same imbalance of sight that the Kural asks us to correct.
Judaism
“I would be surprised if there is anyone in this generation who can receive rebuke. Why? Because if the one rebuking says to him: Remove the splinter from between your eyes, … the other says to him: Remove the beam from between your eyes”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 16b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon laments that people tell a critic to deal with his own bigger faults first.
Both texts note how differently we see small faults in others and large ones in ourselves.
Buddhism
“Easily seen is the fault of others, but one's own fault is difficult to see. Like chaff one winnows another's faults, but hides one's own, even as a crafty fowler hides behind sham branches.”
— Dhammapada 252 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says we winnow others’ faults like chaff in the wind while hiding our own.
An almost exact statement of the Kural’s point — and the winnowing image matches the Tamil tūṟṟu of kural 188.
Jainism
“The opposites of those mentioned in the previous sūtra and humility (namravṛtti) and modesty (anutseka) cause the influx (āsrava) of karmas that lead to high-status (uccagotra).”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.26, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi, given with the sūtra, explains the opposites as censuring oneself, praising others, proclaiming the real qualities of others and not claiming qualities one lacks.
Valluvar asks what harm could come if people saw their own faults as clearly as others' faults, and the Tattvārtha Sūtra praises exactly that reversal: blaming oneself instead of others.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, I am the worst of all. Everyone else is good.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1364, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir, whose saloks are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, models a humility that looks first at one's own faults and sees others as good.
The Kural asks whether any harm could come to living beings if people saw their own faults as clearly as others'; Kabir turns that clear sight wholly on himself.
Greek Mythology
“Hence it is that men are quick to see the faults of others, and yet are often blind to their own failings.”
— Aesop, ‘The Two Bags’ (Townsend) [10]
In Aesop’s fable, every person carries two bags: a small one in front holding the neighbours’ faults and a large one behind holding their own.
The fable explains the very blindness that the Kural asks us to overcome.
Philosophy
“We have other men's vices before our eyes, and our own behind our backs.”
— Seneca, Of Anger (De Ira), Book II, ch. 28 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
Seneca says anger at other people grows from this lopsided vision, and gives the example of a father, worse than his son, who scolds the son for the very extravagance he once indulged in himself.
Seneca names the same blindness as the Kural, and like Valluvar treats it as the root of the harm people do to one another.

Real Life Example

Willy Brandt kneels at the Warsaw Ghetto memorial
7 December 1970 · Warsaw, Poland
On 7 December 1970, the day West Germany signed the Treaty of Warsaw with Poland, Chancellor Willy Brandt laid a wreath at the memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising and then, unplanned, knelt in silence before it. Brandt, who had himself opposed the Nazis from exile, later said he had wanted to ask forgiveness in the name of the German people. The Nobel Committee cited the gesture when it awarded him the 1971 Nobel Peace Prize.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Instead of dwelling on others' wrongs, a German leader publicly acknowledged his own nation's guilt, and the gesture became a lasting symbol of reconciliation.
The kural asks whether any harm could remain if people saw their own faults as clearly as others', and Brandt's gesture shows how facing one's own faults can open the way to healing.
