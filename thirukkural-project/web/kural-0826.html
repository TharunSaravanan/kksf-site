Kural 826

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

நட்டார்போல் நல்லவை சொல்லினும் ஒட்டார்சொல் ஒல்லை உணரப் படும்.
Nattaarpol nallavai sollinum ottaarsol ollai unarap padum.

Meaning

Even when enemies say good things as if they were friends, people soon see what their words really mean.

Elementary

A wolf can try to sound like a sheep, but sooner or later it growls. When someone who doesn’t like you starts talking sweetly, listen carefully; the truth usually shows up quickly.

University

Naṭṭaar (நட்டார்) are friends, those who have made friendship, and oṭṭaar (ஒட்டார்) are those who do not ‘stick’ or join with us, that is, foes. Nallavai sollinum (நல்லவை சொல்லினும்) concedes that the words themselves may be good, and ollai (ஒல்லை) means ‘quickly’. The couplet is partly reassurance: false friendship tends to betray itself in speech, and Kalaignar says the meanness in such words will come out without fail. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read the ‘understanding’ as recognising quickly that the words are not truly meant for our good. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the golden deer (the demon Maricha in disguise) is shot by Rama and cries out for help in Rama’s voice, Sita is frightened, but Lakshmana at once recognises the call as a demon’s trick and insists that no one could harm Rama.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda, Sargas 44-45 [3]
The enemy’s imitation of a beloved voice is seen through by the alert listener.
The Kural’s point that hostile words dressed as friendly ones are quickly understood by the discerning.
Christianity
“Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles?”
— Matthew 7:16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that what people really are becomes evident from what they produce.
Both texts trust that hidden hostility reveals itself.
Islam
“And if We willed, We could show them to you, and you would know them by their mark; but you will surely know them by the tone of [their] speech.”
— Qur’an 47:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says the hypocrites will be recognised by the very way they speak.
The Kural likewise says the foe’s words, however friendly, quickly give him away.
Judaism
“His hatred may be concealed by dissimulation, / But his evil will be exposed to public view.”
— Proverbs 26:26 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs promises that disguised hatred will come into the open.
Same confidence as the Kural that false friendliness is soon unmasked.
Buddhism
“It's through dealing with a person that his purity may be known, and then only after a long period, not a short period; by one who is attentive, not by one who is inattentive; by one who is discerning, not by one who is not discerning.”
— Thana Sutta, AN 4.192 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha teaches that integrity is revealed through dealings, to an attentive and discerning observer.
Both say a false friend’s nature can be read; the Buddha adds that it takes time and attention.
Jainism
“To join oneself to many, and strive many days and examine dispositions, and take (for friends) those who are worthy, is proper. Even with a deadly serpent, to associate and afterwards to part from it will be painful.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 22 ('The choice of friends'), st. 10 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār advises testing people's natures over many days before taking them as friends, because it is painful to part even from a bad companion once a bond has formed.
Valluvar is confident that a foe's friendly words are soon seen through; the Nālaṭiyār sets out the practical discipline behind this, a long examination of character before any friendship is accepted.
Sikhism
“Kabir! I have seen a person who is as shiny as washed wax. He seems very clever and very virtuous, but in reality, he is without understanding, and corrupt.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 139) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks, Kabir describes seeing through a person who looks polished, clever and virtuous but is corrupt underneath.
The Kural says the good words of foes posing as friends are quickly seen for what they are; Kabir likewise sees through the shiny surface to what lies beneath.
Greek Mythology
In the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, the infant Hermes steals Apollo’s cattle and then denies it with innocent, sweet-sounding speeches, but Apollo sees through the lies at once and drags him before Zeus.
— Homeric Hymn to Hermes [10]
Charming, innocent words do not hide the truth from a discerning listener.
As the Kural says, the real meaning behind fair words is soon understood.
Philosophy
“But if we only take reasonable care, it is as easy to separate and distinguish a genuine from a specious friend as anything else that is coloured and artificial from what is sincere and genuine.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 25 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Cicero holds that, with reasonable care, a false friend is as easy to detect as anything counterfeit.
Cicero and Valluvar share the confidence that the specious friend is readily seen through.

Real Life Example

Menelik II rejects Italy's reading of the Treaty of Wuchale
1889-1896 · Ethiopia
In 1889 Emperor Menelik II of Ethiopia signed a treaty of friendship with Italy, known as the Treaty of Wuchale (Wichale). Article 17 in the Amharic text allowed Ethiopia to use Italy's good offices in dealing with other powers, while the Italian text made this obligatory, and Italy used its version to proclaim a protectorate over Ethiopia. Menelik rejected the claim in 1890, denounced the whole treaty in 1893, and after his victory at Adwa in 1896 Italy accepted the treaty's annulment and recognised Ethiopia's independence.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A treaty presented as friendship concealed a claim to control, and Menelik saw through it and refused to accept it.
The kural says that even when enemies speak good words as if they were friends, their real nature is soon seen, and the friendly treaty's hidden meaning was quickly recognised and repudiated by Menelik.
