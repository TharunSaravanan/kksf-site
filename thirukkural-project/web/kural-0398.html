Kural 398

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

ஒருமைக்கண் தான்கற்ற கல்வி ஒருவற்கு எழுமையும் ஏமாப் புடைத்து.
Orumaikkan thaankatra kalvi oruvarku Ezhumaiyum emaap pudaiththu.

Meaning

The learning a person gains in one life can protect and help them for seven lives to come.

Elementary

Planting a tree helps you, your children and your grandchildren long after you have gone. Valluvar says learning is even bigger than that: what you learn now keeps helping you far into the future, even into future lives.

University

This kural draws on the Indian framework of rebirth. ஒருமை (orumai, 'one birth') is set against எழுமை (ezhumai, 'seven births'), a conventional expression for a long chain of future lives. The key word ஏமாப்பு (emaappu) means protection, security or support, so learning is pictured as a lasting safeguard that goes with the soul. Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah follow the traditional rebirth reading. Kalaignar reinterprets it more secularly as learning that protects seven generations of a family. The kural fits the chapter's larger claim that learning, unlike other goods, is not used up or lost. Similar ideas appear in the Gita's teaching that a yogi's progress carries over into the next life and in Plato's theory that learning is recollection from earlier existence.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“On taking such a birth, he revives the divine consciousness of his previous life, and he again tries to make further progress in order to achieve complete success, O son of Kuru.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.43 (Bhaktivedanta, As It Is) [3]
Krishna assures Arjuna that spiritual progress and understanding are never lost at death. They carry into the next birth and continue from there.
A near-exact match to the Kural: what is gained in one life protects and advances a person in later lives.
Christianity
“And they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament; and they that turn many to righteousness as the stars for ever and ever.”
— Daniel 12:3 (KJV) [4]
Daniel's vision promises that the wise will shine for ever, so that their wisdom has an eternal result beyond this life.
Like the Kural, it says the benefit of wisdom reaches past one lifetime. The framework is resurrection and eternity rather than rebirth.
Islam
“When a man dies, his acts come to an end, but three, recurring charity, or knowledge (by which people) benefit, or a pious child, who prays for him (for the deceased).”
— Sahih Muslim 1631 [5]
The Prophet teaches that beneficial knowledge is one of only three things that keep earning reward for a person after death.
Like the Kural, it treats learning as one of the few goods whose benefit outlasts a single lifetime.
Judaism
“For when a man passes away there accompany him neither gold nor silver, nor precious stones nor pearls, but Torah and good deeds alone.”
— Pirkei Avot 6:9 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Yose ben Kisma refuses a fortune to live away from a place of learning, explaining that only Torah and good deeds go with a person beyond death.
A very close match: learning is the possession that goes with a person past the end of this life.
Buddhism
In the Buddhavamsa, the ascetic Sumedha resolves at the feet of the Buddha Dipankara to become a Buddha, then spends countless lives perfecting the paramis, including the perfection of wisdom (pañña). What he builds in each life carries forward to the next.
— Buddhavamsa 2 (story of Sumedha and Dipankara) [7]
Buddhism teaches that cultivated wisdom and virtue are carried from life to life until they reach full Buddhahood.
A strong conceptual match: learning and wisdom gained in one birth protect and lift a person through many later births.
Jainism
“By possession of knowledge he acquires an understanding of words and their meaning; thereby he will not perish in the forest of the fourfold Saṃsâra; as a needle with its thread will not be lost, thus the soul possessing the sacred lore will not be lost in the Saṃsâra.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 29.59 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira's teaching plays on sutta, meaning both 'thread' and 'scripture'. A needle with a thread is not lost, and a soul that holds sacred knowledge is not lost in the cycle of rebirths.
Valluvar says learning gained in one birth protects a person through seven births to come. The Jain image of the threaded needle makes the same promise: knowledge keeps the soul safe across the round of rebirth.
Sikhism
“Where there is no mother, father, children, friends or siblings - O my mind, there, only the Naam, the Name of the Lord, shall be with you as your help and support.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 264, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib (Ashtpadi 2), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Guru Arjan says that where no family or friend can follow, beyond this life, only the Name goes with a person as their support.
The Kural says learning gained in one life protects a person through seven lives to come; Guru Arjan names the Naam as the one possession that goes with the soul beyond this life and keeps protecting it.
Greek Mythology
In the Myth of Er, souls choose their next lives. Plato's Socrates says the soul that has practised philosophy chooses wisely, while unphilosophical souls pick disastrous lives out of ignorance.
— Plato, Republic X, 614b–621d (Myth of Er) [10]
Wisdom gained in one life shapes the quality of the next life a soul chooses.
Like the Kural, learning in one life gives protection across future lives.
Philosophy
In the Meno, Socrates argues that the soul is immortal and has been born many times, so that what we call 'learning' is really recollection (anamnesis) of knowledge gained before.
— Plato, Meno 81b–86b [11]
For Plato, knowledge is carried across lives. The soul brings it into each new birth, and inquiry draws it out again.
A close conceptual parallel: learning is not confined to one lifetime but runs through many.

Real Life Example

Avicenna's Canon of Medicine teaches doctors for six centuries
early 11th century to c. 1650 · Persia (now Iran), and universities across Europe
The Persian physician and philosopher Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, compiled al-Qanun fi al-tibb, the Canon of Medicine, in the early 11th century. Translated into Latin in the 12th century, it became a standard medical textbook in European universities and was still used at Montpellier, France, as late as about 1650. It also remained central to medicine in the Islamic world.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The learning gathered in one lifetime went on guiding and protecting physicians and patients for many generations after its author's death.
The kural says the learning of one life protects through seven more, and Ibn Sina's Canon kept serving long after him, much as Kalaignar reads it of later generations.
