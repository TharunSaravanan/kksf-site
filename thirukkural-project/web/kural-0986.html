Kural 986

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

சால்பிற்குக் கட்டளை யாதெனின் தோல்வி துலையல்லார் கண்ணும் கொளல்.
Saalbirkuk kattalai yaadhenin tholvi thulaiyallaar kannum kolal.

Meaning

If you ask what the test of perfect character is, it is being willing to accept defeat even from people who are not your equals.

Elementary

It is easy to say 'You win' to a big champion. It is much harder to say it to a younger child who beat you at a game. Valluvar says being able to lose gracefully, even to someone smaller, is the real test of a good person.

University

Kattalai (கட்டளை) here is the touchstone (உரைகல்) used to test gold, as Varadarasanar and Pappaiah gloss it: saalbu is gold, and its test is tholvi ... kolal (தோல்வி ... கொளல், 'accepting defeat'). Thulai allaar (துலையல்லார்), literally 'those not of the same balance (thulai, துலை)', are one's inferiors; the particle -um in kannum (கண்ணும், 'even with') makes the point that yielding to a superior is no test at all. Kesikan spells out the contrast: the defeat one would accept from one's betters must be accepted even from lesser people. Pope's verse ('What is perfection's test? The equal mind. / To bear repulse from even meaner men resigned') introduces 'equal mind' as a gloss rather than a translation. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads thulaiyallaar as those 'unfit to accept defeat', a less common construal. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Do thou fight for the sake of fighting, without considering happiness or distress, loss or gain, victory or defeat – and by so doing you shall never incur sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.38 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna asks Arjuna to act with an even mind toward victory and defeat alike.
The Kural's touchstone similarly asks for a mind that can accept defeat without wounded pride.
Christianity
“Now therefore there is utterly a fault among you, because ye go to law one with another. Why do ye not rather take wrong? why do ye not rather suffer yourselves to be defrauded?”
— 1 Corinthians 6:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul rebukes Christians who sue one another and asks why they do not simply accept being wronged.
Like the Kural, it treats willingness to lose to another as a mark of mature character.
Islam
“I guarantee a house in the surroundings of Paradise for a man who avoids quarrelling even if he were in the right, a house in the middle of Paradise for a man who avoids lying even if he were joking, and a house in the upper part of Paradise for a man who made his character good.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4800 (graded hasan by al-Albani; Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet promises reward to those who give up an argument even when they are right.
This mirrors the Kural's test of perfection: being willing to yield even when one could win.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that those who are insulted but do not insult in return, who hear themselves shamed and do not answer, who act out of love and rejoice in suffering, are the ones of whom Scripture says, 'May those who love Him be like the sun rising in its might.'
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 88b (citing Judges 5:31) [6]
The sages praise the person who absorbs humiliation without retaliating, comparing them to the rising sun.
Like the Kural, it sees the willingness to take a loss without hitting back as the mark of the truly great.
Buddhism
“Victory begets enmity; the defeated dwell in pain. Happily the peaceful live, discarding both victory and defeat.”
— Dhammapada 201 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that winning breeds resentment and losing breeds pain, and that the peaceful let go of both.
This supports the Kural's view that a noble person does not need to win and can accept defeat calmly.
Jainism
“If a layman abuses a monk, he should not grow angry against him; because he would be like a child, a monk should not grow angry. If a monk hears bad words, cruel and rankling ones, he should silently overlook them, and not take them to heart.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.24-25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira lists abuse (akrosha) among the hardships a monk must bear calmly, without anger or retaliation.
Like the Kural's touchstone, it asks one to accept humiliation even from those who are spiritually beneath one, without answering back.
Sikhism
“If you are wise, be simple; if you are powerful, be weak; and when there is nothing to share, then share with others. How rare is one who is known as such a devotee.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, salok 128 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
These saloks ask the strong to choose weakness and the wise to choose simplicity, giving up the advantage they could press. A neighbouring salok of Farid (salok 7, Ang 1378) asks the devotee not to strike back but to kiss the feet of those who strike.
The Kural makes the willingness to accept defeat even from those beneath one the touchstone of perfection, and Farid's counsel that the powerful should be weak describes that same chosen yielding.
Greek Mythology
In the Gorgias, Socrates shocks his hearers by maintaining that it is better to suffer wrong than to do wrong, and that he would choose to be wronged rather than wrong another.
— Plato, Gorgias 469b-c (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Socrates holds that losing to injustice harms only one's fortune, while committing injustice harms one's soul.
This gives a philosophical basis for the Kural's view that accepting defeat is a sign of a well-formed character.
Philosophy
“it is grievous, when a candidate for public offices, to be defeated by one's inferiors; therefore let him be defeated by Vatinius … That all men may know that these things, which I have deemed Cato worthy to undergo, are not real evils.”
— Seneca, Of Providence (De Providentia) 3.14 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
Seneca has Nature explain why she put Cato through every hardship, including losing an election to the inferior Vatinius: to show that such defeats are no true evil to a good man.
Cato's calm acceptance of defeat by a lesser man is just the touchstone of perfection the Kural names.

Real Life Example

Eugenio Monti lends his bolt to his British rivals
January-February 1964 · Innsbruck, Austria
During the two-man bobsleigh at the 1964 Winter Olympics in Innsbruck, the British pair Tony Nash and Robin Dixon found that a bolt on their sled had broken, and Italy's Eugenio Monti, already a multiple world champion, lent them one from his own sled. Nash and Dixon won the gold medal and Monti finished third; days later he and his mechanics also helped repair the Canadian four-man sled, and Canada won that event while Monti again took bronze. The IOC gave Monti its first Pierre de Coubertin award for fair play.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Monti, the established master of his sport, accepted being beaten by less decorated rivals rather than profit from their bad luck.
The kural makes the willingness to accept defeat, even at the hands of those who are not one's equals, the touchstone of perfect character, and Monti chose a fair defeat over an unearned victory.
