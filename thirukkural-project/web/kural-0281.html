Kural 281

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

எள்ளாமை வேண்டுவான் என்பான் எனைத்தொன்றும் கள்ளாமை காக்கதன் நெஞ்சு.
Ellaamai venduvaan enbaan enaiththondrum, Kallaamai kaakkathan nenju.

Meaning

If you never want to be looked down on, guard your heart so it never even thinks of stealing anything from anyone, however small.

Elementary

Nobody wants to be laughed at or called a cheat. The way to make sure that never happens is to keep a guard at the door of your own mind, so that even the idea of sneaking away something that isn’t yours — even a pencil — never gets in.

University

The chapter opens by locating fraud (kaḷavu) not in the hand but in the heart (neñcu): the verb is kākka, “let him guard,” and its object is the mind itself. Valluvar’s motive clause is social and reputational — eḷḷāmai, “not being scorned” — which fits the householder-and-ascetic audience of the Tuṟavaṟaviyal section, where public trust is the currency of a moral life. The word eṉaittoṉṟum (“anything whatsoever”) forecloses the familiar rationalisation that small frauds do not count, anticipating the Aristotelian claim that theft admits no virtuous mean. Commentators from Parimēlaḻakar to Mu. Varadarasan read kaḷḷāmai as covering not only burglary but every covert appropriation, including short-changing and deceit in dealings.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Do not covet the wealth of any man!”
— Isha Upanishad 1 (tr. F. Max Müller, SBE vol. 1) [5]
The very first verse of the Isha Upanishad tells the seeker to see everything as belonging to the Lord and, on that basis, never to hanker after anyone else’s property.
Both texts place the prohibition at the level of desire and extend it to any wealth at all, not just large thefts.
Christianity
“He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much: and he that is unjust in the least is unjust also in much.”
— Luke 16:10 (KJV) [6]
Jesus teaches that honesty is tested in trivial matters; a person who cheats in small things reveals a character that will cheat in large ones.
Matches the Kural’s “anything whatsoever” (eṉaittoṉṟum): no fraud is too small to matter.
Islam
“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it, And whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it.”
— Qur’an 99:7-8 (Sahih International) [7]
Surat az-Zalzalah teaches that on the Day of Judgement even the tiniest deed, good or bad, will be brought to light.
Like the Kural, it denies that any wrong is too small to count, and ties wrongdoing to eventual exposure.
Judaism
“Let the property of your fellow be as precious unto you as your own”
— Pirkei Avot 2:12 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [8]
Rabbi Yose’s maxim in the Mishnah asks a person to value a neighbour’s possessions exactly as he values his own, which rules out any scheme to take them.
Both locate honesty in an inner disposition toward others’ property, cultivated before any act.
Buddhism
“He who in this world takes nothing that is not given to him, be it long or short, small or big, good or bad — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 409 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Buddha defines the truly noble person as one who never takes what is not freely given, regardless of its size or value.
The Dhammapada’s “small or big” is the same sweep as the Kural’s eṉaittoṉṟum — honesty admits no exceptions.
Jainism
“I renounce all taking of anything not given, either in a village or a town or a wood, either of little or much, of small or great, of living or lifeless things.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.15, the third great vow (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884, pp. 205-206) [10]
This is the opening of the third great vow (asteya) that every Jain monk takes. It renounces taking anything not given, whatever its size or value.
Valluvar tells the man who wants to avoid contempt to guard his heart against taking “anything at all” by fraud. The Jain vow likewise allows no exception for small things: “of little or much, of small or great.”
Sikhism
“To take what rightfully belongs to another, is like a Muslim eating pork, or a Hindu eating beef.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh (Vaar Maajh, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In a salok of the Vaar in Raag Maajh, Guru Nanak takes what each community holds most forbidden and says that taking another's due is just as forbidden. Sikh teaching sets this beside kirat karni, earning by honest work.
The Kural tells anyone who wants to avoid contempt to guard against taking anything of another's by stealth. Guru Nanak's comparison makes such taking the deepest kind of defilement.
Greek Mythology
Ovid tells how the old herdsman Battus saw Mercury driving off Apollo’s stolen cattle and was bribed with a cow to keep silent. Mercury returned in disguise, offered a cow and a bull for information, and Battus betrayed the secret at once; Mercury turned him into a stone, a lasting emblem of the informer.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.676-707 [12]
A small, greedy gain made through complicity in theft and double-dealing leaves Battus a permanent object of scorn.
Illustrates the Kural’s warning that even a petty fraudulent gain leads to lasting disgrace.
Philosophy
“in the case of actions adultery, theft, murder; for all of these and suchlike things imply by their names that they are themselves bad”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6 (tr. W. D. Ross) [13]
Aristotle, having defined virtue as a mean, stops to insist that some acts, theft among them, have no right amount; any instance of them is simply wrong.
Supplies the philosophical logic behind “anything whatsoever”: there is no acceptable quantity of fraud.

Real Life Example

Rosie Ruiz is stripped of the Boston Marathon title
21-29 April 1980 · Boston, Massachusetts, United States
Rosie Ruiz crossed the finish line first among women at the 1980 Boston Marathon on 21 April. Runners and officials quickly grew suspicious because no checkpoint had recorded her and race photographs showed her only near the end, and witnesses said they saw her join the course close to the finish. On 29 April officials stripped her of the title and named Canada's Jacqueline Gareau the winner; Ruiz always maintained that she had run the whole race.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
One shortcut turned a moment of glory into lasting public ridicule, and 'pulling a Rosie' became slang for cheating one's way to success.
The kural warns that anyone who wishes never to be scorned must guard the heart against taking anything by stealth, and Ruiz's finish became a byword for exactly that scorn.
