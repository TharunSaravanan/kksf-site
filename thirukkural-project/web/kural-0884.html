Kural 884

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

மனமாணா உட்பகை தோன்றின் இனமாணா ஏதம் பலவும் தரும்.
Manamaanaa utpakai thondrin inamaanaa edham palavum tharum.

Meaning

If hidden hostility grows from a mind that is not good, it brings many troubles and turns a person's own people against them.

Elementary

If a person keeps a secret grudge and refuses to let it go, it slowly spoils everything around them. Soon brothers, sisters and friends are arguing and taking sides, and the whole group stops getting along.

University

The verse balances two negated compounds: mana-maanaa (மனமாணா, 'mind not made excellent', i.e. unreformed or unreconciled) and ina-maanaa (இனமாணா, 'kin not made excellent', i.e. kinsfolk who fall out of harmony). When utpakai arises in the first, it yields edham palavum (ஏதம் பலவும், many faults or evils) of the second kind. Mu. Varadarasanar reads the result as many faults that keep one's kin from being set right, while Kalaignar says such a disposition makes one's own people into enemies. Pope's 'secret enmities ... that minds pervert' and the thirukkural.net rendering 'myriad miseries manifest among kin' both preserve the link between inner hostility and outer estrangement. No textual variant is attested between ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For him who has conquered the mind, the mind is the best of friends; but for one who has failed to do so, his very mind will be the greatest enemy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.6 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that an unmastered mind becomes one's own worst enemy.
The Kural traces the troubles of utpakai back to an unreformed mind, just as the Gita locates the enemy in the uncontrolled mind.
Christianity
“From whence come wars and fightings among you? come they not hence, even of your lusts that war in your members?”
— James 4:1 (KJV) [4]
James traces quarrels within the community back to disordered desires inside each person.
Like the Kural, it sees outward discord among one's own people as the fruit of inner hostility.
Islam
The Prophet Muhammad told believers not to hate one another, envy one another or turn their backs on one another, but to be servants of God as brothers.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6065 [5]
The hadith treats hidden hatred and envy as the forces that break a community apart.
Both texts warn that concealed rancour destroys the bonds among one's own people.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him.”
— Leviticus 19:17 (KJV) [4]
The Torah forbids nursing silent hatred and requires open, honest correction instead.
The Kural's 'unreformed' hidden enmity is exactly the hatred in the heart that Leviticus forbids.
Buddhism
“‘He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.’ Those who harbor such thoughts do not still their hatred.”
— Dhammapada 3 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada observes that nursing grievances keeps hatred alive.
This is the 'unreformed mind' of the Kural, from which hidden enmity and its many troubles keep arising.
Jainism
“Anger destroys love, pride destroys modesty, deceit destroys friendship; greed is destructive of everything.”
— Saman Suttam 135 (English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [7]
The Saman Suttam, gathering canonical Jain verses, names how each of the four passions (kaṣāyas) unravels a particular bond: anger kills affection and deceit kills friendship.
The Kural says that when hidden enmity takes root in an unreformed mind it turns one's own people against one, and the Jain verse shows how the untamed passions of such a mind destroy love and friendship.
Sikhism
“No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes the mind reformed in the company of the holy, from which enmity has gone so that no one, kin or stranger, seems hostile.
Where the Kural says hidden enmity in an unreformed mind turns one's own people against one, Gurbani shows the reformed mind in which that enmity cannot take root.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod lists the children of Eris (Strife): Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine, Sorrows, Fightings, Battles, Murders, Quarrels, Lies, Disputes, Lawlessness and Ruin.
— Hesiod, Theogony 226-232 [9]
In Greek myth a single strife gives birth to a whole brood of evils.
The Kural likewise says that one hidden enmity yields 'many evils'.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius reminds himself each morning that he will meet difficult people, but that all are made for cooperation, like hands, feet and eyelids, so that acting against one another is contrary to nature.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1 [10]
The Stoic emperor treats resentment toward one's fellows as a self-inflicted distortion of the mind.
Like the Kural, he links an unreformed, resentful mind to the breakdown of a community meant to work as one body.

Real Life Example

The sons of Louis the Pious rise against their father
830-834 · Frankish Empire (near Colmar and at Compiegne and Soissons, present-day France)
Louis the Pious, son of Charlemagne, faced a revolt by his older sons in 830 after he sought to provide a kingdom for Charles, his son by a second marriage. In 833 his sons Lothar, Pippin and Louis met him near Colmar at the place later called the Field of Lies, where his own followers deserted him; he was deposed and made to perform public penance. He was restored in 834 when the younger sons turned against Lothar, but the quarrels continued, and after his death the empire was divided among his sons by the Treaty of Verdun in 843.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The family quarrel at the top of the Carolingian empire turned father against sons and brother against brother, and ended in the division of Charlemagne's realm.
The kural says hidden enmity in an unreconciled mind brings troubles that turn one's own people against one, as it did within Louis the Pious's family.
