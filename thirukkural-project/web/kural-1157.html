Kural 1157

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

துறைவன் துறந்தமை தூற்றாகொல் முன்கை இறைஇறவா நின்ற வளை.
Thuraivan thurandhamai thootraakol munkai iraiiravaa nindra valai.

Meaning

My bangles are slipping down my thin arm past the wrist. Surely they will tell the whole town that the lord of the seashore has left me.

Elementary

When you are very sad, people can often tell even if you say nothing: your face looks different and you don’t feel like eating. In this couplet the wife has grown so thin with sadness that her bangles slide down her arm, and everyone who sees them knows her husband has gone away.

University

Tuṟaivaṉ (துறைவன்) is ‘lord of the shore’, the hero of the neytal (seashore) landscape, and tuṟantamai (துறந்தமை) ‘his having left’; tūṟṟākol (தூற்றாகொல்) asks ‘will they not proclaim (it abroad)?’, from tūṟṟu, ‘to spread gossip, publicise’. Muṉkai iṟai iṟavā niṉṟa vaḷai (முன்கை இறை இறவா நின்ற வளை) describes bangles ‘passing beyond the wrist-joint of the forearm’ because her arm has wasted, the standard Sangam sign of a woman pining in separation. Most commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan, Kalaignar) read the bangles as announcing his departure to the town; Solomon Pappaiah reads them as telling the heroine herself that he plans to leave, and Pope’s ‘announced before’ follows that anticipatory sense. Pope: ‘The bracelet slipping from my wrist announced before / Departure of the Prince that rules the ocean shore.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the opening of Kalidasa’s Meghaduta, the exiled yaksha, separated from his wife, has grown so thin that his golden bracelet has slipped from his forearm.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Purvamegha 2 (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa uses the slipping bracelet as the visible mark of a lover wasting away in separation.
It is the same sign as the Kural’s slipping bangles, a body that publicly announces the absence of the beloved.
Christianity
“I am weary with my groaning; all the night make I my bed to swim; I water my couch with my tears.”
— Psalm 6:6 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes a sorrow so great that it shows in the body, in exhaustion and endless tears.
As with the Kural’s bangles, grief that is not spoken still becomes visible in the body.
Islam
“And he turned away from them and said, ‘Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,’ and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.”
— Qur’an 12:84 (Sahih International) [5]
Jacob tries to hold his grief for Joseph inside, but it shows in his body as his eyes whiten with sorrow.
Like the Kural’s bangles, Jacob’s eyes show to everyone a grief of separation he did not proclaim himself.
Judaism
When Michal is taken from her husband Paltiel to be returned to David, Paltiel follows her, weeping as he goes, all the way to Bahurim, until he is ordered to go back.
— 2 Samuel 3:15-16 (paraphrase) [6]
The book of Samuel records a husband’s grief so open that he weeps along the road in front of everyone.
Paltiel’s public tears, like the Kural’s slipping bangles, announce a separation to everyone who sees.
Buddhism
Patacara, having lost her husband, children and parents in a single disaster, wanders in grief so deep that her garment slips from her body without her noticing, and people call her Patacara because of it, until she meets the Buddha.
— Dhammapada Commentary on verse 113, story of Patacara (paraphrase) [7]
The Dhammapada commentary tells how her grief became visible to everyone before she found peace in the Dhamma.
As with the Kural’s bangles, her outward state announces a loss she cannot hide.
Jainism
“Firm and decided she cut off her tresses which were black like bees and dressed with a brush and comb.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Forsaken by Arishtanemi, Rajimati gives up her beauty and cuts off her carefully dressed hair as she enters the order.
Her shorn hair, like the Kural’s slipping bangles, is an outward sign everyone can see that her bridegroom has gone.
Sikhism
“O black bird, what qualities have made you black? "I have been burnt by separation from my Beloved.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 794, Raag Soohee, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid asks the black koel why it is black, and the bird answers that separation from its Beloved has scorched it, a visible mark of inner longing.
In the Kural the bangles slipping from the heroine's wasted wrist proclaim to the town that her husband has left her; in Farid's hymn the bird's blackened body likewise displays the separation it suffers.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Hippolytus, the chorus and the nurse notice that Queen Phaedra has kept to her bed and refused food for days, wasting away, and they try to discover the hidden love that is the cause.
— Euripides, Hippolytus, opening scenes (paraphrase) [10]
Phaedra’s body gives away a love she is determined to keep secret.
As with the Kural’s slipping bangles, a lover’s wasting body makes public what she has not said.
Philosophy
Robert Burton lists the bodily symptoms of love-melancholy, including leanness, paleness, loss of appetite and sleeplessness, by which, he says, lovers are easily recognised by others.
— Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy, Part 3, Section 2 (symptoms of love-melancholy) (paraphrase) [11]
The Anatomy of Melancholy treats unhappy love as a condition with visible signs.
The Kural’s slipping bangles are one such sign: the lover’s thinness gives her away to the whole town.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria wears mourning black for the rest of her life
From December 1861 · United Kingdom
After her husband, Prince Albert, died in 1861 at the age of 42, Queen Victoria sank into depression and wore black for the rest of her reign. She kept to mourning far longer than the customary two years for a widow, and until the late 1860s she rarely appeared in public. Her visible grief was so well known that she became famous as a woman in black, and she was criticised for her long seclusion.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Victoria's clothing and withdrawal announced her loss to the whole nation every day for forty years, whether she spoke of it or not.
The kural says that the bangles slipping from a wasted arm will announce to the whole town that her lord has left her, and Victoria's unending black did the same for the loss of Albert.
