Kural 695

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

எப்பொருளும் ஓரார் தொடரார்மற் றப்பொருளை விட்டக்கால் கேட்க மறை.
Epporulum oraar thodaraarmatru apporulai Vittakkaal ketka marai.

Meaning

When the king is talking in secret, do not try to listen in or ask him about it. Hear the secret only if he chooses to tell you.

Elementary

When your parents are talking quietly in the kitchen, it is tempting to sneak up and listen, or to keep asking ‘What were you talking about?’ The polite and trustworthy thing is to wait. If they want you to know, they will tell you.

University

The couplet sets out three stages. Oraar means not listening intently or eavesdropping (oru = to attend closely). Thodaraar means not following up with questions. Vittakkaal ketka means listening only once the king himself lets the matter out. The final word, marai (secret, hidden counsel), is held back to the end, which gives the line a sense of withheld information. Parimelazhagar sets the scene as a king in secret counsel with others, and the logic follows from 693: curiosity about royal secrets is exactly what breeds suspicion. The joined text in older editions reads “எப்பொருளு மோரார் தொடரார்மற் றப்பொருளை / விட்டக்காற் கேட்க மறை.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“No one should keep contact with royal secrets.”
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva, Section 4 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Dhaumya tells the Pandavas that, living at Virata’s court, they must keep clear of the king’s secrets and later never disclose them.
Dhaumya and Valluvar both make keeping away from royal secrets a basic rule of survival at court.
Christianity
“It is not for you to know the times or the seasons, which the Father hath put in his own power.”
— Acts 1:7 (KJV) [4]
When the disciples press the risen Jesus about when the kingdom will be restored, he tells them that some things are kept by the Father and are not theirs to demand.
As in the Kural, the one who holds authority also decides what to reveal, and the follower should not pry.
Islam
“O you who have believed, avoid much [negative] assumption. Indeed, some assumption is sin. And do not spy or backbite each other.”
— Quran 49:12 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran forbids spying (tajassus), prying into matters others have not chosen to share.
The Kural’s ban on overhearing and interrogating is a court-specific form of this general rule against spying.
Judaism
“It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter.”
— Proverbs 25:2 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says that concealing is God’s prerogative and investigating belongs to the king, so knowledge and secrecy are part of sovereign authority.
The Kural treats the king’s marai (secret) as his to keep or reveal, and Proverbs likewise puts the right to conceal and search with the sovereign.
Buddhism
“When the king finds out his moral qualities, his wisdom and his purity of heart, then he learns to trust him and hides not his secrets from him.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 141 [6]
Vidhura teaches that a courtier comes to share the king’s secrets only when the king chooses to share them, after his character has been proved.
This matches the Kural’s final clause: the secret should reach you because the king lets it go, not because you took it.
Jainism
“…if he is always humble, steady, free from deceit and curiosity; if he abuses nobody…”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.10-13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Among the fifteen marks of a well-behaved monk the sutra lists being free from curiosity, alongside humility and steadiness.
The Kural forbids prying into the king's private talk or pressing him with questions; the Jain list counts freedom from curiosity as a mark of good discipline.
Sikhism
“His limits cannot be perceived. What is the Mystery of His Mind?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Jap (Japji Sahib, Pauri 24), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Japji Guru Nanak says the purposes in the mind of the divine King cannot be pried into or exhausted by human effort.
The Kural tells the courtier not to strain after the king's secret counsel but to hear it only if the king reveals it; Guru Nanak treats the Lord's counsel as a mystery beyond human probing, known only as He reveals it.
Greek Mythology
The hunter Actaeon strays into the goddess Diana’s hidden grotto and sees her bathing. For seeing what was not meant for him, he is turned into a stag and torn apart by his own hounds.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.138-252 (Actaeon) [9]
Ovid tells the story as a warning about the terrible cost of intruding on what the powerful keep hidden.
The Kural warns against overhearing a sovereign’s secret, and Actaeon is the mythic example of the price of seeing what is reserved.
Philosophy
“‘Which one of my possessions may I share with you?’ ‘Anything, Sire,’ said Philippides, ‘except your secrets.’”
— Plutarch, On Being a Busybody (De curiositate) 4, Moralia 517 (Loeb trans. W. C. Helmbold) [10]
In his essay on curiosity, Plutarch praises the comic poet Philippides, who asked King Lysimachus for any gift except his secrets. Plutarch adds that people who pry into the affairs of the more powerful destroy themselves.
Philippides is the ideal courtier of this Kural: he positively does not want to know the king’s secrets.

Real Life Example

Senator Truman stops probing the secret Manhattan Project
1943-1945 · Washington, D.C., United States
As chairman of a Senate committee reviewing defense spending, Harry Truman noticed large sums flowing into an unexplained wartime project and asked Secretary of War Henry Stimson about it. Stimson told him the matter was secret for reasons of national security, and Truman dropped the inquiry. He learned the details of the atomic bomb project only after becoming president on 12 April 1945, receiving a full briefing from Stimson on 25 April.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A vigorous investigator accepted that some state secrets were not his to pry into, and waited until the secret was properly entrusted to him.
The kural tells the servant not to eavesdrop or press with questions when the ruler deals in secrets, but to hear them only when they are freely told, which is exactly how Truman came to know of the bomb.
