Kural 362

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

வேண்டுங்கால் வேண்டும் பிறவாமை மற்றது வேண்டாமை வேண்ட வரும்.
Vendungaal vendum piravaamai matradhu Vendaamai venda varum.

Meaning

If you must want something, want to be free from being born again. That freedom comes only when you want to be free of wanting.

Elementary

Imagine you get just one wish. The smartest wish would be: “I wish I didn’t need any more wishes.” Valluvar says that is the one wish worth making, because it is the wish that sets you free.

University

The couplet repeats the verb vendu (“to want”) four times: vendungaal vendum … vendaamai venda. The verbal knot mirrors the paradox it describes. Valluvar admits that a person cannot simply switch off wanting, so he turns the power of desire against itself and makes piravaamai (non-birth, liberation) its one proper object. Parimelazhagar reads vendaamai as the settled state of desirelessness, so the means (wanting not to want) and the goal (not being born again) are joined. The same ‘desire for desirelessness’ paradox appears in the Buddhist distinction between wholesome aspiration (chanda) and craving (tanha), and in the Gita’s call to act without craving the fruits of action.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“No man can be made happy by wealth. Shall we possess wealth, when we see thee? Shall we live, as long as thou rulest? Only that boon (which I have chosen) is to be chosen by me.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.1.27 (trans. Max Müller, SBE 15) [3]
Death offers the boy Nachiketas wealth, long life, kingdoms and pleasures. He refuses them all and insists on the one thing worth wanting: knowledge of what lies beyond death.
Nachiketas acts out the Kural’s advice. If you must want something, want the one thing that frees you from death and birth.
Christianity
“Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth.”
— Colossians 3:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to point their desires toward heavenly things rather than earthly ones.
Both redirect desire instead of simply crushing it: if you must want, want the highest thing.
Islam
“But you prefer the worldly life, While the Hereafter is better and more enduring.”
— Qur’an 87:16-17 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an criticizes people for chasing the passing world instead of the lasting life to come.
Both urge that desire be spent only on what is final and lasting, not on what passes.
Judaism
“Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee.”
— Psalm 73:25 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist says God is the only thing he truly desires, in heaven or on earth.
Both narrow all wanting down to a single highest object. For the psalmist that object is God, and for Valluvar it is freedom from birth.
Buddhism
“Beset by craving, people run about like an entrapped hare. Therefore, one who yearns to be passion-free should destroy his own craving.”
— Dhammapada 343 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada speaks of a person who yearns to be free of passion and who reaches that goal by destroying craving.
This is the same paradox: a yearning for passionlessness is the one legitimate desire, and it works by ending desire.
Jainism
“Sir, what does the soul obtain by the longing for liberation? By the longing for liberation the soul obtains an intense desire of the Law; by an intense desire of the Law he quickly arrives at an (increased) longing for liberation; he destroys anger, pride, deceit, and greed, which reproduce themselves infinitely.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 29.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Saṃvega, the longing for liberation, is the first item in Mahāvīra's lecture on exertion in righteousness. It is the one desire Jainism encourages, because it feeds on itself and destroys the passions, greed included, that otherwise keep reproducing themselves.
Valluvar says that if one must desire, one should desire freedom from birth, which comes through wanting to be free of wanting. Saṃvega is exactly that kind of redirected desire: a longing that wears away greed and leads toward the end of rebirth.
Sikhism
“In the midst of hope, the Guru teaches us to live above hope and desire.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 154, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak says the Guru teaches a person to live among hopes and wants while remaining free of them.
The Kural turns desire against itself, asking us to desire freedom from desire; Guru Nanak's niraas, being free of hope in the midst of hope, is the same paradox lived out.
Greek Mythology
As a young man, Heracles meets two women at a crossroads. Vice promises him an easy road of every pleasure. Virtue promises hard work that leads to true and lasting good, and Heracles chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus’ ‘Choice of Heracles’, in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 (paraphrase) [9]
The fable shows a hero turning down a life of gratified desires in favor of one higher goal.
Like the Kural, it is about choosing what to want. The wise choice gives up the many pleasures for the one lasting good.
Philosophy
“But, for the present, totally suppress desire: for, if you desire any of the things which are not in your own control, you must necessarily be disappointed; and of those which are, and which it would be laudable to desire, nothing is yet in your possession.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 2 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus tells the beginner to set desire aside entirely. Wanting external things brings disappointment, and he is not yet able to want the right things properly.
Both treat suspending desire as the doorway to freedom. The only thing worth pursuing is the state that no longer craves.

Real Life Example

Tolstoy leaves Yasnaya Polyana
October-November 1910 · Yasnaya Polyana and Astapovo, Russian Empire (now Russia)
In his later decades Leo Tolstoy preached a life of simplicity and renunciation, while still living on his family estate at Yasnaya Polyana. On the night of 27-28 October 1910 (Old Style), aged 82, he secretly left the estate with his doctor, leaving a letter saying he was going away for good. He fell ill on the train and died of pneumonia at the small railway station of Astapovo on 7 November 1910 (20 November in the modern calendar).
— Sources: [11] [12]
At the end of his life Tolstoy chose to abandon home and comfort to live out the detachment he had long written about, even though he did not live to carry it through.
The kural says that if one must desire anything it should be freedom itself, the desire to be free of desire, and Tolstoy's final flight was the act of a man whose one remaining wish was to leave his worldly life behind.
