Kural 607

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

இடிபுரிந்து எள்ளுஞ்சொல் கேட்பர் மடிபுரிந்து மாண்ட உஞற்றி லவர்.
Idipurindhu ellunjol ketpar madipurindhu Maanda unjatri lavar.

Meaning

People who give in to laziness and make no real effort will end up hearing harsh scolding and mocking words.

Elementary

When you promise to help clean up and then just lie on the couch, first your friends say, ‘Come on, help!’ If you still don’t, they start to laugh at you or stop counting on you. Laziness turns friends’ nudges into teasing.

University

இடிபுரிந்து எள்ளும் சொல் combines two kinds of speech: இடி, sharp rebuke (the verb இடித்தல் is used elsewhere in the Kural for a true friend’s correction, e.g. ch. 45, 448), and எள்ளல், scornful mockery. Pappaiah and Parimelazhagar read them as a sequence: friends first rebuke the idle man, and when that fails, contempt follows. மடிபுரிந்து, ‘having embraced sloth by choice’, stresses that the laziness is willed, and the second line repeats the மாண்ட உஞற்றிலவர் of 604, so this couplet adds a social consequence (loss of respect) to the material one described there. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads இடிபுரிந்து as ‘slipping and falling’, a minority interpretation. Both sources agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi form இடிபுரிந் தெள்ளுஞ்சொற் கேட்பர்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Udyoga Parva, the Kshatriya mother Vidulā finds her son Sañjaya lying dejected and inactive after a defeat, and scolds him at length, calling him a coward and urging him to rise, fight and restore his house.
— Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva 133-136 (Vidulā-putra-anuśāsana), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Vidulā’s rebuke of her son is a classic Indian example of a close relative using harsh words to shake someone out of inertia.
The idle son hearing sharp rebukes from someone close to him is exactly the இடி (rebuke) of the Kural, delivered before scorn from outsiders follows.
Christianity
“And if any man obey not our word by this epistle, note that man, and have no company with him, that he may be ashamed. Yet count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.”
— 2 Thessalonians 3:14-15 (KJV) [4]
Speaking of members who ‘walk disorderly, working not at all’, Paul tells the church to shame the idle and admonish them as brothers.
Brotherly admonition followed by public shame is the two-stage sequence the commentators find in இடிபுரிந்து எள்ளுஞ்சொல்.
Islam
“They were satisfied to be with those who stay behind, and their hearts were sealed over, so they do not understand.”
— Quran 9:87 (Sahih International) [5]
Surat at-Tawbah reproaches the able-bodied who chose to stay at home instead of going out on the Tabuk expedition.
The Quran’s reproach of those who chose ease matches the Kural’s ‘words of rebuke’ directed at those who embrace sloth.
Judaism
“How long wilt thou sleep, O sluggard? when wilt thou arise out of thy sleep? Yet a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep: So shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth, and thy want as an armed man.”
— Proverbs 6:9-11 (KJV) [4]
The father-teacher of Proverbs mocks the sluggard’s ‘little more sleep’ and warns him of the poverty that will overtake him.
The mocking ‘How long wilt thou sleep?’ is itself an example of the rebuke and scorn the Kural says the idle must hear.
Buddhism
“Arise! Do not be heedless! Lead a righteous life. The righteous live happily both in this world and the next.”
— Dhammapada 168 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The verse is a direct command to wake up and stop being heedless, spoken as a teacher’s correction.
It reads like the ‘rebuke’ (இடி) half of the Kural: a sharp call aimed at someone who has let himself slacken.
Jainism
“'As are bad pupils, so are bad bullocks; I shall leave these lazy donkeys, and shall practise severe austerities.'”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 27.16; cf. 27.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The sage Garga compares pupils who 'break down through want of zeal' to stubborn bullocks, then dismisses them in disgust as lazy donkeys.
The Kural warns that the slothful will hear harsh rebuke and scorn; Garga's scornful words for his lazy pupils are exactly that kind of rebuke.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself a guru or a spiritual teacher, while he goes around begging - don't ever touch his feet.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the same salok in which he mocks the lazy man who pierces his ears to pass as a yogi, Guru Nanak tells his hearers to deny honour to the idle who live by begging.
The Kural says the lazy who make no worthy effort will hear rebuke and scorn; Guru Nanak likewise withholds respect from those who will not work.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad Book 6, Hector finds Paris lounging in his chamber polishing his armour while the Trojans die at the walls, and rebukes him bitterly. Paris admits the rebuke is just and arms himself.
— Homer, Iliad 6.325-341 (trans. S. Butler) [9]
The prince who idles at home while others fight is shamed by his own brother, and the shame finally stirs him to act.
Paris hearing a brother’s just rebuke for his idleness is a dramatised version of this Kural.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius opens Book 5 of the Meditations by scolding himself for wanting to stay in bed at dawn: plants, sparrows, ants, spiders and bees all do their work, so why should a human being refuse to do his?
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1 (trans. G. Long) [10]
The emperor turns the rebuke on himself, using the example of busy creatures to shame his own reluctance to get up and work.
Marcus shows the Kural’s rebuke internalised: a person who would rather scold himself than hear others do it.

Real Life Example

Lincoln rebukes General McClellan for his slowness and then removes him
October-November 1862 · Washington, D.C., and Maryland, United States
After the Battle of Antietam on 17 September 1862, General George McClellan did not pursue the retreating Confederate army for weeks despite President Lincoln's urging. When McClellan reported in October that his cavalry horses were sore-tongued and fatigued, Lincoln telegraphed back asking what the horses had done since Antietam that could have tired them. On 5 November 1862 Lincoln ordered him relieved of command and replaced by General Ambrose Burnside.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Repeated urging gave way to open sarcasm and finally to dismissal, and McClellan never received another military command.
The kural says those who give themselves to idleness will hear sharp rebukes and then scornful words, and McClellan's inaction drew first Lincoln's prodding, then his cutting telegram, and then removal.
