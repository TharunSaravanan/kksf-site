Kural 885

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

உறல்முறையான் உட்பகை தோன்றின் இறல்முறையான் ஏதம் பலவும் தரும்.
Uralmuraiyaan utpakai thondrin iralmuraiyaan edham palavum tharum.

Meaning

If hidden hostility grows among relatives, it brings many deadly troubles.

Elementary

When a family has a big fight that nobody fixes, it can grow worse and worse until it breaks the family apart. The poet says a quarrel inside the family can be the most dangerous kind of all.

University

The couplet turns on a chiming pair: ural-muraiyaan (உறல்முறையான், 'by way of relationship') and iral-muraiyaan (இறல்முறையான், 'by way of death'). Where enmity springs from kinship, it yields evils of a deadly kind. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as 'many sufferings of the kind that kill', and Solomon Pappaiah as 'many faults joined with death'. Pope's explanation speaks of internal hatred 'in a (king's) family' leading to 'fatal crime', showing the royal framing of the traditional commentary, where kin-enmity means dynastic feud. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads iral-muraiyaan differently, as harm extending even to those outside the kin, an interpretive variant. The Tamil text agrees in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
On the field of Kurukshetra, Arjuna sees grandfathers, teachers, uncles, brothers, sons and friends arrayed on both sides and despairs that the destruction of the family will ruin its ancient traditions.
— Bhagavad Gita 1.26-45 (As It Is) [3]
The Mahabharata war is the great Indian epic of kin-enmity ending in near-total destruction.
It is the Kural's warning writ large: enmity within a family brings deadly ruin.
Christianity
“And a man’s foes shall be they of his own household.”
— Matthew 10:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus, echoing Micah 7:6, warns that the deepest divisions may run through one's own home.
Both texts recognize that hostility within the family is especially grave.
Islam
The Qur'an tells of the two sons of Adam: one's offering is accepted, the other's is not, and the jealous brother kills his brother, becoming one of the regretful.
— Qur'an 5:27-31 [5]
The first murder in the Qur'anic story arises from rivalry between brothers.
It illustrates the Kural's teaching that enmity among kin leads to deadly harm.
Judaism
Cain, resentful that his brother Abel's offering is accepted and his is not, rises up against Abel in the field and kills him.
— Genesis 4:3-8 [4]
In Genesis the first killing in human history is fratricide born of jealousy.
The Kural's 'enmity by relationship' that brings 'evils by death' is embodied in Cain and Abel.
Buddhism
King Ajatasattu of Magadha, the son of King Bimbisara, had his own father killed to seize the throne, and later confessed this wrong before the Buddha.
— Digha Nikaya 2, Samaññaphala Sutta (closing confession) [6]
Buddhist texts remember the patricide as a grave evil born of ambition within the royal family.
It exemplifies the Kural's warning about deadly enmity inside a dynasty.
Jainism
“When Marbhuti, out of love for his brother, went to bring Kamatha back, the wretched fellow hurled a stone slab on his brother Marubhuti; consequently he died then and there”
— Story of Kamaṭha and Marubhūti (previous lives of Pārśvanātha), as retold on Jain Square, 'Kamatha and Marabhuti' (jainsquare.wordpress.com) [7]
In the Jain account of Pārśvanātha's former lives, Kamaṭha's hatred for his brother Marubhūti did not end with that murder: across later births he kept tormenting the soul that would become Pārśva, while Marubhūti's soul kept forgiving.
The Kural warns that enmity born within the bond of kinship brings evils of a deadly kind, and Kamaṭha's fratricide, renewed birth after birth, is Jainism's best-known picture of exactly that.
Sikhism
“O anger, you are the root of conflict; compassion never rises up in you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sehskritee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan addresses anger directly as the root of strife and a force that leaves no room for compassion.
The Kural says enmity springing up among kin brings deadly evils, and Gurbani traces such conflict back to anger, which shuts out the compassion that holds a family together.
Greek Mythology
Eteocles and Polynices, the sons of Oedipus, quarrel over the throne of Thebes; Polynices leads seven champions against the city, and the brothers kill each other at the seventh gate.
— Aeschylus, Seven Against Thebes [9]
The feud between brothers ends in mutual destruction, fulfilling their father's curse.
This is the Kural's 'enmity within kinship bringing deadly evils' in its starkest form.
Philosophy
Describing the civil strife at Corcyra, Thucydides observes that kinship became a weaker tie than party, and that fathers killed their sons in the violence.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 3.81-82 [10]
Thucydides analyses stasis, internal faction, as a force that dissolves even family bonds and unleashes every kind of death.
He confirms the Kural's insight that enmity within one's own people brings deadly evils.

Real Life Example

Kublai Khan and his brother Ariq Boke fight for the Mongol throne
1260-1264 · Mongolia and northern China
After the death of the Great Khan Mongke in 1259, his brothers Kublai and Ariq Boke each had himself proclaimed Great Khan in 1260, Kublai at Shangdu and Ariq Boke at Karakorum. The brothers fought a four-year civil war that ended when Ariq Boke submitted in 1264; he died in captivity two years later. The struggle left the Mongol empire effectively split into separate khanates, and Kublai concentrated on China, where he founded the Yuan dynasty.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A war between brothers broke the unity of the largest land empire in history, which never again acted as one.
The kural says enmity that springs from kinship brings deadly evils, and the brothers' war cost the Mongol empire its unity.
