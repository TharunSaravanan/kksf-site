Kural 315

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

அறிவினான் ஆகுவ துண்டோ பிறிதின்நோய் தந்நோய்போல் போற்றாக் கடை.
Arivinaan aaguvadhu undo piridhin noy Thannoy pol potraak kadai.

Meaning

What good is all your learning if you do not feel another creature’s pain as your own and protect it from suffering?

Elementary

You might know lots of facts, like all the planets or every dinosaur. But if your friend falls and scrapes their knee and you don’t care, what good are all those facts? Valluvar says being smart only matters if you feel other people’s hurt like it’s your own.

University

Valluvar here makes empathy the test of knowledge. Arivu (knowledge, understanding) is asked to justify itself, and the only acceptable answer is that it leads one to regard piridhin noy, the pain of another (piridhu can mean any other being, not only humans), as thannoy, one’s own pain. The verb potru (to guard, protect, cherish) makes the demand active: it is not enough to feel sympathy, one must protect the other from harm as one protects oneself. This anticipates a recurring theme in the Kural that learning without moral application is barren. Comparable ideas appear in the Bhagavad Gita’s claim that true knowledge produces equal vision of all beings, in the Buddha’s reflection that since everyone holds themselves dear no one should harm another, and in the Stoic doctrine of oikeiosis, which asks us to draw the circle of concern outward until strangers are treated as kin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The humble sages, by virtue of true knowledge, see with equal vision a learned and gentle brāhmaṇa, a cow, an elephant, a dog and a dog-eater [outcaste].”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.18 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna says real knowledge shows itself in seeing all beings, high or low, human or animal, with the same eye.
Both texts judge knowledge by its moral fruit: the truly wise see every being as equal, and so cannot treat another’s pain as less real than their own.
Christianity
“But a certain Samaritan, as he journeyed, came where he was: and when he saw him, he had compassion on him,”
— Luke 10:33 (KJV), Parable of the Good Samaritan [4]
In Jesus’ parable, a priest and a Levite, both religious experts, walk past a wounded man, but a despised Samaritan feels his pain and cares for him.
The parable makes the Kural’s point in story form: religious learning is useless if it does not lead you to treat another’s pain as your own.
Islam
“The similitude of believers in regard to mutual love, affection, fellow-feeling is that of one body; when any limb of it aches, the whole body aches, because of sleeplessness and fever.”
— Sahih Muslim 2586a (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet compares the community of believers to a single body in which one part’s pain is felt by all.
Both call for feeling another’s pain as your own; the hadith makes it literal through the image of one body.
Judaism
“Rabbi Eliezer said: Let the honor of your friend be as dear to you as your own”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:10 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Eliezer teaches that another person’s dignity should matter to you as much as your own.
Both require treating what belongs to another, whether honour or well-being, as if it were your own.
Buddhism
“Searching all directions with your awareness, you find no one dearer than yourself. In the same way, others are thickly dear to themselves. So you shouldn't hurt others if you love yourself.”
— Udana 5.1, Rajan Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
After King Pasenadi and Queen Mallika agree that no one is dearer to them than themselves, the Buddha draws the conclusion: others feel the same, so do not harm them.
The Kural and the Buddha reason the same way: understanding how precious you are to yourself should make you protect others as you protect yourself.
Jainism
“Knowing what is bad, he who knows it with regard to himself, knows it with regard to (the world) outside; and he who knows it with regard to (the world) outside, knows it with regard to himself: this reciprocity (between himself and) others (one should mind).”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.1.7.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org) [8]
At the close of its first lecture on the 'weapon' that harms living beings, the Ācārāṅga makes true knowledge depend on recognising that what hurts oneself hurts others too.
Valluvar asks what use knowledge is if it does not treat another's pain as one's own. The Ācārāṅga makes the same equation: real knowledge of suffering runs both ways between self and other.
Sikhism
“The God-conscious being delights in doing good to others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 273, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 8, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan describes the brahm giani, the person of true spiritual knowledge, and makes active kindness to others one of the marks of that knowledge.
The Kural asks what use knowledge is if it does not make another's pain one's own; for Guru Arjan the highest knowledge shows itself precisely in doing good to others.
Greek Mythology
At the opening of Sophocles’ Ajax, Athena shows Odysseus his enemy Ajax, driven mad and humiliated. She invites him to gloat, but Odysseus says he pities Ajax though he is a foe, because in Ajax’s ruin he sees his own fate and that of all mortals.
— Sophocles, Ajax 118–126 [10]
Odysseus recognises his enemy’s suffering as the kind of suffering that could be his own.
This is the Kural’s insight in action: true understanding shows up as feeling another’s pain as one’s own, even an enemy’s.
Philosophy
The Stoic Hierocles pictures each person at the centre of concentric circles (self, family, fellow citizens, all humanity) and urges us to keep drawing the outer circles inward, treating those far away as if they were near.
— Hierocles the Stoic, fragment in Stobaeus, Anthology 4.27.23 [11]
Stoic oikeiosis teaches that moral progress means extending to others the care we naturally feel for ourselves.
Hierocles offers a method for the Kural’s demand: widen self-concern until another’s pain is guarded as one’s own.

Real Life Example

Joseph Rotblat leaves the Manhattan Project
December 1944 · Los Alamos, New Mexico, United States
The Polish-born physicist Joseph Rotblat worked on atomic bomb research at Liverpool and from 1944 at Los Alamos, having joined because he feared Nazi Germany might build such a weapon. In late 1944, when it became clear that Germany was not close to developing an atomic bomb, he left the Manhattan Project on grounds of conscience. He turned his research toward the medical uses of radiation, co-founded the Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs in 1957, and shared the 1995 Nobel Peace Prize with Pugwash for efforts to eliminate nuclear weapons.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rotblat refused to let his scientific knowledge serve a weapon whose purpose he could no longer justify, and spent the rest of his life using science to reduce suffering.
The kural asks what good knowledge is if one does not treat others' pain as one's own and guard them from it, and Rotblat chose to put his learning to protecting people rather than harming them.
