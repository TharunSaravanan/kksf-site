Kural 1006

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

ஏதம் பெருஞ்செல்வம் தான்துவ்வான் தக்கார்க்கொன்று ஈதல் இயல்பிலா தான்.
Edham Perunjelvam Thaandhuvvaan Thakkaarkkondru Eedhal Iyalpilaa Thaan

Meaning

A person who does not enjoy their great riches, and does not have it in their nature to give some to people who deserve it, is like a sickness to those riches.

Elementary

Imagine a lovely garden where the owner never sits and never lets anyone else pick a flower. The garden is wasted, and the owner is like a sickness that keeps it from doing any good. Valluvar says a stingy rich person is like that sickness for his own money.

University

The opening word edham (ஏதம், 'harm, fault, affliction, disease') is the predicate, and its subject is the man described in the rest of the couplet: thaan thuvvaan (தான் துவ்வான், 'he himself does not enjoy') and thakkaarkku ondru eedhal iyalbu ilaan (தக்கார்க்கு ஒன்று ஈதல் இயல்பு இலான், 'one who has no disposition to give even one thing to the worthy'). Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar therefore read him as a disease (நோய்) to his own great wealth, as does Pope's explanation ('a disease to his wealth'); Solomon Pappaiah inverts it, making the wealth a disease in his keeping, and Sivayogi Sivakumar reads that he does harm to himself. The word iyalbu (இயல்பு, 'nature') stresses that the fault is temperamental, not circumstantial, and thakkaar (தக்கார், 'the deserving') narrows the duty of giving to worthy recipients, a classical criterion of proper gifts. Pope's verse rendering ('misery to men of churlish heart') follows the alternative sense. The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna teaches that the righteous who eat what remains after offering are freed from sin, while those who prepare food only for their own enjoyment eat nothing but sin.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.13 [3]
Possessions kept wholly to oneself turn into a source of fault rather than blessing.
Like the Kural, it sees self-enclosed wealth as a blight rather than a good.
Christianity
The Preacher calls it a grievous evil he has seen under the sun: riches kept by their owner to his own hurt, which are then lost in some misfortune so that nothing is left.
— Ecclesiastes 5:13–14 [4]
Hoarded riches can harm the very person who guards them.
The Kural similarly makes the miser and his wealth a mutual affliction.
Islam
“And let not those who [greedily] withhold what Allāh has given them of His bounty ever think that it is better for them. Rather, it is worse for them. Their necks will be encircled by what they withheld on the Day of Resurrection.”
— Qur'an 3:180, Al 'Imran (Saheeh International, via quran.com) [5]
The Qur'an teaches that withheld wealth becomes a burden and torment to the one who withholds it.
It matches the Kural's claim that the non-giver's great wealth turns into an affliction.
Judaism
The Mishnah describes four kinds of givers, and calls wicked the one who neither wishes to give himself nor wants others to give.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 5:13 [6]
Total unwillingness to give is classed as wickedness, not mere thrift.
The Kural's man with 'no nature for giving' fits this wicked type.
Buddhism
The Buddha says that if beings knew, as he knows, the results of giving and sharing, they would not eat without having given, and the stain of selfishness would not overcome their minds.
— Itivuttaka 26 [7]
Stinginess is a mental stain; generosity, even of the last mouthful, is the remedy.
The Kural's man without a giving nature is overcome by exactly this stain.
Jainism
“The seeking to accumulate wealth is a cause of vexation. The guarding that collected shining wealth causes vexation. Again, if any of that wealth which is so guarded be diminished, there is vexation. If it be lost, how great the vexation! Truly this said wealth is the very abode of vexation.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 28 ('Illiberality'), st. 10 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain poet shows hoarded wealth as nothing but a source of trouble at every stage.
It matches the Kural's description of great wealth unenjoyed and ungiven as an affliction (ஏதம்).
Sikhism
“He hoards gold and silver, but this wealth is false and poisonous, nothing more than ashes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 937, Raag Ramkali, Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak says that gold and silver piled up for their own sake are false, poisonous and as worthless as ashes.
The Kural calls the man who neither enjoys nor gives a disease to his own great wealth; Guru Nanak calls such hoarded wealth poison and ash.
Greek Mythology
In the underworld Odysseus sees Tantalus standing in a pool beneath trees laden with fruit; whenever he stoops to drink the water vanishes, and whenever he reaches for the fruit the wind tosses it away.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.582–592 [10]
Tantalus suffers eternal want in the very midst of plenty.
Like the Kural's miser, he is surrounded by goods that bring him only torment.
Philosophy
“Referring to a wealthy miser he said, He has not acquired a fortune; the fortune has acquired him.”
— Diogenes Laërtius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 4.50 (Bion), tr. R. D. Hicks (Wikisource) [11]
Bion of Borysthenes jokes that the miser is possessed by his wealth rather than possessing it.
The Kural likewise sees the non-enjoying, non-giving rich man as a bad relation to his own wealth.

Real Life Example

J. Paul Getty installs a pay telephone at Sutton Place
1959 · Sutton Place, Surrey, England, United Kingdom
After the American oil billionaire J. Paul Getty, then counted among the richest men in the world, made the Tudor mansion Sutton Place in Surrey his base in 1959, Time reported that he had cut back the gardening staff and installed a pay telephone for visitors' use. Getty later explained in his autobiography that the coin-box phone was a response to soaring telephone bills run up by the many people passing through the house during renovations. The public nonetheless took the pay phone as a symbol of his tightness with money, and it became the best-known story of his frugality.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The story stuck to Getty because it seemed to sum up a vast fortune that its owner was reluctant to share even in small ways.
The kural calls a man who neither enjoys his wealth nor is disposed to give to others a blight upon that wealth, and the pay telephone became the public emblem of a fortune held back even from guests.
