Kural 1254

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

நிறையுடையேன் என்பேன்மன் யானோஎன் காமம் மறையிறந்து மன்று படும்.
Niraiyutaiyaen enpaenman yaanoa-en kaamam maraiyirandhu mandru patum.

Meaning

I used to say I had strong self-control. But my love has come out of hiding and now shows itself for everyone to see.

Elementary

Imagine being sure you could keep a big secret, and then finding the whole town talking about it. The heroine thought she was strong enough to keep her love quiet, but now everyone can see it.

University

The heroine contrasts what she used to say (niṟaiyuṭaiyēṉ eṉpēṉmaṉ, ‘I would say I have reserve’, the maṉ again marking a claim now undone) with what happens: her kāmam, passing beyond concealment (maṟai iṟantu), appears in the maṉṟu, the village common or assembly place. The word maṉṟu makes the exposure social: her longing is now matter for public talk, recalling the alar (gossip) of chapter 115. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss maṉṟam as the public assembly; Solomon Pappaiah renders the result as ‘known to the townspeople’ (ஊரவர் அறிய). Pope’s English on ytamizh.com (‘In womanly reserve I deemed myself beyond assail; / But love will come abroad, and casts away the veil’) brings out the gendered sense of niṟai in the commentary tradition associated with Parimelazhagar. The couplet is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In her letter to Krishna, Princess Rukmini writes that, having heard of his qualities, her heart has set aside shame and entered into him, and she asks him to carry her off before her unwanted wedding.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.52.37-43 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Bhagavata presents a high-born maiden openly declaring a love that propriety would have kept hidden.
Rukmini’s love, like the heroine’s, passes beyond concealment and declares itself.
Christianity
“Then Joseph could not refrain himself before all them that stood by him… And he wept aloud: and the Egyptians and the house of Pharaoh heard.”
— Genesis 45:1-2 (KJV) [4]
Joseph has long hidden who he is from his brothers, but his feeling finally bursts out so loudly that all Egypt hears it.
As in the Kural, a restraint the person relied on gives way, and the feeling becomes public.
Islam
“And women in the city said, ‘The wife of al-’Azeez is seeking to seduce her slave boy; he has impassioned her with love. Indeed, we see her [to be] in clear error.’”
— Qur’an 12:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an records how a noblewoman’s hidden passion for Joseph became the talk of the city’s women.
The Kural’s heroine finds exactly this: her love has left its hiding place and become public talk.
Judaism
“I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, what will ye tell him? that I am love-sick.”
— Song of Songs 5:8 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman of the Song no longer hides her state; she tells the women of the city outright that she is sick with love.
The Kural’s heroine also finds her love has gone beyond concealment and become known to others.
Buddhism
“Venerable Nanda, they say, has been hired. Venerable Nanda, they say, has been bought.”
— Udana 3.2, Nanda Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
When it became known that Nanda was keeping the holy life only for the sake of the promised nymphs, his fellow monks went around saying this, and he was humiliated and ashamed.
Like the heroine, Nanda finds that the longing he kept inside has become the common talk of his whole community.
Jainism
The Sutrakritanga warns that when relations and friends see a monk becoming intimate with a woman, they grow angry and openly tell him that, being a man, he should protect and support her.
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.14 (paraphrase of tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain text shows a monk’s concealed attachment exposed to public judgement.
Like the heroine, the monk who thought himself restrained finds his attachment has become known to all.
Sikhism
“My shyness and hesitation have died and gone, and I walk with my face unveiled.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 931, Raag Raamkalee, Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak speaks as a soul-bride whose fear of what people will say has vanished, so that she now goes about openly, without her veil.
The Kural's wife admits that the reserve she once claimed is gone and her love now shows in the public square; Guru Nanak's bride likewise has shed her shyness and walks unveiled before everyone.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, the minstrel Demodocus sings how Hephaestus caught Ares and Aphrodite together in an invisible net and then called the gods to come and see, so that their secret love was exposed before the company of Olympus.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.266-366 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer’s comic tale shows a hidden love dragged into public view.
The Kural’s heroine experiences the same thing in gentler form: her love has left its hiding place and stands in the public square.
Philosophy
Aristotle says the person who lacks self-control knows what is right, but under passion holds that knowledge only as a drunk or sleeping man holds it, like someone reciting verses of Empedocles without understanding them.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VII.3, 1147a (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle explains how a person who believes himself self-controlled can still be overrun by passion.
The heroine’s ‘I used to say I had reserve’ is a lover’s version of Aristotle’s akrasia: her restraint existed, but passion overrode it.

Real Life Example

The Bamangwato kgotla debates Seretse Khama's marriage
November 1948 - June 1949 · Bechuanaland Protectorate (now Botswana)
Seretse Khama, heir to the chieftaincy of the Bamangwato people, married Ruth Williams, an Englishwoman, in a London registry office in September 1948, against the wishes of his uncle and regent Tshekedi Khama. The marriage was then debated at three meetings of the kgotla, the people's public assembly; at the December 1948 meeting most of those present opposed it. At the third kgotla, in June 1949, the assembly changed its position and accepted Seretse as chief and Ruth as his wife.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A private marriage became a matter for open debate in the community's assembly and later for the British government, showing how personal love can become public business.
The kural's heroine says her love has left concealment and now shows itself in the maṉṟu, the public assembly; Seretse's love was literally argued over in the kgotla.
