Kural 285

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

அருள்கருதி அன்புடைய ராதல் பொருள்கருதிப் பொச்சாப்புப் பார்ப்பார்கண் இல்.
Arulkarudhi anbudaiya raadhal porulkarudhip, Pochchaappup paarppaarkan il.

Meaning

People who only want other people's things, and wait for them to look away, cannot have real kindness or love.

Elementary

A real friend helps you when you drop your lunch money. Someone who waits for you to drop it so they can grab it can’t be kind at the same time — the waiting-to-grab heart pushes the kindness out.

University

The couplet contrasts two inner orientations: aruḷ karuti, “holding grace (compassion) in regard,” and poruḷ karuti, “holding another’s wealth in regard.” The fraudster’s signature act is poccāppu pārttal — watching for the other person’s forgetfulness or unguardedness — which Valluvar treats as incompatible with aṉpu (love). The couplet ties this chapter to the preceding ones on Aruḷuṭaimai (compassion), showing that for Valluvar honesty in dealings is not a separate commercial rule but a consequence of compassion. Kant’s shopkeeper, the hadith of the wet grain, and Micah’s schemers lying awake all describe the same failure: treating another person’s vulnerability as an opportunity.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…compassion for all living entities; freedom from covetousness; gentleness; modesty…”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 [5]
Krishna lists compassion and freedom from covetousness side by side among the divine qualities.
The Gita pairs exactly what the Kural pairs: compassion (aruḷ) and the absence of coveting others’ wealth.
Christianity
“Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.”
— Romans 13:10 (KJV) [6]
Paul, after citing ‘Thou shalt not steal’ among the commandments, sums them all up as love, which cannot harm a neighbour.
Same logic as the Kural: love and scheming against a neighbour’s goods cannot coexist.
Islam
“When the Prophet put his hand into a heap of grain and found the lower part wet, he said: “Why did you not place this (the drenched part of the heap) over other eatables so that the people could see it? He who deceives is not of me (is not my follower).”
— Sahih Muslim 102 [7]
The seller hid damaged grain where buyers would not notice, relying on their lack of vigilance; the Prophet declared such deception outside his community.
Exactly the Kural’s target: profiting from the other party’s unguarded moment (poccāppu).
Judaism
“Woe to them that devise iniquity And work evil upon their beds! When the morning is light, they execute it, Because it is in the power of their hand. And they covet fields, and seize them…”
— Micah 2:1-2 (JPS 1917) [8]
Micah condemns those who lie awake plotting to seize others’ property and act as soon as the chance comes.
Captures the Kural’s schemer who covets and waits for the right moment to take.
Buddhism
“Abandoning the taking of what is not given, he abstains from taking what is not given. He takes only what is given, accepts only what is given, lives not by stealth but by means of a self that has become pure.”
— Digha Nikaya 2, Samaññaphala Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [9]
Describing the virtue of a monk, the Buddha contrasts a life of stealth with one lived by a purified self.
The Kural’s stealthy watcher and the Buddha’s ‘life by stealth’ are the same figure; purity of heart excludes both.
Jainism
“A thief feels neither pity nor shame, nor does he possess discipline and faith. There is no evil that he cannot do for wealth.”
— Bhagavatī Ārādhanā 862 (Śivārya), English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [10]
Śivārya's Digambara manual on monastic discipline says that the thief's mind has no room for compassion. Its fixation on wealth crowds out pity, shame and faith.
Valluvar says those who covet another's goods and wait for his careless moment cannot be people of compassion (aruḷ). The Jain verse says the same thing directly: “A thief feels neither pity nor shame.”
Sikhism
“Bronze is bright and shiny, but when it is rubbed, its blackness appears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, Guru Nanak sang this hymn at the inn of Sajjan. Sajjan seemed a pious host to travellers but robbed them once they were off guard, and he repented on hearing it.
The Kural says those who covet others' goods and wait for a moment of carelessness cannot have loving compassion. Sajjan's polished hospitality hiding a predator's watchfulness is exactly that bright surface over darkness.
Greek Mythology
At Mecone, when gods and mortals were settling their portions, Prometheus divided an ox deceptively: the meat and fat innards hidden in the ox’s stomach, and the bare bones wrapped in glistening fat. He invited Zeus to choose, hoping he would be fooled by appearances; Zeus in anger withheld fire from mankind.
— Hesiod, Theogony 535-560 [12]
A trick in the dividing of shares, relying on the other party taking the surface at face value, poisons the relationship between gods and humans.
Shows the Kural’s point that a dealer who counts on the other side’s oversight destroys goodwill.
Philosophy
“it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not over charge an inexperienced purchaser”
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals, First Section (tr. T. K. Abbott) [13]
Kant’s example of the honest shopkeeper: exploiting a buyer who cannot judge the price is the plain wrong that honesty forbids.
The inexperienced purchaser is the person whose unguarded moment the Kural’s fraudster is waiting for.

Real Life Example

Colonel Blood's attempt to steal the Crown Jewels
9 May 1671 · Tower of London, London, England, United Kingdom
Thomas Blood, an Irish adventurer, disguised himself as a clergyman and visited the Crown Jewels at the Tower of London, gradually winning the trust and friendship of their elderly keeper, Talbot Edwards, and his family. On 9 May 1671 he returned with accomplices, and once Edwards had unlocked the jewel room they overpowered him and seized the crown, orb and sceptre. The alarm was raised and Blood was caught before he could leave the Tower, but King Charles II later pardoned him.
— Sources: [14] [15]
The plot is remembered as one of history's most audacious thefts, carried out by exploiting the goodwill of a man who had befriended the thief.
The kural says those who covet another's goods and wait for his unguarded moment cannot be people of love, and Blood cultivated a friendship precisely to catch his keeper off guard.
