Kural 230

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

சாதலின் இன்னாத தில்லை இனிததூஉம் ஈதல் இயையாக் கடை.
Saadhalin innaadhadhu illai inidhadhooum Eedhal iyaiyaak kadai.

Meaning

Nothing is more painful than dying. But even dying feels sweet when a person can no longer give to others.

Elementary

Everyone thinks the worst thing in the world is dying. But this kural says a truly kind person would feel even worse if they could never help anyone again. For them, being unable to give is the saddest thing of all.

University

The final couplet of the chapter uses a hyperbolic reversal: death, the ultimate இன்னாதது (painful thing), becomes இனிது (sweet) — the emphatic அதூஉம், ‘even that’ — at the point where giving is no longer possible (ஈதல் இயையாக் கடை). The claim measures the worth of a life by its capacity to give: a life without that capacity is, for the generous person, worse than no life. Commentators read it as describing either the giver who has lost his means or one who faces a suppliant with nothing left to offer. The closing hyperbole makes giving not merely a virtue among others but the very thing that makes life worth living, bringing the chapter to a climax.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, King Shibi is tested by gods who take the form of a hawk chasing a dove. When the dove seeks refuge with him, Shibi refuses to give it up and instead offers the hawk an equal weight of his own flesh, finally placing his whole body on the scales rather than fail to give protection.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, story of King Shibi (Sivi) [3]
Shibi would rather give up his life than fail to give what is asked of him.
Embodies the Kural’s claim that for the generous, being unable to give is worse than death.
Christianity
“For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain. But if I live in the flesh, this is the fruit of my labour... Nevertheless to abide in the flesh is more needful for you.”
— Philippians 1:21-24 (KJV) [4]
Paul says death would be gain for him; the only reason to go on living is to serve others.
Like the Kural, life’s value is measured by what one can give to others; without that, death is preferable.
Islam
“And spend [in the way of Allāh] from what We have provided you before death approaches one of you and he says, ‘My Lord, if only You would delay me for a brief term so I would give charity and be of the righteous.’”
— Qur'an 63:10 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an pictures a dying person whose deepest regret is not having given charity while they could.
Links death and the lost chance to give, as the Kural does.
Judaism
“Treasures of wickedness profit nothing: but righteousness delivereth from death.”
— Proverbs 10:2 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says that righteousness (tzedakah, a word later used for charity) saves from death, while ill-gotten treasure is useless.
Like the Kural, it ties giving and death together, treating righteousness/charity as what gives life its value.
Buddhism
In the Sivi Jataka, King Sivi, who gives alms daily, longs to give something from his own body. When a blind brahmin (Sakka in disguise) asks for an eye, he gladly gives both eyes, and is later rewarded with divine sight.
— Jataka No. 499, Sivi Jataka [6]
The Bodhisatta king values the chance to give more than his own body and sight.
Shows the Kural’s attitude that for a true giver, giving outweighs even the greatest personal loss.
Jainism
Samantabhadra defines sallekhanā, the Jain practice of voluntarily giving up the body by gradual fasting, as something undertaken for the sake of dharma. It is permitted only when calamity, famine, old age or incurable disease makes it impossible to go on living righteously.
— Samantabhadra, Ratnakaraṇḍa Śrāvakācāra 122 (cf. tr. Champat Rai Jain, 1917) [7]
In Jain householder ethics, a person may calmly accept death when life can no longer be used to practise dharma. This 'wise death' is treated as a final religious act, not as a defeat.
Valluvar says death becomes sweet when one can no longer give. Jainism also accepts death once a life can no longer serve its moral purpose, but for Jains that purpose is self-purification rather than giving.
Sikhism
“Without meditating in remembrance on the Lord, one's life is like that of a snake. This is how the faithless cynic lives, forgetting the Naam, the Name of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 239, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan compares a life without remembrance of God to a snake's long but harmful life.
The Kural says death becomes sweet when one can no longer give, measuring life by its usefulness; Guru Arjan likewise judges a long life empty of its true purpose as no better than a snake's.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Alcestis, when no one else will die in place of King Admetus, his wife Alcestis gives her own life for him, choosing death over a life in which she could not give him the gift of survival.
— Euripides, Alcestis [9]
Alcestis treats giving, even of her own life, as more important than living.
Reflects the Kural’s scale of values in which giving outweighs life itself.
Philosophy
“He would prefer a short period of intense pleasure to a long one of mild enjoyment, a twelvemonth of noble life to many years of humdrum existence, and one great and noble action to many trivial ones.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.8 (tr. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle says the good person will give up wealth and even life for friends and country, because a short noble life beats a long empty one.
Like the Kural, it measures life by the noble giving it makes possible, not by its length.

Real Life Example

Julius Rosenwald's fund is required to spend itself out
1917-1948 · Chicago, Illinois, United States
Julius Rosenwald, president of Sears, Roebuck and Company, established the Julius Rosenwald Fund in 1917, chiefly to improve education for African American students, and gave much of his fortune during his lifetime. He opposed permanent endowments and stipulated that the fund spend its assets within 25 years of his death. After he died in 1932 the fund continued its work and closed in 1948.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Rosenwald treated wealth as something to be given away within a lifetime and a generation, not preserved for its own sake.
The kural measures a life by its capacity to give, and Rosenwald arranged his fortune so that its whole purpose would be exhausted in giving.
