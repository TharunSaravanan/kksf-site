Kural 911

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

அன்பின் விழையார் பொருள்விழையும் ஆய்தொடியார் இன்சொல் இழுக்குத் தரும்.
Anbin vizhaiyaar porulvizhaiyum aaydhodiyaar insol izhukkuth tharum.

Meaning

The sweet words of bangle-wearing women who want a man's money, not his love, will in the end bring him shame and trouble.

Elementary

Imagine a kid who suddenly calls you their best friend every day you bring a big bag of candy, and ignores you on days you bring nothing. Their nice words feel good, but they were never about you, and trusting them only ends with hurt feelings. Real friendship is liking someone, not liking what they can give you.

University

The opening couplet sets the chapter’s key contrast between anbu (அன்பு, love) and porul (பொருள், wealth): the women described here vizhaiyaar, ‘do not desire’, out of love but porul-vizhaiyum, ‘desire wealth’. Aaydhodiyaar (ஆய்தொடியார், ‘those wearing choice bangles’) is a conventional ornamental epithet, and the danger lies in their insol (இன்சொல், sweet speech), the same virtue Valluvar praises in chapter 10, here turned into a tool of profit. Izhukku (இழுக்கு) means both moral lapse and resulting disgrace; Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah render it as ‘suffering’, Sivayogi Sivakumar as ‘degradation’. Parimelazhagar places the chapter in the Natpiyal (section on friendship) right after Penvazhicheral (being led by one’s wife), and reads it as a warning against an intimacy that only imitates friendship. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Happiness is said to be in the mode of passion when it is derived from the contact of the senses with their objects. Such happiness is like nectar at first but poison at the end.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.38 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna describes rajasic pleasure as sweet at the start and bitter at the end.
The insincere sweet words of Kural 911 are nectar at first, and the izhukku they bring is the poison at the end.
Christianity
“For they that are such serve not our Lord Jesus Christ, but their own belly; and by good words and fair speeches deceive the hearts of the simple.”
— Romans 16:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns about people who use pleasant language to deceive the naive while serving only their own appetite.
Same picture as the Kural: fair speech covering pure self-interest.
Islam
“And when you see them, their forms please you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech. [They are] as if they were pieces of wood propped up...”
— Qur’an 63:4 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes hypocrites whose looks and words are attractive but who are hollow inside.
Like the women of Kural 911, they please the eye and ear while having no real heart for you.
Judaism
“All love that depends on a something, [when the] thing ceases, [the] love ceases; and [all love] that does not depend on anything, will never cease.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:16 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah separates love that depends on some benefit from love that stands on its own, and says the first ends when the benefit ends.
Valluvar’s women love the porul, not the person, which is exactly the love that ‘depends on a something’.
Buddhism
“Among the ‘foes in the guise of friends’, the Buddha lists the one who renders lip-service, who among other things “tries to gain one’s favor by empty words.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
The Sigalovada Sutta teaches a layman to recognize false friends whose kind words have no substance behind them.
Kural 911’s insol is the same kind of empty, favor-seeking speech.
Jainism
“Meekly and politely they approach him with their manifold arts to win his heart; and talking sweetly in confidential conversation they make him do (what they like).”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga warns that sweet, intimate talk is the tool by which a man is led wherever the woman wants.
Kural 911 also warns that the sweet words of women who want a man's money, not the man, end in disgrace and grief.
Sikhism
“Listen, madman: you have been trapped by false love, which is transitory, like the fading color of the safflower.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 777, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan warns the mind that the love it has fallen into is false, bright for a moment like safflower dye and then gone. In Gurbani the safflower stands for any attachment that looks like love but cannot last.
Like the Kural’s mercenary sweet words, this love is real only in appearance, and the one who trusts it is left with grief.
Greek Mythology
In Hesiod’s myth of Pandora, the gods dress the first woman in beauty and grace, and Hermes puts lies, crafty words and a deceitful nature in her heart, so that she becomes a lovely gift that brings men trouble.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 60-82 [10]
Hesiod’s story is about a beautiful surface hiding deceit and harm.
Kural 911 likewise warns of an adorned figure whose sweet words bring ruin.
Philosophy
“Therefore those who love for the sake of utility love for the sake of what is good for themselves... and not in so far as the other is the person loved but in so far as he is useful or pleasant.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says friendships of utility are not really about the other person but about what one gets.
This is the porul-vizhaiyum (wealth-desiring) affection of Kural 911, described as philosophy.

Real Life Example

Louise de Kéroualle wins the favour and fortune of Charles II
1671-1685 · London, England
Louise de Kéroualle came to England in 1670 in the household of Charles II's sister and, with the encouragement of the French government, which hoped she would be a diplomatic asset, became the king's mistress. She received titles and gifts, including the English dukedom of Portsmouth and the French duchy of Aubigny from Louis XIV, and acted as an intermediary for French interests at court. Britannica notes her unequalled rapacity and records that she was the least popular of the king's mistresses with his subjects.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A favourite whose position brought her great wealth and served a foreign court made the king's attachment a lasting source of public resentment.
The kural warns that the sweet words of women who seek a man for his wealth rather than for love bring him disgrace, and this attachment damaged Charles II's standing with his own people.
