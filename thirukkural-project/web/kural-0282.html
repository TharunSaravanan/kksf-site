Kural 282

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

உள்ளத்தால் உள்ளலும் தீதே பிறன்பொருளைக் கள்ளத்தால் கள்வேம் எனல்.
Ullaththaal ullalum theethe piranporulaik, Kallaththaal kalvem enal.

Meaning

Even thinking in your mind, “I will sneakily steal what belongs to someone else,” is wrong.

Elementary

Imagine thinking, “When no one is looking, I’ll trick my friend out of his toy.” Even if you never do it, just planning it is already a bad step, because every wrong action begins as a thought.

University

Valluvar moves culpability upstream from act to intention: uḷḷattāl uḷḷalum tītē — “even the thinking with the mind is evil.” The phrase kaḷḷattāl kaḷvēm (“we shall steal by craft”) specifies the content of the forbidden thought as a deliberate scheme, not a passing temptation, which is important for the ethical claim. This parallels the Tenth Commandment’s ban on coveting, the Buddhist teaching that mind precedes action, and the Gita’s account of how dwelling on objects breeds desire. Commentators note the psychological realism: fraud requires premeditation, so the only reliable defence is to refuse the premeditation itself.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62 [5]
Krishna traces a chain from mere contemplation of desirable things to attachment and then to consuming desire.
Both texts say the danger starts in thought; dwelling on another’s wealth is the first link in the chain toward fraud.
Christianity
“For from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts, adulteries, fornications, murders, Thefts, covetousness, wickedness, deceit…”
— Mark 7:21-22 (KJV) [6]
Jesus teaches that what defiles a person comes from inside the heart, listing thefts, covetousness and deceit among evils that start as inner thoughts.
Same claim as the Kural: theft and deceit are born in the heart before the hand.
Islam
“Whether you show what is within yourselves or conceal it, Allāh will bring you to account for it.”
— Qur’an 2:284 (Sahih International) [7]
The Qur’an teaches that God judges what lies hidden within a person as well as what is displayed.
Supports the Kural’s claim that a concealed intention to defraud is already morally weighed.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour’s house; thou shalt not covet thy neighbour’s wife, nor his man-servant, nor his maid-servant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour’s.”
— Exodus 20:14 (JPS 1917 numbering; 20:17 in Christian Bibles) [8]
The last of the Ten Commandments forbids not an act but a desire directed at a neighbour’s property.
Like the Kural, it makes the inner wish to take another’s goods itself a transgression.
Buddhism
“Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.”
— Dhammapada 1 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Dhammapada’s opening verse makes mind the source of every action and its consequences.
Grounds the Kural’s claim that the fraudulent plan in the mind is already the root of the harm.
Jainism
“It follows that activity actuated by impure dispositions is stealing (steya), whether external objects are taken or not.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.15, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [10]
Commenting on the sutra that defines stealing as taking what is not given, Pūjyapāda holds that the guilt lies in the passion (pramatta-yoga) behind the act. A theft-minded disposition is therefore already stealing, even if nothing is actually taken.
Valluvar says that merely thinking “I will take his property by cunning” is already a wrong. Pūjyapāda likewise places stealing in the impure disposition itself, whether or not anything is taken.
Sikhism
“False is the mind which covets the wealth of others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 269, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan names each faculty that goes astray: the hands that steal, the ears that slander, and here the mind that covets another's wealth.
The Kural finds wrong in merely thinking of taking another's property by cunning. Guru Arjan likewise condemns the coveting mind itself, before any act.
Greek Mythology
In Glaucon’s tale in Plato’s Republic, the shepherd Gyges finds a ring that makes him invisible; secure from detection, he uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king and seize the kingdom.
— Plato, Republic II, 359c-360b (legend of the Ring of Gyges) [12]
The legend asks what a person would do if he could take anything without being seen, exposing whether honesty is inner or merely fear of exposure.
The Kural answers Gyges: the scheme formed in the unseen mind is already the wrong, detection or not.
Philosophy
“Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will.”
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals, First Section (tr. T. K. Abbott) [13]
Kant opens the Groundwork by locating moral worth entirely in the will, not in outcomes.
Converse of the Kural: if goodness lies in the will, a will set on fraud is already bad before it acts.

Real Life Example

Judge Landis bans the Black Sox, including a player who took no money
3 August 1921 · Chicago, Illinois, United States
Eight Chicago White Sox players were accused of conspiring with gamblers to lose the 1919 World Series, and on 2 August 1921 a jury acquitted them. The next day baseball commissioner Kenesaw Mountain Landis banned all eight for life, declaring that this applied regardless of the jury's verdict and also to any player who sat in meetings where throwing games was discussed and did not tell his club. Third baseman Buck Weaver had accepted none of the money, but he had known of the scheme and kept silent, and his ban was never lifted.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
Landis treated involvement in planning a fraud, even without taking the reward, as disqualifying, which set the standard for integrity in American professional sport.
The kural says that even forming the intention to take another's property by cunning is already a wrong, and Landis's ruling punished involvement in the scheme itself, not only the payoff.
