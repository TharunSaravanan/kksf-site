Kural 1174

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

பெயலாற்றா நீருலந்த உண்கண் உயலாற்றா உய்வில்நோய் என்கண் நிறுத்து.
Peyalaatraa neerulandha un-kan uyalaatraa uyvilnoi en-kan niruththu.

Meaning

My lovely eyes planted in me a sickness I cannot escape or survive. Now they have cried so much that their tears have dried up.

Elementary

Imagine crying so hard and so long that, in the end, no more tears come out. That is how the lady’s eyes feel: they cried and cried, and now they are dry, but the sadness is still there.

University

Peyal (பெயல்) is literally ‘rain’, so peyal āṟṟā nīr ulanta is ‘the water dried up, no longer able to rain’: the tears themselves are exhausted. Uyal āṟṟā uyvil nōy is a doubled image of fatality, ‘a disease with no escape, which one cannot survive’, and eṉkaṇ niṟuttu ‘having fixed it in me’ (with a pun on kaṇ as both ‘eye’ and ‘in’). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar agree that the eyes, having caused the incurable love-sickness, are now too dry even to weep. Pope: ‘Those eyes have wept till all the fount of tears is dry, / That brought upon me pain that knows no remedy.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Cloud Messenger, the exiled Yaksha imagines his wife far away, wasted by separation, her eyes swollen and reddened from incessant weeping, lying awake and counting the days until his return.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta (Uttaramegha), description of the Yaksha’s wife (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa’s poem pictures the separated wife as worn out by tears and sleeplessness.
The Kural’s heroine is the same figure, her eyes exhausted by weeping over an absent husband.
Christianity
“I am weary with my groaning; all the night make I my bed to swim; I water my couch with my tears. Mine eye is consumed because of grief.”
— Psalm 6:6-7 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes nights of weeping until his eye is worn out with sorrow.
‘Mine eye is consumed because of grief’ could serve as a title for the Kural’s chapter, whose eyes have wept themselves dry.
Islam
“And he turned away from them and said, ‘Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,’ and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.”
— Qur’an 12:84 (Sahih International) [5]
Jacob grieves so long for his lost son that his eyes are worn white with sorrow.
As with the Kural’s heroine, the eyes themselves bear the damage of grief for an absent beloved.
Judaism
“Mine eyes do fail with tears, Mine inwards burn, My liver is poured upon the earth…”
— Lamentations 2:11 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The poet of Lamentations weeps until his eyes give out and his whole body is undone by grief.
The Kural’s eyes likewise ‘fail with tears’ until there are no more to shed.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada traces grief to its root in endearment: to hold someone dear is to be exposed to sorrow.
The heroine’s ‘incurable disease’ is the grief that springs from endearment, and her dried-up eyes are its sign.
Jainism
“The mind of those who always live in unfrequented lodgings, who eat low food, and who subdue their senses, will not be attacked by the foe, Love, who is vanquished as disease is by medicine.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana speaks of love as a disease that can be cured by the medicine of discipline and sense restraint.
The Kural shares the image of love as a disease but denies the cure: for the heroine it is uyvil nōy, a sickness with no escape.
Sikhism
“I cannot survive without seeing my Beloved. My eyes are welling up with tears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 94, Raag Maajh, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that without the sight of the Beloved he cannot live, and his eyes overflow with tears.
The Kural's heroine says her eyes, having planted in her a sickness she cannot survive, have wept until no tears are left; Guru Ram Das likewise joins a longing he cannot survive to eyes running with tears.
Greek Mythology
Spurned by Narcissus, the nymph Echo hides in the woods and wastes away with grief until her body is gone and only her voice and bones remain.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.370-401 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Echo is consumed by an unreturned love until nothing of her is left but a voice.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Echo is worn away by a love-sickness she cannot escape, until even its outward signs are spent.
Philosophy
Lucretius describes love as a sore that, if fed, grows inflamed and becomes chronic, a madness that worsens day by day unless it is broken off early.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1068-1072 (paraphrase) [11]
For Lucretius, erotic love is a wound that festers rather than heals when one keeps nourishing it.
The heroine calls her love ‘a disease with no escape’; Lucretius explains why it has become so.

Real Life Example

Theodore Roosevelt's diary on the day his wife died
14 February 1884 · New York City, United States
On 14 February 1884 Theodore Roosevelt's mother died of typhoid fever early in the morning, and his wife, Alice Hathaway Lee Roosevelt, died that afternoon in the same house, two days after giving birth to their daughter. In his pocket diary Roosevelt marked a large X on the page and wrote, The light has gone out of my life. He chose never to speak Alice's name again and did not mention her in his autobiography.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Roosevelt's single line and his lifelong silence show a grief so deep that it could not be cured and was never again put into words.
The kural speaks of eyes that have wept until no tears remain, leaving behind a sickness with no remedy, and Roosevelt's eight-word entry and later silence suggest that kind of exhausted, incurable sorrow.
