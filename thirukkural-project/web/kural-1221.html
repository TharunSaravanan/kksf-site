Kural 1221

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

மாலையோ அல்லை மணந்தார் உயிருண்ணும் வேலைநீ வாழி பொழுது.
Maalaiyo allai manandhaar uyirunnum vaelainee vaazhi pozhudhu.

Meaning

Long live you, evening! But you are not really evening at all. You are a deadly time that eats up the lives of wives whose husbands are far away.

Elementary

Think of the time of day you used to love most, like when a parent came home and played with you. If that parent had to go far away for a long time, the same hour might suddenly feel sad and heavy instead of happy. The wife in this verse feels that way about evening.

University

Chapter 123, poḻutu kaṇṭu iraṅkal (‘lamenting on seeing the hour’), belongs to the kaṟpiyal (wedded love) section: the wife, separated from her husband, finds that dusk (mālai), once the hour of reunion, now sharpens her grief. The opening couplet is an ironic apostrophe: vāḻi (‘may you live long!’) is a blessing turned to reproach, and maṇantār (‘those who were united/wedded’) is glossed by Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan as women who knew union and now live apart. The key word vēlai is read two ways: Varadarasanar and Kesikan take it as ‘time, end-time’ (muṭivu kālam), while Mu. Karunanidhi reads vēl, ‘spear’, which is also Pope’s choice (‘a spear that doth devour / The souls of brides’). The couplet text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (the latter spells the chapter title as two words, poḻutukaṇṭu iraṅkal).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the lake Pampa in springtime, Rama, separated from Sita, finds that the blossoming trees, the bird-calls and the fragrant breeze, which should delight him, only inflame his grief, and he tells Lakshmana that the season itself is tormenting him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda, canto 1 (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki shows a lover for whom the loveliest scenes of nature become instruments of pain because the beloved is absent.
Like the Kural’s evening, Rama’s springtime is a beautiful hour that has turned into an assailant of the separated heart.
Christianity
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Solomon 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs, read in Christian tradition as an image of love between Christ and the soul, gives the bride’s ache when night comes and the beloved is absent.
The Kural’s wife suffers in exactly that hour when she reaches for her husband and finds him gone.
Islam
“And from the evil of darkness when it settles”
— Qur’an 113:3 (Saheeh International) [5]
In Surat al-Falaq the believer seeks refuge in God from the harm that comes as darkness falls.
The Kural also treats the coming of dusk as a menace that preys on the vulnerable, though Islam answers it with refuge in God rather than lament.
Judaism
“She weepeth sore in the night, And her tears are on her cheeks; She hath none to comfort her Among all her lovers”
— Lamentations 1:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Lamentations pictures Jerusalem as a forsaken woman whose grief is sharpest at night, when no one comforts her.
The Kural’s wife is likewise a woman whose sorrow peaks as darkness comes and her beloved is not there.
Buddhism
“Therefore hold nothing dear, for separation from the dear is painful. There are no bonds for those who have nothing beloved or unloved.”
— Dhammapada 211 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names separation from what is dear as a direct cause of pain and advises loosening the bond.
The Kural shows that pain at its sharpest; Buddhism diagnoses its root in attachment, so the fit is real but the response differs.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira’s teaching warns that pleasures which are sweet at first turn deadly in the end.
The evening that once brought the wife sweetness now ‘devours lives’, which is the very reversal the Kimpaka simile describes.
Sikhism
“This dream like happening did not let me meet my beloved. Now the death-like night of separation neither ends nor terminates.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabit Savaiye, Kabit 219 (English translation as given on BaniDB) [9]
Bhai Gurdas laments that the meeting with his Beloved slipped away, leaving a night of separation that feels like death and will not end.
The Kural's wife tells the evening it is no gentle dusk but a killing time for wives whose husbands are away; Bhai Gurdas likewise feels the night of separation as death itself.
Greek Mythology
Penelope tells the disguised Odysseus that by day she can distract herself with her work and household, but when night comes and everyone sleeps she lies awake and sharp cares crowd her grieving heart.
— Homer, Odyssey 19.509-517 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer’s faithful wife finds that the coming of night, not the day, is when the absence of her husband becomes unbearable.
This is the Kural’s complaint exactly: the day is endurable, but the closing hour turns on the waiting wife.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes tells how the original double humans were cut in two, and each half so longed for its other that the halves clung together and began to die of hunger and idleness, unwilling to do anything apart.
— Plato, Symposium 191a-b (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
The myth makes the longing of separated lovers literally life-threatening.
The Kural’s claim that the evening ‘eats the lives’ of separated wives speaks the same idea of love-longing as something that can consume life itself.

Real Life Example

Hürrem Sultan's letters to Suleiman on campaign
16th century · Istanbul (Constantinople), Ottoman Empire (now Turkey)
Hürrem, a captive Slavic woman who became the favourite and later the legal wife of the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, wrote him long letters whenever he was away on military campaigns. Historians describe these letters as filled with her love and longing for him, her distress at his absence and her prayers for his safety in battle.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her letters are one of the best-documented records from the early modern world of a wife's grief at a husband's long absence, written by the wife herself.
The kural calls the evening a killing time for wives whose husbands are away, and Hürrem's letters show exactly that kind of wife, consumed by grief while her husband was far off on campaign.
