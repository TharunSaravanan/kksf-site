Kural 1330

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

ஊடுதல் காமத்திற்கு இன்பம் அதற்கின்பம் கூடி முயங்கப் பெறின்.
Oodudhal kaamaththirku inpam adharkinpam Kooti muyangap perin.

Meaning

Sulking is the joy of love, and the joy of sulking is to make up and be held close again.

Elementary

A pretend quarrel is a little game that makes love fun, and the best part of the game is the big hug when it is over. With this happy thought, the whole Thirukkural comes to an end.

University

The last couplet of the Kural is built as a chain: ūṭutal kāmattiṟku iṉpam (‘sulking is a delight to love’), ataṟku iṉpam (‘its delight in turn’) is kūṭi muyaṅkap peṟiṉ (‘if one gets to come together and embrace’, muyaṅku meaning to clasp). Solomon Pappaiah adds that the embrace should come aḷavu aṟintu (‘knowing the right measure’), i.e. the sulk must not be stretched too far, a point Parimelazhagar’s tradition also stresses, and Mu. Varadarasanar reads the embrace as the delight that crowns the sulk. Pope ends his translation with ‘The pleasure’s crowned when breast is clasped to breast.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; with this couplet both the kaṟpiyal and the whole 1,330-couplet work conclude, on a note of reconciled love.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Gita Govinda ends with Radha and Krishna reunited after her long sulk; Radha, now at ease, playfully asks Krishna to adorn her and rearrange her ornaments, and he gladly obeys.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 12 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s poem completes the cycle of separation, sulking and reconciliation with a tender, playful reunion.
Like the Kural’s last couplet, the Gita Govinda ends with the sulk resolved in loving closeness.
Christianity
“Defraud ye not one the other, except it be with consent for a time, that ye may give yourselves to fasting and prayer; and come together again”
— 1 Corinthians 7:5 (KJV; excerpt) [4]
Paul allows spouses to stay apart only for a short, agreed time, and then tells them to come together again.
The Kural’s cycle is similar: a short separation whose proper end is coming together again.
Islam
Ibn Hazm’s Ring of the Dove, a classic Andalusian treatise on love, devotes chapters to reproach between lovers and to union, treating gentle reproach as part of love and reunion as its sweetest moment.
— Ibn Hazm, The Ring of the Dove (tr. A. J. Arberry; paraphrase) [5]
Classical Arabic love literature sees lovers’ reproach and reconciliation as a natural rhythm of love.
The Kural’s last word, that the joy of a sulk is the reunion, fits the same courtly understanding of love.
Judaism
“His left hand should be under my head, And his right hand should embrace me.”
— Song of Songs 8:3 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs repeats this longing for the beloved’s embrace near its close.
The embrace is the crown of the Kural’s sulk, as it is the woman’s wish in the Song.
Buddhism
Nakulapita and Nakulamata tell the Buddha that since their marriage in youth neither has ever been unfaithful even in thought, and that they wish to see each other in this life and in lives to come; the Buddha tells them how a couple may stay together in future lives.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu; paraphrase) [7]
The Samajivina Sutta honours a married couple whose love has lasted a lifetime.
The Kural ends with lovers reunited after a quarrel; the Buddha’s model couple shows such reunion lasting a whole life and beyond.
Jainism
“Pleasant things (by themselves) do not cause indifference nor emotions (as anger, &c.); but by either hating or loving them, a man undergoes such a change through delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.101 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain text teaches that love and anger both arise from attachment, and that freedom lies in going beyond both.
The Kural celebrates the turn from anger (the sulk) to love (the embrace); Jainism sees both as two faces of the same attachment.
Sikhism
“Auspicious is that day, when God, the lofty, inaccessible and infinite, hugs me close in His embrace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 546, Raag Bihaagraa (Chhant), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan, in the voice of the soul-bride, rejoices in the day the infinite Lord takes her into His embrace.
The Kural's last couplet says the crowning delight of love is to be held close again after the sulk; Gurbani likewise crowns the soul's longing with the Beloved's embrace.
Greek Mythology
In Apuleius’ tale, after Psyche loses Cupid through her own doubt and endures many trials, the lovers are at last reunited, Psyche is made immortal, and their wedding is celebrated among the gods.
— Apuleius, Metamorphoses 6.23-24 (Cupid and Psyche; paraphrase) [10]
The story of Cupid and Psyche moves from estrangement through suffering to a lasting, joyful union.
Like the Kural’s final couplet, the tale ends with separation crowned by loving reunion.
Philosophy
Stendhal describes how, once love is established, a moment of doubt about the beloved gives rise to a second ‘crystallization’, in which love, reassured, becomes stronger than before.
— Stendhal, On Love (De l’amour, 1822), Book 1, chs. 2-3 (paraphrase) [11]
In Stendhal’s analysis, a passing doubt deepens love once it is resolved.
The Kural’s cycle of sulk and embrace follows the same pattern: a short uncertainty, then a stronger, sweeter love.

Real Life Example

Jayadeva composes the Gitagovinda
12th century · Bengal, India
In the 12th century the poet Jayadeva, at the court of King Lakshmanasena of Bengal, composed the Sanskrit song-poem Gitagovinda in twelve cantos. It tells how Radha, seeing Krishna with other cowherd girls, becomes jealous, rebukes him when he comes to her, and slowly softens as he pleads with her, until the lovers are joyfully united. The poem spread across India, inspired many paintings, and is still sung at the Jagannatha temple in Puri.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Gitagovinda is one of the best-known works of Indian literature to treat a lovers' quarrel and reconciliation as the very heart of love.
This last kural says sulking is the delight of love and the delight of sulking is to be held close again, the exact path from jealousy to joyful reunion that the Gitagovinda traces.
