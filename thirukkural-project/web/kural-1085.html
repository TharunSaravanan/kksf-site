Kural 1085

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

கூற்றமோ கண்ணோ பிணையோ மடவரல் நோக்கமிம் மூன்றும் உடைத்து.
Kootramo kanno pinaiyo madavaral nokkamim moondrum udaiththu.

Meaning

Is it Death, is it an eye, or is it a shy female deer? This young girl's look has all three in it at once.

Elementary

Some things can be several things at once: a rainbow is pretty, a bit mysterious and gone in a moment. The young man finds the girl's look like that. It is powerful enough to knock him over, warm like a friendly eye, and shy like a little deer.

University

The question again uses the doubting particle -o: kootramo (கூற்றமோ, 'Death?'), kanno (கண்ணோ, 'an eye?'), pinaiyo (பிணையோ, 'a hind/doe?'). Madavaral (மடவரல்) is a young, innocent woman, and nokkam (நோக்கம்) her look. Commentators explain the three qualities differently: Mu. Varadarasanar simply lists Death, eye and doe; Solomon Pappaiah reads the eye as a glance that spreads all over the viewer's body and the doe as a timid, fearful look; Puliyur Kesikan has 'the afflicting Death, the darting eye, the startled doe'. The point is that one look combines danger, warmth and shyness. Pope: 'Is it death's dart? or eye's bright beams? / Or fawn's shy glance? All three appear.' Identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa's Shakuntala begins with King Dushyanta chasing a frightened deer into a hermitage, where he meets Shakuntala, a girl raised among fawns, whose shy, darting glances the play repeatedly compares to a deer's.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 1 (paraphrase) [3]
Sanskrit love poetry often likens a young woman's timid glance to a fawn's.
The Kural's pinai (doe) uses the same image of a shy, deer-like look that overwhelms the hero.
Christianity
“I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, and by the hinds of the field, that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.”
— Song of Solomon 2:7 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs repeatedly ties love to gentle deer, roes and hinds.
The Kural's look has the shyness of a doe, the same animal the Song uses for love.
Islam
“And [for them are] fair women with large, [beautiful] eyes,”
— Qur'an 56:22 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the companions of Paradise by their large, beautiful eyes, which commentators often compare to the eyes of a gazelle.
The Kural, too, makes a woman's large, deer-like eyes the centre of her beauty.
Judaism
Proverbs urges a man to delight in the wife of his youth and likens her to a loving doe and a graceful mountain goat.
— Proverbs 5:18-19 (paraphrase; cf. JPS Tanakh on Sefaria) [6]
The Hebrew Bible, like Tamil poetry, uses the doe as an image of a beloved woman's grace.
The Kural's pinai, the shy doe in the girl's look, is the same figure.
Buddhism
When Mara fails to break the newly awakened Buddha, his three daughters, Craving, Discontent and Passion, try in many forms to tempt him, and fail.
— Samyutta Nikaya 4.25, Dhitaro Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist texts picture desire as several alluring forms working together.
The Kural's look holds three natures at once; Mara sends three daughters, each a face of desire.
Jainism
“As an impassioned deer allured (by a song) rushes into death, without being satisfied with the sound.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, as given in his summary of vv. 35-47) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana uses the deer, drawn helplessly by music, as an example of how passion for a sense object leads to death.
The Kural brings together a deer and Death in one look; Jain teaching joins the same two images.
Sikhism
“You have thousands of eyes, and yet You have no eyes. You have thousands of forms, and yet You do not have even one.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 13, Sohila, Raag Dhanasari, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the evening prayer Sohila, Guru Nanak marvels at a God who has countless eyes and forms yet is beyond all form.
The Kural's lover cannot decide whether the girl's look is Death, an eye or a shy doe, since it seems to hold all three natures; Guru Nanak is likewise baffled by a Beloved whose eyes and forms are many and none.
Greek Mythology
The hunter Actaeon happens to see the goddess Artemis bathing; she turns him into a stag, and he is killed by his own hounds.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.138-252 (paraphrase) [10]
In this myth a single sight of divine beauty brings a deer's form and death together.
The Kural's look combines Death and a deer in the same way, though gently and playfully.
Philosophy
Edmund Burke separated the beautiful (small, smooth, delicate, inspiring love) from the sublime (vast, powerful, inspiring a kind of terror), while noting that strong experiences can mix the two.
— Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757), Parts 2-3 (paraphrase) [11]
Burke's aesthetics explains how one sight can feel both gentle and terrifying.
The Kural's look is both a gentle doe (the beautiful) and Death (the terrible) at once.

Real Life Example

Leonardo da Vinci paints the Mona Lisa
about 1503–1519 · Florence, Italy (now in the Louvre, Paris)
Leonardo da Vinci began the portrait known as the Mona Lisa in Florence around 1503 and continued working on it for years; it is now in the Louvre. The sitter is traditionally identified as Lisa Gherardini, wife of the Florentine merchant Francesco del Giocondo. Her enigmatic smile and expression, painted with Leonardo's soft sfumato shading, have been studied and debated for centuries.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It is probably the most famous painting in the world, largely because viewers cannot settle on what her expression means.
The kural's lover sees several natures in one young woman's look at once; the Mona Lisa's expression is famous for seeming to hold more than one meaning at the same time.
