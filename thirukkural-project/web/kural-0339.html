Kural 339

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

உறங்கு வதுபோலுஞ் சாக்காடு உறங்கி விழிப்பது போலும் பிறப்பு.
Urangu vadhu polum saakkaadu urangi Vizhippadhu polum pirappu.

Meaning

Death is like falling asleep, and being born is like waking up after sleep.

Elementary

Every night you fall asleep, and every morning you wake up again. This kural says dying is like falling asleep, and being born is like waking up.

University

The couplet is the chapter's most compressed statement: “saakkaadu” (death) is like “uranguvadhu” (sleeping), and “pirappu” (birth) is like “urangi vizhippadhu” (waking after sleep). Most traditional commentators read it within the Indian framework of rebirth: death and birth follow one another as naturally as sleep and waking, so the cycle is familiar rather than terrifying, and the real goal is to escape it (veedu). Some modern readers, including Kalaignar's gloss, take a more secular line, stressing that death is a sleep from which one does not wake. The analogy is found across traditions: the Greek twins Hypnos and Thanatos, Socrates' ‘dreamless sleep’, the biblical ‘those who sleep in the dust’ shall awake, and the Quran's teaching that God takes souls both in death and in sleep.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Death is certain for one who has been born, and rebirth is inevitable for one who has died. Therefore, you should not lament over the inevitable.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.27 [3]
Krishna teaches that death and rebirth follow each other as surely as night and day.
The Kural's sleep-and-waking cycle is the Gita's cycle of death and rebirth.
Christianity
“Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep.”
— John 11:11 (KJV) [4]
Jesus speaks of the dead Lazarus as asleep and of raising him as waking him.
Death as sleep and return to life as waking is exactly the Kural's pair of images.
Islam
“Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die [He takes] during their sleep. Then He keeps those for which He has decreed death and releases the others for a specified term.”
— Quran 39:42 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran teaches that sleep and death are the same kind of event: in both, God takes the soul, returning it only to those who wake.
Both texts see sleep and death as closely related, and waking as a return of life.
Judaism
“And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt.”
— Daniel 12:2 (KJV) [4]
Daniel speaks of the dead as sleepers in the dust who will one day wake.
The same metaphor: death as sleep and a return to life as waking.
Buddhism
“Long is the night to the sleepless; long is the league to the weary. Long is worldly existence to fools who know not the Sublime Truth.”
— Dhammapada 60 [6]
The Buddha likens the long round of births and deaths (samsara) to a long night for those who do not understand the Dhamma.
Both texts use night and sleep imagery for the round of birth and death.
Jainism
Jain doctrine describes passing from one birth to the next as almost instantaneous: at death the soul, carrying its karmic body, moves to its next embodiment in at most a few moments (vigraha-gati), so death and rebirth follow each other continuously, like falling asleep and waking.
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 2.25–30 (on vigraha-gati, the soul's transit between births) [7]
In Jain cosmology there is no long gap between lives; the embodied soul leaves one body and takes the next almost at once until liberation ends the cycle.
The Kural likens death to sleep and birth to waking; Jain teaching on transmigration likewise treats death and rebirth as one continuous passage, though it uses no sleep metaphor.
Sikhism
“Death would not be called bad, O people, if one knew how to truly die.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 579, Raag Vadhans, Alahanian, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Alahanian, his hymns of mourning, Guru Nanak teaches that death is not to be dreaded by one who has learned to die to ego while living.
The Kural makes death as natural as falling asleep and birth as waking; Guru Nanak likewise strips death of its terror, though by a different route.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod makes Hypnos (Sleep) and Thanatos (Death) the twin children of Night, dwelling together at the edge of the world: Sleep gentle to men, Death with a heart of iron.
— Hesiod, Theogony 211-212, 758-766 [9]
In Greek myth, Sleep and Death are brothers, twin sons of Night.
Making Sleep and Death twins is the mythic form of the Kural's claim that death is like sleep.
Philosophy
“... but a sleep like the sleep of him who is undisturbed even by the sight of dreams, death will be an unspeakable gain.”
— Plato, Apology 40c-d (Jowett translation) [10]
Socrates tells his jurors that if death is like a dreamless sleep, it is nothing to fear and may even be a gain.
Socrates and the Kural both remove death's terror by comparing it to ordinary sleep.

Real Life Example

Socrates compares death to a dreamless sleep at his trial
399 BCE · Athens, Greece
In 399 BCE an Athenian jury found the philosopher Socrates guilty and sentenced him to death. In Plato's account of his defence speech, the Apology, Socrates told the jurors that death must be either a dreamless sleep, which he would count as a gain, or a migration of the soul to another place, which would be even better. He therefore argued that death was not something to be feared.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
The speech is one of the founding texts of Western philosophy's reflection on death, and it uses the familiar experience of deep sleep to make death less frightening.
Valluvar likens death to falling asleep and birth to waking, and Socrates, facing execution, likewise reasoned about death through the image of sleep and a passage to another life.
