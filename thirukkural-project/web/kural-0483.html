Kural 483

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

அருவினை யென்ப உளவோ கருவியான் காலம் அறிந்து செயின்.
Aruvinai Yenpa Ulavo Karuviyaan Kaalam Arindhu Seyin.

Meaning

Is any task truly impossible if you do it with the right tools and at the right time?

Elementary

Opening a tight jar is hard with bare hands. With the right tool and a little warm water it is easy. Most ‘impossible’ jobs become possible when you have the right tools and choose the right moment.

University

The couplet is a rhetorical question: aruvinai enpa ulavo, ‘are there things one could call hard deeds?’ The condition has two parts, karuviyaan (by means of instruments) and kaalam arindhu (knowing the time). Parimelazhagar takes karuvi to mean the resources of the state, such as army and treasury, together with the right methods. Neither timing without means nor means without timing is enough. The printed spelling ‘yenpa’ reflects sandhi with the preceding vowel; the word is enpa.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthaśāstra, Book IX, opens with a chapter on knowing power, place and time before invading. It weighs the strength of counsel, treasury and army against the enemy’s, and also the seasons and terrain in which each force works best.
— Kautilya, Arthaśāstra, Book IX, Chapter 1 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya treats means (śakti) and time (kāla) as separate variables to be judged together before any undertaking.
This is exactly the Kural’s pairing of karuvi (means) and kaalam (time).
Christianity
“See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil.”
— Ephesians 5:15–16 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to be careful and to ‘buy up’ the opportune moment (exagorazomenoi ton kairon) rather than waste it.
Wise, careful use of the moment is half of the Kural’s formula for making hard things achievable.
Islam
A man asked the Prophet whether he should leave his camel untied and trust in God, or tie it and trust. The Prophet told him to tie it and then trust.
— Jami‘ at-Tirmidhi 2517 [5]
Reliance on God (tawakkul) is not a reason to neglect practical means. The two go together.
The Kural’s success likewise needs the proper karuvi (means) as well as the favourable moment.
Judaism
“Whoso keepeth the commandment shall feel no evil thing: and a wise man’s heart discerneth both time and judgment. Because to every purpose there is time and judgment…”
— Ecclesiastes 8:5–6 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet says that every undertaking has its proper time and its proper manner (et u-mishpat), and that the wise person perceives both.
‘Time and judgment’ is a close Hebrew counterpart to the Kural’s ‘time and means’.
Buddhism
In the Bhūmija Sutta the Buddha compares a person who seeks results by the wrong method to someone pressing sand for oil or milking a cow by its horn. With the right method, pressing sesame seeds or milking from the udder, the result comes whether one hopes for it or not.
— Majjhima Nikāya 126 (Bhūmija Sutta) [6]
Results depend on using the right means. Wishing does not bring success and does not block it either.
The Kural’s claim that nothing is hard for the one with proper karuvi rests on the same logic of fitting method to goal.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra opens by declaring that right faith, right knowledge and right conduct together form the path to liberation. Jain teaching holds that a soul fit for liberation who takes up these three means will in time reach even that hardest of goals.
— Tattvartha Sutra 1.1 of Umasvati (paraphrase of the sutra's content) [7]
Jainism names the specific means (the three jewels) by which the most difficult of all undertakings, freeing the soul from karma, is accomplished.
The kural asks whether anything is impossible for one who acts with the right means at the right time; Jainism answers similarly about liberation, given the proper means applied over time.
Sikhism
“My strength has been restored, and my bonds have been broken; now, I can do everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1429, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 54) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
This salok answers the preceding cry of helplessness: with strength restored through God, every means (upai) becomes possible. Some traditions attribute it to Guru Gobind Singh, though the Guru Granth Sahib places it among Guru Tegh Bahadur's saloks.
The Kural asks whether anything is impossible for one with the right means at the right time; the salok declares that with strength and means restored, everything can be done.
Greek Mythology
Trapped in the Cyclops’ cave, Odysseus does not attack at once. He prepares an olive-wood stake, hardens it in the fire, gets Polyphemus drunk on strong wine, and blinds him only once the giant has fallen asleep.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 9 [9]
An impossible escape becomes possible through the right tools (stake, wine, a false name) used at the right moment (drunken sleep).
This is the Kural’s formula in story form: karuvi and kaalam together overcome a far stronger foe.
Philosophy
“Further, there can be no doubt that a work is spoilt when not done at the right time? … the husbandman will not make his own plough or mattock, or other implements of agriculture, if they are to be good for anything.”
— Plato, Republic, Book II, 370b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Founding the first city in speech, Socrates argues that work is ruined if its moment is let slip, and that each worker needs good implements made by others, so smiths and carpenters must join the city.
The right time and the right implements, the Kural's kaalam and karuvi, appear together as the two conditions of successful work.

Real Life Example

Eisenhower launches D-Day on Stagg's weather forecast
4-6 June 1944 · Southern England and Normandy, France
Planners of the Allied invasion of Normandy needed the right combination of moonlight, tides and weather, which limited the possible dates to 5-7 or 18-20 June 1944. On 4 June, forecasts of foul weather led General Dwight D. Eisenhower to postpone the landings by 24 hours, and the chief meteorologist, Group Captain James Stagg, then predicted a brief break in the weather, on which Eisenhower ordered the invasion to go ahead on 6 June. German forecasters, by contrast, expected conditions to remain unsuitable for a landing.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Vast resources of men, ships and equipment made the invasion possible, but it was the choice of the exact moment that allowed them to succeed.
The kural asks whether anything is impossible for one who acts with the right means and at the right time, and D-Day joined enormous means to a carefully chosen hour.
