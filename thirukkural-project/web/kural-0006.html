Kural 6

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

பொறிவாயில் யைந்தவித்தான் பொய்தீர் ஒழுக்க நெறிநின்றார் நீடுவாழ் வார்.
Pori-vaayil aindhu avithaan poi-theer ozhukka, neri ninRaar needu vaazhvaar.

Meaning

People who stay firmly on the pure and true path of God, who has controlled the desires of the five senses, will live long and well.

Elementary

We have five senses — seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching — and they can pull us all over the place wanting things. This kural says if you master your five senses instead of letting them boss you around, your life goes really well.

University

“Pori” refers to the five sense-faculties (indriyas); the divine is depicted as the paradigm of complete indriya-nigraha (sense-mastery), a central discipline across nearly all Indian contemplative systems. The verse makes mastery of the senses, rather than mere rule-following, the actual mechanism of a flourishing life — free from the erratic disturbance the unchecked senses cause.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Know the Self as the rider in a chariot, the body as the chariot; the intellect as the charioteer, and the mind as the reins.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.3 [3]
The Upanishad pictures the self as a passenger, the intellect as the driver, and the mind as the reins holding back the senses (the horses) — if the driver is careless, the horses run wild.
Same core image as the Kural: a well-run life depends on the senses being reined in, not left to run free.
Christianity
“But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:27 (KJV) [4]
Paul compares himself to an athlete in training, disciplining his own body so it doesn’t undermine everything else he’s working toward.
Same self-mastery logic as the Kural, applied to bodily discipline specifically.
Islam
“As for him who feared to stand before his Lord and restrained himself from [his] desire, then indeed, Paradise will be his refuge.”
— Qur'an 79:40-41, An-Nazi'at [5]
The verse names two things that earn Paradise: taking God’s judgment seriously, and actively holding oneself back from base desires.
Nearly identical logic to the Kural — restraining desire is the direct path to the best possible outcome.
Judaism
“He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city.”
— Proverbs 16:32 (Tanakh, Ketuvim / KJV) [4]
The proverb ranks self-control above military conquest — ruling your own reactions is harder, and more valuable, than ruling a city.
A vivid version of the Kural’s claim that mastering yourself, not external achievement, is what actually matters.
Buddhism
“Restraint in the eye is good, good is restraint in the ear... restraint everywhere is good. A bhikkhu restrained in every way is freed from all suffering.”
— Dhammapada, verses 360–361 [6]
The Buddha lists each sense one by one and calls restraint over each of them good, then says total restraint frees a person from suffering entirely.
Matches the Kural’s claim almost exactly: mastery of the senses is the direct cause of a settled, suffering-free life.
Jainism
“The five senses, anger, pride, delusion, and greed—difficult to conquer is one's self; but when that is conquered, everything is conquered.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
King Nami, renouncing his throne, tells Indra that the real victory is over the five senses and the passions; the very word Jina means 'conqueror' in this sense.
The Kural praises the one who 'quenched the five senses', and Jainism names its perfected teachers Jinas precisely for this conquest, holding up their path as the one to stand firm in.
Sikhism
“In the one village of the body, live the five thieves; they have been warned, but they still go out stealing. One who keeps his assets safe from the three modes and the ten passions, O Nanak! Attains liberation and emancipation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 503, Raag Gujri, Ashtpadi, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sikh teaching names five thieves living in the body: lust, anger, greed, attachment and ego. Whoever guards against them and against the restless passions reaches liberation.
The Kural ties a flourishing life to mastering the five senses. Guru Nanak ties liberation to holding the body's five inner thieves in check.
Greek Mythology
Knowing the Sirens’ song is fatally tempting, Odysseus has his crew’s ears sealed and has himself tied to the mast — engineering restraint in advance rather than trusting willpower in the moment.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 12 [9]
Odysseus doesn’t try to resist the temptation directly; he removes his own ability to give in to it.
A vivid mythic dramatization of the Kural’s point: real self-mastery over the senses is what gets you through safely.
Philosophy
Plato pictures the soul as a charioteer driving two horses — one noble, one unruly and appetite-driven — who only rises rather than crashes if the charioteer masters both.
— Plato, Phaedrus [10]
The charioteer (reason) has to actively control the wilder horse (appetite) or the whole chariot (the soul) is dragged down.
Essentially the same chariot image as the Hinduism entry above, arrived at independently in Greek philosophy — reinforcing the Kural's claim from a separate tradition.

Real Life Example

Xuanzang's pilgrimage to India
629-645 · Chang'an, China, to Nalanda, India, and back
The Chinese Buddhist monk Xuanzang left Chang'an in 629 without a travel permit and travelled across Central Asia to India to study Buddhism at its source. After years of study at the Nalanda monastery he returned to China in 645 to a hero's welcome, bringing hundreds of Buddhist texts. He spent the rest of his life translating them, and died in 664.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A monk's long fidelity to a disciplined path through deserts and mountains became one of the most productive scholarly lives in Asian history.
The kural promises long, flourishing life to those who hold firm to the pure path of the One who mastered the senses, and Xuanzang's steadfast monastic path carried him through a sixteen-year journey to a lifetime of fruitful work.
