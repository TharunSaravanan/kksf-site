Kural 1214

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

கனவினான் உண்டாகும் காமம் நனவினான் நல்காரை நாடித் தரற்கு.
Kanavinaan undaakum kaamam nanavinaan nalkaarai naatith thararku.

Meaning

Love's joy comes to me in my dreams, because the dream goes looking for the one who is not kind to me when I am awake, and brings him to me.

Elementary

Imagine a friendly dog that runs off every night and comes back pulling your best friend by the sleeve so you can play together. Here the dream is like that helpful dog: it goes and finds the husband who is far away and brings him home, just for the night.

University

Here the dream is not merely a place but an agent: nāṭi, ‘having searched out’, and taraṟku, ‘in order to give, bring’, make it a go-between who fetches the reluctant lover. The forms kaṉaviṉāṉ and naṉaviṉāṉ use the older instrumental ending -āṉ for -āl, a metrical variant noted in Tamil grammatical tradition. Kāmam here is the pleasure of love, not mere desire; nalkārai repeats the epithet ‘him who grants no grace’ from 1213. Varadarasanar reads the couplet as ‘love-incidents arise in dreams in order to seek out and bring the lover’, while Pappaiah and Kalaignar shift the stress to the joy the dream gives by bringing him. Pope: ‘Some pleasure I enjoy when him who loves not me / In waking hours, the vision searches out and makes me see.’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Princess Usha sees in a dream a handsome youth she has never met and falls in love; her friend Chitralekha paints portraits until Usha recognises him as Aniruddha, Krishna’s grandson, and then by her yogic power fetches him from Dvaraka to Usha’s chamber.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.62 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana tells of a dream that first reveals the beloved and then leads to his being searched out and brought to the dreamer.
Usha’s dream, which ends with the beloved literally fetched to her side, is the Kural’s ‘dream that seeks him out and brings him’ made into narrative.
Christianity
“I will rise now, and go about the city in the streets, and in the broad ways I will seek him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Solomon 3:2 (KJV) [4]
In the night-scene of the Song of Solomon, the bride rises from her bed to go searching the city for her beloved.
The night-search the bride undertakes is what the Kural’s dream does on the heroine’s behalf: it goes looking for the lover who does not come.
Islam
“O my father, this is the explanation of my vision of before. My Lord has made it reality.”
— Qur’an 12:100 (Saheeh International) [5]
Joseph, reunited at last with the father from whom he was torn, recognises in the reunion the fulfilment of his boyhood dream.
Joseph’s dream finally brought the separated family together, as the Kural’s dream brings the absent husband to his wife.
Judaism
“A person is shown in his dream only the thoughts of his heart.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 55b (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Shmuel bar Nahmani, citing Rabbi Yonatan, teaches that dreams draw their images from the dreamer’s waking thoughts.
This explains the Kural’s dream: the heart that dwells on the absent husband all day sends the dream to find him by night.
Buddhism
Nagasena tells King Milinda that dreams arise in six ways, among them from what the dreamer has been habitually occupied with, and from portents of what is to come.
— Milindapanha, the dilemma on dreams (paraphrase; cf. tr. T.W. Rhys Davids, SBE 36) [7]
Buddhist analysis recognises that the mind’s habitual preoccupations generate dream-images.
On Nagasena’s account, the Kural’s dream is fetched by the heroine’s own constant preoccupation with her husband.
Jainism
The Bhagavati Sutra’s discussion of dreams distinguishes several kinds, including a dream that arises from what one has been thinking about while awake (cinta-svapna).
— Bhagavati Sutra (Vyakhyaprajnapti), Shataka 16, on dreams (paraphrase) [8]
Jain canonical teaching recognises dreams produced by the waking mind’s preoccupations.
The Kural’s love-dream that ‘seeks out’ the husband is, in Jain terms, a cinta-svapna born of her constant thought of him.
Sikhism
“... the friendly True Guru has come into my dream beautifully attired and adored.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabit Savaiye, Kabit 205 (English translation as given on BaniDB) [9]
In one of his Kabits Bhai Gurdas describes the True Guru coming to him in a dream, dressed in beauty, so that the meeting denied in waking life happens in sleep.
The Kural says love's joy comes in dreams because the dream seeks out and brings the one who will not come by day; Bhai Gurdas likewise receives his Beloved through a dream.
Greek Mythology
Juno sends Iris to the house of Sleep, who dispatches his son Morpheus to take the form of the drowned king Ceyx and stand by the bed of his longing wife Alcyone, so that she may see him.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.583-670 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid has the god of dreams go out and fetch the absent husband’s likeness to his waiting wife.
Morpheus does exactly what the Kural’s dream does: he goes and brings the husband who cannot come in waking life.
Philosophy
Freud argues that, when the work of interpretation is completed, a dream proves to be the fulfilment of a wish.
— Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams, ch. 3 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.A. Brill) [11]
For Freud the dream stages the very satisfaction waking life withholds.
The Kural’s dream, which supplies the love denied by day, is a textbook instance of Freud’s wish-fulfilling dream, stated two thousand years earlier.

Real Life Example

Otto Loewi's dream leads to the frog-heart experiment
1921 · Graz, Austria
The pharmacologist Otto Loewi, professor at Graz, said that one night in 1921 he woke from a dream about an experiment, scribbled notes and in the morning could not read them. The next night the idea returned around 3 a.m. and he went straight to his laboratory. By transferring fluid from a frog heart slowed through its vagus nerve to a second heart, which then also slowed, he showed that nerves pass signals by chemicals, work for which he shared the 1936 Nobel Prize with Henry Dale.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Loewi's own account credits a dream with bringing back an answer that his waking mind had lost.
The kural says love's joy comes in dreams because the dream goes out, finds the one who will not come in waking life, and brings him back; Loewi's dream likewise fetched what waking could not hold.
