Kural 227

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

பாத்தூண் மரீஇ யவனைப் பசியென்னும் தீப்பிணி தீண்டல் அரிது.
Paaththoon mareeiyavanaip pasiyennum Theeppini theendal aridhu.

Meaning

The burning sickness called hunger will hardly ever touch someone who makes a habit of sharing their food.

Elementary

Imagine a kid who always shares their snacks at school. When that kid forgets their lunch one day, everyone is happy to share with them. People who share food are rarely left hungry.

University

பாத்தூண் (pāththūṇ) means ‘divided food’, food portioned out and shared, and மரீஇ indicates something practised until it is second nature. Hunger is called தீப்பிணி, a ‘fiery disease’ or ‘evil illness’, strengthening the medical image in which sharing works as a kind of immunity. The claim can be read at three levels: morally, as merit protecting the giver; socially, as a web of reciprocity in which those who share are shared with; and economically, as the observation that shared resources are used more efficiently. The Dhammapada’s ‘Hunger is the worst disease’ and the hadith that ‘food for two is sufficient for three’ show how widespread both the disease image and the sufficiency claim are.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“THE Gods have not ordained hunger to be our death: even to the well-fed man comes death in varied shape. The riches of the liberal never waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.1 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Rig Vedic hymn to Liberality says the generous never run out, while the stingy are left without help.
Same promise as the Kural: those who share will not be abandoned to want.
Christianity
“Give, and it shall be given unto you; good measure, pressed down, and shaken together, and running over, shall men give into your bosom.”
— Luke 6:38 (KJV) [4]
Jesus promises that those who give will receive generously in return.
Like the Kural, generosity is said to protect the giver from want.
Islam
“The food for two persons is sufficient for three, and the food of three persons is sufficient for four persons.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5392 [5]
The Prophet taught that food, when shared, stretches to feed more people.
Supports the Kural’s claim that sharing food, far from leaving one hungry, provides enough for all.
Judaism
“A person will never become impoverished from giving charity. No harm nor damage will ever be caused because of charity.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Matanot Aniyim 10:2 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides states as a principle that giving charity never makes anyone poor.
The same assurance as the Kural: the habitual giver is protected from want.
Buddhism
“Hunger is the worst disease, conditioned things the worst suffering. Knowing this as it really is, the wise realize Nibbana, the highest bliss.”
— Dhammapada 203 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada calls hunger the worst of diseases.
Shares the Kural’s striking image of hunger as a disease (தீப்பிணி), though it uses it to point to spiritual release rather than sharing.
Jainism
“Give what you are able, even though you have not the thousandth part of a measure of rice in the house, and then eat your meal; the wise call those in this birth wandering beggars whose chimneys smoke not in this earth, surrounded by the deep sea, who gave not alms in a former birth.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 4 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār says to share some food before eating, however little one has. It explains present-day hunger and beggary as the karmic result of not giving in a former birth.
Valluvar says hunger will hardly touch one who habitually shares food; the Nālaṭiyār makes the same link from the other side, since those who never shared become those whose cooking fires go out.
Sikhism
“The Siblings of Destiny meet together, and eat and spend, but these resources do not diminish; they continue to increase.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 186, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes a treasure inherited from the Father that grows rather than shrinks as the community shares and spends it together.
The Kural promises that the habit of sharing food keeps the fiery disease of hunger away; Guru Arjan likewise teaches that what is shared in common is never exhausted.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, King Erysichthon cuts down a sacred oak of Ceres and scorns the gods. Ceres sends Famine (Fames) to breathe insatiable hunger into him; he eats away his whole fortune, sells his daughter, and finally devours his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738-878 [10]
Hunger is portrayed as a personified curse that consumes a greedy, impious man.
The mirror image of the Kural: the man who respects nothing and shares nothing is the one the ‘fiery disease’ of hunger devours.
Philosophy
Kant’s fourth example in the Groundwork imagines a prosperous man who refuses to help others in hardship. Kant argues that he cannot will this as a universal law, because many situations may arise in which he himself would need the love and sympathy of others and would then have robbed himself of all hope of help.
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Section II (Ak. 4:423) [11]
Kant shows that a world without mutual help is one nobody could rationally choose, because everyone may one day be in need.
Offers a rational basis for the Kural’s claim: those who share are part of a practice of mutual aid that protects them too.

Real Life Example

Raiffeisen's bread association in the hunger winter
1846-1847 · Weyerbusch, Westerwald, Prussia (now Germany)
During the hunger winter of 1846-47, when crop failures drove up food prices in the Westerwald, Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen, the local mayor of Weyerbusch, founded an association to obtain bread and grain for the community. It bought flour, partly with private donations, baked bread in its own bakery and gave it to needy people on credit. The association is regarded as a forerunner of the cooperative movement that Raiffeisen later developed.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Pooling and sharing resources carried a poor rural district through famine and grew into a worldwide model of mutual self-help.
The kural says hunger rarely touches those who make sharing food a habit, and Raiffeisen's community kept hunger at bay by sharing its bread.
