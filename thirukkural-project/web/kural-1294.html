Kural 1294

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

இனிஅன்ன நின்னொடு சூழ்வார்யார் நெஞ்சே துனிசெய்து துவ்வாய்காண் மற்று.
Ini-anna ninnodu soozhvaaryaar nenjae thuniseydhu thuvvaaikaan matru.

Meaning

My heart, you will not sulk first and then enjoy making up. So who will ever plan such things with you again?

Elementary

Imagine you and a friend plan to act a little upset with someone to show your feelings, and then make up. But the moment that person walks in, your friend runs and hugs him. You might say, ‘I’m never planning anything with you again!’ That is what the wife says to her heart.

University

Tuṉi is a strong form of ūṭal (lovers’ sulking), and tuvvāy means ‘you will not enjoy’; the commentators read the line as ‘you will not first sulk and then enjoy (the reunion that follows)’. In Tamil love poetics the reconciliation after ūṭal is itself a pleasure, which is why the wife complains that her eager heart skips it. Iṉi aṉṉa niṉṉoṭu cūḻvār yār, ‘hereafter who will consult with you about such things?’, is her mock threat to stop taking the heart into her counsels; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar add ‘I will not’. Pope renders ‘See, thou first show offended pride, and then submit, I bade; / Henceforth such council who will share with thee my heart?’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, when Krishna comes back to Radha, she at first holds on to her wounded pride (mana), and her friend urges her to give up the sulk and go to him while the moment lasts.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 9 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva stages the lovers’ quarrel and its reconciliation as a set stage of love.
Radha can sustain the sulk that the Kural’s heart cannot; both poems treat quarrel-then-reunion as part of love’s pleasure.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath:”
— Ephesians 4:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul allows anger but tells believers to let it go quickly.
The wife’s heart lets its anger go even faster than Paul asks, before it has served its purpose.
Islam
“Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good;”
— Qur’an 3:134 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who hold back anger and forgive.
The wife’s heart forgives at once, but she wishes it had held its displeasure a little longer.
Judaism
“I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them? My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door, and my heart was moved for him.”
— Song of Songs 5:3-4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman in the Song first makes excuses not to open to her beloved, then her heart is stirred and she rises.
Her show of reluctance melts the moment he appears, much as the wife’s heart gives up its sulk at once.
Buddhism
“Just as a fletcher straightens an arrow shaft, even so the discerning man straightens his mind — so fickle and unsteady, so difficult to guard.”
— Dhammapada 33 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha calls the mind fickle and hard to keep on a chosen course.
The wife’s heart could not keep to the plan they had made; it wavered the moment he came home.
Jainism
“Pleasant things (by themselves) do not cause indifference nor emotions (as anger, &c.); but by either hating or loving them, a man undergoes such a change through delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.101 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that it is one’s own love or aversion, not the object, that throws the mind from one state to another.
The wife’s heart swings from planned anger to eager love the moment it sees him, just the kind of change the sutra describes.
Sikhism
“The fickle mind continuously runs after fleeting things.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 222, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes a mind that will not stay put or follow sound counsel but chases whatever draws it.
The Kural's speaker complains that her heart will not keep to her plan of sulking first; Gurbani likewise names the restless mind that cannot be held to any plan.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus finally returns, Penelope does not rush to embrace him; though her son calls her heart harder than stone, she holds back and tests him with the secret of their bed before she yields and runs to him.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.85-240 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows a wife who keeps her composure before the joyful reunion.
Penelope manages the reserve before reunion that the Kural’s wife wanted, and her heart would not allow.
Philosophy
In Terence’s comedy The Girl from Andros, a character remarks that lovers’ quarrels are the renewal of love (amantium irae amoris integratio est).
— Terence, Andria, line 555 (paraphrase) [11]
The line, which became proverbial, holds that a quarrel between lovers ends by making their love fresh again.
This is exactly the pleasure the wife says her heart throws away by refusing to quarrel first.

Real Life Example

Gustav Mahler walks with Sigmund Freud in Leiden
August 1910 · Leiden, Netherlands
In July 1910 the composer Gustav Mahler, then fifty and at the height of his fame, discovered that his wife Alma had become romantically involved with the young architect Walter Gropius. In August he travelled to Leiden in the Netherlands to consult Sigmund Freud, who came by tram from his holiday at Noordwijk aan Zee. The two men talked for about four hours while walking through the city; Mahler died nine months later.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Faced with the threat of losing Alma, Mahler did not break with her but sought to understand his own feelings and keep the marriage.
The kural’s wife complains that her heart will not first sulk and then enjoy the making-up; Mahler, instead of turning his hurt into a quarrel, put his effort into holding on to the one he loved.
