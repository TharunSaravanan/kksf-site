Kural 807

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

அழிவந்த செய்யினும் அன்பறார் அன்பின் வழிவந்த கேண்மை யவர்.
Azhivandha seyyinum anbaraar anbin vazhivandha kenmai yavar.

Meaning

People whose friendship has grown through many years of love do not stop loving, even when their friends do things that cause them great harm.

Elementary

Think of a very old tree with deep roots. A storm can break some branches, but the tree is still standing the next day. A friendship that has grown for years is like that: even a big hurt doesn’t pull it out of the ground.

University

Azhivandha (அழிவந்த) are ‘things that bring destruction’, and the concessive seyyinum (‘even if they do’) leads to anbaraar (அன்பறார், ‘they do not cut off love’). The subject is anbin vazhivandha kenmaiyavar, ‘those whose kenmai (கேண்மை, friendship) has come along the way of love’, which commentators take as long-standing, inherited affection. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as friendship come down ‘from of old’ (தொன்றுதொட்டு), and Solomon Pappaiah as long-standing friendship. Puliyur Kesigan alone shifts the subject, reading it as ‘our love for them will not be cut off’. The couplet is the strongest statement of the chapter’s ideal and has been criticised by modern readers as possibly encouraging tolerance of harm; the traditional commentators keep it within the framework of a tested, virtuous friendship (compare chapter 80). The text is identical in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After Rama helps him win back his kingdom, Sugriva forgets his promise to search for Sita while he enjoys his restored court. Rama is angered and sends Lakshmana to warn him, but when Sugriva repents, Rama receives him back and the alliance holds.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda [3]
In the Kishkindha Kanda, a friend’s neglect threatens Rama’s whole cause, yet Rama does not abandon the friendship.
Rama keeps his affection despite an act that threatened ruin, though his initial anger is greater than the Kural’s ideal allows.
Christianity
Peter, one of Jesus’ closest companions, denies him three times on the night of his arrest. After the resurrection, Jesus asks Peter three times whether he loves him and entrusts him with feeding his sheep.
— Luke 22:54-62; John 21:15-17 [4]
The betrayal is answered with restored intimacy and trust, not rejection.
Jesus’ love for Peter does not end even after the deepest failure, the Kural’s anbaraar in Gospel form.
Islam
“And let not those of virtue among you and wealth swear not to give [aid] to their relatives and the needy and the emigrants for the cause of Allāh, and let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allāh should forgive you?”
— Qur’an 24:22 (Sahih International); occasion of revelation per Ibn Kathir [5]
Classical commentators link this verse to Abu Bakr, who had vowed to stop supporting his relative Mistah after Mistah joined in slandering Aisha. After the verse came down, Abu Bakr resumed the support.
A long-standing bond survives a grievous injury, which is what the Kural asks of those whose friendship comes down the path of love.
Judaism
When Saul rages at Jonathan that as long as David lives Jonathan will never be king, Jonathan still defends David, warns him, and renews their covenant, giving up his own claim to the throne.
— 1 Samuel 20:30-42 [6]
Jonathan’s loyalty to David costs him his inheritance and his father’s favour.
His love continues even though the friendship ruins his prospects, as the Kural describes.
Buddhism
Devadatta, the Buddha’s cousin and an early follower, tries to split the Sangha and to have the Buddha killed. The Buddha meets him without hatred, and later tradition says Devadatta will himself eventually attain awakening.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Cullavagga VII; later commentarial tradition [7]
Even repeated attempts at destruction by a kinsman and former companion do not turn the Buddha to enmity.
This is the outer limit of the Kural’s ideal: love not cut off despite ruinous acts.
Jainism
“Men will not be angry, but be patient concerning the friendship of those whom they themselves have courted, though these persons act towards them with constant hate.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 2 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet compares friends to good water that keeps bursting the bank yet is still drawn up: those who have chosen a friend stay patient even when the friend treats them harshly.
The Kural says those whose friendship has come a long way through love do not stop loving even when friends bring them ruin, and the Naladiyar likewise asks patience toward chosen friends who repeatedly do harm.
Sikhism
“Farid, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger. Your body shall not suffer from any disease, and you shall obtain everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381-1382, Salok Sheikh Farid 78 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, counsels returning good for harm and refusing to let anger take root in the mind.
The Kural says those whose friendship has come down a long road of love do not stop loving even when their friends bring them ruin; Farid's counsel to answer evil with goodness is the disposition that makes such love possible.
Greek Mythology
Theseus joins Pirithous on his reckless plan to carry off Persephone from the underworld, though he knows it is folly, and both are trapped on enchanted seats in Hades until Heracles frees Theseus.
— Apollodorus, Epitome 1.23-24; Plutarch, Theseus 31-35 [10]
Theseus stays loyal to his friend’s ruinous plan and pays for it.
He keeps the bond even when the friend leads him to ruin, a vivid if cautionary version of the Kural.
Philosophy
“If they are capable of being reformed one should rather come to the assistance of their character or their property, inasmuch as this is better and more characteristic of friendship.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says that when a friend goes wrong, the friendly response is to help him recover rather than to break off at once.
Like Valluvar he favours keeping faith with an erring friend, though he allows a break with the incurable.

Real Life Example

Joachim keeps playing Brahms and accepts the Double Concerto after their rift
1880s; October 1887 · Baden-Baden and Cologne, Germany
During the breakdown of violinist Joseph Joachim's marriage, Johannes Brahms wrote a private letter of support to Amalie Joachim that was later read in the divorce proceedings, and Joachim broke off their long friendship. Joachim nevertheless kept performing Brahms's music, and in 1887 Brahms wrote the Double Concerto for violin and cello as a gesture of reconciliation. Joachim rehearsed it with Brahms at Baden-Baden, played it at the premiere in Cologne in October 1887, and received the manuscript inscribed to him for whom it was written.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A friendship of more than thirty years survived a deep personal injury, and the reconciliation produced one of the great works of the violin repertoire.
The kural says those whose friendship has come down a long road of love do not stop loving even when friends do things that bring them ruin, and Joachim went on championing Brahms's music after Brahms's letter had harmed him in court.
