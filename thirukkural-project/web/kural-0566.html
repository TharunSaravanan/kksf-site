Kural 566

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

கடுஞ்சொல்லன் கண்ணிலன் ஆயின் நெடுஞ்செல்வம் நீடின்றி ஆங்கே கெடும்.
Kadunjollan kannilan aayin nedunjelvam Needindri aangae kedum.

Meaning

If a ruler speaks harshly and shows no kindness, his great riches will not last. They will disappear right then.

Elementary

A shopkeeper who is rude and never smiles at customers soon has an empty shop, no matter how many goods are on the shelves. The kural says a leader with harsh words and no kindness loses even great riches very quickly.

University

கடுஞ்சொல்லன் is one given to harsh words, and கண்ணிலன், literally ‘eyeless’, means lacking kaṇṇōṭṭam, the gracious, considerate regard that is the subject of the very next chapter (58, கண்ணோட்டம்). The couplet thus bridges two chapters: without kindly regard, even நெடுஞ்செல்வம் (vast, long-accumulated wealth) perishes நீடின்றி ஆங்கே, without delay, on the spot. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses கண்ணிலன் as கண்ணோட்டம் இல்லாதவன் and Pappaiah as lacking முகதாட்சண்யம் (considerateness), while Kalaignar renders it as a ‘merciless heart’. The contrast நெடும் (long) and நீடு இன்றி (without lasting) is a deliberate verbal play. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna counts gentle, non-agitating speech as a form of spiritual discipline (tapas).
The Kural’s கடுஞ்சொல்லன் fails exactly this discipline of speech, with worldly as well as spiritual cost.
Christianity
“Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another.”
— Ephesians 4:31–32 (KJV) [4]
Paul pairs the rejection of harsh speech with a call to tender-hearted kindness.
The same two faults the Kural names, harsh words and lack of kindly regard, are what Paul tells believers to put away.
Islam
“So by mercy from Allāh, [O Muḥammad], you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude [in speech] and harsh in heart, they would have disbanded from about you.”
— Quran 3:159 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran tells the Prophet that his gentleness held the community together, and that rudeness and hardness of heart would have scattered it.
‘Rude in speech and harsh in heart’ is almost a translation of கடுஞ்சொல்லன் கண்ணிலன், and the loss it brings is immediate.
Judaism
“A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger.”
— Proverbs 15:1 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that harsh words provoke hostility while gentle ones defuse it.
The ruler’s grievous words stir up the anger that quickly undoes his fortune.
Buddhism
“He who utters gentle, instructive and truthful words, who imprecates none — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 408 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada counts gentle, non-cursing speech among the marks of the truly noble person.
The Kural’s harsh-tongued ruler is the opposite of the Buddha’s gentle speaker.
Jainism
“Disobedient, rough speaking, ill-behaved pupils will exasperate even a gentle teacher; but those will soon win even a hot-tempered teacher who humour him and are polite.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The text sets rough speech, which turns away even the gentle, against courtesy, which wins over even the hot-tempered.
The Kural says a harsh-tongued ruler without kindly regard loses his wealth at once; the Jain verse likewise treats harsh speech as something that loses goodwill and courtesy as what earns it.
Sikhism
“who speaks insipid words with his tongue, is ruined, again and again.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 594, Raag Vadhans, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das warns that the person whose tongue utters harsh, graceless (phikka) words brings ruin on himself again and again.
The Kural says the vast wealth of a ruler who is harsh in speech and lacks kindly regard will not last; Guru Amar Das likewise ties harsh speech to repeated ruin.
Greek Mythology
Ovid tells how King Erysichthon scornfully cut down Ceres’ sacred oak, ignoring pleas for mercy; the goddess cursed him with endless hunger, and he devoured his whole fortune until he was left with nothing.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738–878 [9]
A proud, pitiless king sees his great wealth vanish almost at once.
Harshness and lack of pity destroy even ‘long wealth’ on the spot, as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
Xenophon’s Cyrus holds his empire together by philanthropia, courteous speech, generous gifts and kindly attention to others, arguing that goodwill is a surer treasure than hoarded gold.
— Xenophon, Cyropaedia, Book 8, chs. 2–4 [10]
The Cyropaedia presents kindness and pleasant speech as the true foundations of lasting power and wealth.
The positive image of the Kural: a ruler who is gentle in word and kindly in regard keeps both his people and his riches.

Real Life Example

Sultan Osman II's harsh measures against the Janissaries end his reign
1621-1622 · Istanbul, Ottoman Empire (now Turkey)
After the Ottoman army's failure at Khotin in 1621, the young Sultan Osman II blamed the Janissary corps, cut their pay and closed their coffee shops, and planned to raise a new army to break their power. Hearing of his plans and already resentful of his treatment, the Janissaries revolted in May 1622. They deposed him on 19 May and killed him the following day, restoring his uncle Mustafa I to the throne.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Osman's reign of about four years ended almost at once when the soldiers he had treated with open contempt turned against him.
The kural warns that a ruler harsh in speech and lacking kindly regard loses even great wealth on the spot, and Osman's treatment of his own troops cost him his throne in a matter of days.
