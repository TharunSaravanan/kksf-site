Kural 257

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

உண்ணாமை வேண்டும் புலாஅல் பிறிதொன்றன் புண்ணது உணர்வார்ப் பெறின்.
Unnaamai vendum pulaaal pirithondran, Punnathu unarvaarp perin.

Meaning

If people understood that meat is really the wound of another living creature, they would stop eating it.

Elementary

If you saw a hurt animal with a sore, you would never want to eat that sore. The Kural says meat is really just that — a wound cut from another living creature. If we remembered that, we wouldn’t want to eat it.

University

The force of the couplet lies in a single noun, புண் (wound, sore, ulcer). By renaming meat as “another creature’s wound,” Valluvar strips away the culinary vocabulary that disguises its origin, a technique of moral re-description. The conditional உணர்வார்ப் பெறின் (“if one gains those who perceive / if one comes to perceive”) makes the argument depend on clear perception: the problem is not ignorance of a rule but a failure to see what is in front of us. The metrical lengthening in புலாஅல் (alapedai) lingers on the word “flesh,” as if forcing the reader to look at it. Plutarch and Ovid’s Pythagoras make the very same move, calling meat “sores” and “pitiful wounds.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Having well considered the (disgusting) origin of flesh and the (cruelty of) fettering and slaying corporeal beings, let him entirely abstain from eating flesh.”
— Manusmriti 5.49 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu tells the reader to reflect on where meat comes from — the binding and killing of living beings — and then to give it up entirely.
Same method as the Kural: once one sees the true origin of flesh, the desire to eat it should end.
Christianity
“Prove thy servants, I beseech thee, ten days; and let them give us pulse to eat, and water to drink.”
— Daniel 1:12 (KJV) [4]
Daniel and his friends refuse the Babylonian king’s meat and wine and ask for vegetables and water instead, and emerge healthier.
An example of deliberate refusal of meat as a matter of conscience, though Daniel’s concern is ritual purity rather than the animal’s wound.
Islam
“Prohibited to you are dead animals, blood, the flesh of swine, and that which has been dedicated to other than Allah, and [those animals] killed by strangling or by a violent blow or by a head-long fall or by the goring of horns…”
— Quran 5:3 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran forbids blood, carrion and animals killed violently, focusing attention on how an animal died.
Both texts ask the eater to look at the death and bleeding behind the meat; Islam responds with purity laws, the Kural with abstention.
Judaism
“But flesh with the life thereof, which is the blood thereof, shall ye not eat.”
— Genesis 9:4 (KJV) [4]
Even when meat is permitted, the Torah forbids eating it with its blood, because the blood is the animal’s life.
Like the Kural, it insists that meat is bound up with another creature’s life and must not be eaten as if it were mere food.
Buddhism
“Taking life, beating, wounding, binding, stealing, lying, deceiving, worthless knowledge, adultery; this is stench. Not the eating of meat.”
— Amagandha Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.2 [6]
In the Amagandha Sutta, a Buddha answers a vegetarian Brahmin by saying true impurity lies in evil deeds, not in eating meat.
An honest counterpoint: this early text denies that meat itself defiles, whereas the Kural insists meat is inherently a wound — yet it still lists wounding and taking life as the real impurity.
Jainism
“all beings, those with two, three, four senses, plants, those with five senses, and the rest of creation, (experience) individually pleasure or displeasure, pain, great terror, and unhappiness.” … “some kill (them) for the sake of their flesh, some kill them for the sake of their blood”
— Acaranga Sutra I.1.6.2, I.1.6.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [7]
The Acaranga first insists that every creature feels pain and terror, then lists the killing of animals for their flesh and blood among the sinful acts a wise man must give up.
Valluvar says that if people truly saw flesh as another creature's wound they would not eat it; the Acaranga likewise asks the reader to see the pain and terror behind every piece of flesh taken.
Sikhism
“You seize a living creature, and then bring it home and kill its body; you have killed only the clay.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1350, Raag Prabhaatee, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir strips away the religious language around ritual slaughter and describes what actually happens: a living creature is caught and its body destroyed, and he adds that this 'offering' gains the slayer nothing.
Valluvar makes people see meat for what it is, another creature's wound; Kabir similarly describes the slaughter in plain words so it can no longer be dressed up as piety.
Greek Mythology
Ovid’s Pythagoras compares flesh-eaters to the Cyclops Polyphemus, who devoured Odysseus’ companions raw: people “tear, with cruel teeth, at pitiful wounds, recalling Cyclops’s practice.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses XV (trans. A. S. Kline); Homer, Odyssey 9 [9]
The monstrous cannibal Cyclops becomes a mirror for ordinary meat-eating.
By invoking the Cyclops, the myth makes the eater see the torn body in front of him — the Kural’s ‘wound.’
Philosophy
“How was it that the pollution did not turn away his taste, which made contact with the sores of others and sucked juices and serums from mortal wounds?”
— Plutarch, Moralia, On the Eating of Flesh I.1 (993B; Loeb trans.) [10]
Plutarch asks how the first meat-eater could bear to put the sores and wounds of another creature to his lips.
Precisely the Kural’s image: flesh is another creature’s wound, and seeing it so should end the appetite.

Real Life Example

Upton Sinclair's The Jungle exposes the meatpacking industry
1906 · Chicago, Illinois, and Washington, D.C., United States
Upton Sinclair's novel The Jungle, first serialised in 1905 and published as a book in 1906, described the conditions inside Chicago's meatpacking plants. Sinclair wrote it to draw attention to the workers, but readers were most shocked by what it revealed about how meat was produced. President Theodore Roosevelt ordered an investigation, and in 1906 Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act.
— Sources: [11] [12]
When the public was made to see clearly where its meat came from, it recoiled and demanded change.
The kural says people would stop eating flesh if they truly perceived it as another creature's wound, and The Jungle showed how strongly people react when the reality behind meat is laid bare.
