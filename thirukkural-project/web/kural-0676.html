Kural 676

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

முடிவும் இடையூறும் முற்றியாங்கு எய்தும் படுபயனும் பார்த்துச் செயல்.
Mudivum idaiyoorum mutriyaangu eydhum Padupayanum paarththuch cheyal.

Meaning

Act only after looking at what it takes to finish the task, the problems that may come up along the way, and the gain it will bring at the end.

Elementary

Before starting a big jigsaw puzzle, ask: How long will it take to finish? What could go wrong, like the cat scattering pieces? And will the finished picture be worth it? If yes, begin.

University

Mudivu here is read by Parimelazhagar as the effort (muyarchi) required to bring the work to completion, idaiyooru as the hindrances that may come in between, and padupayan as the large yield obtained when it is completed (mutriyaangu eydhum). The kural thus sets out a three-part cost-benefit appraisal: cost of completion, risk, and return. Mu. Varadarasanar reads mudivu more simply as 'the way of completing the task', while Kalaignar reorders the three items but keeps the sense. The older sandhi text reads முடிவு மிடையூறு முற்றியாங் கெய்தும், with no difference of meaning. The idea anticipates the Gospel image of counting the cost before building, and Epictetus' advice to consider what comes before and after any undertaking.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That action is declared to be in the mode of ignorance, which is begun out of delusion, without thought to one’s own ability, and disregarding consequences, loss, and injury to others.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.25 [3]
Krishna describes the lowest (tamasic) kind of action as one started without weighing consequences, loss and one's own capacity.
The Gita condemns exactly what the Kural forbids: acting without first looking at effort, obstacles and outcome.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?”
— Luke 14:28 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses the image of a sensible builder who calculates whether he can complete a tower before laying its foundation.
Counting the cost of finishing is the Kural's mudivu: weighing what completion will demand before beginning.
Islam
“O Messenger of Allah! Shall I tie it and rely (upon Allah), or leave it loose and rely (upon Allah)?” He said: “Tie it and rely (upon Allah).”
— Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2517 (graded hasan by Darussalam) [5]
The Prophet tells a man to take sensible precautions against a foreseeable risk and then trust in God.
Tying the camel is guarding against the Kural's idaiyooru, the obstacles that can be foreseen and prevented before acting.
Judaism
“Reckon the loss [that may be sustained through the fulfillment] of a commandment against the reward [accruing] thereby, and the gain [that may be obtained through the committing] of a transgression against the loss [entailed] thereby.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Judah the Prince teaches weighing losses against rewards before acting.
This is the Kural's balance of cost and padupayan (great gain), applied to moral choices.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells his son Rahula to reflect before any action whether it will lead to harm for himself or others, to reflect again while doing it, and again after it is done.
— Majjhima Nikaya 61, Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta [7]
Like looking in a mirror, one checks an act's results at every stage.
The Buddha, like the Kural, asks that consequences be looked at before acting rather than discovered afterwards.
Jainism
“Three merchants set out on their travels, each with his capital; one of them gained there much, the second returned with his capital, and the third merchant came home after having lost his capital.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.14-15 (cf. 7.19) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The parable treats human life as capital that can be increased, preserved or lost, and adds that, considering what is at stake, one should weigh the outcomes in one's mind.
The kural's call to weigh the effort, the obstacles and the gain before acting is reflected in this Jain parable of reckoning the stakes and possible returns of a venture.
Sikhism
“So throw the dice in such a way, that you shall not lose with your Lord and Master. Do those deeds which shall bring you profit.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (Pauri 21), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa ki Vaar Guru Nanak uses the image of a calculated move in a game, urging that one's efforts be chosen so that they end in gain rather than loss before the Lord.
The Kural asks one to weigh the effort, the obstacles and the great gain of a task before acting; Guru Nanak likewise asks for deeds weighed by their final profit.
Greek Mythology
A thirsty fox falls into a well; a goat jumps in after being told the water is fine; the fox climbs out on the goat's back and leaves it stranded, saying it should have looked for a way out before jumping in.
— Aesop, The Fox and the Goat [10]
Aesop's fable is traditionally summed up as look before you leap.
The goat fails to weigh the Kural's mudivu: how the venture is to be completed and brought safely to an end.
Philosophy
“In every affair consider what precedes and follows, and then undertake it. Otherwise you will begin with spirit; but not having thought of the consequences, when some of them appear you will shamefully desist.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 29 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus uses the example of the Olympic athlete, who must count the training, hardships and possible defeat before committing.
His advice lines up item by item with the Kural: effort to completion, obstacles along the way, and whether the gain is worth it.

Real Life Example

Ferdinand de Lesseps builds the Suez Canal
1854-17 November 1869 · Isthmus of Suez, Egypt
In 1854 the Egyptian ruler Said Pasha granted Ferdinand de Lesseps a concession to form a company to dig a canal between the Mediterranean and the Red Sea. Despite opposition from Britain, difficulty selling shares outside France, a cholera epidemic in 1865 and labour problems, digging began on 25 April 1859 and the waters of the two seas met in August 1869. The canal was formally opened on 17 November 1869, greatly shortening the sea route between Europe and Asia.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The project faced heavy costs and repeated obstacles, but the enormous gain it promised for world trade carried it through, and it remains one of the most important waterways in the world.
The kural asks one to weigh the effort needed, the obstacles on the way and the great gain at the end before acting, and the Suez Canal was a venture whose huge eventual return justified a decade of costly struggle.
