Kural 447

Seeking the Aid of Great Men · Periyaaraith Thunaikkodal · பெரியாரைத் துணைக்கோடல்

இடிக்குந் துணையாரை ஆள்வாரை யாரே கெடுக்குந் தகைமை யவர்.
Idikkun thunaiyaarai aalvaarai yaare Kedukkun thakaimai yavar.

Meaning

Who can ruin people who keep friends that scold them when they are wrong?

Elementary

A good friend will tell you when you are doing something wrong, even if it hurts to hear. This kural asks: if you keep friends like that close, who could ever bring you down?

University

‘Idikkum’ means to strike or rebuke — Parimelazhagar glosses it as reproving the king when he errs. The couplet is a rhetorical question that assumes its answer: none. What protects the ruler is not deference but correction. The idea is especially pointed in a political treatise, since rulers are the persons least likely to hear criticism. ‘Aalvaar’ (those who rule / keep) suggests the king must actively maintain such critics in his service rather than merely tolerate them.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Sulabhāḥ puruṣā rājan satataṃ priyavādinaḥ; apriyasya ca pathyasya vaktā śrotā ca durlabhaḥ.”
— Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda (Maricha to Ravana); also Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura Niti) [4]
‘O king, men who always speak pleasantly are easy to find; but one who speaks what is unpleasant yet wholesome, and one who listens to it, are hard to find.’
Both value the rare counselor who says what the ruler needs rather than wants to hear.
Christianity
“Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (KJV) [5]
A friend’s painful honesty is trustworthy; an enemy’s flattery is a trap.
The rebuking friend as a safeguard, as in the Kural.
Islam
The Prophet said that the believer is the mirror of his fellow believer, and his brother who guards him against loss and protects him in his absence.
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4918 [6]
A true brother shows you your faults as a mirror does and protects your interests.
The corrective companion who shields from ruin mirrors the Kural’s rebuking ally.
Judaism
The prophet Nathan confronts King David over Bathsheba and Uriah with the parable of the poor man’s lamb, declaring “Thou art the man”; David repents, and though punished, his throne is preserved.
— 2 Samuel 12:1–13 [5]
David keeps a prophet who dares to rebuke even the king, and his acceptance of rebuke saves his dynasty.
A king who keeps a rebuker survives his own error — the Kural’s point exactly.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure. It is always better, and never worse, to cultivate such an association.”
— Dhammapada 76 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares a person who points out our faults to a guide who leads us to buried treasure.
Near-identical: the reproving companion is a treasure and a protection.
Jainism
“Any instruction the wise ones may give me in a kind or a rough way, I shall devotedly accept, thinking that it is for my benefit.” … “Wise, fearless monks consider even a rough instruction as a benefit, but the fools hate it, though it produces patience and purity of mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.27, 1.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's lecture on discipline teaches welcoming correction from the wise, even when it is harsh, because it brings patience and purity, while fools resent it.
The kural asks who could ruin one who keeps companions able to rebuke him. The Jain text counts that rebuke itself as a benefit and marks the wise by their readiness to welcome it.
Sikhism
“If my heart is pure, and I am slandered, then the slanderer washes my clothes.” … “One who slanders me is my friend.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 339, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir welcomes those who expose his faults, saying such critics cleanse him and are really his friends.
The Kural asks who can ruin one who keeps companions able to rebuke him; Kabir likewise treats the one who points out his faults as a friend who keeps him clean.
Greek Mythology
In Odyssey 12, Circe warns Odysseus of the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis; heeding her, he has his men stop their ears with wax and bind him to the mast, and passes the Sirens safely.
— Homer, Odyssey 12 [10]
Odysseus survives because he accepts hard, unwelcome instructions — even about his own weakness.
Keeping and obeying a counselor who tells one hard truths keeps one from ruin.
Philosophy
Plutarch argues that frank speech (parrhesia) is the language of true friendship and a kind of medicine, and that the flatterer who only pleases is the greatest danger to the powerful.
— Plutarch, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend (Moralia) [11]
Real friends tell painful truths for our good; flatterers ruin us.
The same distinction between the rebuking friend and the destructive flatterer.

Real Life Example

Gandhi answers Tagore's public criticism with respect
1921 · Calcutta (now Kolkata) and Ahmedabad, India
In 1921 Rabindranath Tagore published an essay, The Call of Truth, in the Modern Review, criticising parts of Mahatma Gandhi's non-cooperation movement as a negative and inward-looking turning away from cooperation with the wider world. Gandhi replied in his journal Young India on 13 October 1921 in an essay he titled The Great Sentinel, engaging Tagore's arguments seriously. The two continued to debate openly in print over the following years while remaining close friends.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gandhi kept the country's most respected critic close and answered him in public rather than dismissing him, and their exchange remains a model of principled disagreement.
The kural asks who can ruin one who keeps companions able to rebuke him, and Gandhi honoured Tagore precisely as the friend who would tell him where he was wrong.
