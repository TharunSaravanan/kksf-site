Kural 93

Charming Utterance · Iniyavai Kooral · இனியவை கூறல்

முகத்தான் அமர்ந்துஇனிது நோக்கி அகத்தானாம் இன்சொ லினதே அறம்.
Mugatthaan amarndhu inidhu nokki agatthaanaam, Inso linadhe aram.

Meaning

A welcoming face, a kind look, and sweet words that come from the heart: that is true goodness.

Elementary

When someone talks to you while looking at their phone, even nice words feel cold. But when a person turns to you, smiles, actually looks at you, and means what they say — that feels completely different. This kural says that whole package isn't just being polite. It IS being good.

University

The couplet is structured as an ascending sequence of three organs of kindness: முகத்தான் (mugatthaan, by the face), நோக்கி (nokki, by the look), and அகத்தான் (agatthaan, from within). The claim in the final word is unusually strong — Valluvar does not say such conduct leads to அறம் (aram, virtue/dharma) or is a part of it, but that it is aram, full stop. Given that aram is the governing category of the entire first book of the Kural, encompassing householder duty, ascetic restraint and social justice, identifying it with a warm face and a heartfelt word is a striking democratisation of virtue: it makes the moral life available in every ordinary encounter, requiring neither wealth nor renunciation. The insistence that the word must arise அகத்தான் — from the interior — also forecloses the reading that this is mere etiquette.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [4]
Krishna classifies pleasant, non-agitating speech as a form of tapas — spiritual discipline — placing it on the same plane as bodily and mental austerity.
Like the Kural, it treats gentle speech not as social lubricant but as a religious practice in its own right.
Christianity
“A merry heart maketh a cheerful countenance: but by sorrow of the heart the spirit is broken.”
— Proverbs 15:13 (King James Version) [5]
Proverbs traces the visible face directly back to the inner state, treating the countenance as an honest readout of the heart.
It underwrites the Kural's chain from within (agatthaan) to the face (mugatthaan).
Islam
“Do not belittle any good deed, even meeting your brother with a cheerful face.”
— Sahih Muslim 2626 (narrated by Abu Dharr) [6]
The Prophet ranks a welcoming expression as a genuine act of ma'ruf (good), not a trivial courtesy.
This is the Kural's claim exactly: the welcoming face is itself moral action, not a preliminary to it.
Judaism
“Make your Torah fixed, say little and do much, and receive every person with a cheerful countenance.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 1:15 (Sefaria translation) [7]
Shammai, famously the stricter of the two great first-century sages, nonetheless makes a welcoming face one of his three foundational rules of conduct.
The Kural's mugatthaan amarndhu — the settled, glad face turned toward the other — is precisely Shammai's sever panim yafot.
Buddhism
“Mind is the forerunner of all states... If with a pure mind a person speaks or acts, happiness follows him like his never-departing shadow.”
— Dhammapada 2 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's opening pair establishes that speech and action take their entire moral quality from the mental state that generates them.
It grounds the Kural's insistence that the sweet word must come agatthaan — from within — to count as virtue.
Jainism
“He who does not think crookedly, does not act crookedly, does not speak crookedly and does not hide his own weaknesses, observes the virtue of straightforwardness.”
— Saman Suttam v. 91 (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [9]
The Jain virtue of straightforwardness (ārjava) requires thought, action and speech to line up, with nothing hidden behind the words.
Valluvar counts a welcoming face and gaze, with sweet words that come from within, as virtue itself, and the Jain verse likewise makes inner and outer straightness of thought, deed and word a virtue (dharma) in its own right.
Sikhism
“My Dear Lord and Master, my Friend, speaks so sweetly. I have grown weary of testing Him, but still, He never speaks harshly to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 784, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan describes God himself as a Friend whose speech is unfailingly sweet, making gentle speech an attribute of the divine.
If sweetness of word is the Lord's own character, then, as the Kural says, a kind word from the heart is itself virtue rather than a mere means to it.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod names the three Charites — Aglaia, Euphrosyne and Thalia — daughters of Zeus and Eurynome, from whose glancing eyes a melting sweetness flows; they are the goddesses who make gracious social exchange possible, and Greek thought derived the whole vocabulary of charis (grace, favour, gratitude) from them.
— Hesiod, Theogony 907–911 [11]
The Graces divinise exactly the cluster the Kural lists: the warm look, the pleasing manner, the reciprocity of goodwill.
They mythologise the gaze and countenance the Kural names as the outward face of virtue.
Philosophy
“Such as a man's character is, he immediately shows it in his eyes, just as he who is beloved forthwith reads everything in the eyes of lovers … The good and simple and benevolent show all these things in the eyes, and there is no mistaking.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book XI, §15 (tr. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius mocks the man who announces that he means to deal fairly, saying real goodness needs no announcement because it shows itself at once in a person’s eyes and bearing.
The Kural likewise locates virtue in the welcoming face and kindly gaze, and treats these as outward signs of what is inside rather than as decoration.

Real Life Example

Pope John XXIII's Moonlight Speech
11 October 1962 · St Peter's Square, Vatican City
On the evening of the opening day of the Second Vatican Council, Pope John XXIII came unexpectedly to his window to greet a large crowd holding candles in St Peter's Square. Speaking without a prepared text, he remarked that even the moon seemed to have hurried out to watch, called himself a brother speaking to them, and asked them to go home and give their children a caress from the Pope, to dry any tears and to say a good word. The improvised greeting became one of the best-remembered moments of his papacy.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The speech is remembered not for doctrine but for its warmth, simplicity and evident sincerity, which reached people far beyond the square.
The kural says a welcoming face, a kindly gaze and sweet words that come from within are themselves virtue, and John XXIII's unscripted greeting was exactly such heartfelt kindness.
