Kural 1230

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

பொருள்மாலை யாளரை உள்ளி மருள்மாலை மாயும்என் மாயா உயிர்.
Porulmaalai yaalarai ulli marulmaalai maayumen maayaa uyir.

Meaning

I think of him, whose mind is set on earning money. In this confusing evening, my life, which has lasted until now, is dying away.

Elementary

Imagine a parent who works far away to earn money for the family. Even if it is for a good reason, the child at home can feel very lonely at night. The wife feels this way: her husband went to earn wealth, and in the evening she misses him so much it feels unbearable.

University

The closing couplet names the cause of the separation: the husband is poruḷ-mālaiyāḷar, ‘one whose nature (mālai) is wealth’, who has gone away to earn; Solomon Pappaiah sharpens this to ‘one for whom the delusion of wealth is everything’. The poet plays on mālai three ways, ‘nature, disposition’ in poruḷmālai, ‘evening’ in maruḷ mālai (‘bewildering dusk’), and by sound with māyum (‘dies’) and māyā (‘undying’): the life that did not perish (māyā uyir) through the separation so far now perishes (māyum) at this dusk. Varadarasanar glosses māyā uyir as a life that ‘stood as if by magic’ despite grief. Pope: ‘This darkening eve, my darkling soul must perish utterly; / Remembering him who seeks for wealth, but seeks not me.’ Both web sources give identical text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Offered wealth, long life and every pleasure by Yama, the boy Nachiketas refuses them all, saying that no man can be made happy by wealth and that he wants only the knowledge of what lies beyond death.
— Katha Upanishad 1.1.26-27 (paraphrase) [3]
The Katha Upanishad sets the pursuit of wealth below the pursuit of what truly matters.
The Kural’s wife suffers because her husband has chosen wealth over her presence, the very trade-off Nachiketas refuses.
Christianity
“For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”
— 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that the pursuit of wealth leads people astray and brings many sorrows.
In the Kural, it is the wife who is ‘pierced with sorrows’ because her husband has gone after wealth.
Islam
“Competition in [worldly] increase diverts you Until you visit the graveyards.”
— Qur’an 102:1-2 (Saheeh International) [5]
Surat at-Takathur warns that the race for more distracts people from what matters until death overtakes them.
The wife sees her husband diverted by the pursuit of wealth while her own life ebbs away in the evening.
Judaism
“The more property, the more anxiety”
— Pirkei Avot 2:7 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
Hillel, in Pirkei Avot, observes that increasing possessions brings increasing worry.
In the Kural the anxiety falls on the wife left at home while her husband pursues property abroad.
Buddhism
“That is not a strong fetter, the wise say, which is made of iron, wood or hemp. But the infatuation and longing for jewels and ornaments, children and wives — that, they say, is a far stronger fetter”
— Dhammapada 345-346 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names longing for both wealth and family as the strongest of bonds.
The Kural’s couple are held by both fetters at once: he by wealth, she by love for him, and the strain kills her at evening.
Jainism
“Misery ceases on the absence of delusion, delusion ceases on the absence of desire, desire ceases on the absence of greed, greed ceases on the absence of property.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana traces misery back through a chain of causes that ends in property.
The Kural’s whole chapter of misery begins with a husband who has gone after property, the first link in the Jain chain.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, the snake of separation from the Lord abides within my mind; it does not respond to any mantra. One who is separated from the Lord does not live; if he does live, he goes insane.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1368, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 76) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir describes separation from God as a snakebite no charm can cure, one that either kills or drives the sufferer mad.
The Kural's wife says the life that has held on until now is dying this evening as she thinks of her husband gone after wealth; Kabir likewise says that one parted from the beloved cannot go on living.
Greek Mythology
Disguised as a beggar, Odysseus tells Penelope that her husband is alive and could have been home long ago, but chose to travel from land to land gathering wealth before returning.
— Homer, Odyssey 19.282-286 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer has the faithful wife hear that her husband’s long absence was stretched out by the pursuit of riches.
Like Penelope, the Kural’s wife waits, night after night, for a husband whose return is delayed by wealth-seeking.
Philosophy
“The life of money-making is one undertaken under compulsion, and wealth is evidently not the good we are seeking; for it is merely useful and for the sake of something else.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.5, 1096a5-7 (tr. W.D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle ranks wealth as a mere means, never the final good of a human life.
The Kural’s wife suffers because her husband is ‘a man of wealth’ by nature; Aristotle reminds us that wealth is only ever for the sake of something else, such as the shared life she is losing.

Real Life Example

Gold Mountain wives wait in China for husbands working in America
Late 1800s to mid-1900s · Southern China and the United States
Many Chinese men went to work in the United States, which they called Gold Mountain, to earn money for their families, and laws such as the Page Law of 1875 and the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 kept most of them from bringing their wives. The wives who stayed behind, sometimes called grass widows or living widows, lived apart from their husbands for years or even decades, relying on letters and the money sent home.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Their long separations show the human cost borne by families when husbands went abroad to earn a living.
The kural's wife pines at evening for a husband whose heart is set on earning wealth far away, which was the daily life of the Gold Mountain wives.
