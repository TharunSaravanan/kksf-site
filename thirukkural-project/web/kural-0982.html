Kural 982

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

குணநலம் சான்றோர் நலனே பிறநலம் எந்நலத்து உள்ளதூஉம் அன்று.
Kunanalam saandror nalane piranalam ennalaththu ulladhooum andru.

Meaning

The real goodness of good people is the goodness of their character. No other kind of goodness counts as real goodness at all.

Elementary

A shiny new bike, a nice haircut or a fancy pair of shoes can look great. But Valluvar says the thing that really makes a person beautiful is a kind and honest heart. Everything else is just wrapping paper.

University

The couplet sets kunanalam (குணநலம், 'the goodness of qualities, beauty of character') against piranalam (பிறநலம், 'other goods'). The noble (saandror) have only one nalam (நலம், 'good, beauty, excellence') worth the name; the other kinds are not counted 'among any good' (ennalaththu ulladhooum andru, எந்நலத்து உள்ளதூஉம் அன்று), where ulladhooum shows the alapedai (vowel lengthening) that fills out the metre. Solomon Pappaiah and Mu. Karunanidhi read nalam as beauty (அழகு), contrasting inner beauty of character with outward beauty of form, while Mu. Varadarasanar keeps the more general 'good'; Pope's prose rendering, following the older commentarial tradition associated with Parimelazhagar, understands the other goods as sensual delights. Both readings make the same claim: for the perfected person only character counts. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Katha Upanishad, Yama tells the boy Naciketas that the good (shreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) are two different things; the wise person chooses the good, while the fool chooses the pleasant for the sake of gain and comfort.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2 (cf. tr. F. Max Müller, SBE 15) [3]
The Upanishad separates true good from attractive but lesser goods and praises the one who prefers the former.
The Kural similarly says that only the good of character is real good, and the rest do not count.
Christianity
“But the LORD said unto Samuel, Look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; because I have refused him: for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart.”
— 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV) [4]
When Samuel is impressed by a tall, handsome candidate for king, God tells him that what matters is the heart, not appearance.
Like the Kural, it ranks inner character above every outward excellence.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth but He looks to your heart and to your deeds.”
— Sahih Muslim 2564c (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that outward looks and riches carry no weight with God; only the heart and conduct do.
This mirrors the Kural's claim that character is the only real good and other goods do not count.
Judaism
“Thus said GOD: Let not the wise glory in their wisdom; Let not the strong glory in their strength; Let not the rich glory in their riches. But only in this should one glory: In being earnestly devoted to Me. For I GOD act with kindness, Justice, and equity in the world; For in these I delight —declares GOD.”
— Jeremiah 9:22-23 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Jeremiah dismisses wisdom, strength and wealth as grounds for pride and points instead to kindness, justice and equity.
Like the Kural, it sets aside every other excellence in favour of goodness of character.
Buddhism
“Not by mere eloquence nor by beauty of form does a man become accomplished, if he is jealous, selfish and deceitful. But he in whom these are wholly destroyed, uprooted and extinct, and who has cast out hatred — that wise man is truly accomplished.”
— Dhammapada 262-263 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says that eloquence and good looks do not make a person accomplished; only freedom from jealousy, selfishness, deceit and hatred does.
This closely echoes the Kural's view that the accomplished person's only real excellence is character.
Jainism
“One does not become a Sramana by the tonsure, nor a Brâhmana by the sacred syllable ôm, nor a Muni by living in the woods, nor a Tâpasa by wearing (clothes of) Kusa-grass and bark. One becomes a Sramana by equanimity, a Brâhmana by chastity, a Muni by knowledge, and a Tâpasa by penance.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.31-32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Jayaghosha tells a Brahmin priest that religious status comes from inner qualities, not from outward marks like shaved heads, mantras or forest dwelling.
Like the Kural, it holds that only goodness of character makes a person truly noble.
Sikhism
“Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 62, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak puts lived conduct (sach aachaar) above every other attainment, even above truth held only as an idea. In the Sikh view character shown in life is the excellence that counts.
The Kural says the only real excellence of the noble is goodness of character and that other excellences do not count, and Guru Nanak likewise ranks truthful living above everything else.
Greek Mythology
“I tell you that virtue is not given by money, but that from virtue come money and every other good of man, public as well as private.”
— Plato, Apology 30b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
At his trial Socrates says that his whole mission has been to urge people to care for the soul before body or property.
Like the Kural, it makes virtue the one real good on which every other good depends.
Philosophy
Kant opens the First Section of the Groundwork by saying that nothing in the world, or even out of it, can be called good without qualification except a good will; talents, fortune and even health can become bad when the will that uses them is not good.
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals, First Section (tr. T. K. Abbott) [11]
Kant argues that every other excellence is only conditionally good, while goodness of will is good in itself.
This is the Kural's claim in philosophical form: only goodness of character is good in every case.

Real Life Example

Eric Liddell declines to run the 100 metres on a Sunday
July 1924 · Paris, France
Learning months in advance that the heats of the 100 metres at the 1924 Paris Olympics would be run on a Sunday, the Scottish sprinter Eric Liddell, a devout Christian, declined to enter his best event and also withdrew from the relays held on Sundays. He was criticised in the press, but trained instead for the 200 and 400 metres, winning bronze in the 200 metres. On 11 July 1924 he won the 400 metres in an Olympic-record time of 47.6 seconds.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Liddell set his convictions above the most likely route to a gold medal, and his reputation rests as much on that choice as on his victory.
The kural says the true excellence of the noble lies in character, with other kinds of excellence not counted at all, and Liddell ranked his principles above athletic glory.
