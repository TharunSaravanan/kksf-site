Kural 1047

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

அறஞ்சாரா நல்குரவு ஈன்றதா யானும் பிறன்போல நோக்கப் படும்.
ARanjaaraa nalkuravu eendradhaa yaanum piRanpoala noakkap padum.

Meaning

A person in poverty that has no goodness about it is looked at like a stranger, even by the mother who gave birth to them.

Elementary

A mother’s love is the strongest love there is. Valluvar warns that if someone becomes poor because of bad choices and also turns away from doing right, people may turn away from him, and even his own family may feel hurt and distant.

University

The decisive qualifier is aRanjaaraa (அறஞ்சாரா, ‘not joined to aram, virtue’). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it as ‘poverty not in keeping with virtue’, i.e. destitution that is unrighteous in cause or accompaniment; Solomon Pappaiah glosses it as poverty without just reasons; Kalaignar makes it moral, the person who abandons virtue because of poverty. Eendra thaai (ஈன்ற தாய், ‘the mother who bore him’) is the strongest possible bond, and piRan poala (‘like another, a stranger’) marks its collapse. Read with the qualifier, the couplet does not say that every poor person is rejected, only that want combined with the loss of aram estranges even the closest kin. The ytamizh.com English explanation (‘reduced to absolute poverty’) passes over aRanjaaraa, a reminder that translations differ on how much moral weight to give it. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He that has wealth has friends. He that has wealth has kinsmen.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna says that friendship and kinship follow wealth.
The converse is the Kural’s picture: when wealth goes, even kin may look on a man as a stranger.
Christianity
“And when he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land; and he began to be in want... and no man gave unto him.”
— Luke 15:14-16 (KJV) [4]
The prodigal son, having squandered his inheritance, is left starving with no one to help, yet his father later runs to embrace him.
The parable matches the Kural’s self-caused, unvirtuous poverty and its isolation, but reverses its ending: the parent does not treat the son as a stranger.
Islam
“On the Day a man will flee from his brother And his mother and his father”
— Qur’an 80:34-35 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an depicts the Day of Judgement as a time when even the closest kin flee from one another, each absorbed in his own fate.
It shares the Kural’s image of a mother’s bond giving way, though in an eschatological, not economic, setting.
Judaism
“The poor are despised even by their peers, But the rich have many friends.”
— Proverbs 14:20 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023) [6]
Proverbs observes that poverty brings contempt even from one’s own circle.
Being despised by one’s own peers is the Kural’s being looked upon as a stranger by one’s own.
Buddhism
“A trustworthy person is the best kinsman.”
— Dhammapada 204 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada locates true kinship in trustworthiness rather than in blood or wealth.
The Kural’s qualifier, poverty without virtue, finds an echo here: it is character, not birth, that makes and keeps kin.
Jainism
“Mother, father, daughter-in-law, brother, wife, and sons will not be able to help me, when I suffer for my own deeds.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk reflects that no relative can share or lift the consequences of his own karma.
Like the Kural, it sees family ties failing the person who has fallen through his own conduct, but draws from this a call to detachment.
Sikhism
“When the poor man goes to the rich man, and sits right in front of him, the rich man turns his back on him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1159, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the same hymn, Kabir pictures the poor man sitting face to face with a wealthy acquaintance who simply turns away, treating him as a stranger.
The Kural says one sunk in poverty is looked upon as a stranger even by his own mother; Kabir shows the same estrangement, as the rich man turns his back on the poor man in front of him.
Greek Mythology
Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as poor travellers, were turned away from a thousand homes in Phrygia before the aged Baucis and Philemon took them in.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Baucis and Philemon) [10]
Ovid’s tale shows how readily people treat the poor wayfarer as an unwelcome stranger.
The thousand closed doors illustrate the Kural’s observation that the destitute are looked upon as strangers.
Philosophy
“...though he is destitute and begging for a bite, no one will help him with a meal, no one offer him board or shelter.”
— Juvenal, Satire 3 (tr. G.G. Ramsay, 1918) [11]
Juvenal describes the poor scholar Codrus, whose few belongings burn and whom nobody will help.
Codrus, abandoned by all, embodies the Kural’s destitute man looked upon as a stranger.

Real Life Example

Edgar Allan Poe's gambling debts estrange him from his foster father
1826-1827 · Charlottesville and Richmond, Virginia, United States
Edgar Allan Poe, raised in Richmond as the foster son of the merchant John Allan, was sent to the University of Virginia in 1826 with too little money to cover his expenses. When Allan ignored his requests for help, Poe turned to gambling, ran up debts of about 2,000 dollars that Allan refused to pay, and left the university in December 1826. Estranged from Allan and facing his creditors, he left Richmond for Boston in 1827.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The quarrel over debts incurred through gambling began a lasting breach between Poe and the man who had brought him up.
The kural says that want unconnected with virtue makes a person a stranger even to the one who raised him, and Poe's gambling debts turned his foster father against him.
