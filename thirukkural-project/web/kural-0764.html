Kural 764

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

அழிவின்றி அறைபோகா தாகி வழிவந்த வன்க ணதுவே படை.
Azhivindri araipogaa thaagi vazhivandha vankanadhuve padai.

Meaning

A true army is one that has never been beaten, cannot be bribed by the enemy, and has courage passed down from its fathers.

Elementary

Imagine a family that has played in the same village band for generations. Nobody could pay them to play for a rival band, and they never give up halfway through a parade. Valluvar says a true army is like that: loyal, steady, and brave because bravery runs in its history.

University

Three marks define the true army here: aḻiviṉmai (not being broken in battle), aṟaipōkāmai (அறைபோகாமை, not being undermined or won over by enemy intrigue, i.e. free of treachery and desertion), and vaḻivanta vaṉkaṇ (வழிவந்த வன்கண், valour inherited through lineage). Parimelazhagar takes aṟaipōtal as being corrupted by the enemy’s secret approaches, and Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as ‘not falling prey to the enemy’s deceit’. There is a small textual variant: ytamizh prints அழிவின்றி, while the sandhi-joined thirukkural-thamizh text reads அழிவின் றறைபோகா, i.e. அழிவின்று; the sense, ‘without destruction’, is unchanged.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“...ready to share in the weal or woe of the king, and consequently not falling foul with him...”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book VI, Chapter 1 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
In Kautilya’s description of the best army, hereditary troops are prized because they share the king’s fortunes and do not turn against him.
This is the Kural’s aṟaipōkāmai and vaḻivanta vaṉkaṇ: loyalty and inherited valour.
Christianity
“No man that warreth entangleth himself with the affairs of this life; that he may please him who hath chosen him to be a soldier.”
— 2 Timothy 2:4 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes the good soldier as one whose loyalty to his commander is not divided by other interests.
An army that cannot be drawn away by other inducements is what the Kural means by one that does not ‘go over’.
Islam
The Qur’an warns believers that when they meet the enemy in battle they must not turn their backs, except to manoeuvre or to rejoin their own company.
— Qur’an 8:15–16 [5]
Desertion in the face of the enemy is condemned as a grave fault.
It parallels the Kural’s demand that a true army never breaks or goes over to the enemy.
Judaism
When David flees Jerusalem during Absalom’s revolt, the foreigner Ittai the Gittite refuses to leave him, vowing that wherever the king is, in death or in life, there his servant will be.
— 2 Samuel 15:19–22 [6]
Ittai and his men stay loyal to David at the lowest point of his reign, when many others had gone over to Absalom.
This is the Kural’s aṟaipōkāmai, troops who will not be won over by the other side.
Buddhism
“What have you heard, Ananda: do the Vajjis neither enact new decrees nor abolish existing ones, but proceed in accordance with their ancient constitutions?”
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahaparinibbana Sutta 1.4 (trans. Sister Vajira & Francis Story) [7]
When King Ajatasattu plans to attack the Vajjis, the Buddha says they cannot be overcome so long as they keep to their ancient ways and remain united.
Strength drawn from long-held tradition is the Kural’s vaḻivanta vaṉkaṇ, valour that comes down through lineage.
Jainism
“Though they tempt him with pleasures, and though they should bind him and carry him home, if he does not care for a (worldly) life, they will not captivate him or make him return to domestic life.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.2.1.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
A monk set firmly on the path cannot be won back by pleading relatives, by temptation or even by force; his resolve can be neither bought nor broken.
The Kural's true army is one that is not broken and cannot be suborned by the enemy, and the Jain monk shows the same incorruptibility under every kind of pressure.
Sikhism
After Anandpur was abandoned, Guru Gobind Singh's two youngest sons, Zorawar Singh and Fateh Singh, were taken before Wazir Khan, governor of Sirhind, and offered rewards and an easy life if they would give up their faith. They refused and were put to death in December 1705; popular tradition says they were bricked up alive.
— Wikipedia, 'Zorawar Singh (Sikhism)' (paraphrase of traditional accounts) [9]
The younger Sahibzade are honoured in Sikh tradition as children who could not be bought, holding to the courage of their father and their grandfather Guru Tegh Bahadur.
The Kural's true army cannot be won over by the enemy and carries a courage handed down through generations; the Sahibzade refused every bribe and showed the inherited resolve of their line.
Greek Mythology
Demaratus tells Xerxes that the Spartans are free but have law as their master, and that law commands them never to flee from battle whatever the size of the enemy, but to stand in their ranks and conquer or die.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.104 [10]
Spartan steadfastness is presented as an inherited discipline rather than individual bravado.
It matches the Kural’s army whose courage is handed down and which never breaks.
Philosophy
Machiavelli argues that a principality without its own arms is never secure, and that mercenary and auxiliary troops are useless and dangerous because they have no loyalty but pay.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, chapters 12–13 [11]
He urges rulers to rely on armies composed of their own subjects.
It is the political-philosophical form of the Kural’s point that only a native, long-established army will not desert.

Real Life Example

The Swiss Guard's stand during the Sack of Rome
6 May 1527 · St Peter's Basilica, Rome (now Vatican City)
When the troops of Emperor Charles V stormed Rome, 189 men of the Pontifical Swiss Guard defended Pope Clement VII. Of these, 147 were killed, many on the steps of St Peter's, while the pope escaped to Castel Sant'Angelo. New recruits to the Swiss Guard have been sworn in each year on 6 May in commemoration of that day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Guard held to its duty to the end rather than abandon or betray the pope, and the corps has kept that memory as its living tradition for five centuries.
The kural names as a true army one that cannot be won over by the enemy and that carries courage handed down through generations, and the Swiss Guard's loyalty and its yearly oath on the anniversary embody both.
