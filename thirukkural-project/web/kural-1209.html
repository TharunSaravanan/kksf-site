Kural 1209

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

விளியுமென் இன்னுயிர் வேறல்லம் என்பார் அளியின்மை ஆற்ற நினைந்து.
Viliyumen innuyir vaerallam enpaar aliyinmai aatra ninaindhu.

Meaning

My dear life is slipping away as I keep thinking how unkind he is now, the one who used to say, "We two are not separate."

Elementary

Imagine a best friend who always said, ‘We are like twins, we will never be apart,’ and then went away. You might feel very sad remembering those words. The lady in this poem feels like that, and the memory makes her heart ache.

University

The couplet sets the husband’s past words against his present conduct. Vēṟu allam, ‘we are not different’, is his former vow of oneness (Kalaignar: ‘we are one, not two’); aḷiyiṉmai, ‘lack of tender grace’ (aḷi, ‘kindness, compassion’), is how she now reads his absence. Thinking on it too much (āṟṟa niṉaintu), her ‘sweet life’ (iṉṉuyir) is perishing (viḷiyum). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both stress the contrast between what he ‘used to say’ and his present want of love, and Pope renders: ‘Dear life departs, when his ungracious deeds I ponder o’er, / Who said erewhile, “We’re one for evermore.”’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa opens the Raghuvamsha by saluting Parvati and Parameshvara, the parents of the world, as joined together like a word and its meaning.
— Kalidasa, Raghuvamsha 1.1 (paraphrase) [3]
The image of Shiva and Parvati as inseparable as word and sense is the classical Indian emblem of conjugal oneness.
‘We are not separate’ is the ideal the husband once proclaimed; Kalidasa’s divine couple show the oneness the heroine feels has been betrayed.
Christianity
“Wherefore they are no more twain, but one flesh. What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder.”
— Matthew 19:6 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that husband and wife are no longer two but one, and should not be parted.
The husband’s ‘we are not two’ echoes this teaching, which makes his apparent unkindness all the more painful to her.
Islam
Rumi tells of a lover who knocked at his beloved’s door and, asked ‘Who is there?’, answered ‘I’, and was turned away; after a year of separation he returned and answered ‘It is you’, and was let in, since there was no room for two I’s in one house.
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book I (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.A. Nicholson) [5]
The Masnavi presents true love as the end of separateness between lover and beloved.
The beloved’s ‘we are not separate’ is Rumi’s ‘It is you’, and the heroine grieves that the oneness seems to have been withdrawn.
Judaism
“Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife, and they shall be one flesh.”
— Genesis 2:24 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Genesis founds marriage on the man cleaving to his wife until the two become one.
The husband’s vow that ‘we are not separate’ is this founding ideal, which his absence now seems to contradict.
Buddhism
In the Canda-Kinnara Jataka, when a king shoots the kinnara Canda to seize his mate, the kinnari Candā laments over her husband, refuses the king, and appeals to the gods until Sakka restores her husband to life.
— Canda-Kinnara Jataka (Jataka 485) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Jataka celebrates a wife whose devotion holds her husband’s life as inseparable from her own.
Candā lives the oneness the Kural’s husband once spoke of, and her grief at his loss matches the heroine’s ebbing life.
Jainism
On his wedding day Prince Arishtanemi turns back, renounces the world and leaves his bride Rajimati, who, by the tradition of their love through earlier births, had been his companion; she grieves, then follows him into renunciation.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45); the earlier births are told in Hemachandra’s Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 8 [8]
The Neminatha-Rajimati story is Jainism’s great tale of a bride left by the one she counted as her own.
Rajimati, abandoned by one who had been joined to her, mirrors the heroine wasting at the thought of a beloved who once said ‘we are not separate’, though Jainism praises his departure.
Sikhism
“You are me, and I am You-what is the difference between us?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 93, Sri Raag, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas declares that the devotee and the Lord are not really separate, going on to compare them to gold and the bracelet made of it, or water and its waves.
The Kural's wife recalls her husband's old vow, 'We two are not separate,' and her life ebbs as she sets it against his present unkindness; Ravidas makes the same vow of oneness, but as a truth the separation cannot break.
Greek Mythology
Alcyone begs her husband Ceyx not to sail, or to take her with him, saying she would rather share any danger than be parted; he goes alone, and she keeps vigil for him, until in the end the gods turn both into kingfishers so that they are never separated again.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.410-748 (Ceyx and Alcyone) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s couple, devoted to the point of refusing separation, are finally made inseparable by the gods.
Alcyone grieving a husband who left despite their oneness echoes the heroine wasting away at his apparent lack of tenderness.
Philosophy
In Aristophanes’ speech, Hephaestus offers to weld two lovers into one so that they will be no longer two but one, sharing a single life and a single death, and each lover says this is exactly what he has always longed for.
— Plato, Symposium 192d-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato’s Aristophanes defines love as the desire to be made one with the beloved.
‘We are not two’ is the lovers’ wish in the Symposium, and the Kural’s heroine suffers because that oneness now seems denied.

Real Life Example

Napoleon divorces Josephine
15 December 1809 · Tuileries Palace, Paris, France
On 15 December 1809, in Napoleon's apartments at the Tuileries, his civil marriage to Josephine was formally dissolved; he said he acted for the good of France, since she had not given him an heir. Josephine was so overcome that she could not finish reading her prepared statement and an attendant read the rest. She kept the rank and title of empress and lived at Malmaison until her death in 1814.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The man who had once written that he could not spend a day without loving her put her aside for reasons of state.
The kural's heroine feels her life ebbing as she thinks of the unkindness of the man who once said 'we two are not separate', and Josephine's divorce is a documented case of such a vow of oneness being broken.
