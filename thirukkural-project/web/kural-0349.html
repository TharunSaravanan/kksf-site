Kural 349

Renunciation · Thuravu · துறவு

பற்றற்ற கண்ணே பிறப்பறுக்கும் மற்று நிலையாமை காணப் படும்.
Patratra kanne pirapparukkum matru Nilaiyaamai kaanap padum.

Meaning

The moment a person lets go of all attachments, the cycle of rebirth is cut. Otherwise, they keep going round and round in an endless, unsteady cycle of birth and death.

Elementary

A spinning top keeps going as long as you keep flicking it; stop flicking, and it finally rests. This kural says once you stop clinging, the cycle of being born again and again stops — and if you don't, everything keeps wobbling and changing.

University

Kanne (“at that very point”) stresses immediacy: liberation coincides with the cessation of patru rather than following it as a later reward. The second line is read in two ways. Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasan take matru as 'otherwise': without detachment, instability — the alternation of births and deaths — will be seen. Puliyur Kesikan reads it as 'moreover': with detachment, the impermanence of the world is then clearly seen. Either way the couplet binds this chapter to the preceding one, Nilaiyaamai (Impermanence, ch. 34). The claim that the end of desire is the end of rebirth is shared almost word for word by the Katha Upanishad and the Dhammapada, and appears inverted in the Orphic hope of escaping the 'wheel'.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When all desires that dwell in his heart cease, then the mortal becomes immortal, and obtains Brahman.”
— Katha Upanishad II.6.14 (2.3.14) (tr. F. Max Müller, SBE vol. 15) [3]
Death himself (Yama) teaches Nachiketas that the cessation of all desires is the moment the mortal becomes immortal.
Same claim and same immediacy as the Kural: when attachment ends, birth-and-death ends.
Christianity
“While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal.”
— 2 Corinthians 4:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul directs believers' attention away from visible, temporary things to invisible, eternal ones.
Both contrast the instability of what is clung to with the permanence gained by looking beyond it.
Islam
“And present to them the example of the life of this world, [its being] like rain which We send down from the sky, and the vegetation of the earth mingles with it and [then] it becomes dry remnants, scattered by the winds.”
— Quran 18:45 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran likens worldly life to vegetation that flourishes after rain and is soon blown away as dry debris.
The instability (nilaiyaamai) the Kural says is seen by those still attached is here pictured as vegetation scattered by the wind.
Judaism
“Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity.”
— Ecclesiastes 1:2 (KJV) [4]
Qoheleth opens by declaring all worldly striving to be hevel — vapour, fleeting breath.
The same insight into the instability of everything grasped, without the Kural's promise of release.
Buddhism
“Let go of the past, let go of the future, let go of the present, and cross over to the farther shore of existence. With mind wholly liberated, you shall come no more to birth and death.”
— Dhammapada 348 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha urges total letting-go and promises that the wholly liberated mind will not return to birth and death.
Exactly the Kural's claim: complete release from clinging ends the cycle of birth.
Jainism
“Misery ceases on the absence of delusion, delusion ceases on the absence of desire, desire ceases on the absence of greed, greed ceases on the absence of property.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 185) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana traces misery back through delusion, desire and greed to possession. Remove the last link and the whole chain collapses.
Valluvar says that births are cut at the very moment attachment ceases. The Jain chain likewise makes the end of misery depend directly on the end of grasping.
Sikhism
“Coming and going in reincarnation is ended, O Nanak, for those whose minds are filled with the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 251, Raag Gauree (Bavan Akhree, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan teaches that the round of birth and death ends for those in whose minds God dwells.
The Kural says the cycle of birth is cut the moment attachment ceases, and otherwise the round of coming and going continues; Gurbani uses the same coming and going and ties its end to a mind filled with God.
Greek Mythology
On gold tablets buried with Orphic initiates, the soul declares to the underworld powers that it has flown out of the grievous, weary circle (or wheel) of existence and asks to be admitted among the blessed.
— Orphic gold tablet from Thurii (4th century BCE) [9]
Orphic initiates hoped to escape a cycle of reincarnation through purification and ritual.
The Orphic 'wheel' is the Greek counterpart to the cycle of births the Kural says is cut when attachment ends.
Philosophy
In the Consolation of Philosophy, Lady Philosophy shows Boethius that Fortune turns her wheel ceaselessly, so anyone who ties his happiness to her gifts must expect to be thrown down.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2 [10]
Imprisoned and ruined, Boethius learns that clinging to the goods of Fortune binds one to her endless instability.
Like the Kural's 'otherwise, instability is seen', Boethius shows attachment to worldly goods locking one into perpetual change.

Real Life Example

Bill Wilson and Dr Bob Smith found Alcoholics Anonymous
1935 · Akron, Ohio, United States
In May 1935 the New York stockbroker Bill Wilson, who had stopped drinking, met the Akron surgeon Dr Robert Smith, and the two men began helping each other and other alcoholics to stay sober. Dr Smith's last drink, on 10 June 1935, is celebrated as the founding date of Alcoholics Anonymous. The fellowship, built on mutual support and reliance on a spiritual power greater than oneself, published its basic text in 1939 and spread around the world.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The movement helped millions break a cycle of craving, relapse and harm that had seemed endless. Its founders found that the cycle stopped only when the craving itself was surrendered.
Valluvar says that at the very moment attachment ceases the cycle is cut, while otherwise endless instability is seen, and AA's founders found that ending the craving was what ended the cycle of relapse.
