Kural 1025

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

குற்றம் இலனாய்க் குடிசெய்து வாழ்வானைச் சுற்றமாச் சுற்றும் உலகு.
Kutram ilanaaik kudiseydhu vaazhvaanaich sutramaach chutrum ulagu.

Meaning

The world gathers around a person who lifts up their family without doing wrong, and treats them like one of its own family.

Elementary

Think of a kind neighbour who works hard for her family and never cheats anyone. Soon the whole street treats her like family, bringing food and asking her advice. Valluvar says that is what happens to honest family-builders: everyone wants to be their relative.

University

Kutram ilanaai (குற்றம் இலனாய், ‘being without fault’) is the condition: the family must be raised by blameless means. The reward is a pun on sutram (சுற்றம், ‘kin, relatives’) and sutrum (சுற்றும், ‘surrounds, encircles’): the ulagu (உலகு, ‘world’) surrounds him as if it were his own relatives. Varadarasanar and Kesikan say people seek to become his kin; Pappaiah narrows ulagu to ‘the great’ (உயர்ந்தோர்), in keeping with the commentarial habit of reading ulagam as the worthy. Pope translates ‘The world shall circle him, and kindred claim.’ The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Whatever action a great man performs, common men follow. And whatever standards he sets by exemplary acts, all the world pursues.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.21 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna notes that the world gathers behind and imitates the person of exemplary conduct.
Like the Kural's blameless family-builder whom the world surrounds, the exemplary person draws everyone to follow.
Christianity
Proverbs tells the reader to keep mercy and truth close, and so to find favour and good understanding in the sight of God and man.
— Proverbs 3:3-4 (KJV) [4]
The sage promises that a life of faithfulness wins goodwill from people as well as from God.
The Kural similarly says a blameless life makes the world want to be one's kin.
Islam
“Indeed, those who have believed and done righteous deeds - the Most Merciful will appoint for them affection.”
— Qur'an 19:96 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an promises that God will place love for the righteous in people's hearts.
Like the Kural, it says the upright are surrounded by affection as if they were family.
Judaism
“which is the straight path that a man should choose for himself? One which is an honor to the person adopting it, and [on account of which] honor [accrues] to him from others.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Judah the Prince defines the right path as one that is honourable in itself and earns honour from others.
The Kural's blameless family-builder, embraced by the world, follows exactly this path.
Buddhism
“He who is full of faith and virtue, and possesses good repute and wealth — he is respected everywhere, in whatever land he travels.”
— Dhammapada 303 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha says the virtuous householder of good name and honest wealth is honoured wherever he goes.
The Kural likewise says the world embraces the blameless one who has raised his family.
Jainism
Hemachandra's Yogashastra lists, as the first quality of a householder fit for the Jain path, wealth acquired by just and honest means (nyaya-sampanna-vibhava).
— Hemachandra, Yogashastra, Book 1 (the marganusari qualities of the householder) [8]
Jain lay ethics makes honest earning the foundation of a good household life.
This is the Kural's ‘without fault’ condition for raising one's family.
Sikhism
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, Guru Nanak stayed in Saidpur (Eminabad) with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter who lived by honest labour, and declined a feast offered by the rich official Malik Bhago, saying that Lalo's coarse bread was earned honestly while Bhago's food was wrung from the poor.
— Janamsakhi tradition, as retold in M. A. Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak [9]
The story is told to show that honest work, not wealth or rank, wins the Guru's company and honour.
The Kural says the world gathers round and claims as kin the person who raises his family without doing wrong; in the story the Guru himself chooses to make his home with the blameless, honest-working Lalo.
Greek Mythology
Plutarch tells how Theseus gathered the scattered villages of Attica into one city, Athens, and the people came together under him as one community.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 24 [10]
The hero's work for his people turned many separate households into a single family-like city.
It loosely matches the Kural's picture of people drawn around a builder as his own kin.
Philosophy
“For nothing inspires love, nothing conciliates affection, like virtue. Why, in a certain sense we may be said to feel affection even for men we have never seen, owing to their honesty and virtue.”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia, ch. 8 (§§27-28) (tr. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Laelius argues that friendship springs from the natural pull of virtue: goodness draws affection so strongly that we love even good men we have never met.
This matches the Kural's promise that people gather around the blameless man and treat him as their own kin.

Real Life Example

Robert Owen's New Lanark becomes a place of pilgrimage
1800-1825 · New Lanark, Scotland
From 1800 Robert Owen managed the New Lanark cotton mills, where he shortened the working day, stopped children under ten from working in the mills, opened a nursery and infant school in 1816, and set up a village store selling good goods at fair prices. Paid for out of the mills' profits, these reforms drew visitors from many countries, and Britannica notes that the mills became a place of pilgrimage for political leaders, social reformers and royalty.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Owen showed that a working community could be raised without exploitation, and the world came to see and learn from it.
The kural says the world gathers around the one who raises his people without fault, and New Lanark under Owen drew admirers from far and wide.
