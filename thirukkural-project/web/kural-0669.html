Kural 669

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

துன்பம் உறவரினும் செய்க துணிவாற்றி இன்பம் பயக்கும் வினை.
Thunpam uRavarinum seyka thuNivaatri Inpam payakkum vinai.

Meaning

Even if a lot of hardship comes your way, keep going bravely with work that will bring happiness in the end.

Elementary

Learning to swim can be scary: water gets in your nose and your arms get tired. But if you keep going, one day you’ll zoom across the pool and love it. Some good things feel hard at first and happy at the end, so keep being brave.

University

The couplet sets tuṉpam (suffering) against iṉpam (joy), with the concessive uṟa variṉum, ‘even if it comes in abundance’ (uṟa, intensely), and the command ceyka tuṇivu āṟṟi, ‘act, sustaining courage’. Parimelazhagar explains that the suffering belongs to the beginning or the course of the undertaking while the joy is its fruit at the end, so a firm mind must not judge a task by its first taste; Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah add ‘in the end’ (muṭivil) to make this explicit. Kalaignar reverses the emphasis, defining a joy-bringing act as one carried through despite suffering. The couplet sets up the chapter’s conclusion in 670. The three sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.37 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [4]
Krishna defines sāttvika happiness as the kind that is bitter at first and sweet in the end.
Almost a gloss on the Kural: accept early pain for the joy the deed will yield.
Christianity
“Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby.”
— Hebrews 12:11 (KJV) [5]
Hebrews admits that discipline is painful while it lasts but says it later bears good fruit.
The same pattern as the Kural, grievous now and fruitful afterward.
Islam
“For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease. Indeed, with hardship [will be] ease.”
— Quran 94:5-6 (Sahih International) [6]
Repeated for emphasis, the verse assures that relief comes with and after hardship.
It gives the same encouragement as the Kural, to persist through tuṉpam toward iṉpam.
Judaism
“They who sow in tears shall reap with songs of joy.”
— Psalms 126:5 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, via Sefaria) [7]
The psalm pictures labour done in sorrow that yields a joyful harvest.
Sowing in tears and reaping in joy is the Kural’s arc from tuṉpam to iṉpam.
Buddhism
“There is the taking on of a practice that is painful in the present but yields pleasure in the future.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 45, Culadhammasamadana Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha classifies undertakings by their present and future results and commends the path that is hard now but brings happiness later.
This is almost exactly the kind of act the Kural tells us to press on with.
Jainism
“If any misfortune happens and he suffers pain, he should cheerfully steady his mind, and bear the ills that attack him.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
In the lecture on troubles the monk is told to meet misfortune and pain with a steadied, cheerful mind, bearing it for the sake of the welfare of his soul.
This parallels the kural's call to press on courageously through great hardship toward the work that brings joy at the end.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, describes the true hero as one who holds the field whatever he suffers.
The Kural tells us to carry on boldly with a worthy task even when great hardship comes; Kabir's hero who never leaves the field gives the same counsel of perseverance.
Greek Mythology
“...between us and Goodness the gods have placed the sweat of our brows: long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the first; but when a man has reached the top, then is she easy to reach...”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 289-292 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod pictures the path to excellence as steep and rough at first but easy once the summit is reached.
Both texts tell us to accept the early hardship for the good at the end.
Philosophy
Diogenes Laertius reports Aristotle’s saying that the roots of education are bitter but its fruit is sweet.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 5.18 (trans. R. D. Hicks) [12]
The maxim, attributed to Aristotle, holds that the hard early stages of learning are repaid by its rewards.
Bitter root and sweet fruit is the Kural’s suffering-then-joy in another image.

Real Life Example

Junko Tabei reaches the summit of Everest after an avalanche
16 May 1975 · Mount Everest, Nepal
In 1975 Junko Tabei helped lead a Japanese all-women expedition to Mount Everest. While the team slept at Camp II, an avalanche struck their tents and knocked Tabei unconscious, but the expedition continued. On 16 May 1975 she became the first woman to reach the summit of Everest.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Tabei's climb opened the highest mountain to women climbers and showed what persistence could achieve after a near-fatal setback.
The kural urges boldly carrying on with a task that brings joy in the end, even if much hardship comes along the way, and Tabei went on from being buried by an avalanche to a historic summit.
