Kural 661

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

வினைத்திட்பம் என்பது ஒருவன் மனத்திட்பம் மற்றைய எல்லாம் பிற.
Vinaiththitpam enpadhu oruvan manaththitpam Matraiya ellaam piRa.

Meaning

Being firm in action really means being firm in mind. Every other kind of strength is something different.

Elementary

Imagine two kids building a tall block tower. Both have the same blocks, but one gives up when it wobbles and the other keeps going. The blocks are not what made the difference. The strong part was inside the kid who kept going. That inside strength is what gets things done.

University

The chapter title vinai-t-tiṭpam pairs vinai (undertaking, action) with tiṭpam (solidity, firmness), and the opening couplet at once relocates that firmness from the deed to the doer: vinai-t-tiṭpam is maṉa-t-tiṭpam, firmness of mind. Parimelazhagar glosses maṟṟaiya ellām (all the others) as the external supports a minister or king might count on, such as army, fortifications, wealth and allies, and says these are piṟa, other things, not what is meant by the word. The chapter follows directly on vinai-t-tūymai (ch. 66, purity of means), so the Amaichiyal sequence runs: choose clean means, then carry them through with an unshaken mind. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah follow this reading closely (Pappaiah: the resolve not to abandon a task midway), while Kalaignar turns it conditionally: whatever else one has, without firmness of mind there is no firmness in action. The text is identical in ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net and the sandhi-joined edition at thirukkural-thamizh.org.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who are on this path are resolute in purpose, and their aim is one. O beloved child of the Kurus, the intelligence of those who are irresolute is many-branched.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.41 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [4]
Krishna contrasts the single-pointed, resolute intellect (vyavasāyātmikā buddhi) with the scattered mind of the irresolute.
Both texts locate the power to act in a firmly resolved mind rather than in outward means.
Christianity
“A double minded man is unstable in all his ways.”
— James 1:8 (KJV) [5]
James warns that a divided, wavering mind makes a person unreliable in everything he does.
The negative form of the Kural’s claim: an unsteady mind produces unsteady action.
Islam
“Indeed, those who have said, "Our Lord is Allah" and then remained on a right course - the angels will descend upon them, [saying], "Do not fear and do not grieve...”
— Quran 41:30 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse praises istiqāma, standing firm on a course once it has been professed, and promises divine support to those who do.
Steadfastness after the decision, not the decision alone, is what counts, as in the Kural.
Judaism
“They are not afraid of evil tidings; their heart is firm, they trust in GOD.”
— Psalms 112:7 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, via Sefaria) [7]
The psalm describes the righteous person whose firm heart keeps him unshaken by bad news.
‘Their heart is firm’ is almost a translation of maṉa-t-tiṭpam, the firmness of mind the Kural makes central.
Buddhism
“Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought.”
— Dhammapada 1-2 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada opens by teaching that every deed and its result flows from the state of the mind behind it.
Like the Kural, it makes the mind, not outward factors, the true source of what an action becomes.
Jainism
“The mind is that unruly, dreadful, bad horse; I govern it by the discipline of the Law”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23.58 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
In the dialogue of Kesi and Gautama, Gautama explains that the horse which never runs off with him is his own mind, held in check by the discipline of the Law.
Like the kural, Jainism locates real strength in the steadied mind rather than in any outward power, since everything else follows from whether the mind is mastered.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Jap (Japji Sahib, Pauri 28), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Japji Guru Nanak makes mastery of one's own mind the true victory, from which mastery of the world follows.
The Kural says firmness in action is really firmness of mind, and all other strength is something else; Guru Nanak likewise places the decisive strength in the conquered mind.
Greek Mythology
“Heart, be still, you had worse than this to bear on the day when the terrible Cyclops ate your brave companions...”
— Homer, Odyssey 20 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Disguised in his own house, Odysseus holds his anger in check by speaking to his heart, so that his plan against the suitors is not wrecked by impulse.
Odysseus’s success comes from mastering his own mind, the firmness the Kural calls the real power in action.
Philosophy
“Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [12]
Epictetus divides the world into what belongs to our own mind and will, and externals such as body, property and office, which ‘belong to others’.
His split mirrors the Kural’s: the mind’s firmness is one’s own, and ‘all the others are other’.

Real Life Example

Churchill tells the Commons that Britain will fight on after Dunkirk
4 June 1940 · House of Commons, London, United Kingdom
After the evacuation of more than 338,000 Allied troops from Dunkirk, which had left much of the British Army's equipment behind in France, Prime Minister Winston Churchill reported on the war situation to the House of Commons on 4 June 1940. He warned that wars are not won by evacuations and prepared the country for a possible invasion. He declared that Britain would fight on the beaches, the landing grounds, the fields, the streets and the hills, and that it would never surrender.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
At a moment when Britain's material strength was at its lowest, the speech made national resolve itself the country's main asset and helped carry Britain through the summer of 1940.
The kural says that real firmness in action is firmness of mind and that every other kind of strength is something else, and Churchill rallied a nation that had just lost its army's equipment by appealing to its resolve rather than its resources.
