Kural 544

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

குடிதழீஇக் கோலோச்சும் மாநில மன்னன் அடிதழீஇ நிற்கும் உலகு.
Kutidhazheeik Kolochchum Maanila Mannan Atidhazheei Nirkum Ulaku.

Meaning

The whole world holds on to the feet of a great king who rules with love and keeps his people close.

Elementary

A team captain who looks after every player, helping the slow ones and cheering up the scared ones, ends up with a team that would follow her anywhere. People hold on to a leader who holds on to them first.

University

The couplet turns on a chiasmus of the verb தழுவு (embrace), used twice in the lengthened form தழீஇ: the king embraces the people (குடி தழீஇ), and the world embraces his feet (அடி தழீஇ). The long vowel (இசைநிறை அளபெடை) fills out the metre and gives the word emphasis. கோல் ஓச்சுதல், ‘wielding the sceptre’, is the idiom for ruling. Parimelazhagar explains ‘embracing the subjects’ as protecting them with love and learning what they lack; ‘clinging to his feet’ means the world will not leave him, so his kingdom stands firm. Varadarasan: ‘the world holds fast to the feet of the king who rules embracing his people with love’. Pappaiah turns it into people living ‘around the feet’ of such a ruler, and Kalaignar has the whole earth honouring the footsteps of a good government. In சுற்றந்தழால் (Ch. 53) Valluvar used the same verb for embracing kin; here the embrace is extended to the whole people. The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“In the happiness of his subjects lies his happiness; in their welfare his welfare; whatever pleases himself he shall not consider as good, but whatever pleases his subjects he shall consider as good.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.19 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya tells the king to treat his people’s happiness and welfare as his own.
This is what குடி தழீஇ means in practice: the king takes the people’s good as his own.
Christianity
“But so shall it not be among you: but whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister: And whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all.”
— Mark 10:43–44 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts rulers who lord it over others with his own rule, that the greatest must be the servant of all.
Greatness that comes from serving the people is the kind the Kural says the world will cling to.
Islam
“The best of your rulers are those whom you love and who love you, who invoke God's blessings upon you and you invoke His blessings upon them.”
— Sahih Muslim 1855a [5]
The Prophet describes the best rulers as those bound to their people in mutual love and prayer.
This is the same two-way embrace: the ruler holds the people and the people hold the ruler.
Judaism
“If thou wilt be a servant unto this people this day, and wilt serve them, and answer them, and speak good words to them, then they will be thy servants for ever.”
— 1 Kings 12:7 (JPS 1917) [6]
The elders advise the young king Rehoboam that if he serves the people kindly, they will be loyal to him for ever; he rejects their advice and loses most of the kingdom.
The same promise as the Kural: embrace the people and they will cling to you for good.
Buddhism
“All men are my children. What I desire for my own children, and I desire their welfare and happiness both in this world and the next, that I desire for all men.”
— Ashoka, Kalinga Rock Edict I (trans. S. Dhammika) [7]
The Buddhist emperor Ashoka tells his judicial officers that he wants every subject’s welfare as a father wants his children’s.
Ashoka’s paternal embrace of all subjects is the குடி தழீஇ ideal carved in stone.
Jainism
“During his reign he taught, for the benefit of the people, the seventy-two sciences, of which writing is the first, arithmetic the most important, and the knowledge of omens the last, the sixty-four accomplishments of women, the hundred arts, and the three occupations of men.”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Ṛṣabha §211 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Kalpa Sūtra presents Rishabha, called ‘First King’ before he became the first Tīrthaṅkara, as a ruler who spent his reign giving his people every skill they needed to live.
The Kural pictures a king who embraces his people and is embraced in return by the whole world, and Rishabha, the Jain model of a king devoted to his subjects' welfare, is still revered by Jains at his feet.
Sikhism
“Let no one chase after and attack anyone else. Let all abide in peace, under this Benevolent Rule.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 74, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan proclaims halemi raj, a gentle and benevolent rule under the merciful Lord's command, in which no one harms another and all live at ease.
The Kural says the world clings to the feet of the king who rules embracing his people; Guru Arjan's benevolent rule, where all abide in peace, is the Sikh picture of such rule.
Greek Mythology
In the Ithacan assembly, Mentor protests that no one now remembers Odysseus, who ruled his people gently, like a father, and bitterly suggests that kings might as well be harsh and unjust from now on.
— Homer, Odyssey 2.229–234 [10]
Odysseus is held up as the model king who treated his people like a father, and the people’s forgetfulness is condemned.
It gives the Kural’s ideal, the king who embraces his people, and complains when the loyalty that should follow is missing.
Philosophy
“That man's greatness alone rests upon a secure foundation, whom all men know to be as much on their side as he is above them … at whose approach they do not fly in terror, as though some evil and dangerous animal had sprung out from its den, but flock to him as they would to the bright and health-giving sunshine.”
— Seneca, Of Clemency (De Clementia), Book I, ch. 3 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
Seneca adds that the subjects of such a ruler guard his sleep, surround and defend him, and offer their own bodies to protect him.
The ruler who stands on his people's side is held fast by them, as the Kural's world clings to the feet of the king who embraces his people.

Real Life Example

Thailand honours King Chulalongkorn, the Great Beloved King
1868-1910 · Bangkok, Thailand
King Chulalongkorn (Rama V) came to the throne of Siam in 1868 and, after his coronation in 1873, carried out sweeping reforms beginning with the abolition of slavery, which was completed by the Slave Abolition Act of 1905, along with new law courts, schools, railways and a system of twelve ministries. He died on 23 October 1910 after ruling for 42 years and became known as Phra Piya Maharaj, the Great Beloved King. The anniversary of his death is a national holiday on which Thais lay wreaths at his equestrian statue in Bangkok's Royal Plaza and at other statues of him, in addition to the flowers that many offer there throughout the year.
— Sources: [12] [13]
More than a century after his death, his people still gather at the feet of his statue, a lasting sign of the affection earned by a ruler remembered for caring for his subjects.
The kural says the world clings to the feet of the great king who rules while embracing his people, and Thais still gather at the foot of Chulalongkorn's statue to honour him.
