Kural 771

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

என்னைமுன் நில்லன்மின் தெவ்விர் பலரென்னை முன்நின்று கல்நின் றவர்.
Ennaimun nillanmin thevvir palarennai Munnindru kalnin Ravar.

Meaning

A soldier warns the enemy: do not stand against my king. Many who once stood before him are now only stones that mark where they died.

Elementary

Imagine a champion team whose fans warn the visitors: “Lots of teams came here thinking they would win, and now their names are just on the list of teams we beat.” The warrior is proud of his leader in the same way, and the proud words themselves are meant to make the other side think twice.

University

The chapter title padaiccherukku joins padai (army) and serukku (pride, exultant spirit), and this opening couplet is cast as a battlefield vaunt addressed to thevvir (“you enemies”). Parimelazhagar takes ennai not as “me” but as en + ai, “my lord” (ai = master, chief), so the soldier boasts of his commander rather than of himself; some modern renderings (e.g., Sivayogi Sivakumar on ytamizh.com) read it as the speaker’s own challenge. The punning repetition of nil- / nin- turns on “standing”: those who stood (nindru) against the lord now stand as kal (stone), alluding to the nadukal, the hero-stone erected for fallen warriors, a custom described in the Tolkappiyam’s Purattinai and attested in Sangam puram poetry. Both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net give the couplet identically; the sandhi split “கல்நின் றவர்” = kal ninravar.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Therefore get up. Prepare to fight and win glory. Conquer your enemies and enjoy a flourishing kingdom. They are already put to death by My arrangement, and you, O Savyasācī, can be but an instrument in the fight.”
— Bhagavad Gita 11.33 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
After revealing his cosmic form, Krishna tells Arjuna that the opposing warriors are as good as fallen already; Arjuna need only step forward.
Like the soldier’s vaunt that foes who face his lord are already “stones,” Krishna declares the enemy’s fall decided before the battle.
Christianity
“Then said Jesus unto him, Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword.”
— Matthew 26:52 (KJV) [4]
When a disciple draws a sword to defend Jesus at his arrest, Jesus orders it sheathed and warns that violence returns on those who use it.
The Kural boasts that those who stood against the lord perished; Jesus turns the same observation into a warning against taking up the sword at all, a real divergence from martial pride.
Islam
“So do not weaken and do not grieve, and you will be superior if you are [true] believers.”
— Qur’an 3:139 (Saheeh International) [5]
Revealed after the setback at Uhud, the verse tells the believers not to lose heart, promising them the upper hand if their faith holds.
It shares the Kural’s confident spirit that the righteous side will prevail over those who oppose it.
Judaism
“Hear, O Israel! You are about to join battle with your enemy. Let not your courage falter. Do not be in fear, or in panic, or in dread of them. For it is the ETERNAL your God who marches with you to do battle for you against your enemy, to bring you victory.”
— Deuteronomy 20:3-4 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Before battle a priest addresses the army, telling them that their true commander fights at their head.
Like the Kural’s soldier, Israel’s fighters draw courage from the one who leads them, not from their own strength.
Buddhism
“Victory begets enmity; the defeated dwell in pain. Happily the peaceful live, discarding both victory and defeat.”
— Dhammapada 201 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada observes that every victory plants new hostility and every defeat leaves suffering, so the peaceful let go of both.
Where the Kural takes pride in a long line of defeated foes, the Buddha sees in that same line a chain of enmity, an honest divergence.
Jainism
“Though a man should conquer thousands and thousands of valiant (foes), greater will be his victory if he conquers nobody but himself.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
King Nami answers Indra's call to subdue rival princes by ranking conquest of the self above any number of battlefield victories.
The Kural's warrior boasts of the many foes his lord has turned into memorial stones, and Jainism answers that boast with a greater victory, one won over oneself.
Sikhism
“The battle-drum beats in the sky of the mind; aim is taken, and the wound is inflicted. The spiritual warriors enter the field of battle; now is the time to fight!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir's salok uses the language of the battlefield, with drums, aim and the hero entering the field, to call the seeker to the inner fight.
The Kural gives a warrior's defiant battlefield cry to the enemy; Kabir's salok has the same martial ring of a hero announcing that the hour of battle has come.
Greek Mythology
When Achilles, still unarmed, appears at the trench and shouts three times, the Trojans are thrown into panic and draw back, letting the Greeks recover Patroclus’ body.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 18 (paraphrased from the Butler translation) [10]
In the Iliad the mere presence and war cry of the great warrior is enough to break the enemy’s nerve.
The Kural’s warning “do not stand before my lord” rests on the same idea: a hero’s reputation fights before he strikes.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that the rash man is often a boaster and a pretender to courage, bold before danger but failing in it, while the truly brave are calm beforehand and keen in action.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.7 (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, loud confidence is not proof of courage; it must be matched by steadiness when danger actually comes.
The Kural’s vaunt is not empty because it points to deeds already done, the many who fell; Aristotle explains why such a track record matters.

Real Life Example

Darius I carves the Behistun relief over his defeated rivals
c. 521-520 BCE · Bisotun (Behistun), Kermanshah Province, Iran
After winning the Persian throne, Darius I had a great relief and inscription carved high on the cliff at Bisotun, beside the ancient road from Mesopotamia to the Iranian plateau. The relief shows Darius, holding a bow as a sign of sovereignty, with his foot on a figure identified as the usurper Gaumata, and a line of bound captives standing before him. The text, in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian, records how he overthrew Gaumata, defeated the rebels who claimed the kingdom, and took the throne.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The monument turned the defeat of a king's opponents into a permanent warning in stone for everyone who travelled the road.
The kural's warrior warns enemies not to stand against his lord, since many who did now stand as stones, and at Behistun those who rose against Darius stand forever carved in stone before him.
