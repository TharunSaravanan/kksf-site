Kural 1182

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

அவர்தந்தார் என்னும் தகையால் இவர்தந்தென் மேனிமேல் ஊரும் பசப்பு.
avardhandhaar ennum thakaiyaal ivardhandhaen maenimael oorum pasappu

Meaning

Proud that he gave it to me, this paleness climbs all over my body and spreads as if it owned it.

Elementary

Think of a sticker your favourite person gave you: you might put it everywhere, proud of it because of who gave it. The wife says the pale colour on her skin acts like that sticker. It spreads all over her, proud because her husband ‘gave’ it to her by going away.

University

The heroine personifies pasappu (பசப்பு) as a proud guest: avar tantār ennum takaiyāl, ‘with the dignity of “he gave it”’, it ivarntu (இவர்ந்து, ‘mounting, climbing’) and ūrum (ஊரும், ‘creeps, spreads, rides’) over her mēni (மேனி, body, complexion). Takai here is ‘worth, pride’; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both render the pallor as spreading ‘with pride’ (பெருமிதம்) because the lover caused it. Pope’s rendering takes ūrum in the sense of riding: ‘“He gave”: this sickly hue thus proudly speaks, / Then climbs, and all my frame its chariot makes.’ The conceit, that even the affliction is precious because it comes from him, is characteristic of the karpiyal chapters and of the traditional reading associated with Parimelazhagar, in which the wife cannot fully resent what her husband’s absence has given her. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Meghaduta the exiled yaksha pictures his wife at home, wasted and pale with separation, lying on one side of her bed like the last thin crescent of the moon, her beauty itself changed by her grief for him.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Uttaramegha (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa makes the wife’s altered, pallid body the very sign and record of her husband’s absence.
The Kural’s heroine says the same: the pallor spreading over her is his gift, the mark his absence has left on her.
Christianity
“From henceforth let no man trouble me: for I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus.”
— Galatians 6:17 (KJV) [4]
Paul treats the scars he has suffered for Christ as marks of belonging that he carries with pride.
The heroine’s pallor is likewise a bodily mark she wears proudly because it comes from the one she loves.
Islam
“And he turned away from them and said, ‘Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,’ and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.”
— Qur’an 12:84 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s long grief for his absent son visibly changes his body, whitening his eyes.
As in the Kural, separation from a beloved writes itself on the body as a change of colour.
Judaism
“Set me as a seal upon thy heart, As a seal upon thine arm; For love is strong as death, Jealousy is cruel as the grave; The flashes thereof are flashes of fire, A very flame of the LORD.”
— Song of Songs 8:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved asks to be pressed onto the lover’s heart and arm like a seal, a mark that shows whose they are.
The heroine’s pallor is that kind of seal: a mark on her body that proclaims whose love caused it.
Buddhism
“From what’s dear is born grief, from what’s dear is born fear. For one freed from what’s dear there’s no grief — so how fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha traces grief back to its source in holding someone dear.
The heroine says her pallor was ‘given by him’; the Dhammapada agrees that the grief is born from the dear one, though it draws the opposite lesson.
Jainism
“Pleasant things (by themselves) do not cause indifference nor emotions (as anger, &c.); but by either hating or loving them, a man undergoes such a change through delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.101 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that inner and outer changes come not from objects or other people but from one’s own attachment to them.
The heroine credits her lover with ‘giving’ her pallor; the Jain view would locate the cause in her own attachment, an honest contrast to the Kural’s conceit.
Sikhism
“Your actions seem so sweet to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 394, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that whatever the Lord does, including what brings pain, tastes sweet to the devotee, because it comes from Him.
The Kural's pallor spreads proudly over her body because it was given by her husband; Gurbani likewise finds even hardship sweet because the Beloved sent it.
Greek Mythology
Sappho describes what happens to her when she sees the beloved: her tongue breaks, a thin fire runs under her skin, her eyes see nothing, sweat pours down, trembling seizes her, and she becomes greener than grass, seeming to herself little short of dying.
— Sappho, fragment 31 (paraphrase) [10]
In this famous poem love shows itself as a physical change of colour, a pallor or greenness that takes over the whole body.
The Kural’s pasappu that ‘climbs over my body’ is the same idea: love’s pallor spreading across the lover’s frame.
Philosophy
Lucretius warns that lovers waste their strength and are worn out with toil, their bodies and fortunes spent while they live at another’s beck and call.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1121-1124 (paraphrase) [11]
The Epicurean poet treats love’s physical toll on the lover as a reason for caution.
The Kural’s pallor is that physical toll, but where Lucretius sees only harm, the heroine sees a gift from her husband.

Real Life Example

Ramesses II honours Queen Nefertari in temple and tomb
13th century BCE · Abu Simbel and the Valley of the Queens, Egypt
Nefertari was the first wife and favourite queen of the pharaoh Ramesses II. Ramesses had her likeness carved on temple walls and statues long after he had taken other wives, and at Abu Simbel a temple was dedicated to the goddess Hathor and to Nefertari. Her tomb in the Valley of the Queens was decorated with paintings regarded as masterpieces of the age.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nefertari's monuments show a queen whose standing came from the honour her husband publicly gave her.
In the kural the pallor spreads proudly over the wife's body because 'he gave it', and Nefertari's image was spread across Egypt's monuments with the pride of something her husband had bestowed.
