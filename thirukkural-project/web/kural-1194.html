Kural 1194

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

வீழப் படுவார் கெழீஇயிலர் தாம்வீழ்வார் வீழப் படாஅர் எனின்.
Veezhap paduvaar kezhee-iyilar thaamveezhvaar veezhap pataa-ar enin.

Meaning

Even a woman loved by many is truly unlucky if the one she loves does not love her back.

Elementary

Imagine everyone at school likes your drawing, but the one person you made it for doesn’t care about it. All the other praise wouldn’t feel like enough. The Kural says love works like that.

University

The couplet turns on a contrast within the same verb: vīḻappaṭuvār, ‘those who are desired’ (by others, or by the world), are kezhīiyilar, ‘not endowed (with good fortune)’, if they are vīḻappaṭār, ‘not desired’, by tām vīḻvār, ‘those whom they themselves desire.’ Parimelazhagar’s line, followed by Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan, glosses kezhīiyilar as lacking nalviṉai (the fruit of past merit); Pappaiah sharpens it to being under tīviṉai, while Kalaignar instead reads the lover as lacking the friendly feeling. Pope: ‘Those well-beloved will luckless prove, / Unless beloved by those they love.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net, both with the alapeṭai spelling படாஅர்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Shakuntala, married in secret to King Dushyanta, comes to his court with her child, but under a curse he fails to recognise her and sends her away; a woman honoured by sages and loved by her forest home is left desolate because the one she loves does not know her.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 5 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [3]
Kalidasa’s heroine is cherished by everyone except the one person whose love she needs.
Shakuntala’s rejection is the Kural’s point exactly: being loved by many is no fortune without the beloved’s love.
Christianity
“and though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that every gift and achievement counts for nothing without love.
The Kural similarly says every other kind of admiration counts for little without the love that matters most.
Islam
“And you will never be able to be equal [in feeling] between wives, even if you should strive [to do so]. So do not incline completely [toward one] and leave another hanging.”
— Qur’an 4:129 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns husbands not to leave a wife ‘hanging’, neither truly married nor free, because his heart has turned away.
The neglected wife of this verse, honoured in name but unloved in fact, is the woman the Kural calls unfortunate.
Judaism
“And Michal Saul’s daughter loved David”
— 1 Samuel 18:20 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Michal, a king’s daughter, is one of the few women in the Bible explicitly said to love a man, yet the text never says David loved her in return, and her story ends in estrangement.
A princess with every advantage who is not loved by the man she loves is the Kural’s ‘unfortunate’ in biblical form.
Buddhism
In the Sambula Jataka, Princess Sambula faithfully nurses her husband through a loathsome illness in the forest, but once he is healed and becomes king he neglects her; though she is his chief queen in name, she wastes away until her father intervenes.
— Sambula Jataka (Jataka 519) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Jataka tells of a virtuous queen honoured by all yet left without her husband’s love.
Sambula is the very woman of this couplet: honoured in every way, yet unfortunate because her husband does not love her.
Jainism
In the Jain Ramayana tradition, the princess Anjana is married to Pavananjaya, who, over a misunderstanding, refuses to speak to or look at her for many years; honoured as a queen, she lives in grief until he finally realises his error and returns to her, and she becomes the mother of Hanuman.
— Hemachandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 7 (Jain Ramayana), story of Anjana and Pavananjaya (paraphrase; cf. tr. Helen M. Johnson); also in Vimalasuri’s Paumacariya [8]
Anjana’s story is the classic Jain tale of a noble, admired wife who is ignored by the one husband whose love she wants.
Anjana is the Kural’s unfortunate woman exactly: esteemed by all, yet bereft because her beloved does not love her.
Sikhism
“Without the Name of the Lord, the beautiful are just like the noseless ones.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 528, Raag Dayv Gandhaaree, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that beauty and the admiration it wins mean nothing without love for the Lord's Name; such a person is as disgraced as one whose nose has been cut off.
The Kural says that even a woman everyone admires is unfortunate if the one she loves does not love her back; Gurbani likewise says the admired and beautiful are without real worth if they lack the love that matters.
Greek Mythology
Ariadne, the Cretan princess who saved Theseus from the Labyrinth, wakes on the shore of Naxos to find him sailing away, and pours out her grief at being abandoned by the man for whom she gave up everything.
— Catullus, Poem 64.52-201 (paraphrase) [10]
A royal heroine with every claim to honour is left wretched because the man she loves has not loved her as she loved him.
Ariadne illustrates the Kural’s claim that no rank or regard makes up for unreturned love.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that the fortunate man still needs friends, since no one would choose to have every good thing on condition of being alone.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.9, 1169b (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, all other goods together cannot make a life happy without the bond of love with others.
The Kural applies the same insight to lovers: all other blessings leave one unfortunate without the beloved’s love.

Real Life Example

Catherine of Aragon is set aside by Henry VIII
1527–1536 · England
In 1527 Henry VIII asked the pope to annul his long marriage to Catherine of Aragon, arguing that it broke a biblical rule. Catherine appealed to Rome and insisted she was his lawful wife; Britannica notes that she had always been loved by the English people. Henry separated from her in 1531, Archbishop Thomas Cranmer declared the marriage void on 23 May 1533, and she died at Kimbolton in January 1536, still signing herself queen.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Catherine kept the affection and respect of the country, yet none of it could make up for the husband who had turned away from her.
The kural says a woman admired by everyone is still unfortunate if the one she loves does not love her back, which is the shape of Catherine's last years.
