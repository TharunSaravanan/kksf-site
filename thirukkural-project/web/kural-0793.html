Kural 793

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

குணமும் குடிமையும் குற்றமும் குன்றா இனனும் அறிந்தியாக்க நட்பு.
Gunamum kudimaiyum kutramum kundraa inanum arindhiyaakka natpu.

Meaning

Make someone your friend only after you know their character, their family, their faults, and the people they spend time with.

Elementary

Before you join a new team, you watch how the players behave, who their coach is, what mistakes they make, and who they hang out with. Valluvar says to do the same four checks before you choose a close friend.

University

This couplet supplies the checklist for the chapter’s ‘investigation’: gunam (குணம், disposition or character), kudimai (குடிமை, lineage, the family in which one is raised), kutram (குற்றம், faults), and kundraa inam (குன்றா இனம், one’s abiding associates or kin). Parimelazhagar takes inam as the circle of relatives and companions who stand by a person, on the principle, developed in chapter 46 (Sitrinam Seraamai), that a person takes on the quality of the company he keeps; Puliyur Kesigan accordingly glosses it as ‘the companions who stay with him’. Arindhu yaakka (அறிந்து யாக்க) means ‘form it having known’. The ytamizh and thirukkural.net texts read குணமும், while the sandhi-joined edition reads குணனுங் குடிமையுங், an older spelling with the same meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Ravana’s brother Vibhishana comes to Rama asking for refuge and friendship, Rama asks his counsellors for their views; Sugriva and others suspect a spy because of his lineage, while Hanuman argues from Vibhishana’s conduct and speech that he is sincere, and only after weighing these views does Rama accept him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 17-18 [4]
The episode in the Yuddha Kanda is a formal inquiry into a would-be ally’s family, character and motives before a bond is formed.
Rama’s council examines exactly what the Kural lists: nature, lineage, possible faults and associations.
Christianity
“Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles?”
— Matthew 7:16 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that a person’s true nature shows in what he produces, as a tree is known by its fruit.
Both texts say a person’s character must be read from evidence before you trust him.
Islam
“A man follows the religion of his friend; so each one should consider whom he makes his friend.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4833 (graded hasan by al-Albani) [6]
The Prophet warns that a person takes on the ways of his close friend, so the choice of friend must be weighed carefully.
This hadith gives the reason for Valluvar’s checklist: you become like the people you befriend.
Judaism
“Rabbi Joshua said, a good companion; Rabbi Yose said, a good neighbor … Rabbi Joshua said, an evil companion; Rabbi Yose said, an evil neighbor”
— Pirkei Avot 2:9 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Asked which path a person should cleave to and which he should shun, Rabban Yohanan’s disciples name the good or evil companion and neighbour among the deciding factors.
Like the Kural, the Mishnah treats the people around a person as something to examine and choose with care.
Buddhism
“These four, young householder, should be understood as foes in the guise of friends: (1) he who appropriates a friend’s possessions, (2) he who renders lip-service, (3) he who flatters, (4) he who brings ruin.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
The Buddha gives Sigala a list of signs that reveal a false friend, each broken down into four observable behaviours.
Like the Kural, the sutta turns friendship into something to be checked against specific marks of character.
Jainism
“A Sramana engaged in austerities, who longs for righteousness, should eat the proper quantity of allowed food, should select a companion of right understanding, and should live in a place suited to seclusion.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahāvīra's teaching on the path to liberation tells the monk to choose his companion deliberately, for that companion's sound understanding.
The Kural tells us to make friends only after weighing a person's character, family, faults and associates, and the Jain canon likewise makes the careful choice of a companion part of the disciplined life.
Sikhism
“Kabir! The mind has become a bird; it soars and flies in the ten directions. According to the company it keeps, so are the fruits it eats.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir 86 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir says the restless mind settles wherever its company is, and what it becomes depends on that company, so the people around a person are a true index of what that person will become.
The Kural tells us to check a would-be friend's character, faults and the circle he keeps close; Kabir explains why the circle matters, since a person takes the fruit of the company he keeps.
Greek Mythology
On the battlefield before Troy, Diomedes asks the Lycian Glaucus who he is; Glaucus recites his lineage back to Bellerophon, and Diomedes realises their grandfathers were guest-friends, so instead of fighting they clasp hands, renew the friendship and exchange armour.
— Homer, Iliad 6.119-236 [11]
In Iliad 6 an enemy becomes a friend only after his family and ancestral ties are laid out.
The episode shows the Kural’s concern with kudimai and inam: lineage and associations are checked before friendship is sealed.
Philosophy
“Now the qualities we ought to look out for in making our selection are firmness, stability, constancy. There is a plentiful lack of men so endowed, and it is difficult to form a judgment without testing.”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia §17 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [12]
Cicero’s Laelius complains that people count their sheep carefully but choose friends with no criteria, and he names the qualities to look for.
Cicero, like Valluvar, gives a checklist of character traits to verify before friendship.

Real Life Example

Britain introduces positive vetting after the Burgess and Maclean defections
1952 · London, United Kingdom
In 1951 the British diplomats Guy Burgess and Donald Maclean, who had been recruited by Soviet intelligence before the Second World War, defected to the Soviet Union. In response, the British government in 1952 introduced positive vetting, a procedure intended to establish the integrity of civil servants doing exceptionally secret work. Earlier screening had focused on Communist or Fascist sympathies and associations, but positive vetting widened the inquiry to a person's character, and in 1956 the government declared publicly that character defects could affect a civil servant's posting or promotion.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The reform reflected a hard lesson: trusted men had been given access to vital secrets without anyone examining closely their character and associations.
The kural advises learning a person's character, background, faults and associates before admitting him to close friendship, and positive vetting applied that same examination before admitting a person to the state's secrets.
