Kural 647

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

சொலல்வல்லன் சோர்விலன் அஞ்சான் அவனை இகல்வெல்லல் யார்க்கும் அரிது.
Solalvallan sorvilan anjaan avanai Ikalvellal yaarkkum aridhu.

Meaning

It is hard for anyone to beat a person in an argument when that person speaks powerfully, never slips up, and is not afraid.

Elementary

Picture a class debate. The student who explains clearly, doesn’t forget their points, and isn’t scared to speak up is very hard to beat. This kural says those three things together make someone almost unbeatable.

University

Three predicate nouns build the ideal speaker: colal vallan (strong in speaking), cōrvilan (without lapse; Parimelazhagar glosses cōrvu as forgetfulness of what is to be said, echoing 642) and añcān (one who does not fear, above all fear of the assembly, the theme of chapter 73, Avaiyañcāmai). Ikal means enmity, rivalry or contention, so ikal vellal is victory through opposition; Pope renders it as intrigue or “hostile power”. Solomon Pappaiah expands the three terms to the ability to make others accept one’s thought, persistence even when the message is hard, and fearlessness even before hostile hearers. The older edition prints சோர்வில னஞ்சா னவனை / யிகல்வெல்லல் in sandhi form.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Before the Kurukshetra war, Krishna goes alone as envoy to the Kaurava court and argues the Pandavas’ case fearlessly and without faltering before Duryodhana and the assembled elders, who cannot answer him even though they reject him.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Bhagavad-yana), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
The peace embassy shows the ideal envoy: articulate, unflinching and not intimidated by a hostile court.
Krishna has all three of the Kural’s qualities: power of speech, no lapse, and no fear.
Christianity
“And they were not able to resist the wisdom and the spirit by which he spake.”
— Acts 6:10 (KJV) [4]
Stephen’s opponents in the synagogues cannot stand against his words, though they later have him killed.
Stephen is the Kural’s speaker whom no one can overcome in open argument.
Islam
“[Allah] said, “Fear not. Indeed, I am with you both; I hear and I see.”
— Qur’an 20:46 (Sahih International) [5]
Sent to confront Pharaoh, Moses and Aaron admit their fear, and God tells them to go without fear and speak.
Freedom from fear before a hostile power is the añcān (fearless) quality in the Kural’s portrait.
Judaism
The prophet Nathan confronts King David over Bathsheba with a parable about a rich man who takes a poor man’s only lamb, leading David to condemn himself before Nathan says, “You are the man.
— 2 Samuel 12:1-13 (JPS 1917) [6]
Nathan speaks fearlessly to a king, and his carefully built story leaves David with no defence.
Skill, precision and courage together make Nathan’s speech unanswerable, as the Kural describes.
Buddhism
Prince Abhaya is sent by the Jain leader Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta with a two-horned question meant to trap the Buddha, but the Buddha answers calmly and exactly, and the trap fails.
— Majjhima Nikaya 58 (Abhayarājakumāra Sutta) [7]
The sutta shows the Buddha as a speaker who cannot be cornered because he is clear, careful and unafraid.
A speaker no trap can catch fits the Kural’s ideal of one whom no rival can overcome.
Jainism
“A monk living single should not ridicule heretical doctrines, and should avoid hard words though they be true; he should not be vain, nor brag, but he should without embarrassment and passion (preach the Law). A monk should be modest though he be of a fearless mind.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.21–22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The ideal Jain preacher speaks without embarrassment and with a fearless mind, yet without hard words, boasting or passion.
The kural describes the speaker who is strong, does not slip and does not fear; the Jain preacher shares the fearlessness and composure, though his goal is to teach, not to defeat rivals.
Sikhism
“Nanak speaks the Word of Truth; he proclaims the Truth at this, the right time.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 723, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
This line closes Guru Nanak's hymn in Tilang on Babur's invasion, in which he openly condemned the violence of the conquest while it was happening.
The Kural says no one can overcome a speaker who is forceful, never slips, and does not fear; Guru Nanak's fearless proclamation of truth to power shows that kind of unshaken speaker.
Greek Mythology
“but when he raised his voice, and the words came driving from his deep chest like winter snow before the wind, then there was none to touch him”
— Homer, Iliad 3.216-224, trans. Samuel Butler [10]
Antenor recalls Odysseus’ embassy to Troy: unimpressive at first sight, but when he spoke no one could match him.
“None to touch him” is the Homeric version of the Kural’s “hard for anyone to defeat”.
Philosophy
Quintilian defines the ideal orator, following Cato, as a good man skilled in speaking (vir bonus dicendi peritus), who combines moral steadiness and courage with eloquence.
— Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria 12.1 [11]
For Quintilian, eloquence without character and courage is not true oratory.
Quintilian’s combination of skill, integrity and courage reflects the three qualities of the Kural.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela's statement from the dock at the Rivonia Trial
20 April 1964 · Supreme Court, Pretoria, South Africa
Facing charges of sabotage that could carry the death penalty, Nelson Mandela opened the defence case at the Rivonia Trial by making a statement from the dock instead of giving evidence under cross-examination. In a long statement he explained his actions and the cause of a free and democratic society, ending with the declaration that it was an ideal for which he was prepared to die. On 12 June 1964 Judge Quartus de Wet sentenced him and seven others to life imprisonment, saying he had decided not to impose the supreme penalty.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mandela spoke without fear, without faltering and with great force in a hostile courtroom, and his statement became one of the most famous speeches of the twentieth century.
The kural says no one can easily overcome a speaker who is powerful, never slips and does not fear, and Mandela's statement from the dock was not defeated even though he lost the case.
