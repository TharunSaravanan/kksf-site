Kural 949

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

உற்றான் அளவும் பிணியளவும் காலமும் கற்றான் கருதிச் செயல்.
Utraan alavum piniyalavum kaalamum katraan karudhich cheyal.

Meaning

A trained doctor should think about the patient, how serious the sickness is, and the time or season before treating it.

Elementary

A baby and a grown-up with the same cold do not get the same dose of medicine. Valluvar says a good doctor thinks about who is sick, how sick they are, and what time it is before deciding what to do.

University

Three measures must be weighed: utraan alavu (உற்றான் அளவு), the measure of the one afflicted, which Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss as age and the like, and Kesikan as the patient's strength; pini alavu (பிணியளவு), the extent and nature of the disease; and kaalam (காலம்), which Sivayogi Sivakumar reads as the climate of the season, Kalaignar as the right time for treatment, and Pappaiah as both the duration of the illness and the time needed to cure it. The agent is katraan (கற்றான்), 'the one who has learned', i.e. the physician trained in the medical texts, and karudhi (கருதி) means 'having deliberated'. The couplet thus individualises treatment, echoing Ayurveda's attention to the patient's constitution and to season (ritu). Pope renders it 'the habitudes of patient and disease, the crises of the ill these must the learned leech think over well'. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Charaka lays down a tenfold examination of the patient, including his constitution, strength, body measure, power of digestion, capacity for exercise and age, together with attention to place and season, before any therapy is chosen.
— Charaka Samhita, Vimanasthana 8.94 (dashavidha pariksha), paraphrase [3]
Ayurvedic treatment is tailored to the individual patient, the state of the disease and the time.
This is the Kural's weighing of patient, disease and season before acting.
Christianity
“My son, prove thy soul in thy life, and see what is evil for it, and give not that unto it. For all things are not profitable for all men, neither hath every soul pleasure in every thing.”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 37:27-28 (KJV Apocrypha) [4]
Ben Sira teaches that what helps one person may harm another, so each must test what suits him.
The Kural's attention to the particular patient rests on the same insight that not everything suits everyone.
Islam
The Prophet said that whoever practises medicine without being known for medical knowledge is liable for the harm he causes.
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4586, paraphrase [5]
The hadith makes learning the condition for the right to treat.
The Kural likewise entrusts treatment to katraan, the one who has learned.
Judaism
“All of these beneficial habits which we have stated apply only to a healthy man. In contrast, a sick person, or one who has a single organ which is not healthy, or one who has followed a harmful way of life for many years, each of these must choose different patterns of behavior in accordance with his [particular] illness as it is explained in the medical literature.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:21 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides insists that the sick must be treated according to their own illness and condition, following the medical books.
This is the Kural's rule that treatment must fit the particular patient and disease as the learned texts direct.
Buddhism
The Buddha distinguishes three kinds of sick people: those who recover whether or not they get suitable food, medicine and nursing, those who do not recover either way, and those who recover only if they get them.
— Gilana Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.22, paraphrase [7]
The sutta recognises that different patients respond differently to treatment.
Like the Kural, it asks the carer to weigh the particular patient rather than treat all alike.
Jainism
“Abstinence is briefly of five kinds: with regard to a. substance; b. place; c. time; d. state of mind; e. development.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain discipline calibrates even reduced eating by dravya, kshetra, kala and bhava, the substance, place, time and inner state of the one practising it.
The Kural's weighing of the person, the measure of the illness and the time follows the same habit of judging every practice by its circumstances.
Sikhism
“The physician was called in; he touched my arm and felt my pulse. The foolish physician did not know that the pain was in the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1279, Raag Malaar, Malaar ki Vaar, salok of Guru Nanak (Mahala 1) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the salok just before Guru Angad's, Guru Nanak describes a physician who takes the pulse but misses the real illness, the pain of separation from God within.
The Kural asks the learned physician to weigh the patient's real condition before acting; Guru Nanak shows what goes wrong when a physician does not grasp the patient's true state.
Greek Mythology
Pindar sings that Asclepius relieved all who came to him, whether with ulcers, wounds, or bodies wasted by summer heat or winter cold, treating some with gentle incantations, some with soothing potions, some with remedies bound on the limbs, and some with the knife.
— Pindar, Pythian Ode 3.47-53, paraphrase [10]
The divine healer chose a different treatment for each sufferer and condition, including those brought on by the season.
Asclepius fits the Kural's learned healer who weighs patient, disease and season before acting.
Philosophy
“...if ten pounds are too much for a particular person to eat and two too little, it does not follow that the trainer will order six pounds; for this also is perhaps too much for the person who is to take it, or too little- too little for Milo, too much for the beginner in athletic exercises.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle's trainer sets the right amount relative to the person, not by a fixed rule.
The Kural's physician likewise sets treatment by the measure of the particular patient.

Real Life Example

Dominique-Jean Larrey introduces flying ambulances and triage
1790s · French Revolutionary and Napoleonic armies, Europe
Dominique-Jean Larrey, a surgeon in the French army, introduced horse-drawn flying ambulances in the 1790s to carry wounded soldiers from the battlefield quickly instead of leaving them until the fighting ended. He also insisted that the wounded be treated in order of how badly they were hurt and how urgently they needed care, not according to their rank. His system of sorting casualties is regarded as the origin of modern triage.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Larrey made the patient's condition and the urgency of the moment, rather than social standing, the basis for medical decisions.
The kural asks the physician to weigh the patient, the extent of the disease and the time before acting, and Larrey's triage did exactly that on the battlefield.
