Kural 1169

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

கொடியார் கொடுமையின் தாம்கொடிய இந்நாள் நெடிய கழியும் இரா.
Kotiyaar kodumaiyin thaamkotiya innaal netiya kazhiyum iraa.

Meaning

These long, slow nights are even crueler than the cruel man who left me.

Elementary

When you are waiting for something you really want, like a birthday, the days feel super long. The wife waiting for her husband says the long nights are even meaner to her than her husband was for going away.

University

The couplet plays on koṭu (கொடு, cruel): koṭiyār (கொடியார், ‘the cruel one’, the departed husband), koṭumai (கொடுமை, cruelty) and koṭiya (கொடிய, cruel) are stacked so that the nights, iṉṉāḷ neṭiya kaḻiyum irā (‘the nights which in these days pass so long’), outdo the husband in cruelty. The comparison lets the heroine blame the nights rather than the husband, softening the reproach of 1165. There is a sandhi variant in the first line: ytamizh.com prints தாம்கொடிய இந்நாள், while thirukkural.net prints தாம்கொடிய விந்நாள், with the glide v of koṭiya + iṉṉāḷ written out; the reading is the same. Pope: ‘More cruel than the cruelty of him, the cruel one, / In these sad times are lengthening hours of night I watch alone.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Meghaduta, the separated yaksha wishes that the night with its long watches could somehow be shortened to a moment, and that his days apart might pass more quickly.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Uttaramegha (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa’s lover feels the night of separation stretch out unbearably.
The Kural’s heroine says the same: the long nights of separation are her worst tormentors.
Christianity
“So am I made to possess months of vanity, and wearisome nights are appointed to me. When I lie down, I say, When shall I arise, and the night be gone? and I am full of tossings to and fro unto the dawning of the day.”
— Job 7:3-4 (KJV) [4]
Job describes nights that will not end, spent tossing and waiting for dawn.
Job’s wearisome nights are the Kural’s nights that ‘pass so long’, felt as a torment in themselves.
Islam
“…to the point that the earth closed in on them in spite of its vastness and their souls confined them…”
— Qur’an 9:118 (Sahih International) [5]
The three companions who were shunned after staying behind felt the wide earth itself shrink around them in their anguish.
As anguish made the vast earth feel narrow to them, longing makes the heroine’s nights feel endlessly long.
Judaism
“In the morning thou shalt say, Would God it were even! and at even thou shalt say, Would God it were morning!”
— Deuteronomy 28:67 (KJV) [4]
Among the curses of Deuteronomy is a dread so constant that every hour seems too long and the next one is wished for.
The Kural’s heroine lives this restlessness at night, when every hour drags and she longs for it to pass.
Buddhism
“Long is the night to the sleepless; long is the league to the weary. Long is worldly existence to fools who know not the Sublime Truth.”
— Dhammapada 60 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada begins its chapter on the fool by noting how the night stretches out for one who cannot sleep.
The Kural’s heroine knows exactly this: for the sleepless, the night is long and cruel.
Jainism
“The day that goes by will never return; the days elapse without profit to him who acts contrary to the Law.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Bhrigu’s sons warn that time spent without the Law is lost; Jacobi notes that the original literally says ‘the nights’, the old way of counting time.
The heroine counts her separation in nights that pass painfully; the Jain verse also counts life in nights, but urges using them for the Law rather than in longing.
Sikhism
“When I could not be with You for just one moment, the Dark Age of Kali Yuga dawned for me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 96, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan says that even a moment apart from his Beloved Guru stretched into a whole dark age; Sikh tradition places this verse among his letters from Lahore.
The Kural's heroine finds the long nights of separation crueller even than the husband who left; Guru Arjan likewise describes how time apart from the beloved swells into an age of darkness.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Penelope tells the disguised Odysseus that by day she can find relief in weeping and in her household tasks, but when night comes and all others are asleep, she lies awake with sharp cares crowding her heart.
— Homer, Odyssey 19 (paraphrase) [9]
Penelope finds the nights of her husband’s long absence harder than the days.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Penelope finds the long nights the cruellest part of separation.
Philosophy
Augustine argues that time is measured in the mind itself, so that a ‘long future’ is really a long expectation of the future, stretched out in the soul that waits.
— Augustine, Confessions, Book XI (paraphrase) [10]
For Augustine, how long time seems depends on the stretching of the waiting mind.
The heroine’s nights are long because her mind is stretched out in waiting for her husband’s return.

Real Life Example

The Mother of Michitsuna's poem on a night spent alone
Heian period (diary covers 954-974) · Heian-kyo (Kyoto), Japan
The Heian court poet known as the Mother of Michitsuna recorded about twenty years of her marriage to the courtier Fujiwara no Kaneie, from 954 to 974, in the Kagero Diary, a marriage that ended in separation. One of her poems, later chosen as poem 53 of the Hyakunin Isshu anthology, asks whether he can possibly know how long a night is for someone who lies alone grieving until dawn.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Her poem became one of the classic Japanese expressions of the endless night of a wife waiting for a husband who does not come.
The kural says the long nights are crueller even than the cruel husband who left, and this poem is built on exactly that complaint about the length of a night spent alone.
