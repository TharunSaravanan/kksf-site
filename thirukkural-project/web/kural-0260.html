Kural 260

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

கொல்லான் புலாலை மறுத்தானைக் கைகூப்பி எல்லா உயிருந் தொழும்.
Kollaan pulaalai maruththaanaik kaikooppi, Ellaa uyirum thozhum.

Meaning

All living creatures will put their hands together and honor the one who does not kill and refuses to eat meat.

Elementary

Imagine every animal in the world putting its paws together to say “thank you” to one person. That’s the picture here: the person who never kills and never eats animals is loved and honoured by every living thing.

University

The chapter closes with an image of cosmic reverence: எல்லா உயிரும் (all living beings) perform கைகூப்பித் தொழுதல் — the joined-palm gesture of worship. The two qualifications, கொல்லான் (one who does not kill) and புலாலை மறுத்தான் (one who has refused flesh), sum up the chapter and anticipate the next section on கொல்லாமை. The hyperbole of animals folding hands is deliberate: the one who renounces power over other lives receives from them the honour usually given to gods. Parallel visions appear in Isaiah’s peaceable kingdom, Empedocles’ age when “all things were tame and gentle to man,” and the Buddhist teaching that one who cultivates goodwill is dear to human and non-human beings alike.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He for whom no one is put into difficulty … is very dear to Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.15 [3]
Krishna says the person who causes no living being distress is especially beloved of God.
Like the Kural, the one who harms no creature receives the highest honour.
Christianity
In the Little Flowers of St Francis, birds gather around Francis as he preaches to them, stretching their necks and bowing their heads, and do not fly away until he blesses them; later tradition tells of the wolf of Gubbio made gentle by him.
— The Little Flowers of St Francis (Fioretti), ch. 16 and ch. 21 [4]
Christian legend pictures creatures responding with reverence to a saint of radical gentleness.
A near-literal image of the Kural’s “all living beings worship him.”
Islam
“Yes, there is a reward for serving any animate (living being).”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2466 [5]
The Prophet told of a man forgiven by God for giving water to a thirsty dog, and taught that kindness to any living creature is rewarded.
Both texts see kindness to all living beings as earning the highest esteem; in Islam the honour comes from God.
Judaism
“The wolf also shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid… They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain: for the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea.”
— Isaiah 11:6-9 (KJV) [6]
Isaiah envisions a messianic age when no creature harms another.
The peaceable kingdom is the social form of the Kural’s ideal: a world where no one kills, honoured by all creatures.
Buddhism
“One is dear to human beings, dear to non-human beings. The devas protect one.”
— Mettanisamsa Sutta, AN 11.16 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Among the eleven benefits of cultivating goodwill (metta), the Buddha lists being beloved by humans and non-humans and protected by the gods.
Very close to the Kural’s promise that the non-harming person is revered by all living beings.
Jainism
The opening verse of the Dasavaikalika Sutra, placed in the Saman Suttam's Dharmasutra, calls dharma the highest good, names its essence as non-violence (ahimsa), self-control (samyama) and penance (tapas), and says that even the gods bow down to one whose mind is always set on dharma.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 1.1, as Saman Suttam v. 82 (Dharmasutra) [8]
Jain tradition holds that one whose life is grounded in non-violence wins the reverence even of the gods.
Valluvar says all living beings will join their palms in worship of the one who neither kills nor eats flesh; the Dasavaikalika says the gods themselves bow to the one whose dharma is rooted in ahimsa.
Sikhism
“And when, O Nanak, he is merciful to all beings, only then shall he be called a Muslim.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Vaar Maajh (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Vaar Maajh Guru Nanak lists the real marks of a believer and ends by saying that only someone merciful to all living beings has earned the name.
The Kural promises the reverence of all living beings to the one who neither kills nor eats flesh; Guru Nanak likewise makes mercy toward all beings the condition for true religious honour.
Greek Mythology
Orpheus, the legendary singer linked to the Orphic way of life that abstained from killing, drew wild beasts, birds, trees and stones to gather around him in peace when he sang.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses X-XI (Orpheus) [10]
Myth pictures all nature gathering in rapt attention around the gentle Orpheus.
An image of all creatures honouring one figure of harmlessness, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“For all things were tame and gentle to man, both beasts and birds, and friendly feelings were kindled everywhere.”
— Empedocles, fr. 130 DK (trans. J. Burnet) [11]
Empedocles recalls an age of Love when humans did not kill, and in return every creature was friendly toward them.
The same reciprocal harmony the Kural promises: renounce killing, and all creatures become your friends.

Real Life Example

Francis of Assisi is proclaimed patron of those who promote ecology
29 November 1979 · Vatican City
In the apostolic letter Inter Sanctos, Pope John Paul II declared Saint Francis of Assisi the heavenly patron of those who promote ecology. The letter praised Francis for revering nature as a gift of God and pointed to his Canticle of the Creatures. Francis, who died in 1226, had long been remembered for his gentleness toward animals and all living things.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nearly eight centuries after his death, Francis's reputation for kindness to every creature made him a figure honoured around the world.
The kural says all living beings will revere the one who harms none, and Francis, remembered for his gentleness to creatures, became a universally honoured symbol of that ideal.
