Kural 224

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

இன்னாது இரக்கப் படுதல் இரந்தவர் இன்முகங் காணும் அளவு.
Innaadhu irakkap padudhal irandhavar Inmugam kaanum alavu.

Meaning

Being asked for help is painful for a giving person, but only until they see the face of the one who asked light up with joy.

Elementary

When a friend is sad and asks for help, you feel a little sad too. But when you help them and they smile, your sadness melts away. This kural says the giver’s pain lasts only until they see that smile.

University

The verb இரக்கப்படுதல், ‘to be begged of’, frames the scene from the giver’s side: Parimelazhagar explains that the sight of a suppliant’s distress is itself painful to a compassionate person. That pain ends at a precise moment — அளவு, ‘up to the point’ — when the giver sees the இன்முகம், the pleasant face, of the one who has been helped. The couplet therefore locates the reward of giving in shared emotion rather than in merit, reputation or return. It also implies a standard for how to give: the gift must be large and gracious enough to change the receiver’s face, which links it with teachings such as Maimonides’ rule that charity must be given with a pleasant countenance.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But charity performed with the expectation of some return, or with a desire for fruitive results, or in a grudging mood, is said to be charity in the mode of passion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.21 (Prabhupada translation) [3]
The Gita classes grudging, reluctant giving as a lower (rajasic) kind of charity.
By implication the right way to give is gladly; the Kural pictures the giver’s joy when the receiver brightens.
Christianity
“Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks Christians to give willingly and cheerfully rather than reluctantly.
Shares the Kural’s emotional picture of giving: sorrow gives way to gladness in the act of giving.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not invalidate your charities with reminders [of it] or injury as does one who spends his wealth [only] to be seen by the people.”
— Qur'an 2:264 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says charity is cancelled if the giver hurts the receiver by reminding them of it or humiliating them.
Both texts care about the receiver’s feelings; a gift is complete only when it leaves the receiver glad, not wounded.
Judaism
“Whenever a person gives charity to a poor person with an unpleasant countenance and with his face buried in the earth, he loses and destroys his merit even if he gives him 1000 gold pieces.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Matanot Aniyim 10:4 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides rules that charity given with a sour face loses its merit; it must be given with a pleasant face and with joy, sympathising with the poor person.
The Kural speaks of the receiver’s இன்முகம் (pleasant face); Maimonides insists on the giver’s. Both make faces and feelings central to true giving.
Buddhism
“Before giving, glad; while giving, the mind is bright & clear; having given, one is gratified: This is the consummation of the sacrifice.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 6.37, Dana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes the ideal donor’s state of mind as joyful before, during and after the gift.
Like the Kural, it puts joy at the end of a true act of giving.
Jainism
“To those men the gates of heaven shall never be closed, who with tender hearts and with a mind in accordance with their alms, greatly rejoicing, give even in poverty according to their ability, even as they did in the day of prosperity.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār praises givers who give gladly, with tender hearts, and keep giving even when they have become poor.
Valluvar describes the generous person's discomfort at being asked, which ends in joy once the asker is satisfied. The Nālaṭiyār likewise places the giver's joy and tender-heartedness at the centre of a true gift.
Sikhism
“The Great Giver keeps on giving, while those who receive grow weary of receiving.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Japji Sahib, Pauri 3, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the third pauri of Japji Guru Nanak contrasts the tireless generosity of God with the limited capacity of those who receive.
The Kural pictures a generous person whose discomfort at being asked ends in the asker's joy; Gurbani's image of the Giver who never tires of giving is the nearest Sikh picture of giving as glad and unforced.
Greek Mythology
In Odyssey Book 17, the suitor Antinous refuses the disguised Odysseus, who is begging for food, insults him, and throws a footstool at him, while others at the feast give him scraps. Even the other suitors warn that the gods sometimes walk among men disguised as strangers.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 17 [10]
Antinous is the anti-model of a giver: he meets a beggar’s plea with contempt and violence.
A contrast to the Kural: instead of sharing the suppliant’s pain and ending it by giving, Antinous adds to it — and later dies first when Odysseus takes revenge.
Philosophy
“Above all we should give willingly, quickly, and without any hesitation; a benefit commands no gratitude if it has hung for a long time in the hands of the giver, if he seems unwilling to part with it, and gives it as though he were being robbed of it.”
— Seneca, On Benefits II.1 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
Seneca argues that the manner of giving matters: a gift given reluctantly loses its value.
Supports the Kural’s picture of a giver who hurries to end the asker’s pain and delights in the result.

Real Life Example

Chris Greicius's wish and the birth of Make-A-Wish
1980 · Phoenix, Arizona, United States
In 1980 Chris Greicius, a seven-year-old boy with leukemia in the Phoenix area, wished to become a police officer. Arizona Department of Public Safety officers and family friends gave him a helicopter ride, a tailored uniform and an honorary swearing-in, and he died days later. The people who granted his wish went on to found the Make-A-Wish organisation, which has since granted hundreds of thousands of wishes to seriously ill children.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A small, spontaneous act of kindness grew into a worldwide charity built entirely around the joy of the child who receives.
The kural locates the giver's reward in the moment the asker's face lights up, and the whole Make-A-Wish idea rests on bringing that joy to a child's face.
