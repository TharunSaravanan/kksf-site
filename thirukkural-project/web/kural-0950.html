Kural 950

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

உற்றவன் தீர்ப்பான் மருந்துழைச் செல்வானென்று அப்பால் நாற்கூற்றே மருந்து.
Utravan theerppaan marundhuzhaich chelvaanendru appaal naarkootre marundhu.

Meaning

Medicine has four parts: the patient, the doctor who cures, the medicine, and the helper who stays close to give it.

Elementary

Getting better is like a team game. The sick person must follow the plan, the doctor must know what to do, the medicine must be good, and someone like a parent or nurse must help every day. If one player is missing, the team struggles.

University

The chapter ends by defining medicine as naarkootru (நாற்கூற்று, 'fourfold'): utravan (உற்றவன், the one afflicted), theerppaan (தீர்ப்பான், the one who cures), marundhu (மருந்து, the remedy), and marundhuzhaich chelvaan (மருந்துழைச் செல்வான், literally 'he who goes near the medicine'). This last term divides the commentators: Varadarasanar reads it as the one who gives the medicine from close by, Kalaignar as the attendant at the bedside, Pappaiah and Kesikan as the preparer or compounder, and Pope's explanation as the compounder. The words appaal (அப்பால், 'that domain') and the closing marundhu frame the whole. Pope's gloss, following Parimelazhagar, adds that each of the four has four qualities in turn, sixteen in all. The fourfold scheme closely matches the Charaka Samhita's 'four feet' of therapy (physician, drug, attendant, patient), which is strong evidence that Valluvar knew the classical medical tradition. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Charaka names the four feet (pada-chatushtaya) of successful treatment as the physician, the drugs, the attendant and the patient, each of which must have its proper qualities.
— Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 9.3 (Khuddakachatushpada Adhyaya), paraphrase [3]
Classical Ayurveda rests all therapy on these four pillars.
This is the Kural's fourfold medicine, item for item, including the qualities of each that Parimelazhagar's tradition mentions.
Christianity
“With such doth he heal [men,] and taketh away their pains. Of such doth the apothecary make a confection; and of his works there is no end; and from him is peace over all the earth,”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 38:7-8 (KJV Apocrypha) [4]
Ben Sira describes healing as the joint work of God-given medicines, the physician and the apothecary who compounds them.
The physician, the medicine and the compounder here are three of the Kural's four parts of medicine.
Islam
The Prophet commanded his followers to feed the hungry, visit the sick and set free the captive.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5649, paraphrase [5]
Visiting and caring for the sick is made a duty for every Muslim, not just for physicians.
It honours the Kural's fourth part, the one who stays near the patient.
Judaism
“A Torah Sage is not permitted to live in a community which does not have the following: a doctor, a bloodletter, a bathhouse, a latrine, an available source of water such as a river or a spring, a synagogue, a teacher of children, a scribe, a charity supervisor, a rabbinical court empowered to impose corporal punishment and jail sentences.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:23 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides counts a doctor and a medical practitioner among the minimum institutions of a community fit to live in.
Like the Kural, it treats medicine as a structured set of roles that a well-ordered society must provide.
Buddhism
“A patient with five qualities is easy to care for. What five? They do what is suitable. They know moderation in what is suitable. They take their medicine. Because their carer wants what's best for them, they accurately report their symptoms by saying when they're getting worse, getting better, or staying the same. And they can endure physical pain...”
— Pathama-upatthaka Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.123 (tr. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha lists the duties of a good patient toward medicine and carer, and in the next sutta the duties of a good carer.
The sutta divides healing into patient, medicine and carer, three of the Kural's four parts, each with its proper qualities.
Jainism
“Then the best physicians came to my help, who cure by their medical art and by spells, who were versed in their science, and well knew spells and roots. They tried to cure me according to the fourfold science which they had been taught; but they could not rid me of my pains: hence I say that I am without protection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.22-30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The young Anathi recalls that physicians using the fourfold medical science, his father, mother, siblings and devoted wife at his bedside all failed to relieve him, which led him to renounce the world.
The Jain text names the same fourfold medicine as the Kural, then turns it into a lesson that no physician, remedy or attendant can protect the soul from karma.
Sikhism
During the fighting at Anandpur, Bhai Kanhaiya, a Sikh of Guru Gobind Singh, carried water to every wounded man, Sikh or enemy. When the Sikh soldiers complained, he told the Guru that he saw no friends or foes, only people, and the Guru is said to have given him medical aid to dress their wounds as well.
— Account of Bhai Kanhaiya at Anandpur (c. 1704), as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Bhai Kanhaiya' (paraphrase) [9]
Bhai Kanhaiya is remembered as the founder of the Sevapanthi order and as the model of seva to the sick and wounded without distinction.
The Kural counts the one who stays at the bedside to give the remedy as one of medicine's four parts; Bhai Kanhaiya is the Sikh model of that attendant, with the Guru supplying the remedy.
Greek Mythology
When Machaon is wounded, Idomeneus urges Nestor to carry him off, since a physician is worth many other men; later Patroclus sits by the wounded Eurypylus, cuts out the arrow, washes the wound and spreads on it the healing root Achilles learned from Chiron.
— Homer, Iliad 11.514-515 and 11.828-848 (tr. Samuel Butler), paraphrase [10]
Homer shows both the valued physician and the companion who stays to tend the wounded with the right remedy.
Physician, patient, remedy and attending friend, the Kural's four parts, all appear in these scenes.
Philosophy
“Life is short, and Art long; the crisis fleeting; experience perilous, and decision difficult. The physician must not only be prepared to do what is right himself, but also to make the patient, the attendants, and externals cooperate.”
— Hippocrates, Aphorisms I.1 (tr. Francis Adams) [11]
The first Hippocratic aphorism says that healing succeeds only when physician, patient, attendants and outward circumstances work together.
This is the Kural's fourfold medicine almost exactly: patient, physician, attendants and the external means of cure.

Real Life Example

Albert Alexander becomes the first patient treated with penicillin
12 February 1941 · Radcliffe Infirmary, Oxford, England
Albert Alexander, a police constable with a severe infection, became the first patient to receive penicillin made by Howard Florey's team at Oxford, and within a day his fever fell and the infection began to heal. The drug was so scarce that the team recovered it from his urine to use again, but after five days the supply ran out; he relapsed and died on 15 March 1941. Norman Heatley, who had turned the laboratory into a small penicillin factory, later travelled with Florey to the United States to help start large-scale production.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The case showed that a cure needs not only a sick person, a skilled doctor and a good remedy, but also someone able to prepare enough of that remedy.
The kural names four parts of medicine, patient, physician, remedy and the one who prepares it, and Alexander's case showed that when the fourth part could not keep up, even a true cure failed.
