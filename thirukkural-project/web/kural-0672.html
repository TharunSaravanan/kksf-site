Kural 672

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

தூங்குக தூங்கிச் செயற்பால தூங்கற்க தூங்காது செய்யும் வினை.
Thoonguga thoongich cheyarpaala thoongarka Thoongaadhu seyyum vinai.

Meaning

Take your time with tasks that should be done slowly. But never delay tasks that must be done right away.

Elementary

Planting a tree is something you can take your time over, choosing the best spot. But if the bathtub is overflowing, you turn off the tap right now. A wise person knows which jobs can wait and which cannot.

University

The couplet is a famous piece of verbal play: the verb thoongu appears five times, in the sense of 'to delay, to be slow, to tarry' (literally to droop or hang, and by extension to sleep). Parimelazhagar explains that some undertakings (such as those whose moment has not yet come) are properly done by waiting, while others (such as those where the opportunity or the enemy will not wait) must be done without any delay; the skill lies in telling the two apart. The kural therefore qualifies 671: the rule is not simply 'hurry' but correct timing, echoing the earlier chapter on knowing the right time (kaalam aridhal, ch. 49). Modern commentators Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah read it the same way, and there are no significant textual variants. It pairs naturally with the Latin-Greek motto festina lente and with Francis Bacon's essay on delays.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Gautama orders his son Chirakari (the slow-acting one) to kill his mother in anger; Chirakari, by nature deliberate, weighs the command at length, and by the time he has finished Gautama has repented and rejoices that his son was slow.
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, the story of Chirakari (Ganguli trans., Santi Parva section 266) [3]
Bhishma tells this story to show that in grave, irreversible matters, slowness can be the wise course.
Chirakari's story illustrates the first half of the Kural, that some deeds are rightly slept over, while Bhishma's wider teaching insists that urgent duties must not be delayed.
Christianity
“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath.”
— James 1:19 (KJV) [4]
James tells believers that some responses should be quick and others slow.
Like the Kural, James does not praise speed or slowness in themselves; he assigns each to the right kind of act.
Islam
“There is hesitation in everything except in the actions of the next world.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4810 (graded sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet teaches that careful, unhurried deliberateness (tu'ada) is good in most matters, but deeds that serve the hereafter should be done promptly.
The hadith draws exactly the Kural's line between tasks that may wait and tasks that must not.
Judaism
“To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven.”
— Ecclesiastes 3:1 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet opens his famous poem by saying that each activity has its own proper time.
The Kural's two-speed rule depends on this same idea: each task has its right time, fast or slow.
Buddhism
The Buddha explains that even when he has something true and beneficial to say, he waits for the proper time to say it, choosing the moment with care.
— Majjhima Nikaya 58, Abhayarajakumara Sutta [6]
In the Abhaya Sutta the Buddha describes his speech as governed by a sense of the right time as well as by truth and benefit.
Like the Kural, the Buddha treats timing as part of doing an act well: some things are best held back until their moment.
Jainism
“At the right time a monk should sally forth, and he should return at the right time; avoiding to do anything out of time, he should do what is appropriate for each period of the day.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Jain monastic discipline assigns each activity its proper time and forbids doing things out of season.
The kural's rule that some tasks may be done slowly while others must not be delayed is a version of this principle that every act has its due time.
Sikhism
“In all ten directions, the branches are green and alive. That which ripens slowly, is sweet.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1109, Raag Tukhaari, Baarah Maahaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In his Baarah Maahaa (song of the twelve months) Guru Nanak observes that fruit which ripens in its own time is the sweet one, an image of patient, unhurried growth toward the Beloved.
The Kural's first half says some tasks are properly done slowly; Guru Nanak's image of slow ripening honours exactly that kind of patience, while Kabir's call to act at once (see Kural 671) matches the second half.
Greek Mythology
The emperor Augustus often repeated the Greek saying speude bradeos, hasten slowly, and held that whatever is done well enough is done quickly enough.
— Suetonius, Life of Augustus 25 [9]
Suetonius reports that Augustus disliked rashness in a commander and liked this paradoxical motto.
Hasten slowly captures the Kural's balance: urgency where needed, patience where haste would spoil the work.
Philosophy
Francis Bacon advises that the beginnings of great actions be entrusted to Argus with his hundred eyes, and their ends to Briareus with his hundred hands: watch patiently first, then act swiftly.
— Francis Bacon, Essays, 'Of Delays' (1625) [10]
In his essay on delays Bacon argues that there is a ripe moment for every action, to be waited for patiently and then seized at once.
Bacon's two giants stand for the two halves of the Kural: slowness where waiting helps, speed where it does not.

Real Life Example

Danes ferry almost all of Denmark's Jews to Sweden within weeks
October 1943 · Denmark and Sweden
In late September 1943 the German diplomat Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz leaked word of a planned roundup of Denmark's Jews, and Danish contacts quickly warned the Jewish community. When the roundup began on the night of 1 October, most Jews had already gone into hiding, and resistance workers, ordinary citizens and fishermen moved them to the coast and ferried them across the narrow strait to neutral Sweden. Within a few weeks about 7,200 Jews and some 700 non-Jewish relatives reached safety, while fewer than 500 were caught and deported.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Because the warning was acted on at once rather than debated, almost the whole Jewish population of Denmark survived the Holocaust.
The kural says never to delay tasks that must be done at once, and the Danish rescue succeeded because people acted within hours and days of the warning.
