Kural 768

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

அடல்தகையும் ஆற்றலும் இல்லெனினும் தானை படைத்தகையால் பாடு பெறும்.
Adalthagaiyum aatralum illeninum thaanai padaiththagaiyaal paadu perum.

Meaning

Even if an army cannot attack well or hold its ground, it can still win fame by looking strong and well equipped.

Elementary

A marching band in smart uniforms, all in step, can make a whole crowd stop and stare, even before it plays a note. Valluvar says an army that is neatly lined up and well equipped can earn respect just by how it looks and moves.

University

Aṭaltakai (அடல்தகை) is the capacity to attack and destroy, āṟṟal (ஆற்றல்) the capacity to withstand; paṭaittakai (படைத்தகை) is the excellence of the army’s equipment and array, and pāṭu (பாடு) means ‘renown, dignity’. Parimelazhagar understands the couplet as saying that order and splendour alone can deter or impress an enemy, and Solomon Pappaiah stresses the disciplined beauty of its formation. Kalaignar reads it slightly differently, as saying the array should be even more excellent than attack and defence. The two sources agree (சந்தி: அடல்தகையு மாற்றலு மில்லெனினுந் தானை).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya devotes Book X of the Arthashastra to the arraying of troops (vyuha), naming formations such as the staff, the snake, the circle and the detached array, and how each is to be ordered for battle.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book X, Chapters 5–6 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
The treatise treats the correct array of an army as a science in itself.
It shares the Kural’s conviction that an army’s formation and order are a source of strength and prestige.
Christianity
“Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace.”
— Luke 14:32 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus’ parable, the weaker king, seeing the size of the advancing army, sues for peace before any battle.
An army can win its ends by its mere appearance and strength, as the Kural says.
Islam
Before the conquest of Mecca, the Muslim army lit many campfires and paraded past Abu Sufyan, whose sight of its numbers and order contributed to the city’s surrender largely without fighting.
— Sira accounts of the conquest of Mecca (Ibn Ishaq) [5]
The display of a well-ordered force secured the result that battle might otherwise have decided.
It illustrates the Kural’s claim that an army can win distinction by its array alone.
Judaism
“And he made in Jerusalem engines, invented by skilful men, to be on the towers and upon the corners, wherewith to shoot arrows and great stones. And his name spread far abroad; for he was marvellously helped, till he was strong.”
— 2 Chronicles 26:15 (JPS 1917, Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Uzziah’s equipment and engines spread his fame far beyond Judah.
Renown earned through the excellence of an army’s equipment is the Kural’s paṭaittakaiyāl pāṭu.
Buddhism
“A tamed elephant is led into a crowd, and the king mounts a tamed elephant. Best among men is the subdued one who endures abuse.”
— Dhammapada 321 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The trained elephant is the one fit to be shown before crowds and to carry the king.
Discipline that earns public honour parallels the Kural’s army renowned for its order.
Jainism
“As a valiant hero bestriding a trained horse, with heralds singing out to his right and left, (has no equal), neither has a very learned monk.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The verse borrows the splendour of a mounted hero with heralds on either side as a picture of a learned monk's dignity.
The Kural says an army can win renown by the splendour of its array alone, and the Jain simile likewise treats outward magnificence as something that commands respect.
Sikhism
Guru Gobind Singh held the first Hola Mahalla at Anandpur in February 1701, turning the spring festival of Holi into an organised procession and display of martial skill, with simulated battles near Holgarh Fort.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, 'Hola Mahalla' (paraphrase; text as reprinted by Asia Samachar, 2015) [9]
Hola Mahalla was a public show of the Khalsa's array and training, and Nihangs still lead the procession at Anandpur today.
The Kural says an army can win renown by the splendour of its array alone; Hola Mahalla was exactly such a display of ordered strength.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus describes Xerxes reviewing his vast army at Doriscus, riding past nation after nation in their distinctive arms and dress, an array famed throughout Greece.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.59–100 [10]
The splendour of the Persian host was itself a weapon, meant to awe the Greeks into submission.
It shows the renown an army can gain by its array alone, though the campaign later failed.
Philosophy
“Hence to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy’s resistance without fighting.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 3.2 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu prizes winning through position and strength over actual combat.
The Kural’s army that wins distinction by its array without fighting is a case of this ideal.

Real Life Example

Zheng He's treasure fleets sail to the Indian Ocean
1405-1433 · From Nanjing, China, to South-East Asia, India, the Persian Gulf and East Africa
Between 1405 and 1433 the Ming court sent seven great expeditions under the eunuch admiral Zheng He, with fleets far larger than any then sailing, to South-East Asia, India's Malabar Coast, Hormuz and the coast of East Africa. The fleets carried gifts and were armed, but their chief purpose was diplomatic. On his return in 1415 Zheng He brought envoys of many states of South and South-East Asia to pay homage to the emperor.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sheer scale and splendour of the fleets carried Ming prestige across the Indian Ocean and drew many rulers into tributary relations, largely without war.
The kural says that even without using its strength to attack or defend, a force can win renown by the splendour of its array, and Zheng He's fleets won China honour chiefly by being seen.
