Kural 395

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

உடையார்முன் இல்லார்போல் ஏக்கற்றுங் கற்றார் கடையரே கல்லா தவர்.
Udaiyaarmun illaarpol ekkatrum katraar Kadaiyare kallaa thavar.

Meaning

People who learn by standing humbly before their teachers, like the poor before the rich, are truly great. Those who do not learn are the lowest of all.

Elementary

A hungry child waits eagerly for food. A good student waits just as eagerly to learn from a teacher, and is not too proud to ask. The kural says being too proud to learn is what really makes you low.

University

The kural draws on a social image any reader would recognize: the needy person standing anxiously (ஏக்கற்று) before the wealthy. It applies that image to the student before the teacher. What looks like humiliation is really the road to greatness, since those who learn this way are the truly elevated (கற்றார்), while those who refuse to learn are கடையர், the lowest. Solomon Pappaiah reads the second line as condemning those who are ashamed to stand and learn, which makes pride the real barrier to education. The inversion of rank is typical of Valluvar: outward humility produces inner eminence. Many traditions make the same point, from the Gita's call to approach a teacher with submission (echoed in the Sanskrit maxim vidyā dadāti vinayam, 'learning gives humility') to the Mishnah's warning that the shy or ashamed cannot learn.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized souls can impart knowledge unto you because they have seen the truth.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 (Bhaktivedanta, As It Is) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that real knowledge is gained by approaching a teacher with humility (pranipata), sincere questioning and service.
A near-exact match to the Kural's picture of the student standing humbly before the teacher as a precondition for learning.
Christianity
“Whoso loveth instruction loveth knowledge: but he that hateth reproof is brutish.”
— Proverbs 12:1 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that those who welcome instruction grow in knowledge, while those too proud to be corrected sink to the level of beasts.
Like the Kural, it ranks the teachable above the proud and unteachable, whom it calls 'brutish', much as the Kural calls them the lowest.
Islam
“Moses said to him, “May I follow you on [the condition] that you teach me from what you have been taught of sound judgement?”
— Qur'an 18:66 (Sahih International) [5]
Even the prophet Moses humbly asks the mysterious servant of God (traditionally al-Khidr) to be allowed to follow him as a student.
A strong match for the Kural's first line: even the greatest must stand as seekers before those who know more.
Judaism
“Ben Zoma said: Who is wise? He who learns from every man.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (with 2:5), Kulp translation, Sefaria [6]
True wisdom, Ben Zoma teaches, belongs to the person humble enough to learn from anyone at all. Elsewhere in Avot (2:5), Hillel warns that 'a timid person [cannot] learn', meaning one ashamed to ask questions.
Both teachings match the Kural: wisdom belongs to those who humbly seek it, and pride or shame keeps people ignorant.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure.”
— Dhammapada 76 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha urges disciples to follow a wise teacher eagerly, as someone would follow a person who knew where treasure was buried.
The treasure-seeker image mirrors the Kural's 'poor before the rich'. The learner approaches the teacher as the needy approach wealth.
Jainism
“He who always acknowledges his allegiance to his teacher, who has religious zeal and ardour for study, who is kind in words and actions, deserves to be instructed.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana makes humble devotion to one's teacher, and staying in the teacher's lineage (kula), the condition for receiving learning. Its first lecture is devoted entirely to a disciple's humility (vinaya).
Valluvar says those who stand humbly before their teachers, eager as the poor before the rich, are the superior ones. The Jain text likewise names lowly allegiance to the teacher as what makes a student worthy of instruction.
Sikhism
“Bowing down, again and again, I fall at the Feet of my Guru; through Him, I have seen the Lord, the Divine Self, within.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 353, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes humbly bowing again and again before the Guru, through whose teaching he came to see the Lord within himself.
The Kural says those who stand humbly before their teachers, like the needy before the rich, are truly superior; Guru Nanak shows the same humility before the teacher as the way to the highest understanding.
Greek Mythology
Young Telemachus, sent to seek news from old King Nestor, confesses he feels ashamed and inexperienced about questioning an elder. Athena, disguised as Mentor, tells him to take courage and ask.
— Homer, Odyssey 3.14–28 [10]
The goddess of wisdom herself pushes the young man past his shyness so that he can learn from an experienced elder.
This fits the Kural closely. The learner must humbly approach the one who knows, and shame about asking is the obstacle to overcome.
Philosophy
Socrates explains that the Delphic oracle called him wisest only because, unlike others, he knew that he did not know. His wisdom began with admitting his own poverty of knowledge.
— Plato, Apology 21a–23b [11]
For Socrates, recognizing one's ignorance, the stance of the 'poor' before knowledge, is the starting point of all real learning.
Like the Kural, it makes humble awareness of one's own need the basis of true superiority in learning.

Real Life Example

Frederick Douglass trades bread for reading lessons
c. 1826-1833 · Baltimore, Maryland, United States
As an enslaved boy in Baltimore, Frederick Douglass was taught the alphabet by Sophia Auld until her husband forbade the lessons. In his 1845 Narrative, Douglass recorded that he then made friends with the white boys he met in the street and turned as many as he could into teachers, giving bread to the hungry ones in return for lessons. He also taught himself to write by copying letters from ship timbers and a schoolboy's copy-books.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Douglass sought learning from anyone who could give it, however young or humble, and his literacy became the foundation of his later life as a writer and abolitionist leader.
The kural praises those who eagerly seek learning, like the needy before the rich, and Douglass begged lessons from street children with whatever he had to offer.
