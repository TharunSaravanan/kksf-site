Kural 912

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

பயன்தூக்கிப் பண்புரைக்கும் பண்பின் மகளிர் நயன்தூக்கி நள்ளா விடல்.
Payandhookkip panburaikkum panbin magalir nayandhookki nallaa vidal.

Meaning

Some women of bad character first weigh what they can get from a man, then speak sweetly to him. Think about what they are really like, and stay away.

Elementary

Some people check how much pocket money you have before deciding how friendly to be, and then talk about how loyal they are. Valluvar says: notice that pattern, think about it, and do not get close to someone who measures you by your wallet.

University

The couplet rests on a play of the verb thookku (தூக்கு, ‘to weigh, to measure’): the women payan-thookki, ‘weigh the profit’, so the wise man must nayan-thookki, ‘weigh their character’, and then nallaa vidal (நள்ளா விடல்), ‘leave without joining’. There is also deliberate irony in panburaikkum panbin magalir, ‘women of no virtue (panbu) who speak of virtue’: the speech is a performance tuned to the size of the purse. Puliyur Kesigan reads nayan as their behaviour, while Mu. Varadarasanar takes it as the ‘pleasure’ they offer, which the wise man evaluates and declines. Parimelazhagar treats the couplet as a practical rule of discernment rather than a moral sermon, in keeping with the Porutpal’s concern with prudent conduct. No textual variants appear between ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Kathasaritsagara’s tale of Lohajangha, the courtesan Rupinika’s mother Makaradamshtra drives away the penniless young man her daughter genuinely loves, insisting that affection must follow money; the story then shows how calculation and greed undo themselves.
— Somadeva, Kathasaritsagara, Book II (tale of Lohajangha), tr. C. H. Tawney [3]
Somadeva’s story collection uses this tale to satirize affection that is weighed against wealth.
Makaradamshtra is a portrait of payan-thookki, weighing the gain before the man.
Christianity
“And when he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land; and he began to be in want.”
— Luke 15:14-16 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the Prodigal Son, the companions of the young man’s wasteful days disappear once his money is gone; the story adds that ‘no man gave unto him’.
The Kural’s mercenary friends likewise last only as long as the purse does.
Islam
“And of the people is he whose speech pleases you in worldly life, and he calls Allah to witness as to what is in his heart, yet he is the fiercest of opponents.”
— Qur’an 2:204 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns of people who speak pleasantly and even swear to their sincerity while being enemies at heart.
Kural 912’s women ‘speak of virtue’ while weighing gain, the same gap between pleasing words and real intent.
Judaism
“With her much fair speech she causeth him to yield, With the blandishment of her lips she enticeth him away.”
— Proverbs 7:21 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs describes the enticing woman whose skilled talk leads a simple young man astray.
Both texts warn against flattering speech that is a means to an end.
Buddhism
“One is a bottle friend; one says, ‘friend, friend’ only to one’s face; one is a friend and an associate only when it is advantageous.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha describes people whose friendship lasts only while there is something in it for them.
Exactly the friendship Kural 912 says to weigh and walk away from.
Jainism
“A young woman, putting on fine ornaments and clothes, will say to a Sramana: 'I shall give up (my former way of life) and practise the rough (viz. control). Reverend sir, teach me the Law!'”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The text describes a woman who talks piously of religion in order to win over a monk, and it warns him not to trust that show of virtue.
This fits Kural 912's women who first weigh their profit and then 'speak of virtue' (panburaikkum), so that the wise man must weigh their character and stay away.
Sikhism
“Bronze is bright and shiny, but when it is rubbed, its blackness appears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
This shabad opens with the image of polished bronze that leaves black marks when rubbed: a fine surface hiding a dark interior. According to the Janamsakhi tradition Guru Nanak sang it to Sajjan, a man who kept a pious-looking rest-house for travellers and robbed them, and who repented on hearing it.
The Kural tells the wise to weigh the character behind sweet talk of virtue, and Guru Nanak’s bronze shows exactly what such testing reveals.
Greek Mythology
Eriphyle, wife of the seer Amphiaraus, was bribed by Polynices with the golden necklace of Harmonia and in return persuaded her husband to join the doomed expedition against Thebes, where he died.
— Apollodorus, Library 3.6.2 [10]
The myth became the Greek byword for affection and loyalty sold for a jewel.
Eriphyle weighs gold against the man, as the women of Kural 912 weigh profit before speaking.
Philosophy
“...there is a dishonour in being overcome by the love of money, or of wealth, or of political power... For none of these things are of a permanent or lasting nature; not to mention that no generous friendship ever sprang from them.”
— Plato, Symposium 184a-b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Pausanias’ speech holds that attachments bought by money are shameful and cannot produce real friendship.
Valluvar likewise tells the wise to weigh such attachments and refuse them.

Real Life Example

Gandhi vows to avoid wine, women and meat before sailing to England
1888 · Gujarat, India
When the young Mohandas Gandhi planned to study law in London, his mother feared he would be led astray, since she had heard that young men in England took to meat and liquor. A family adviser administered an oath, and Gandhi vowed not to touch wine, women or meat, after which his mother gave her permission. He sailed for England on 4 September 1888, and he kept the vow while he was abroad.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Before entering a world of new temptations, he weighed the dangers in advance and bound himself to keep away from them.
The kural advises weighing the conduct of those who seek gain and keeping away from them, and Gandhi's vow was a decision to stay clear of such attachments before he ever met them.
