Kural 507

Selection and Confidence · Therindhu Thelithal · தெரிந்து தெளிதல்

காதன்மை கந்தா அறிவறியார்த் தேறுதல் பேதைமை எல்லாந் தரும்.
Kaadhanmai kandhaa arivariyaarth thaerudhal Paedhaimai ellaan tharum.

Meaning

Trusting people who do not have the knowledge they need, just because you like them, will lead to every kind of foolish mistake.

Elementary

Your best friend is awesome, but if they’ve never baked anything, you wouldn’t make them in charge of the cake for the whole school fair just because you like them. Liking someone is not the same as them being good at the job.

University

காதன்மை கந்தா means ‘with affection as the post’: கந்து is the post or pillar to which something is tied, so affection becomes the only support of the choice. அறிவு அறியார், ‘those who do not know what should be known’, names the risk, and the result is பேதைமை எல்லாம், every kind of folly. Pappaiah reads it as the folly produced by favouritism in appointments, and Kalaignar adds that such choices bear only useless results. Varadarasan’s printed gloss says the chooser’s ‘ignorance will perish’, which appears to be a typographical slip given his other readings and Pope’s ‘the height of folly’. The text reads எல்லாந் தரும் (sandhi of எல்லாம் + தரும்) in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The blind king Dhritarashtra, out of love for his son Duryodhana, repeatedly sets aside the counsel of Vidura and Bhishma and lets Duryodhana and his companions direct the kingdom, leading to the ruinous Kurukshetra war.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura’s counsels and Dhritarashtra’s responses) [3]
The Mahabharata presents Dhritarashtra’s affection as the root of the Kuru downfall.
Trusting an unwise person out of love brings ‘every folly’, as the Kural warns.
Christianity
“My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons.”
— James 2:1 (KJV) [4]
James forbids partiality, favouring some people for reasons other than merit.
Favouritism is the trap the Kural names.
Islam
“When honesty is lost, then wait for the Hour (Doomsday).” The Bedouin said, “How will that be lost?” The Prophet (ﷺ) said, “When the power or authority comes in the hands of unfit persons, then wait for the Hour (Doomsday.)”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 59 (Sunnah.com translation) [5]
Giving authority to people unfit for it is described as a betrayal of trust and a sign of the end times.
Entrusting affairs to the unqualified is the ‘folly’ the Kural warns of.
Judaism
“But he forsook the counsel of the old men which they had given him, and took counsel with the young men that were grown up with him, that stood before him.”
— 1 Kings 12:8 (JPS 1917) [6]
Rehoboam rejects experienced advisers in favour of the friends he grew up with, and their harsh advice splits the kingdom of Israel.
Trusting familiar friends over the wise is exactly the folly the Kural describes.
Buddhism
“Should a seeker not find a companion who is better or equal, let him resolutely pursue a solitary course; there is no fellowship with the fool.”
— Dhammapada 61 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha advises going alone rather than attaching oneself to a fool.
The Kural similarly warns against binding oneself to the unwise, however dear.
Jainism
“But he who puts bad bullocks before his car, will be tired out with beating them; he will feel vexation, and his goad will be broken (at last).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 27.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The teacher Garga compares unfit pupils to bad bullocks yoked to a cart: whoever harnesses them reaps only exhaustion and frustration.
The kural says trusting the unfit out of fondness brings every folly; Garga's image shows the same result of yoking unfit helpers to one's work.
Sikhism
“Friendship with a fool never works out right.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in Asa Ki Vaar, Guru Angad says that a bond formed with someone who lacks understanding cannot succeed, however it began.
The Kural warns that trusting people who lack the needed knowledge, merely out of fondness, brings every kind of folly; Guru Angad says affection for a fool never works out.
Greek Mythology
Having sworn by the Styx to grant his son Phaethon any wish, the loving sun-god Helios lets the untrained boy drive the chariot of the Sun; Phaethon loses control, scorches the earth, and is struck down by Zeus.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.1-328 [10]
A father’s love leads him to hand a great task to someone without the skill for it.
Affection as the only ‘post’ of trust ends in disaster, just as the Kural predicts.
Philosophy
Machiavelli writes that the choice of ministers is of no small importance to a prince, and that the first opinion people form of a ruler’s intelligence comes from the men he keeps around him.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 22 [11]
A ruler is judged by the quality of those he appoints.
Choosing incompetent favourites exposes the chooser’s own folly, which is the Kural’s point.

Real Life Example

Charles IV makes the guardsman Manuel Godoy his chief minister
1792 · Madrid, Spain
Manuel Godoy, a young officer of the royal bodyguard and a protege of Queen Maria Luisa, was raised by King Charles IV to the highest offices and made chief minister in 1792 at about the age of 25. His rule was marked by costly wars, financial strain and growing unpopularity. In March 1808 the Revolt of Aranjuez forced his dismissal and the abdication of Charles IV, which opened the way for Napoleon to take control of Spain.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A government handed to a royal favourite without proven ability ended in the fall of the king himself.
The kural warns that trusting unqualified people merely because one is fond of them brings every kind of folly, and Godoy's career shows the result.
