Kural 1156

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

பிரிவுரைக்கும் வன்கண்ணர் ஆயின் அரிதவர் நல்குவர் என்னும் நசை.
Pirivuraikkum vankannar aayin aridhavar nalkuvar ennum nasai.

Meaning

If he is cruel enough to tell me to my face that he is leaving, it is hard to keep hoping he will come back and be kind to me.

Elementary

Imagine a friend who can say ‘I’m going, bye!’ without looking sad at all. You might think, ‘If leaving me is that easy for them, will they really hurry back?’ The wife in this couplet thinks the same about her husband.

University

Pirivu uraikkum (பிரிவு உரைக்கும்) is ‘who speaks of parting’ and vaṉkaṇṇar (வன்கண்ணர்) ‘the hard-eyed, the cruel’: the very fact that he can announce the separation shows a hardness she did not expect. Nalkuvar (நல்குவர்) is ‘he will show grace or love’ and nacai (நசை) ‘longing hope’, which is arito, ‘hard to hold’ or, as most commentators read it, futile. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses vaṉkaṇ as ‘a heart of stone’ and Kalaignar calls the hope ‘vain’; Solomon Pappaiah adds that the parting is for work. Pope: ‘To cherish longing hope that he should ever gracious be, / Is hard, when he could stand, and of departure speak to me.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Uddhava brings Krishna’s message from Mathura, the gopis, addressing a bee as if it were Krishna’s messenger, complain that he who once loved them has left them and doubt that he will ever come back to them.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47, Bhramara Gita (paraphrase) [3]
In the ‘Bee Song’ the gopis voice hurt and bitterness at a beloved who was able to leave and stay away.
Their doubt is the Kural’s: one who could leave so easily is unlikely to return with tenderness.
Christianity
“But because I have said these things unto you, sorrow hath filled your heart.”
— John 16:6 (KJV) [4]
Jesus notes that his disciples are filled with grief simply because he has told them he is going away.
As in the Kural, it is the spoken announcement of departure that wounds.
Islam
“O my sons, go and find out about Joseph and his brother and despair not of relief from Allah. Indeed, no one despairs of relief from Allah except the disbelieving people.”
— Qur’an 12:87 (Sahih International) [5]
Even after years of separation from Joseph, Jacob refuses to give up hope of reunion.
The verse is a counterpoint: the Kural’s heroine concludes her hope is futile, while Jacob holds that hope must never be abandoned.
Judaism
“Hope deferred maketh the heart sick; But desire fulfilled is a tree of life.”
— Proverbs 13:12 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that hope endlessly postponed wears down the heart, while longing fulfilled gives life.
The Kural’s heroine sees nothing ahead but hope deferred, and her heart is already sick with it.
Buddhism
“From craving springs grief, from craving springs fear. For one who is wholly free from craving there is no grief; whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 216 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha traces grief and fear to craving (tanha), the hungry wanting that will not let go.
The Kural’s nacai, her longing hope, is just such craving, and as the verse says, it brings her grief.
Jainism
“If you have no intention of abandoning pleasure, and still long for undertakings and property, my long talk has been to no purpose. I go, king, farewell.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Citra, having failed to persuade his former brother to renounce, announces his departure plainly and leaves.
A plainly spoken farewell like Citra’s is what the Kural’s heroine dreads; one who can say ‘I go’ so firmly is unlikely to turn back.
Sikhism
“He Himself unites us in His Union; there is no more separation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 37, Sri Raag, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das teaches that the Lord Himself brings the soul back into union, and once He does so the separation is over.
The Kural's heroine thinks the hope that a husband who can coolly announce his departure will return in kindness is hard to keep; Gurbani holds out the opposite hope, that the divine Beloved Himself ends the separation.
Greek Mythology
Phyllis, queen of Thrace, writes to Demophoon, who left promising to return by an agreed time; the time has long passed, and she slowly realises that he does not mean to come back.
— Ovid, Heroides 2 (Phyllis to Demophoon) (paraphrase) [10]
In Ovid’s Heroides, Phyllis’s hope fades as each day without his ship shows how easily he could leave her.
She lives out the Kural’s conclusion that the hope of a departed lover’s kindness is hard to keep.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines hope as an inconstant joy and fear as an inconstant sadness, both born of the image of a future thing whose outcome we doubt, and notes that there is no hope without fear and no fear without hope.
— Baruch Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Definitions of the Affects 12-13 (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, hope and fear are two sides of the same uncertainty.
The Kural’s heroine shows this: her hope that he will be kind on his return is inseparable from her fear that he will not.

Real Life Example

Paul Gauguin leaves his wife and children to paint in Tahiti
1891 · Copenhagen, Denmark, and Tahiti, French Polynesia
Paul Gauguin, married to Mette Sophie Gad since the early 1870s, had five children with her, and in 1884 the family moved to Copenhagen to live with her relatives, while Gauguin returned to Paris the next year. In 1891, after a successful sale of his work, he paid a final visit to his family in Copenhagen and then set sail for Tahiti. He and Mette remained apart for the rest of his life, never divorcing and corresponding until 1897.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gauguin chose his art over family life, came in person to say goodbye, and then left, and the separation that followed was never repaired.
The kural says that a husband hard enough to announce his departure to his wife's face leaves her little reason to hope he will return and be kind, and the Gauguins' lasting separation bears that fear out.
