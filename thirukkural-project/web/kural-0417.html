Kural 417

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

பிழைத்துணர்ந்தும் பேதைமை சொல்லார் இழைத்துணர்ந்து ஈண்டிய கேள்வி யவர்.
Pizhaiththunarndhum pedhaimai sollaar izhaiththunarndhu Eendiya kelvi yavar.

Meaning

People who have studied things closely and gathered much learning by listening will not say foolish things, even when they have understood something wrongly.

Elementary

Everyone makes mistakes sometimes. But a person who has listened carefully to lots of wise people won’t say silly things even when they get confused, because all that good listening keeps them careful.

University

The paired participles பிழைத்துணர்ந்தும் (‘even having understood wrongly’) and இழைத்துணர்ந்து (‘having understood finely, as if threading’) frame the couplet. இழை (thread) suggests minute, careful examination; ஈண்டிய (‘amassed’) describes accumulated hearing. The kural is realistic: the learned may still err in perception, but their trained habit of mind prevents error from becoming foolish speech (பேதைமை). Hearing thus functions as a safeguard not of infallibility but of judgment.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna tells Arjuna to learn the truth by approaching the wise, through reverent submission, thorough questioning (paripraśnena) and service; the knowers of truth will instruct him.
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 [4]
Pariprashna — questioning repeatedly and thoroughly — is the Gita’s equivalent of இழைத்துணர்தல், careful probing examination of what is heard.
Knowledge carefully examined through hearing and questioning produces sound judgment and speech.
Christianity
“A good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is good… for of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh.”
— Luke 6:45 (KJV) [5]
What one has stored within shapes what one says.
The Kural’s ‘amassed hearing’ is such a treasure — it ensures that even in error the speech is not foolish.
Islam
The Prophet said: whoever believes in God and the Last Day should speak what is good or keep silent.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6018; Sahih Muslim 47 [6]
Restraint in speech — speaking only good or not at all — is framed as a mark of faith.
Like the Kural’s learned hearers, the believer avoids foolish speech even when uncertain, by choosing silence.
Judaism
Among the seven traits of the wise: he does not interrupt, is not hasty to answer, asks and answers to the point, and about what he has not heard he says, “I have not heard.
— Pirkei Avot 5:7 [7]
The Mishnah’s portrait of the sage emphasises careful listening and restraint in speaking where knowledge is lacking.
The well-heard sage avoids foolish speech — precisely the Kural’s claim about those of amassed hearing.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares one who learns the teaching without examining its meaning to a man grasping a water-snake by the tail and being bitten, while one who examines the meaning with wisdom grasps it correctly, like seizing a snake with a forked stick.
— Majjhima Nikāya 22 (Alagaddūpama Sutta) [8]
Mere hearing is dangerous; hearing coupled with careful examination yields right understanding and right use.
Corresponds to the Kural’s இழைத்துணர்ந்து — finely examined hearing that prevents foolish error.
Jainism
“Again, under the influence of his (scriptural) knowledge, he becomes firm in his faith, meditation, observance of vows and self-restraint, and lives a life of purity throughout his lifetime without any wavering.”
— Saman Suttam v. 246 (Samyagjnanasutra) (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [9]
In the Jain view, knowledge received from scripture gives steadiness, so a person stays firm in faith and conduct without wavering.
Valluvar says those who have searched deeply and heard much will not speak foolishly even when they have misunderstood something. The Jain verse likewise credits well-absorbed teaching with keeping a person steady.
Sikhism
“Listening-dive deep into the ocean of virtue.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib (pauri 11), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the eleventh pauri of Japji Sahib Guru Nanak says that listening lets a person explore the depths of virtue; Sahib Singh glosses it as gaining insight into endless virtues.
The Kural says those who have examined things finely and gathered much learning by hearing will not speak foolishly; Japji says listening gives a deep understanding of virtue.
Greek Mythology
Nestor, the aged king of Pylos, ‘from whose tongue speech flowed sweeter than honey’, rises to calm the quarrel between Agamemnon and Achilles, drawing on the counsel of the great men he has heard and lived among.
— Homer, Iliad 1.247–284 [11]
Nestor’s long experience of listening to heroes of earlier generations makes his speech measured and wise.
Amassed hearing yields speech free of folly, even amid a crisis.
Philosophy
Aristotle says we ought to attend to the undemonstrated sayings and opinions of experienced and older people, or of people of practical wisdom, no less than to demonstrations, because experience has given them an eye to see aright.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.11, 1143b11–14 [12]
Practical wisdom accumulated through experience produces sound judgment even without formal proof.
Like the Kural, it credits accumulated, well-examined learning with reliable judgment and speech.

Real Life Example

Kepler refuses to ignore an eight-minute error in the orbit of Mars
1600-1609 · Prague, Bohemia (now Czech Republic)
Working with Tycho Brahe's precise observations of Mars, Johannes Kepler spent years trying to account for the planet's motion with combinations of circles, as astronomers had done since antiquity. His best circular model still disagreed with Tycho's data by about eight minutes of arc, and because he trusted those observations to about two minutes, he would not accept it. Re-examining the problem from its foundations, he found that Mars moves in an ellipse, and published his first two laws of planetary motion in Astronomia Nova in 1609.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Kepler's refusal to let a near-miss stand turned a mistaken assumption into one of the great discoveries of science.
The kural says those who have examined matters minutely will not speak foolishly even when they have misunderstood something, and Kepler's careful checking kept his early, mistaken model from becoming his published conclusion.
