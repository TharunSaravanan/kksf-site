Kural 819

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

கனவினும் இன்னாது மன்னோ வினைவேறு சொல்வேறு பட்டார் தொடர்பு.
Kanavinum innaadhu manno vinaiveru solveru pattaar thodarpu.

Meaning

Friendship with people whose actions and words do not match hurts you even in a dream.

Elementary

If a friend promises to save you a seat and then gives it to someone else, and does it again and again, you start feeling uneasy even when you’re just thinking about them. A friend’s words and actions should match.

University

The couplet names the fault precisely: vinai veru, sol veru (வினை வேறு, சொல் வேறு), ‘deed apart, word apart’, the divorce of action from speech. Kanavinum (கனவினும், ‘even in a dream’) intensifies: such a connection hurts not only in waking life, as Puliyur Kesigan spells out, but even in sleep. Manno (மன்னோ) is an emotive particle of emphasis or lament, which Sivayogi Sivakumar renders as a rhetorical question, ‘will it not give pain even in a dream?’ The word here is thodarpu (தொடர்பு, ‘connection, association’) rather than natpu, perhaps suggesting such people never achieve true friendship. All three texts agree; the sandhi-joined edition prints ‘கனவினு மின்னாது’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Duryodhana’s side invites the Pandavas to Varanavata with gracious words and builds them a fine house there, secretly made of lac and other flammable materials so that it can be set on fire while they sleep.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva (the house of lac, Jatugriha) [4]
The gracious invitation and the deadly intent are the starkest split between word and deed in the epic.
This is the Kural’s vinai veru, sol veru, and a reason such ties haunt even one’s dreams.
Christianity
“My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth.”
— 1 John 3:18 (KJV) [5]
John insists that love must be shown in action, not only spoken.
This is the positive form of the Kural’s complaint against those whose words and deeds diverge.
Islam
“O you who have believed, why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do.”
— Qur’an 61:2-3 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an condemns the gap between what people say and what they actually do.
The Kural’s vinai veru, sol veru is exactly the fault these verses denounce.
Judaism
“The words of his mouth were smoother than butter, but war was in his heart: his words were softer than oil, yet were they drawn swords.”
— Psalm 55:21 (Hebrew Bible, KJV rendering) [5]
The psalmist describes a former companion whose gentle speech covered hostile intent.
It is the Kural’s split between word and deed, and the anguish of the psalm matches ‘painful even in a dream’.
Buddhism
“(i) he makes friendly profession as regards the past, (ii) he makes friendly profession as regards the future, (iii) he tries to gain one’s favor by empty words, (iv) when opportunity for service has arisen, he expresses his inability.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [7]
The ‘lip-service’ friend is all professions of goodwill about past and future, with nothing done now.
This is the Kural’s friend whose words go one way and deeds another.
Jainism
“माया मित्ताणि नासेइ” (māyā mittāṇi nāsei), “Deception destroys friendship”
— Daśavaikālika Sūtra 8.38, Prakrit text with English rendering on tattvagyan.com ('Destroyer of friends') [8]
The Dasavaikalika names deceit (maya), one of the four passions (kashayas) that bind karma to the soul, as the passion that specifically destroys friendship.
The Kural says friendship with people whose deeds differ from their words is painful even in a dream, and the Jain canon names this kind of double-dealing as the very thing that destroys friendship.
Sikhism
“Farid, there are dozens who say they are friends; I search, but I cannot find even one.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Salok Sheikh Farid 87 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Farid laments that many claim friendship in words, but when he looks for one who is a friend in deed, he cannot find any.
The Kural says friendship with those whose deeds differ from their words is painful even in a dream; Farid names the same gap between people who call themselves friends and the friendship that is actually there.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Achilles tells the embassy sent by Agamemnon that he hates like the gates of Hades the man who hides one thing in his heart and says another.
— Homer, Iliad 9.312-313 [10]
Achilles names the gap between inner intent and outward speech as the thing he most despises.
Achilles’ loathing echoes the Kural’s judgment that such people are a torment even in dreams.
Philosophy
“… when he has been deceived by the pretences of the other person, it is just that he should complain against his deceiver; he will complain with more justice than one does against people who counterfeit the currency, inasmuch as the wrongdoing is concerned with something more valuable.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, IX.3, 1165b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says the bitterest complaints between friends arise when one pretended to love the other for his character while really loving him for profit or pleasure, and he ranks this deception above counterfeiting money.
Aristotle, like the Kural, treats a friendship in which outward profession differs from the real disposition as a grievous wrong.

Real Life Example

Pizarro takes Atahualpa's ransom and executes him
1532-1533 · Cajamarca, Peru
In November 1532 the Spanish conquistador Francisco Pizarro captured the Inca ruler Atahualpa at Cajamarca. Atahualpa offered to fill a large room with gold, and twice over with silver, in exchange for his release, and over the following months his people delivered the treasure. In 1533, having received the ransom, Pizarro had Atahualpa tried on a series of charges and executed, and the treasure was melted down and shared among the Spaniards.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The ransom agreement showed how worthless Pizarro's undertakings were once he had what he wanted.
The kural says that ties with people whose deeds are one thing and whose words another are painful even in a dream, and Atahualpa kept his side of the bargain only to find that Pizarro's deeds bore no relation to the terms agreed.
