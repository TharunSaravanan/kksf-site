Kural 134

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

மறப்பினும் ஓத்துக் கொளலாகும் பார்ப்பான் பிறப்பொழுக்கங் குன்றக் கெடும்.
Marappinum oththuk kolalaagum paarppaan Pirappozhukkam kundrak kedum.

Meaning

A Brahmin (a member of the priestly class) who forgets the Vedas (holy texts) can learn them again. But if he loses the good behaviour of his birth, his birth itself is ruined.

Elementary

If you forget your spelling words, you can study them again. But if you become a bully, no amount of studying fixes that. You lose something that is much harder to get back.

University

This couplet is the chapter's sharpest test case. The paarppaan (Brahmin) is the figure whose status depends most on birth and learning. Valluvar concedes that forgotten Vedic learning (oththu) can be recovered, but if pirappozhukkam, the conduct attached to that birth, declines (kundra), the birth itself is ruined (kedum). The logic is asymmetric: knowledge is a stock that can be replenished, while character is a foundation whose collapse brings down everything built on it. Commentators split between reading the couplet as upholding Brahmin duty (Parimelazhagar, Pappaiah) and as ranking birth below conduct (Karunanidhi). It closely echoes Manu 1.109, where a Brahmin who departs from the rule of conduct ‘does not reap the fruit of the Veda’, and Yudhishthira's answer to the Yaksha in the Mahabharata.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is neither birth, nor study, nor learning, that is the cause of Brahmanahood, without doubt, it is behaviour that constitutes it. One's behaviour should always be well-guarded, especially by a Brahmana.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 311 (Yaksha Prashna), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Asked by the Yaksha what makes a Brahmin, Yudhishthira rules out birth and study and names conduct alone. He adds that a Brahmin in particular must guard it.
This is nearly the same argument as the Kural: study is secondary, and conduct is what preserves Brahmin standing.
Christianity
“And though I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries, and all knowledge; and though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that even perfect knowledge counts for nothing without love.
Like the Kural, it ranks sacred knowledge below the quality of one's conduct.
Islam
The Prophet taught that the person whose deeds hold him back will not be carried forward by his lineage.
— Sahih Muslim 2699 [5]
Noble descent cannot make up for a lack of good deeds.
This fits the Kural's claim that a high birth is ruined when conduct fails.
Judaism
“anyone whose deeds exceed his wisdom, his wisdom is enduring, but anyone whose wisdom exceeds his deeds, his wisdom is not enduring.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:9 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Hanina ben Dosa teaches that learning only lasts when it rests on deeds.
Both rank conduct above learning and warn that learning without conduct collapses.
Buddhism
“There are brahmans born in the family of preceptors, kinsmen of (veda) hymns. They are often seen committing evil deeds. In this life itself they are despised, in the next they are born in an evil state of existence. High birth does not prevent them from falling into a woeful state, or from censure.”
— Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7, vv. 25–26 (tr. Piyadassi Thera) [7]
The Buddha points to Brahmins of Vedic families who do evil, and says their birth cannot save them from contempt.
It is the same case as the Kural, a Vedic Brahmin undone by misconduct despite his birth.
Jainism
“Those who are full of anger and pride, who kill, lie, steal, and own property, are Brâhmaṇas without pure birth and knowledge; they are very bad fields. You are only the bearer of words as it were, you do not understand their meaning, though you have learned the Vêdas.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.13–15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Answering Brahmin priests who claim to be the best recipients by pure birth and learning, the Jain monk says that their misconduct has already destroyed both their birth and their knowledge.
Valluvar says a Brahmin who forgets the Vedas can learn them again, but one who falls from conduct ruins his birth; this passage likewise says that conduct, not Vedic learning, decides whether a Brahmin's birth counts as pure.
Sikhism
“He alone is a Brahmin, who knows God. He chants and meditates, and practices austerity and good deeds. He keeps to the Dharma, with faith, humility and contentment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1411, Salok Vaaran te Vadheek, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak redefines the Brahmin by inner knowledge and conduct, not by descent or ritual learning.
The Kural says a Brahmin's conduct, not his Vedic recitation, is what keeps his birth from ruin; Guru Nanak goes further and makes conduct the whole of what a Brahmin is.
Greek Mythology
Achilles, son of a goddess and pupil of the wise centaur Chiron, drags Hector's corpse day after day. Apollo protests to the gods that Achilles has destroyed pity and has no shame, and the gods intervene against him.
— Homer, Iliad 24.33–54 [10]
Neither divine birth nor the finest education protects Achilles from the gods' anger once his conduct becomes shameful.
This echoes the Kural: high birth and learning are undone by a lapse in conduct.
Philosophy
Boethius, through Lady Philosophy, argues that nobility is only praise earned by one's ancestors' merits. If it has any good at all, it lies in obliging the nobly born not to fall short of their ancestors' virtue.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy III, prose 6 [11]
Noble birth carries no worth of its own; it only puts the heir under an obligation of conduct.
This matches the Kural: birth keeps its value only while the matching conduct is kept up.

Real Life Example

Francis Bacon falls from office for taking gifts from litigants
1621 · London, England
Francis Bacon, the philosopher and essayist who had been Lord Chancellor of England since 1618, was charged in Parliament in 1621 with accepting gifts from people whose cases were before him. He pleaded guilty, was heavily fined and sentenced to imprisonment in the Tower of London; the fine was later waived and he spent only a few days in the Tower, but he was never again allowed to sit in Parliament or hold political office. Bacon maintained that the gifts had not affected his judgments, and he devoted his remaining years to writing and philosophy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode shows that even one of the most learned men of his age could not recover his public standing once his conduct in office was found wanting, while his intellectual work continued and grew.
The kural says lost learning can be regained but a lapse in conduct ruins one's standing; Bacon's learning stayed with him and kept producing books, but his lapse ended his public career for good.
