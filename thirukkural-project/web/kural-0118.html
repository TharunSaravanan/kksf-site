Kural 118

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

சமன்செய்து சீர்தூக்குங் கோல்போல் அமைந்தொருபால் கோடாமை சான்றோர்க் கணி.
Saman seydhu seerthookkung kolpol amaindhorupaal Kodaamai saandrork kani.

Meaning

Like a scale that is first set level and then weighs things exactly, a good person stays steady and does not lean to either side. This is their jewel.

Elementary

A good weighing scale sits perfectly flat before you put anything on it, then shows the true weight, no matter whose apples they are. A fair person is like that scale: steady and not tipping toward anyone.

University

This is the chapter’s central image. The துலாக்கோல் (tulaakkol, balance-beam) first சமன்செய்து (“makes itself even”), then சீர்தூக்கும் (“weighs rightly”), and does not ஒருபால் கோடாமை (“incline to one side”). Varadarasanar and Pappaiah stress the two-stage sequence: the judge must first be internally level, free of prior bias, and only then evaluate. That is a sharp account of impartiality as a disposition as well as a procedure. The scale is a near-universal emblem of justice: Zeus’s golden scales in Homer, the Qur’anic mīzān, the “just balances” of Leviticus, and the Dhammapada’s sage who weighs good and evil “as if holding a balance-scale.” Aristotle’s description of the judge as “animate justice” and “intermediate” states the same idea philosophically. The second line repeats the refrain of Kural 115, linking inner steadiness under fortune to outer impartiality in judgment.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Shibi, when a hawk demands a dove that has taken refuge with him, refuses to wrong either bird. He offers his own flesh equal in weight to the dove, placing it on a scale, and finally climbs onto the scale himself, after which the gods (Indra and Agni in disguise) reveal themselves and praise him.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (Shibi and the dove) [4]
In the Mahabharata, Shibi will not favour the dove at the hawk’s expense or the hawk at the dove’s, and a literal pair of scales settles the matter.
An Indian story in which the scale itself is the emblem of refusing to lean toward either party.
Christianity
“Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment.”
— John 7:24 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells his critics to judge by what is truly right, not by surface impressions.
Like the level scale, righteous judgment weighs the real substance and not appearances or bias.
Islam
“That you not transgress within the balance. And establish weight in justice and do not make deficient the balance.”
— Qur’an 55:8–9 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an says God set up the cosmic balance (al-mīzān) and commands people not to violate it but to weigh with justice.
The same image of the balance as the model of justice, which must not be tipped.
Judaism
“Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin, shall ye have”
— Leviticus 19:35–36 (KJV) [5]
The Torah puts judgment and weights and measures in the same command: both must be exact and fair.
The Kural’s scale-and-judgment pairing appears here in one sentence.
Buddhism
“Not by observing silence does one become a sage, if he be foolish and ignorant. But that man is wise who, as if holding a balance-scale accepts only the good.”
— Dhammapada 268 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada uses a balance-scale image: the true sage weighs things carefully, taking the good and rejecting the evil.
A direct balance-scale metaphor for the discerning person, as in the Kural.
Jainism
“He, in whom attachment and aversion do not create any disturbance, (is said to have) steadfast equanimity, according to the preaching of the omniscient.”
— Kundakunda, Niyamasāra 9.128 'Supreme Equanimity' (English rendering on jainworld.com) [8]
Kundakunda defines true equanimity (sāmāyika) as a mind that neither attachment nor aversion can tip.
Valluvar's balance-beam first sets itself level and leans to neither side, and Kundakunda's sage, unmoved by attachment or aversion, is that balance in Jain terms.
Sikhism
“Mind is the scale, consciousness the weights, and the performance of Your service is the appraiser.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 731, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak uses the merchant's balance as an image for the inner discipline by which a person weighs rightly.
The Kural's balance-beam first sets itself level and then weighs without leaning; Guru Nanak likewise makes the mind itself the scale that must be true.
Greek Mythology
“the father of all balanced his golden scales and placed a doom in each of them, one for Achilles and the other for Hector. As he held the scales by the middle, the doom of Hector fell down deep into the house of Hades”
— Homer, Iliad 22 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Even Zeus, who pities Hector, does not decide by favour; he weighs the two fates on his golden scales, holding them “by the middle,” and accepts the result.
The balance held level at the centre, deciding without favour, is the Kural’s image.
Philosophy
“to go to the judge is to go to justice; for the nature of the judge is to be a sort of animate justice; and they seek the judge as an intermediate”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V.4 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle describes the judge as justice in living form: a mean point that restores equality between two parties.
Aristotle’s judge-as-intermediate is the philosophical version of the Kural’s level balance-beam.

Real Life Example

Athens chooses jurors by allotment machine
4th century BCE (procedure described); surviving machines mid-2nd century BCE · Athenian Agora, Athens, Greece
Classical Athens chose many officials and jurors by lot rather than by election. The Aristotelian Constitution of the Athenians gives a detailed account of how jurors for the courts were allotted, and stone allotment machines called kleroteria, found in and around the Athenian Agora, confirm that picture: citizens' bronze tickets were inserted into rows of slots, and an attached mechanical device made the selection by chance. The surviving inscribed machines date to the mid-second century BCE.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Athenians built the removal of favouritism into the procedure itself, so that the panel was settled by chance before any case was weighed.
The kural compares the just to a balance that first sets itself level and then weighs; the allotment machine levelled the scale before judgment began.
