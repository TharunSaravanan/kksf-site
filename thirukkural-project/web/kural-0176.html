Kural 176

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

அருள்வெஃகி ஆற்றின்கண் நின்றான் பொருள்வெஃகிப் பொல்லாத சூழக் கெடும்.
Arulvehki aatrinkan nindraan porulvehkip Pollaadha soozhak kedum.

Meaning

Even someone who wants to be kind and has stayed on the right path will be ruined if they start wanting others' riches and plotting evil ways to get them.

Elementary

Think of a kid who has always been kind and helpful. If one day they start scheming to take someone else’s things, all that goodness can come crashing down. This kural says no one is so good that greed can’t ruin them.

University

The kural turns on a deliberate repetition: the same verb வெஃகு (vehku, ‘to desire’) is used for the noble desire for அருள் (arul, grace/compassion) and the base desire for பொருள் (porul, wealth). The same faculty of longing can be aimed upward or downward, and a person who has aimed it well can still redirect it disastrously. ஆற்றின்கண் நின்றான் (aatrinkan nindraan, ‘one who stood in the path’) is read by Parimelazhagar as the householder’s path by which grace is attained, and more broadly by modern commentators as the path of virtue; பொல்லாத சூழ (pollaadha soozha) stresses that plotting is itself ruinous, even before any theft. The kural is a warning against spiritual complacency, echoed in the Gita’s admission that the senses carry away even a striving sage, and in the Qur’anic portrait of the man raised by signs who ‘adhered to the earth’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The senses are so strong and impetuous, O Arjuna, that they forcibly carry away the mind even of a man of discrimination who is endeavoring to control them.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.60 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna warns that even a wise person who is actively striving can be carried off by desire.
The Kural warns the same: one who has stood on the path of grace can still be ruined by desire for wealth.
Christianity
“For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”
— 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that some who were once in the faith coveted money, wandered away, and brought great pain on themselves.
An almost exact parallel: people already on the path of faith were ruined by coveting.
Islam
“And if We had willed, we could have elevated him thereby, but he adhered [instead] to the earth and followed his own desire. So his example is like that of the dog: if you chase him, he pants, or if you leave him, he [still] pants.”
— Qur’an 7:176 (Sahih International; the account begins at 7:175) [5]
The Qur’an tells of a man who had been given God’s signs and could have been raised high by them, but he clung to worldly things and followed his desire.
Exactly the Kural’s fallen seeker: someone given grace who throws it away for worldly desire.
Judaism
“But Gehazi, the servant of Elisha the man of God, said, Behold, my master hath spared Naaman this Syrian, in not receiving at his hands that which he brought: but, as the LORD liveth, I will run after him, and take somewhat of him.”
— 2 Kings 5:20-27 (KJV) [4]
Gehazi, the servant of the prophet Elisha, schemed to get the gifts his master had refused, lied to cover it, and was struck with leprosy (2 Kings 5:27).
Gehazi lived in a prophet’s household (on the path of grace), coveted another’s wealth, plotted deceit to get it, and was ruined, just as the Kural describes.
Buddhism
“There is one who, turning away from desire (for household life) takes to the life of the forest (i.e., of a monk). But after being freed from the household, he runs back to it. Behold that man! Though freed, he runs back to that very bondage!”
— Dhammapada 344 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha describes someone who had renounced worldly desire but then ran back to it.
Like the Kural, it describes the person already on the path who returns to worldly desire and loses what was gained.
Jainism
“When suppressed, passion can bring about the spiritual degeneration of even the most virtuous monk, who in his conduct is akin to Jina himself, what can we say of monks who are under the sway of attachment?”
— Saman Suttam, verse 132 (Samyamasutra; English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [7]
Jain teaching warns that passions (kaṣāya), greed among them, merely held down rather than destroyed can bring down even a monk whose conduct resembles a Jina's.
Valluvar says even one who has longed for grace and stood on virtue's path will be ruined once he covets others' wealth, and the Jain verse warns that passion can make even the most virtuous fall.
Sikhism
“The man-eaters say their prayers.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 471, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak mocks officials who devour people through bribery and oppression while still keeping up the forms of prayer and piety.
The Kural warns that even one who has stood on the path of virtue is ruined once he covets others' wealth and plots to get it; Guru Nanak shows such outward piety emptied by greed.
Greek Mythology
Tantalus, so favoured by the gods that he dined at their table, stole their nectar and ambrosia to share with mortals, and was cast down to eternal punishment in the underworld.
— Pindar, Olympian Ode 1 [9]
Someone admitted to the gods’ own company threw it all away by taking what was not his.
Like the Kural’s seeker who stood on the path of grace, Tantalus had divine favour and lost it through coveting and theft.
Philosophy
In the myth of the Ring of Gyges, a shepherd who finds a ring of invisibility uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king, and seize the kingdom. Glaucon argues that even a supposedly just man would do the same if he could act with impunity.
— Plato, Republic II, 359c-360d [10]
Plato uses the story to ask whether anyone’s goodness would survive the chance to take whatever they wanted without consequence.
The Kural answers Glaucon’s challenge: even the person who seemed to stand on the path will be ruined if he lets covetousness plot for him.

Real Life Example

Televangelist Jim Bakker is convicted of fraud
October 1989 · Charlotte, North Carolina, United States
Jim Bakker built the PTL television ministry into a large religious enterprise that included the Heritage USA resort in South Carolina. Prosecutors showed that he had sold far more lifetime partnerships, promising yearly lodging at the resort, than could ever be honoured, and diverted the money to other uses. In October 1989 a federal jury convicted him on all 24 counts of fraud and conspiracy, and he was sentenced to 45 years in prison, later reduced; he served almost five years.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A preacher who had led a large ministry lost his ministry, his freedom and his reputation through greed for his followers' money.
The kural says even one who has stood on the path of virtue and grace will be ruined if he covets others' wealth, and Bakker's fall from ministry to prison shows exactly that.
