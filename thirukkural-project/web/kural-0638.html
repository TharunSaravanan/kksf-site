Kural 638

Ministry of State · Amaichu · அமைச்சு

அறிகொன்று அறியான் எனினும் உறுதி உழையிருந்தான் கூறல் கடன்.
Arikondru ariyaan eninum urudhi Uzhaiyirundhaan kooral kadan.

Meaning

Even if the king ignores good advice and does not know better himself, the adviser by his side still has the duty to tell him what is truly good.

Elementary

Imagine your friend is about to do something unsafe and will not listen. A true friend still tells them the truth, even if they get grumpy. This kural says a leader’s adviser must always tell the leader what is good, even when the leader does not want to hear it.

University

Arikondru (‘having killed knowledge’) is usually taken to mean that the king destroys or ignores the counsel of those who know. Ariyaan eninum (‘even if he himself does not know’) adds that he also lacks knowledge of his own. Even then urudhi (what is truly and lastingly beneficial) must be told (kooral kadan, ‘speaking is a duty’) by the uzhaiyirundhaan, ‘the one who is at his side’, meaning the minister. Solomon Pappaiah stresses that the minister must speak without anger and without fear. Kalaignar applies the verse to those near any ruler. The couplet is the moral centre of the chapter: the minister’s duty to give frank counsel does not depend on whether it will be welcomed. The thirukkural-thamizh.org edition prints ‘arikon rariyaa neninu murudhi / yuzhaiyirundhaan’, a difference of word division only.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“They are abundant, O king, that can always speak agreeable words. The speaker, however, is rare, as also the hearer, of words that are disagreeable but medicinal.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva 37 (Vidura Niti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
Vidura tells the blind king Dhritarashtra, whose sons are heading for ruin, that honest but unwelcome advice is rare.
Vidura is doing what the Kural requires: telling a king who will not listen what is good for him.
Christianity
“For John had said unto Herod, It is not lawful for thee to have thy brother’s wife.”
— Mark 6:18 (KJV) [5]
John the Baptist confronts King Herod about his unlawful marriage, and it later costs him his life.
He shows the Kural’s duty to tell a ruler the truth whatever the cost.
Islam
“The Religion is sincerity.” We said, “To whom?” He said “To Allah, to His Book, To His Messenger, and to the leaders of the Muslims and their masses.”
— Sahih Muslim 55a [6]
The Prophet defines religion as nasiha, sincere good counsel, owed even to those in authority.
This makes the Kural’s duty to give leaders sincere advice part of faith itself.
Judaism
“And Nathan said to David: ‘Thou art the man.’”
— II Samuel 12:7 (JPS 1917) [7]
After telling David a parable about a rich man who stole a poor man’s lamb, the prophet Nathan confronts the king directly over his sin with Bathsheba and Uriah.
Nathan, a member of David’s court, tells his king the unwelcome truth, as the Kural requires of the one at the ruler’s side.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them.”
— Abhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 58 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha tells Prince Abhaya that he will speak true and beneficial words even when they are unwelcome, choosing the right moment.
This matches the Kural’s urudhi, beneficial counsel given even when it will be disliked.
Jainism
“If you have no intention of abandoning pleasure, and still long for undertakings and property, my long talk has been to no purpose. I go, king, farewell.” And Brahmadatta, king of the Pañkâlas, did not act on the counsel of the saint …”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.33-34 (cf. 13.15) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The monk Citta, out of old friendship and concern for the king's welfare, gives the emperor Brahmadatta honest advice again and again; the king admits he knows the Law but will not follow it, and comes to ruin.
The Kural says that even when the king rejects good counsel and is ignorant himself, the one beside him must still tell him what is truly good; Citta does this to the end, though the king will not listen.
Sikhism
“Nanak speaks the Word of Truth; he proclaims the Truth at this, the right time.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 723, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
This line closes a hymn of Raag Tilang that belongs to Guru Nanak's Babarvani, his hymns about Babur's invasion, in which he speaks plainly of the cruelty of the powerful.
The Kural says the one who stands beside the king must still tell him what is truly good, even if the king rejects advice; Guru Nanak insists on proclaiming the truth to the powerful at the very moment it is needed.
Greek Mythology
Before the fighting at the Greek ships, the Trojan counsellor Polydamas urges Hector to withdraw the army inside the city walls now that Achilles has returned. Hector angrily rejects the advice, and the Trojans suffer heavy losses. Hector later admits that he should have listened.
— Homer, Iliad 18 and 22 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Polydamas gives honest counsel to a leader who will not listen.
This is the Kural’s situation: a counsellor at the leader’s side whose sound advice is spurned.
Philosophy
Plutarch argues that the true friend is known by his frank speech (parrhesia). He is willing to point out a man’s faults for his good, while the flatterer only says what pleases. Plutarch adds that this frankness should be used with tact and at the right moment.
— Plutarch, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend (Moralia) [12]
For Plutarch, honest criticism given for the other person’s benefit is the sign of real loyalty.
This is the minister’s duty in the Kural to speak urudhi, what is truly beneficial.

Real Life Example

Hai Rui's memorial rebukes the Jiajing Emperor
1566 · Beijing, China
The Ming Jiajing Emperor had for more than twenty years largely stopped holding court audiences and spent heavily on Daoist rituals and the search for longevity. In 1566 Hai Rui, a secretary in the Ministry of Revenue, submitted a memorial criticising the emperor for this neglect of government, and the emperor had him thrown into prison. Early in 1567, a few days after the emperor's death, Hai Rui was released and restored to his rank, and he was later honoured with the posthumous name Loyal and Incorruptible.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Hai Rui spoke plainly to a ruler who was known to punish critics, and his courage made him a lasting model of the honest official in China.
The kural says that even when the ruler rejects wise advice and is ignorant himself, the one at his side must still tell him what is truly good, and Hai Rui did so at the cost of his freedom.
