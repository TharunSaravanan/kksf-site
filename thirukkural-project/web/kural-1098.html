Kural 1098

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

அசையியற்கு உண்டாண்டோர் ஏஎர்யான் நோக்கப் பசையினள் பைய நகும்.
Asaiyiyarku undaandor eeryaan nokkap pasaiyinal paiya nagum.

Meaning

When I look at her, pleading, she softens and smiles gently. At that moment this delicate girl looks especially lovely.

Elementary

When you look at someone with a hopeful, friendly face, and their serious look melts into a soft smile, it feels wonderful. That smile is a sign they are happy to be your friend.

University

Asaiyiyal (அசையியல்) is ‘she whose nature sways’, a soft, delicate girl; ēer (ஏஎர், ēr with alapeṭai) means beauty or grace, and āṇṭu (ஆண்டு) ‘then, there’. Pasaiyinaḷ (பசையினள்) is ‘she who has softened, become tender’. The commentators differ on ēer: Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it as a new beauty she has at that moment; Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan read the line as saying there is a sign (kuṟippu) or token of goodness in her smile; Kesikan also understands yān nōkka as ‘when I looked as if begging’. The ytamizh Sivayogi gloss reads differently again, showing real interpretive variety in this couplet. Pope: ‘I gaze, the tender maid relents the while; / And, oh the matchless grace of that soft smile!’ Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Shakuntala (to herself): “Oh, why do I have such feelings when I see this man? They seem wrong in a hermitage.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
At her first sight of the king, Shakuntala feels her heart soften toward him and wonders at it.
Her heart softening at his look is the inner side of the Kural’s gentle, relenting smile.
Christianity
“Then was I in his eyes as one that found favour.”
— Song of Solomon 8:10 (KJV) [4]
The beloved in the Song says that in her lover’s eyes she has found favour.
Finding favour in the other’s eyes is the moment of mutual grace the Kural captures in the softened smile.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquility in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an names the affection and tenderness placed between spouses as one of God’s signs.
The softening tenderness (pasai) of the Kural’s beloved is the ‘affection and mercy’ the verse praises.
Judaism
“And it was so, when the king saw Esther the queen standing in the court, that she obtained favour in his sight; and the king held out to Esther the golden sceptre that was in his hand.”
— Esther 5:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
When Esther appears before the king, his look softens and he extends his sceptre to her.
A look that brings about a relenting, gracious response is the turn the Kural describes.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha says a husband should honour his wife by courtesy and by not despising her, and that a wife who is so treated cares for him with love in return.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta [7]
Kindness offered is answered by kindness returned between partners.
The beloved’s softening in answer to his tender look is a small instance of this mutual courtesy.
Jainism
“Pleasant things (by themselves) do not cause indifference nor emotions (as anger, &c.); but by either hating or loving them, a man undergoes such a change through delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.101 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching says it is our own attachment, not the object, that stirs our emotions.
Jainism would trace the delight the lover finds in her smile to his own attachment, offering a sober counterpoint to the Kural.
Sikhism
“Renouncing selfishness and conceit, I seek His Sanctuary, and speak sweet words to Him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 136, Raag Maajh, Barah Maha, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Barah Maha in Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan describes the soul-bride humbly pleading with her Husband Lord in the hope that He will turn to her with kindness.
In the Kural the lover looks at the girl imploringly and she softens and smiles; Guru Arjan's bride also pleads humbly and hopes the Beloved will soften toward her.
Greek Mythology
“Thus he spake, honouring her; and she cast her eyes down with a smile divinely sweet; and her soul melted within her, uplifted by his praise.”
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.1008-1010 (tr. R. C. Seaton, 1912) [10]
After Jason pleads with Medea as a suppliant, she looks down, smiles sweetly and melts toward him.
A pleading look answered by a softened, gentle smile is the Kural’s scene exactly.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus, Socrates says the beloved, receiving the lover’s devoted gaze, comes to feel a returning love (anteros), an image of love reflected back, though he hardly knows what it is.
— Plato, Phaedrus 255c-d (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Love given through the eyes calls forth love in return.
The beloved’s softening smile under the lover’s gaze is this returning love made visible.

Real Life Example

Mark Twain courts Olivia Langdon
1868–1870 · Elmira, New York, United States
Samuel Clemens (Mark Twain) proposed to Olivia Langdon in 1868, soon after meeting her, and she refused. He kept writing to her for about seventeen months, sending more than 180 letters, and kept visiting Elmira. They became engaged in February 1869 and married at the Langdon home in Elmira on 2 February 1870.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Their marriage lasted until Olivia's death and was central to Twain's life and work.
The kural's lover looks at his beloved imploringly until she softens; Twain's long, pleading courtship slowly turned Olivia's refusal into acceptance.
