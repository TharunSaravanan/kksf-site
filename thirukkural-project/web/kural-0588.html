Kural 588

Detectives · Otraadal · ஒற்றாடல்

ஒற்றொற்றித் தந்த பொருளையும் மற்றுமோர் ஒற்றினால் ஒற்றிக் கொளல்.
Otrotrith thandha porulaiyum matrumoar Otrinaal otrik kolal.

Meaning

Even news that a spy has brought in should be believed only after another spy has checked it.

Elementary

If one friend tells you there is no school tomorrow, you would probably check with another friend or the school website before staying home. This kural says a wise leader always double-checks a report with a second source.

University

The couplet plays on the root ஒற்று, which appears four times (ஒற்று ஒற்றித் தந்த … ஒற்றினால் ஒற்றி): ‘what a spy has spied out and delivered, have it spied out again by another spy, and then accept it’. The உம் in பொருளையும் (‘even the information’) means that even a reliable spy’s report is not exempt. Mu. Varadarasanar says it should be taken as true only after the two accounts are found to agree (ஒப்புமை கண்டபின்). Kalaignar stresses comparing the two reports before deciding. கொளல் is an infinitive-style imperative (‘one should accept’), the same form Valluvar uses for maxims elsewhere. This couplet and 589 together set out a clear doctrine of corroborating intelligence. The text agrees across all three sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The immediate officers of the institutes of espionage (saṃsthānāmantevāsina) shall, by making use of signs or writing (saṃjñā lipibhi), set their own spies in motion (to ascertain the validity of the information).”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.12 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [4]
Kautilya builds a second layer of spies whose job is to check the reports of the first.
Spies sent to test other spies’ information are the Kural’s மற்றுமோர் ஒற்றினால் ஒற்றிக் கொளல் put into practice.
Christianity
“Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.”
— 1 Thessalonians 5:21 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells the church to test every claim before holding on to it.
Test first, then accept: the Kural’s rule for spy reports in its most general form.
Islam
“O you who have believed, if there comes to you a disobedient one with information, investigate, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become, over what you have done, regretful.”
— Quran 49:6 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran orders that a report from an unreliable source be investigated before anyone acts on it, to avoid harming innocent people.
Verify before acting, especially where lives are at stake: the heart of this Kural.
Judaism
“Then shalt thou enquire, and make search, and ask diligently; and, behold, if it be truth, and the thing certain …”
— Deuteronomy 13:14 (KJV; 13:15 in Hebrew numbering) [5]
Deuteronomy says that a rumour of grave wrongdoing in a town must be investigated thoroughly and found true and certain before anything is done.
Enquire, search and ask diligently until it is certain: the Kural’s demand for a second check.
Buddhism
“Come, Kalamas. Do not go upon what has been acquired by repeated hearing; nor upon tradition; nor upon rumor; nor upon what is in a scripture; nor upon surmise …”
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.65, Kalama Sutta (trans. Soma Thera) [7]
The Buddha tells the Kalamas not to accept a claim just because it was reported to them, but to test it until they know for themselves.
Refusing to act on a report alone is the Kural’s refusal to accept even one spy’s word unchecked.
Jainism
Samantabhadra opens his Āptamīmāṃsā (also called Devāgamastotra) by refusing to accept even the Jina as an authority because of outward signs such as the arrival of gods, travel through the sky or royal fans, since such marvels are also seen among magicians; he then tests the Jina's teaching by reason and the logic of anekāntavāda before accepting it.
— Samantabhadra, Āptamīmāṃsā (Devāgamastotra) v. 1 and overall argument (described in Vijay K. Jain's translation, 2016; PhilArchive record) [8]
This classic Digambara work makes critical examination the ground for trust, even toward the most authoritative source.
The Kural says even a trusted spy's report must be re-checked by another spy before it is accepted; Samantabhadra applies the same rule of verification to the highest authority Jainism recognises.
Sikhism
“and placed in the Court of our Lord and Master, and there, the genuine and the counterfeit are separated.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 789, Raag Soohee (salok in Soohee ki Vaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Using the image of coins brought before their master to be assayed, Guru Nanak says that whatever is offered as true is tested in the Lord's court and the false is set apart.
The Kural tells the king to accept even a spy's report only after another spy has checked it; Guru Nanak's assay of coins shares the conviction that what is offered as genuine must be tested before it is accepted.
Greek Mythology
The Trojans capture the Greek Sinon, who tells a moving story that the wooden horse is a sacred offering. Without checking his story against any other source, and ignoring Laocoön’s warning, they drag the horse into the city, and Troy falls.
— Virgil, Aeneid 2 (paraphrase; the story belongs to the Greek Trojan cycle) [10]
The most famous intelligence failure in myth comes from trusting one planted informant.
This is the disaster the Kural guards against by never accepting a single source unchecked.
Philosophy
“Without subtle ingenuity of mind, one cannot make certain of the truth of their reports.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 13.17 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu warns that spy reports cannot simply be taken at face value; the ruler must use his judgment to test them.
Both texts insist that a spy’s report must be tested before it is trusted.

Real Life Example

American codebreakers confirm that AF means Midway
May 1942 · Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, and Midway Atoll
In spring 1942 US Navy codebreakers at Station Hypo in Pearl Harbor, led by Joseph Rochefort, read Japanese messages referring to an objective called AF and suspected it was Midway, but analysts in Washington doubted this. To test the reading, the Navy had Midway report by secure cable and then broadcast an uncoded message that its fresh-water plant had broken down. When a Japanese intelligence report soon stated that AF was short of water, the identification was confirmed, and the US Navy prepared the ambush that won the Battle of Midway in June 1942.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Rather than act on a single reading of intercepted traffic, the Navy found an independent way to check it before staking the Pacific Fleet on it.
The kural says that even information a spy has brought in should be accepted only after it has been checked by another, and the water-shortage test was exactly such a second check.
