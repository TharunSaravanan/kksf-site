Kural 984

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

கொல்லா நலத்தது நோன்மை பிறர்தீமை சொல்லா நலத்தது சால்பு.
Kollaa nalaththadhu nonmai pirardheemai sollaa nalaththadhu saalbu.

Meaning

Self-discipline is built on the goodness of not killing. Perfect character is built on the goodness of not talking about other people's wrongs.

Elementary

A good person does not hurt any living creature, not even a bug. And a really good person also does not go around telling everyone the bad things someone else did. Valluvar says the first is the heart of being holy, and the second is the heart of being truly good.

University

A balanced couplet built on two parallel phrases: kollaa nalam (கொல்லா நலம், 'the goodness of not killing') grounds nonmai (நோன்மை, 'austerity, penance', glossed as தவம் by Varadarasanar and Kesikan), while sollaa nalam (சொல்லா நலம், 'the goodness of not telling') grounds saalbu. Pirar theemai (பிறர்தீமை) may mean either others' faults or the wrongs others have done; Karunanidhi takes the second ('not pointing out the harm others do'), the majority the first. The pairing ties this chapter to Kollaamai (ch. 33) and Purangooraamai (Not Backbiting, ch. 19), and suggests that just as non-killing is the essence of the ascetic, refusal to broadcast faults is the essence of the noble householder. Pope's 'penitence' for nonmai is archaic; 'austerity' is closer. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads nonmai more broadly as 'sustaining life without destroying'. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of the body consists in worship of the Supreme Lord, the brāhmaṇas, the spiritual master, and superiors like the father and mother, and in cleanliness, simplicity, celibacy and nonviolence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
The Gita counts nonviolence among the elements of bodily austerity (tapas), and the next verse adds truthful, non-agitating speech as austerity of speech.
Like the Kural, it links tapas with non-harm and, in the following verse, with restraint in speech.
Christianity
“Speak not evil one of another, brethren. He that speaketh evil of his brother, and judgeth his brother, speaketh evil of the law, and judgeth the law: but if thou judge the law, thou art not a doer of the law, but a judge.”
— James 4:11 (KJV) [4]
James forbids believers to speak evil of one another, treating such talk as a usurpation of judgment.
This directly matches the Kural's second clause: perfection lies in not speaking of others' faults.
Islam
“O you who have believed, avoid much [negative] assumption. Indeed, some assumption is sin. And do not spy or backbite each other. Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his brother when dead? You would detest it. And fear Allah; indeed, Allah is Accepting of repentance and Merciful.”
— Qur'an 49:12 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an compares backbiting to eating a dead brother's flesh.
Like the Kural, it treats talking about others' faults as a grave failure of character.
Judaism
“Do not deal basely with members of your people. Do not profit by the blood of your fellow: I am GOD.”
— Leviticus 19:16 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Leviticus forbids slander and talebearing (the translators note 'deal basely' as, for example, slander) and, in the same verse, standing idly by another's blood.
The verse pairs a ban on harmful speech with a concern for life, much as the Kural pairs not speaking ill with not killing.
Buddhism
“Let none find fault with others; let none see the omissions and commissions of others. But let one see one's own acts, done and undone.”
— Dhammapada 50 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha tells his followers to stop examining other people's failings and to look at their own.
This matches the Kural's teaching that perfection lies in not speaking of others' faults.
Jainism
“The Arhats and Bhagavats of the past, present, and future, all say thus, speak thus, declare thus, explain thus: all breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain, nor treated with violence, nor abused, nor tormented, nor driven away.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.4.1.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
The Acaranga states the core Jain law of ahimsa: no living being may be killed, harmed or abused.
This is the Kural's first clause in its most rigorous form: non-killing as the very ground of ascetic discipline.
Sikhism
“It is not good to slander anyone, but the foolish, self-willed manmukhs still do it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 755, Raag Suhi, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das condemns nindaa (speaking ill of others) as the mark of the manmukh, the self-willed person turned away from the Guru. Gurbani often treats slander as a serious spiritual fault.
The second line of the Kural grounds perfection in not speaking of others' faults, and Guru Amar Das likewise counts refusing to slander as part of a good life.
Greek Mythology
In Book XV of Ovid's Metamorphoses, the philosopher Pythagoras urges people to stop killing animals for food, arguing that all souls pass between bodies and that slaughter defiles human beings.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses XV.60-478 (trans. A. S. Kline) [10]
Pythagoras, a semi-legendary Greek sage, made abstention from killing a central religious discipline.
This echoes the Kural's view that real austerity begins with not killing.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises keeping silent for the most part and, if one must speak about other people, not to blame them, praise them or compare them.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 (cf. tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
The Stoic teacher counts restraint in speaking about others as part of the disciplined life.
This matches the Kural's teaching that the noble do not talk about other people's faults.

Real Life Example

Eisenhower drafts a note taking all blame if D-Day fails
5 June 1944 · Southern England
On 5 June 1944, hours after giving the final order to launch the Allied invasion of Normandy, General Dwight D. Eisenhower wrote a short statement to be released if the landings failed. In it he praised the troops, the air forces and the navy for doing all that bravery and devotion to duty could do, and declared that any blame or fault for the attempt was his alone. He misdated the note July 5; the invasion succeeded and the statement was never used, and it is now held at the Eisenhower Library.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Even in imagined defeat Eisenhower refused to place the fault on the men under his command.
The kural says perfection rests on not speaking of other people's faults, and Eisenhower prepared to shoulder all blame himself rather than point to anyone else's failings.
