Kural 1106

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

உறுதோறு உயிர்தளிர்ப்பத் தீண்டலால் பேதைக்கு அமிழ்தின் இயன்றன தோள்.
Urudhoru uyirthalirppath theendalaal pedhaikku amizhdhin iyandrana thol.

Meaning

Every time I hold her, my drooping life grows fresh shoots again. This innocent girl's shoulders must be made of amrita (the drink that makes gods live forever).

Elementary

Think of a droopy plant that perks up the moment you give it water. When someone you love gives you a hug after a hard day, you can feel the same way. The lover says her hugs work like magic drink that makes him feel alive and fresh again every single time.

University

Uyir taḷirppa (உயிர் தளிர்ப்ப, ‘so that life puts forth tender shoots’) is a plant image: the lover’s withered life sprouts again, as Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah gloss it (vāṭiya uyir taḷirkka, ‘the wilted life sprouting’). Uṟutōṟu means ‘every time I come close’, and from this repeated reviving effect the lover infers that her shoulders are amiḻtiṉ iyaṉṟaṉa (அமிழ்தின் இயன்றன), ‘fashioned of amizhtu’, the nectar of immortality (Sanskrit amṛta). Pētai (பேதை) here means a simple, innocent young woman; it is also the name of the youngest of the seven traditional stages of womanhood, but the commentators read it in the general sense. Pope: ‘Ambrosia are the simple maiden’s arms; when I attain / Their touch, my withered life puts forth its buds again!’ Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In a famous Rig Vedic hymn the singers declare that they have drunk the Soma and become immortal, have come to the light and found the gods.
— Rig Veda 8.48.3 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The sacred drink Soma, identified with amrita, is praised as giving fresh life and immortality to those who taste it.
The Kural borrows exactly this idea of a life-giving nectar to describe the beloved’s touch.
Christianity
“As the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my beloved among the sons. I sat down under his shadow with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste.”
— Song of Solomon 2:3 (KJV) [4]
The bride finds rest and sweetness in her beloved, like shade and fruit from a tree.
As the Kural’s lover is refreshed by the beloved’s nectar, the bride is refreshed by her beloved’s sweetness.
Islam
When the Prophet came home trembling after the first revelation, he asked Khadijah to cover him; she wrapped him, comforted him and reassured him until his fear left him.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3 [5]
The loving presence of his wife restored the Prophet’s calm and strength at the hardest moment of his life.
Like the Kural, this story shows the beloved spouse’s nearness reviving a shaken spirit.
Judaism
“And Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah’s tent, and took Rebekah, and she became his wife; and he loved her. And Isaac was comforted for his mother.”
— Genesis 24:67 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Isaac, still grieving his mother, finds his grief eased by his love for Rebekah.
As in the Kural, love for the beloved brings a drooping spirit back to life.
Buddhism
“Having savored the taste of solitude and peace (of Nibbana), pain-free and stainless he becomes, drinking deep the taste of the bliss of the Truth.”
— Dhammapada 205 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes the peace of Nibbana as a taste one drinks deeply, leaving one free of pain.
Buddhism also speaks of a drink that renews life, but it finds that nectar in Nibbana, not in the beloved’s touch.
Jainism
“He will get rid of all misery which always afflicts mankind; recovered from the long illness, as it were, and glorious, he becomes infinitely happy, and obtains the (final) aim.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.110 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira describes the liberated soul as one who has recovered from a long illness and become infinitely happy.
Jainism also speaks of life restored, but it finds that revival in liberation from desire, not in the beloved’s embrace.
Sikhism
“The dried branches are made green again in an instant.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 191, Raag Gauri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gauri, Guru Arjan describes the Guru's grace bringing spiritually withered people back to life at once, like dry wood putting out green shoots.
In the Kural each touch of the beloved makes the lover's wilting life sprout again; Guru Arjan uses the same picture of the withered made green.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, when Zeus and Hera lie together on Mount Ida, the earth beneath them sends up fresh grass, dewy lotus, crocus and hyacinth, and a golden cloud covers them.
— Homer, Iliad 14.346-351 [10]
The union of the gods makes the very ground burst into new growth.
The Kural’s lover feels his life sprout new shoots in the embrace, as the earth blooms beneath the divine lovers.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus Socrates says that when the lover gazes on beauty, warmth flows into his soul, the stiffened roots of its wings soften, and the wings begin to swell and grow again.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251a-c (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato the sight of the beloved makes the soul’s withered wings sprout anew.
This is the Kural’s image of life putting forth fresh shoots in the beloved’s presence.

Real Life Example

Fanny Osbourne nurses Robert Louis Stevenson back from illness
1879–1880 · Monterey and San Francisco, California, United States
In August 1879 the Scottish writer Robert Louis Stevenson crossed the Atlantic and the American continent in cheap emigrant conditions to join Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne, the American woman he loved, arriving in Monterey thin and unwell. In early 1880 he fell gravely ill, and Fanny, who had divorced her first husband in December 1879, took him into her home and nursed him. They married in San Francisco on 19 May 1880, and she cared for his fragile health for the rest of his life.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The journey nearly ruined Stevenson's health, yet it gave him the wife who became his lifelong nurse and companion.
The kural says that every time the lover is close to his beloved, his wilting life puts out fresh shoots; Stevenson, worn down to skin and bone, was nursed back to life by the woman he had crossed the world to reach.
