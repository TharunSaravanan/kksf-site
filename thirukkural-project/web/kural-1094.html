Kural 1094

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

யான்நோக்கும் காலை நிலன்நோக்கும் நோக்காக்கால் தான்நோக்கி மெல்ல நகும்.
Yaannokkum kaalai nilannokkum nokkaakkaal thaannokki mella nagum.

Meaning

When I look at her, she looks at the ground. When I do not look, she looks at me and smiles softly to herself.

Elementary

Picture a shy kid in class. Whenever you turn to look, they quickly stare at their desk. But when you turn back to your book, you can feel them peeking at you with a small smile. That little game means they want to be your friend.

University

The couplet builds on a chiastic play of nōkku (நோக்கு, ‘look’): yān nōkkum kālai (when I look) she looks at nilan (நிலன், the ground); nōkkākkāl (when I do not look) she looks and mella nakum (மெல்ல நகும், smiles gently). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah take nakum as smiling or rejoicing to herself, and Kalaignar adds that this is a sign announcing her love. Commentators in the Parimelazhagar tradition treat the lowered gaze as nāṇam and the secret smile as the kurippu of consent. Pope: ‘I look on her: her eyes are on the ground the while: / I look away: she looks on me with timid smile.’ The two source sites agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When I was near, she could not look at me; / She smiled–but not to me–and half denied it.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act II (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
King Dushyanta tells his friend how the shy Shakuntala avoided his eyes yet smiled in a way that half gave her away.
Avoiding his gaze while smiling is precisely the Kural’s picture of the beloved who looks down when watched and smiles when unwatched.
Christianity
“Behold, he standeth behind our wall, he looketh forth at the windows, shewing himself through the lattice.”
— Song of Solomon 2:9 (KJV) [4]
The beloved peers through the window lattice, watching while only half seen.
Looking at the other from hiding is the game the Kural describes.
Islam
“And with them will be women limiting [their] glances, with large, [beautiful] eyes.”
— Qur’an 37:48 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises the modest companions of paradise for restraining their gaze.
Lowered and restrained eyes as a mark of modesty is the same virtue the Kural sees in the beloved who looks at the ground.
Judaism
“Then she fell on her face, and bowed down to the ground, and said unto him: ‘Why have I found favour in thy sight, that thou shouldest take cognizance of me, seeing I am a foreigner?’”
— Ruth 2:10 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Ruth bows to the ground before Boaz, wondering why he has noticed her kindly.
Ruth lowers her eyes to the ground when Boaz notices her, and their story too ends in marriage.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that two bright qualities protect the world: hiri (a sense of shame) and ottappa (moral dread), without which people would not know any restraint.
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.9 (Cariya Sutta) [7]
Modesty is honored in Buddhism as one of the guardians of the moral life.
The beloved’s lowered eyes show the same hiri the Buddha praises, even if the Kural’s setting is romantic.
Jainism
“Not to look at, nor to long for, not to think of, nor to praise, womankind: this is becoming the meditation of the noble ones.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
For the Jain monk, keeping the eyes from looking at women is part of the discipline of chastity.
Jainism also values the downcast eye, but as a monk’s renunciation, not as a shy sign of love.
Sikhism
“He watches over all, but none see Him. How wonderful this is!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 7, Japji Sahib (pauri 30), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak marvels that God looks on every creature while remaining unseen by them.
In the Kural the girl looks at the lover only when he is not looking; Guru Nanak's wonder is a Lord who always looks on us while we fail to see Him.
Greek Mythology
“And now both were fixing their eyes on the ground abashed, and again were throwing glances at each other, smiling with the light of love beneath their radiant brows.”
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.1022-1024 (tr. R. C. Seaton, 1912) [10]
At their secret meeting Medea and Jason look down in shyness, then steal smiling looks at each other.
Eyes on the ground, then a smiling glance: this is the Kural’s scene almost exactly.
Philosophy
Aristotle, discussing shame, cites the proverb that shame dwells in the eyes, which is why people feel it most before those who look at them.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.6, 1384a (tr. W. Rhys Roberts) [11]
For Aristotle, shame shows itself in how we meet or avoid another’s gaze.
The beloved who drops her eyes when looked at shows exactly this shame-in-the-eyes.

Real Life Example

Alexander Graham Bell and Mabel Hubbard
1873–1877 · Boston and Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States
Mabel Hubbard, who had been left deaf by scarlet fever at the age of five and had learned to lip-read, met Alexander Graham Bell in 1873 when she came to him to improve her speech. Her journal records that at first she both did and did not like him. Her feelings grew into admiration and love; they became engaged in 1875 and married on 11 July 1877 in Cambridge, Massachusetts.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Their long marriage, carried on through speech, lip-reading and letters, was a lifelong partnership.
The kural pictures a young woman whose love shows only in shy, hidden ways at first; Mabel's early mixed feelings slowly gave way to open love.
