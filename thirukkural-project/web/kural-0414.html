Kural 414

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

கற்றிலன் ஆயினும் கேட்க அஃதொருவற்கு ஒற்கத்தின் ஊற்றாந் துணை.
Katrilan aayinum ketka aqdhoruvarku Orkaththin ootraandh thunai.

Meaning

Even if you have not studied, listen to learned people. What you hear will hold you up like a walking stick when you are weak or in trouble.

Elementary

Maybe you never got to go to school or read many books. That’s okay — just listen to wise people! What you hear will be like a walking stick that holds you up when life gets hard.

University

Here Valluvar extends the chapter’s value to the unlettered: கற்றிலன் ஆயினும் (‘though he has not learned’) concedes lack of formal study, but கேட்க is an imperative — listening remains possible for everyone. ஒற்கம் means weakness, falling, or distress; ஊற்று is a prop or support (the same word recurs in 415 as ஊற்றுக்கோல், ‘supporting staff’). The kural thus democratises knowledge in an oral society: hearing is a route to wisdom for those without access to texts, and its value is practical — it sustains one when fortune slips.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna says there are others who, not knowing the paths of knowledge themselves, hear the teaching from others and worship accordingly; by devotion to what they have heard, they too cross beyond death.
— Bhagavad Gita 13.25 (13.26 in some editions) [4]
The Gita explicitly accommodates the untrained seeker who relies only on hearing from the wise, and promises that such hearing carries him through.
Close match: the unlearned who listens is supported and saved by what he hears.
Christianity
“Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled; and they took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus.”
— Acts 4:13 (KJV) [5]
On trial before the council, two fishermen without formal schooling speak with confidence; their strength came from having heard Jesus.
Unlearned yet sustained in adversity by what they had heard — a vivid enactment of the Kural.
Islam
The Prophet prayed for God to brighten the face of one who hears a saying, memorises it and conveys it — for many a carrier of knowledge is not himself learned, and many carry it to one who understands it better.
— Sunan Abi Dawud 3660; Jami‘ at-Tirmidhi 2656 [6]
The hadith honours the listener-transmitter even if he is not a scholar, recognising hearing as a path to knowledge open to all.
Both texts affirm that the unlearned can still become possessors of knowledge through hearing.
Judaism
“Then Samuel answered, Speak; for thy servant heareth.”
— 1 Samuel 3:7–10 (KJV) [5]
The boy Samuel, of whom the text says he ‘did not yet know the LORD’ (3:7), is taught by Eli simply to listen; his hearing makes him a prophet who later sustains Israel in crisis.
Lack of learning is no barrier: Samuel begins with nothing but a listening ear, which becomes his lifelong support.
Buddhism
Paṭācārā, having lost her husband, children, parents and brother in quick succession, wanders half-mad with grief; on hearing the Buddha’s words she regains her mind and later becomes an arahant and teacher.
— Dhammapada 113, 288–289 with commentary (Paṭācārā story) [7]
The commentary on the Dhammapada tells how a woman with no learning, in the depth of misfortune, was restored by listening to the Buddha.
Hearing as ஒற்கத்தின் ஊற்று — the prop that held her up when she had fallen.
Jainism
Rauhineya, a thief, had been told by his dying father never to listen to Mahavira. Passing the assembly with his ears covered, he trod on a thorn, took a hand away to pull it out, and briefly heard Mahavira describing the gods: their feet do not touch the ground, their eyes do not blink, they do not sweat, and their garlands do not wither. Later, minister Abhayakumara tried to trick him into confessing by staging a fake heaven. The few words Rauhineya had overheard let him see through the trap, and he went on to become Mahavira's disciple.
— Hemacandra, Trisastisalakapurusacaritra, Parva X, ch. 11, 'Story of Rauhineya' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In this Jain story a few words, heard unwillingly by an unlettered man, saved him in his worst crisis and changed his life.
Valluvar urges even someone who has never studied to listen, because what he hears will be a staff to lean on in adversity. Rauhineya's few overheard words served him in exactly that way.
Sikhism
“Listening-even the blind find the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib (pauri 11), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the eleventh pauri of Japji Sahib Guru Nanak says that listening lets even those without sight, read by commentators as the spiritually ignorant, find their way.
The Kural tells the unlettered to listen anyway, since hearing will be their support in hardship; Japji promises that listening guides even the blind.
Greek Mythology
Lost and unable to find his way home, Odysseus is sent by Circe to the underworld to hear the prophet Tiresias, whose words guide him through the dangers that follow.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.490–495; 11.90–137 [10]
Odysseus, for all his cunning, lacks the knowledge he needs; he must go and listen to one who knows.
Hearing the wise supplies what one’s own learning lacks, and supports one through adversity.
Philosophy
In the myth of Theuth, King Thamus warns that writing will give pupils the appearance of wisdom without its reality, since they will have ‘heard many things without instruction’; Socrates argues that living speech exchanged with a knowledgeable teacher is superior to written texts.
— Plato, Phaedrus 274c–276a [11]
Plato privileges oral teaching over book-learning as the true vehicle of understanding.
Valluvar likewise reassures the one without book-learning that listening to the learned is itself a reliable path to wisdom.

Real Life Example

Bookbinder's apprentice Michael Faraday attends Humphry Davy's lectures
1812-1813 · Royal Institution, London, England
Michael Faraday, who had been apprenticed to a London bookbinder at fourteen and had no university education, was given a ticket in 1812 to hear Humphry Davy's chemistry lectures at the Royal Institution. He took careful notes, bound them into a volume, and presented them to Davy in the hope of scientific work. On 1 March 1813 he was appointed laboratory assistant at the Royal Institution, the beginning of a career that made him one of the greatest experimental scientists of the nineteenth century.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A few lectures heard by a young tradesman opened the way to discoveries in electricity and magnetism that shaped the modern world.
The kural urges those who have not studied to listen to the learned, since hearing will be a staff to them in adversity, and Faraday, without formal schooling, rose from the bookbinder's bench by listening to Davy.
