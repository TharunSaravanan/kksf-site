Kural 1205

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

தம்நெஞ்சத்து எம்மைக் கடிகொண்டார் நாணார்கொல் எம்நெஞ்சத்து ஓவா வரல்.
Thamnenjaththu emmaik katikondaar naanaarkol emnenjaththu ovaa varal.

Meaning

He keeps me out of his own heart. Is he not ashamed to keep coming into mine again and again, without a break?

Elementary

Imagine a friend who never lets you into their clubhouse, but keeps walking into yours whenever they like! The lady in this poem jokes that her husband does that with hearts: he keeps visiting her thoughts all day, but she wonders if he lets her into his.

University

The couplet turns the previous question into a playful accusation. Kaṭi koṇṭār means ‘those who have set a guard against’ (kaṭi, ‘watch, prohibition’): Mu. Varadarasanar glosses ‘he who has kept guard in his heart so that we may not enter’, and Pope renders ‘Me from his heart he jealously excludes: / Hath he no shame who ceaseless on my heart intrudes?’ Ōvā varal is ‘coming without ceasing’, and nāṇār-kol asks ‘is he not ashamed?’, invoking nāṇ, the sense of shame and propriety prized in the Kural. The reproach is affectionate: his constant ‘visits’ are simply her inability to stop thinking of him. One modern gloss (Sivayogi Sivakumar on ytamizh.com) reads kaṭi as ‘scold’, but the guarding sense is standard. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhramara Gita, a gopi addresses a bee she takes for Krishna’s messenger, scolding Krishna for forgetting the women of Vraja after leaving them, while confessing that they cannot give up talking and thinking of him.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47.12-21, Bhramara Gita (paraphrase; cf. vedabase.io) [3]
The gopis rebuke Krishna for his indifference even as they admit he fills their minds.
The gopi’s complaint is the Kural’s: he shuts us out of his heart, yet will not leave ours.
Christianity
“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone: my soul failed when he spake: I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Solomon 5:6 (KJV) [4]
The bride finds her beloved withdrawn and unresponsive, though her whole soul goes out to him.
As in the Kural, the beloved seems closed to her while she remains wholly open and occupied with him.
Islam
Ibn Hazm devotes chapters of The Ring of the Dove to the pain of loving one who turns away, describing lovers whose thoughts are never free of a beloved who keeps them at a distance.
— Ibn Hazm, The Ring of the Dove (Tawq al-Hamama), chapters on aversion and separation (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Arberry) [5]
The Andalusian scholar analyses one-sided love and the beloved’s aloofness as recognised stages of love.
The Kural’s heroine, barred from his heart but haunted by him in hers, is a case Ibn Hazm describes.
Judaism
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth; I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Songs 3:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved seeks her lover through the night and cannot find him.
Her lover fills her nights as the Kural’s husband fills his wife’s heart, yet he is nowhere to be reached.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that when a man who longs for sensual objects is frustrated in his desire, he is afflicted as if pierced by an arrow.
— Sutta Nipata 4.1, Kama Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Desire that is not met in return becomes a wound that keeps returning to the mind.
The beloved who keeps ‘coming’ into her heart is, in Buddhist terms, the dart of unfulfilled longing.
Jainism
Prince Pavananjaya, offended on the eve of his marriage, neglects his bride Anjana for twenty-two years, never speaking to her, while she remains devoted to him in thought, until he finally realises her grief and returns to her.
— Hemachandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 7, story of Anjana and Pavananjaya (paraphrase; cf. tr. Helen M. Johnson); also in Vimalasuri’s Paumacariya [8]
The Jain Ramayana tradition tells of a wife wholly given in heart to a husband who has shut her out of his.
Anjana lives the Kural’s situation exactly: excluded from his heart, while he never leaves hers.
Sikhism
“I do not forget the Lord in my mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1120, Raag Kaydaaraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan speaks of a mind in which the Lord is always present and never forgotten.
The Kural's wife teases that her beloved, who has barred her from his own heart, keeps coming into hers without a pause; Gurbani likewise describes a Beloved who never leaves the devotee's mind.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s letter, Phyllis writes to Demophoon, who promised to return to her within a month and has not come, reproaching him for forgetting her while she counts the days and watches the sea for his sails.
— Ovid, Heroides 2 (Phyllis to Demophoon) (paraphrase) [10]
Phyllis is consumed by thought of a lover who seems to have put her out of his mind.
She makes the Kural’s complaint: he has no room for me, yet I can think of nothing but him.
Philosophy
Lucretius observes that even when the one you love is absent, images of the beloved are still present and the sweet name keeps sounding in your ears.
— Lucretius, On the Nature of Things 4.1061-1062 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.E. Leonard) [11]
For Lucretius the beloved’s image keeps returning to the mind unbidden, which is why love is hard to escape.
The Kural’s husband who ‘comes ceaselessly’ into his wife’s heart is Lucretius’s haunting image of the absent beloved.

Real Life Example

Frida Kahlo paints Diego on My Mind
1943 · Mexico
The painter Frida Kahlo married the muralist Diego Rivera in 1929; they divorced in late 1939 and remarried in 1940. In 1943 she painted Self-Portrait as Tehuana, also titled Diego on My Mind, a self-portrait whose title names Rivera as the one constantly in her thoughts.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The painting turns a marriage marked by separation and return into an image of a woman whose mind is occupied by one man.
The kural's heroine complains that the man who has shut her out keeps walking into her heart without pause, and Kahlo's Diego on My Mind, painted after he had once divorced her, shows him lodged in her thoughts.
