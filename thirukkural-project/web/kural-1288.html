Kural 1288

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

இளித்தக்க இன்னா செயினும் களித்தார்க்குக் கள்ளற்றே கள்வநின் மார்பு.
Iliththakka innaa seyinum kaliththaarkkuk kallatrae kalvanin maarbu.

Meaning

You thief! Even though you do hurtful, shameful things to me, your chest draws me the way strong drink draws someone who craves it.

Elementary

Some people keep going back to something even when it is bad for them, because they just can’t resist it. The wife playfully calls her husband a ‘thief’ who stole her heart: even when he hurts her by being away, she can’t stop wanting to be close to him.

University

The wife addresses her husband as kaḷva (‘thief’, a term of endearment for one who has stolen her heart, with a hint of reproach). Iḷittakka iṉṉā ceyiṉum means ‘though it does painful things fit to bring disgrace’; kaḷittārkku kaḷ aṟṟē, ‘like toddy to those who have revelled in it’; niṉ mārpu, ‘your breast’, i.e. his embrace. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss it as toddy that, despite the shame it brings, makes drinkers crave it more and more; Kalaignar renders kaḷva as ‘thief who stole my heart’; Solomon Pappaiah uses ‘deceiver’ and adds that the husband reads her thoughts in her glance and comforts her, a bridge to 1290. The wine comparison returns from 1281 but now stresses compulsive attraction despite harm, recalling the ruinous drinker of chapter 93. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Krishna comes to Radha at dawn bearing the marks of a night spent elsewhere, she rebukes him bitterly as a deceiver, yet her love for him remains undiminished.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 8 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s Radha is wounded by Krishna but cannot stop loving him.
The Kural’s wife likewise calls her husband a ‘thief’ yet craves his embrace like a drinker his wine.
Christianity
“They have stricken me, shalt thou say, and I was not sick; they have beaten me, and I felt it not: when shall I awake? I will seek it yet again.”
— Proverbs 23:35 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs describes the drunkard who, though harmed by wine, resolves to seek it again as soon as he wakes.
The Kural’s simile is the same: the drinker returns to wine despite the shame and pain, as the wife to her husband’s embrace.
Islam
“They ask you about wine and gambling. Say, ‘In them is great sin and [yet, some] benefit for people. But their sin is greater than their benefit.’”
— Qur’an 2:219 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an acknowledges that wine has some appeal but declares its harm the greater.
The Kural’s comparison rests on wine’s pull despite its harm, which the Qur’an names directly.
Judaism
“Go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend and an adulteress, even as the LORD loveth the children of Israel, though they turn unto other gods, and love cakes of raisins.”
— Hosea 3:1 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Hosea is told to keep loving a wife who has wronged him, as God keeps loving Israel.
The Kural’s wife keeps loving a husband who causes her pain; Hosea models love that persists despite injury.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares a man given to sensual pleasure to a leper who scratches his sores and cauterises them over a fire, finding relief in the very thing that worsens his wounds.
— Majjhima Nikaya 75, Magandiya Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Magandiya Sutta, sensual craving is pictured as finding pleasure in what actually harms.
The Kural’s drinker who craves wine despite its shame is the same pattern the Buddha describes.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of ‘colours,’ will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana compares one enslaved by attraction to a moth drawn into the flame.
The Kural’s wife is drawn to the embrace that pains her as a drinker to wine; the Jain moth image captures the same fatal attraction.
Sikhism
“Even if You give me hunger, I will still feel satisfied; I am joyful, even in the midst of sorrow.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 757, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that whatever the Beloved gives, even hunger and sorrow, he accepts with joy.
The Kural's wife says her husband's embrace still draws her like drink draws the drinker, even though it brings her pain; Guru Ram Das likewise clings to the Beloved even when it brings hardship.
Greek Mythology
Helen scorns Paris after his defeat by Menelaus, but when Aphrodite insists, she goes to him nonetheless, unable to resist the goddess of desire.
— Homer, Iliad 3.383-447 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
In the Iliad, Helen despises Paris’s weakness yet is drawn back to him by Aphrodite’s power.
The Kural’s wife likewise reproaches the ‘thief’ yet cannot keep from his embrace.
Philosophy
Plato tells of Leontius, who, angry at himself, still ran to gaze at corpses by the city wall, crying to his eyes to take their fill of the sight.
— Plato, Republic IV, 439e-440a (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
The Republic uses the story to show desire overpowering the part of the soul that disapproves.
The Kural’s wife, like Leontius, is drawn irresistibly to what she half-reproaches.

Real Life Example

Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera remarry in San Francisco
8 December 1940 · San Francisco, California, United States
The Mexican painters Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera married in 1929, but their marriage was strained by a series of extramarital affairs, notably Rivera’s with Kahlo’s younger sister, and they divorced in 1939. In late 1940 Kahlo travelled to San Francisco, where Rivera was working for the Golden Gate International Exposition, and the two reconciled. They remarried there on 8 December 1940, barely a year after their divorce.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14] [15]
Despite the real and public hurt Rivera had caused her, Kahlo could not stay away from him.
The kural’s wife calls her husband a thief and says that though he brings her shameful pain, his embrace is to her what wine is to the drinker who keeps craving more, which fits Kahlo’s return to Rivera after all he had done.
