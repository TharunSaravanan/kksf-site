Kural 628

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

இன்பம் விழையான் இடும்பை இயல்பென்பான் துன்பம் உறுதல் இலன்.
Inpam vizhaiyaan itumpai iyalpenpaan Thunpam urudhal ilan.

Meaning

A person who does not chase after pleasure, and who accepts that hard times are a normal part of life, never really falls into misery.

Elementary

If you expect every day to be a party, a rainy day feels awful. But if you know that some days are rainy and that’s normal, you just grab an umbrella. The Kural says people who don’t demand fun all the time don’t get crushed when hard times come.

University

The couplet pairs two participial nouns, iṉpam viḻaiyāṉ, ‘one who does not crave pleasure’, and iṭumpai iyalpeṉpāṉ, ‘one who says hardship is natural (iyalpu)’, and concludes that such a person ‘has no falling into sorrow’ (tuṉpam uṟutal ilaṉ). Solomon Pappaiah restricts the pleasure in question to bodily pleasure, in line with 627’s focus on the body, while Kalaignar reads it more broadly as not roaming in search of enjoyment. The shift from iṭumpai (hardship, the event) to tuṉpam (suffering, the experience) is significant: hardship still comes, but it no longer becomes suffering. The text is the same in both sources, and Pope renders it ‘He seeks not joy, to sorrow man is born, he knows’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One whose mind remains undisturbed amidst misery, who does not crave for pleasure, and who is free from attachment, fear, and anger, is called a sage of steady wisdom.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna defines the sthita-prajña, the person of steady wisdom, as one who neither craves pleasure nor is shaken by misery.
The Gita’s two conditions, no craving for pleasure and no disturbance in misery, are the two halves of this Kural.
Christianity
“These things I have spoken unto you, that in me ye might have peace. In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world.”
— John 16:33 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his disciples to expect tribulation as a normal part of life in the world, yet to stay cheerful.
Jesus’ ‘ye shall have tribulation’ is the Kural’s iṭumpai iyalpu, and his ‘be of good cheer’ its promise that such a person is not undone.
Islam
“Do the people think that they will be left to say, ‘We believe’ and they will not be tried?”
— Qur’an 29:2 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that trial is the normal, expected lot of anyone who claims faith.
Like the Kural, the verse teaches that hardship is part of life’s nature, so no one should be shocked into despair by it.
Judaism
“One is obligated to recite a blessing for the bad that befalls him just as he recites a blessing for the good that befalls him.”
— Mishnah Berakhot 9:5 (William Davidson Edition, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah requires Jews to bless God for misfortune as well as for good fortune, treating both as part of life’s ordinary course.
Blessing the bad as one blesses the good is a ritual form of the Kural’s acceptance of hardship as natural.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha traces grief back to attachment to what is pleasing; without that attachment, grief has no root.
The Kural says the one who does not crave pleasure does not fall into sorrow; the Dhammapada gives the reason, since sorrow grows from craving.
Jainism
“Not resentful, having few wants, begging from strangers, and not being dainty, a wise man should not long for pleasant things, nor be sorry afterwards (for not having got them).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Under the trouble of honour and respectful treatment, the monk is told not to crave pleasant things and not to regret their absence.
The Kural describes one who does not chase pleasure and takes hardship as natural, and so never sinks into misery; the Jain verse prescribes both halves, not longing for pleasant things and not grieving at their lack.
Sikhism
“Suffering is the medicine, and pleasure the disease, because where there is pleasure, there is no desire for God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak turns the usual valuation upside down: hardship heals and comfort sickens, because comfort makes a person forget God.
The Kural praises the one who does not chase pleasure and accepts hardship as natural; Guru Nanak goes further and calls hardship the medicine and pleasure the disease.
Greek Mythology
Offered ease and immortality on Calypso’s island, Odysseus chooses instead the hard road home to Ithaca, saying that if some god wrecks him on the sea he will bear it, since he has already endured much on land and sea and can add this to the rest.
— Homer, Odyssey 5.203-224 [10]
Odysseus turns down endless pleasure and treats further hardship as simply part of his lot.
He is the Kural’s man who does not crave pleasure and calls hardship natural, and so is not broken by it.
Philosophy
“Nothing happens to any man which he is not formed by nature to bear.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.18 (tr. George Long) [11]
Marcus Aurelius reminds himself that whatever befalls a human being is within human nature’s capacity to endure.
The Kural’s view that hardship is iyalpu, natural to us, is Marcus’s view that we are formed by nature to bear it.

Real Life Example

The Zen poet Ryokan chooses a life of poverty in a mountain hut
c. 1790s-1831 · Echigo province (Niigata), Japan
Ryokan (1758-1831), a village headman's son who became a Zen Buddhist priest, never took charge of a temple. After years as a wandering mendicant he returned to his native Echigo province, where he lived mostly as a hermit in a small mountain hut, begging for alms. He is remembered as one of the great poets and calligraphers of the Edo period, and he often wrote with twigs or practised brushstrokes in the air when he could not afford brushes or paper.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ryokan accepted hunger, cold and poverty as the ordinary conditions of his chosen life, and his poetry and calligraphy came out of that simplicity rather than in spite of it.
The kural says one who does not chase pleasure and accepts hardship as natural never falls into real misery, and Ryokan, who sought no comfort and lived on alms, is remembered for serenity rather than suffering.
