Kural 854

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இன்பத்துள் இன்பம் பயக்கும் இகலென்னும் துன்பத்துள் துன்பங் கெடின்.
Inbaththul inbam payakkum ikalennum thunbaththul thunbang kedin.

Meaning

When hostility, the worst of all pains, is gone, the greatest of all joys comes.

Elementary

Have you ever made up with a friend after a big fight? That warm, happy feeling afterwards is one of the best feelings there is. The poet says being angry at someone is the worst kind of unhappy, so ending it brings the best kind of happy.

University

The couplet is a chiasmus of superlatives: inbaththul inbam (இன்பத்துள் இன்பம், joy among joys) answers thunbaththul thunbam (துன்பத்துள் துன்பம், sorrow among sorrows). The verb payakkum (பயக்கும், yields, produces) makes joy the direct fruit of hostility’s destruction (kedin, கெடின், ‘if it perishes’). Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read it straightforwardly as ‘the greatest misery gone, the greatest delight comes’. The same formula ‘துன்பத்துள் துன்பம் கெடின்’ appears in Kural 369 on desire (avaa), so Valluvar ranks hostility alongside craving as the chief sources of suffering. Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not connected with the Supreme ... can have neither transcendental intelligence nor a steady mind, without which there is no possibility of peace. And how can there be any happiness without peace?”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.66 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna argues that real happiness depends on inner peace.
The Kural says the same: once the turmoil of hostility ends, the greatest joy follows.
Christianity
“Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God.”
— Matthew 5:9 (KJV) [4]
Jesus declares a special blessedness for those who end enmity.
Both promise the highest happiness to those who end hostility.
Islam
“Do not hate one another, and do not be jealous of one another, and do not desert each other, and O, Allah's worshipers! Be brothers.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6065 [5]
The Prophet forbids mutual hatred and calls believers to live as brothers.
Brotherhood in place of hatred is the joy the Kural describes.
Judaism
“Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!”
— Psalm 133:1 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
The Psalmist celebrates the sweetness of people living together without strife.
This is the ‘joy among joys’ that follows when hostility is gone.
Buddhism
“Happy indeed we live, friendly amidst the hostile. Amidst hostile men we dwell free from hatred.”
— Dhammapada 197 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha describes the happiness of those who stay free of hatred even among enemies.
Both texts tie the highest happiness to freedom from hostility.
Jainism
“By begging forgiveness he obtains happiness of mind; thereby he acquires a kind disposition towards all kinds of living beings; by this kind disposition he obtains purity of character and freedom from fear.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana traces a chain of fruits from the practice of asking forgiveness (kṣamāpana): first happiness of mind, then goodwill toward all beings, then purity and freedom from fear.
Valluvar says that once hostility, the sorrow of sorrows, is destroyed, it yields the joy of joys, and the Jain text likewise makes happiness of mind the first fruit of ending hostility through forgiveness.
Sikhism
“Do not harbor evil intentions against others in your mind, and you shall not be troubled, O Siblings of Destiny, O friends.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 386, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan teaches that whoever keeps ill will toward others out of the mind is spared trouble in his own life.
The Kural says that destroying hostility, the worst of sorrows, yields the greatest joy; Guru Arjan likewise links the absence of ill will with freedom from trouble.
Greek Mythology
At the end of the Odyssey, Athena halts the feud between Odysseus and the kinsmen of the slain suitors, and Zeus wills that they make a solemn peace so that friendship, wealth and peace may return to Ithaca.
— Homer, Odyssey 24.472-548 [9]
The epic ends not with victory but with the end of a blood feud and the return of prosperity.
The poem’s happy ending is precisely the end of hostility the Kural praises.
Philosophy
Spinoza writes that one who seeks to overcome hatred with love fights joyfully and confidently, while one who avenges wrongs with reciprocal hatred lives in misery.
— Spinoza, Ethics IV, Prop. 46 and Scholium [10]
For Spinoza, answering hatred with love is itself a source of joy.
The Kural likewise links ending hostility to the highest delight.

Real Life Example

The Berlin Wall opens and Berliners celebrate
9 November 1989 · Berlin, Germany
On the evening of 9 November 1989 an unclear announcement of new travel rules by East German official Gunter Schabowski sent thousands of East Berliners to the border crossings. At Bornholmer Strasse the overwhelmed guards opened the checkpoint, and people poured through to be greeted by West Berliners with flowers and champagne, while others climbed onto the Wall and began chipping pieces from it. More than two million East Berliners visited West Berlin that weekend.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The opening of the Wall ended nearly three decades of division in Berlin and opened the way to German reunification in 1990.
The kural says that when hostility, the worst of miseries, is destroyed it yields the greatest of joys, and the night the symbol of Cold War enmity was breached became one of the most joyful public celebrations of the century.
