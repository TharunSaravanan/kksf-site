Kural 693

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

போற்றின் அரியவை போற்றல் கடுத்தபின் தேற்றுதல் யார்க்கும் அரிது.
Potrin ariyavai potral kaduththapin Thetrudhal yaarkkum aridhu.

Meaning

If you want to stay safe, avoid serious mistakes. Once the king becomes suspicious of you, no one can make him trust you again.

Elementary

A glass vase with a crack can be glued, but you can always see the crack. Trust works the same way. It is much easier to be careful and never break it than to try to fix it after someone starts to doubt you.

University

The first line plays on potru (to guard, to preserve): potrin (if you would guard [yourself]) and potral (guard against). Ariyavai are, in Parimelazhagar’s gloss, grave faults the king will find hard to pardon. The key word is kaduththal, to suspect or to be incensed, and the second line states the psychology bluntly: once suspicion has formed, thetrudhal (reassuring or convincing) is difficult for anyone (yaarkkum). The Kural’s wider theory of trust helps here. Chapter 51 (Therindhu Thelidhal) warns the king against doubting a servant he has tested, so this couplet gives the courtier’s side of the same danger. The sandhi text in older editions runs “போற்றி னரியவை” and “றேற்றுதல் யார்க்கு மரிது.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Panchatantra’s first book, the jackal Damanaka poisons the friendship between the lion-king Pingalaka and his trusted minister, the bull Sanjivaka, by whispering suspicions to each of them. Once the lion suspects the bull, nothing can undo it, and Sanjivaka is killed.
— Panchatantra, Book I (Mitrabheda), frame story of Pingalaka and Sanjivaka [3]
The frame story of ‘The Loss of Friends’ shows how quickly a ruler’s suspicion, once planted, destroys even a favoured servant.
This is the Kural’s warning played out: after suspicion arises, reassurance is almost impossible.
Christianity
“Then the presidents and princes sought to find occasion against Daniel concerning the kingdom; but they could find none occasion nor fault; forasmuch as he was faithful, neither was there any error or fault found in him.”
— Daniel 6:4 (KJV) [4]
At the Persian court, Daniel’s jealous rivals search for a fault to use against him and cannot find one, because he has been careful and faithful.
Daniel lives out the Kural’s first line: guard against faults so that suspicion has nothing to hold on to.
Islam
“And the king said, ‘Bring him to me.’ But when the messenger came to him, [Joseph] said, ‘Return to your master and ask him what is the case of the women who cut their hands. Indeed, my Lord is Knowing of their plan.’”
— Quran 12:50 (Saheeh International) [5]
Joseph has been imprisoned under suspicion. When the king summons him, he refuses to leave until the king has formally investigated the accusation and cleared his name.
Joseph knows the Kural’s truth: suspicion at court does not fade by itself, so a full clearing is needed before he can safely serve.
Judaism
“If the spirit of the ruler rise up against thee, leave not thy place; for yielding pacifieth great offences.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:4 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet advises that when a ruler turns angry with you, you should stay calm at your post and yield, because only calm submission soothes great offences.
Both texts see how hard it is to recover once a ruler’s mind turns against you. Kohelet gives the remedy, the Kural the prevention.
Buddhism
“Let not the wise man, thinking himself to be held in honour, ever venture to speak roughly to the suspicious king.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 156 [6]
Vidhura warns that kings are naturally suspicious, so a courtier must never give offence, even when he feels secure.
Both texts treat the king’s suspicion as a hazard that the courtier must avoid provoking in the first place.
Jainism
“A weak point in one's lord, even though small, must be guarded against. By means of a hole even small, water destroys a dam.”
— Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra 1.5, Part 1, 'Negotiations with Bahubali' (Hemachandra, tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS 1931) [7]
Suvega warns Bahubali that failing to wait on his elder brother leaves room for informers to poison the king's mind, and that even a small breach in the lord's trust must be guarded against.
The Kural warns that once a king grows suspicious no one can calm him; the Jain narrative likewise says even a small fault in the ruler's eyes must be guarded against before it bursts the dam.
Sikhism
“Why do you do such evil deeds, that you shall have to suffer so? Do not do any evil at all; look ahead to the future with foresight.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (Pauri 21), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa ki Vaar Guru Nanak warns against any deed whose consequences one will later have to bear, urging foresight before acting.
The Kural advises guarding against grave faults because a ruler's suspicion, once roused, cannot be undone; Guru Nanak likewise counsels avoiding wrong entirely by looking ahead to its consequences.
Greek Mythology
At the court of King Proetus, the queen falsely accuses the young hero Bellerophon. Proetus believes her and sends Bellerophon away with a sealed tablet asking the king of Lycia to kill him. Bellerophon has to survive monsters and ambushes before he is trusted again.
— Homer, Iliad 6.155-195 (Bellerophon and Proetus) [9]
Homer’s story shows how a single suspicion at court can turn a king against a guest and set off a long ordeal.
Proetus’s suspicion, once formed, could not be talked away, which is exactly the Kural’s warning.
Philosophy
Seneca tells of a man who had grown old in the service of kings. Asked how he had managed something so rare at court, he replied that it was by accepting injuries and thanking the king for them.
— Seneca, De Ira (On Anger) 2.33 [10]
The Roman Stoic uses the anecdote to show how precarious life at a royal court is, where one misstep or show of resentment can be fatal.
Both texts assume that a ruler’s displeasure, once aroused, is almost impossible to undo, so survival means never provoking it.

Real Life Example

Naser al-Din Shah turns against his reforming minister Amir Kabir
1851-1852 · Tehran and Kashan, Iran
Mirza Taqi Khan, known as Amir Kabir, helped secure the throne for the young Naser al-Din Shah in 1848 and as chief minister launched sweeping reforms, including the founding of the Dar al-Fonun college. His reforms antagonized excluded notables, who formed a coalition against him in which the queen mother was active, and she convinced the young shah that the minister wanted to usurp the throne. In the autumn of 1851 the shah dismissed him and exiled him to Kashan, where he was put to death on the shah's orders in January 1852.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The minister's record of service and even his marriage to the shah's sister could not undo the suspicion once it had been planted in the ruler's mind.
The kural warns that once a king has grown suspicious, no one can set his mind at rest, and no past loyalty saved Amir Kabir once the shah came to believe he sought the throne.
