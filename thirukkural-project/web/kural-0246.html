Kural 246

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

பொருள்நீங்கிப் பொச்சாந்தார் என்பர் அருள்நீங்கி அல்லவை செய்தொழுகு வார்.
Porulneengip pochchaandhaar enbar arulneengi, Allavai seidhozhugu vaar.

Meaning

Wise people say that those who give up kindness and live by doing wrong have lost sight of life's real purpose and forgotten the pain they once suffered.

Elementary

Imagine someone who once got badly hurt, forgets how much it hurt, and then starts hurting others. Valluvar says people who throw away kindness and do mean things have forgotten what really matters, as if they lost the map of their own life.

University

The kural turns on the word porul, which can mean ‘wealth’, ‘meaning’ or ‘the real object (of life)’. Varadarasan and Pulavar Kesikan read porul neengi as ‘having left the true end, that is, virtue’, and pochchaandhaar as ‘those who have forgotten their life’s purpose’. Parimelazhagar reads it karmically: such people neglected virtue in an earlier life and have now forgotten the sufferings it brought. Solomon Pappaiah combines the two senses: they lose their wealth and also forget the pain they once endured. The unifying idea is moral amnesia. Cruelty is presented less as malice than as a forgetting, of one’s purpose and of the fellow-feeling one’s own suffering should have taught. This anticipates the reciprocal logic of Kural 250.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Parth, the qualities of those who possess a demoniac nature are hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness, and ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.4 (trans. Mukundananda) [3]
The Gita’s list of demonic qualities includes pāruṣya (harshness) and ends with ajñāna (ignorance).
Like the kural, the Gita pairs pitilessness with a kind of ignorance or forgetting of the real goal.
Christianity
“For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Matthew 16:26 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that someone can gain everything while losing the one thing that gives life its worth.
Both texts describe losing the true porul, life’s real object, while pursuing the wrong things.
Islam
“A woman entered the (Hell) Fire because of a cat which she had tied, neither giving it food nor setting it free to eat from the vermin of the earth.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3318 [5]
A single sustained act of cruelty to a helpless animal was enough to ruin the woman.
Abandoning compassion and persisting in cruelty (seidhozhugu, ‘continually doing’) means losing the true gain of life.
Judaism
“The merciful man doeth good to his own soul: but he that is cruel troubleth his own flesh.”
— Proverbs 11:17 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs presents cruelty as self-harm and mercy as self-benefit.
The cruel person in the kural has likewise lost their own true good; they are their own chief victim.
Buddhism
“He who inflicts violence on those who are unarmed, and offends those who are inoffensive, will soon come upon one of these ten states”, among them “loss of wealth, or houses destroyed by ravaging fire”.”
— Dhammapada 137-140 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada lists the calamities that follow cruelty toward the harmless, ending with rebirth in hell.
Includes the literal loss of porul (wealth), matching the double meaning in the kural.
Jainism
“A cut-throat enemy will not do him such harm as his own perversity will do him; the man without pity will feel repentance in the hour of death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The sage warns that a man without pity is ruined by his own perversity more surely than by any enemy, and will know it only at death.
Valluvar says those who abandon compassion and do wrong have lost sight of life's real purpose; the Uttaradhyayana likewise says the pitiless man is ruined by his own perversity and learns too late what he has lost.
Sikhism
“The Creation is in the Creator, and the Creator is in the Creation, totally pervading and permeating all places.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1350, Raag Prabhaatee, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Kabir teaches that God dwells in all creatures and all creatures dwell in God.
The Kural says those who abandon compassion and do wrong have lost sight of life's true purpose; Kabir supplies the Sikh reason such cruelty is a forgetting, since the one harmed carries the same divine presence.
Greek Mythology
“Even so has Achilles flung aside all pity, and all that conscience which at once so greatly banes yet greatly boons him that will heed it.”
— Homer, Iliad 24 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Apollo rebukes the gods for tolerating Achilles, who drags Hector’s corpse daily and ‘knows neither right nor ruth’.
Achilles, having cast off pity and persisting in outrage, has lost his true self, until Priam reminds him of his father (see Kural 250).
Philosophy
“And, generally, we feel pity whenever we are in the condition of remembering that similar misfortunes have happened to us or ours, or expecting them to happen in the future.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric, Book II, ch. 8 (1386a) (tr. W. Rhys Roberts) [10]
In his analysis of the emotions, Aristotle says pity depends on remembering or expecting suffering of one’s own, so those who think themselves beyond harm feel little of it.
This gives the psychology behind the Kural’s charge: those who drop compassion have forgotten what they themselves have suffered.

Real Life Example

Massachusetts Bay banishes Roger Williams
October 1635 - 1636 · Massachusetts Bay Colony and Providence, New England
The Puritan colony of Massachusetts Bay, founded by people who had left England in search of freedom to worship in their own way, banished the minister Roger Williams in October 1635 for his dangerous opinions, including his view that magistrates had no right to interfere in matters of religion. Williams fled and in 1636 founded Providence on land from the Narragansett, as a refuge for people troubled in conscience. It became a haven for Baptists, Quakers, Jews and other dissenters.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A community shaped by the experience of religious pressure imposed the same pressure on a dissenter in its midst, while the man it expelled built a refuge for others.
The kural says those who abandon compassion have forgotten what they themselves suffered, and the colony's leaders treated a dissenter as they had once complained of being treated.
