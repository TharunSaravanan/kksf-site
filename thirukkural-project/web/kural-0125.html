Kural 125

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

எல்லார்க்கும் நன்றாம் பணிதல் அவருள்ளும் செல்வர்க்கே செல்வம் தகைத்து.
Ellaarkkum nandraam panidhal avarullum Selvarkke selvam thakaiththu.

Meaning

Being humble is good for everyone. But in rich people, humility shines like a second kind of riches.

Elementary

Imagine a kid with the coolest new bike who still lets others ride it and never brags. Everyone likes that kid even more. Being humble when you have a lot is like having a bonus treasure on top of everything else.

University

Panidhal (‘bowing, humility’) makes explicit the social side of adakkam. Parimelazhagar explains why the rich are singled out: learning and noble birth already tend to restrain a person, so humility in the learned or well-born does not stand out, but wealth naturally breeds pride (perumitham). Humility in the rich is therefore especially striking, a ‘wealth added to wealth’ (selvam thakaiththu). He also points out that couplets 121–125 treat restraint in general, while 126 turns to bodily restraint, 127–129 to restraint of speech, and 130 to restraint of mind. The couplet reflects a view found in many traditions that prosperity, more than hardship, is the real test of character.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He, who is the master of riches but not of his senses, certainly loseth his riches in consequence of his want of mastery over his senses.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 34 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura warns King Dhritarashtra that wealth without self-mastery destroys itself.
Vidura puts from the negative side what Valluvar puts positively: for the wealthy, restraint is what keeps and crowns their wealth.
Christianity
“Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy;”
— 1 Timothy 6:17 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells Timothy to warn the wealthy in particular against pride.
Like Valluvar, Paul singles out the rich as the people for whom humility matters most.
Islam
“Charity does not decrease wealth, no one forgives another except that Allah increases his honor, and no one humbles himself for the sake of Allah except that Allah raises his status.”
— Sahih Muslim 2588 [6]
The Prophet teaches that giving doesn’t diminish wealth and that humility raises a person’s standing.
Both sayings treat humility as an enrichment, something that adds to a person’s worth rather than taking away from it.
Judaism
“and when thy herds and thy flocks multiply, and thy silver and thy gold is multiplied, and all that thou hast is multiplied; then thy heart be lifted up, and thou forget the LORD thy God”
— Deuteronomy 8:13–14 (JPS 1917) [7]
Moses warns Israel that prosperity is exactly when pride and forgetfulness of God set in.
Same insight as the Kural: wealth is the moment humility is most needed and most tested.
Buddhism
“To be respectful, humble, contented and grateful; and to listen to the Dhamma on due occasions — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Maha-mangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.4 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
In his list of life’s highest blessings, the Buddha includes humility.
The Buddha counts humility among life’s greatest blessings, close to Valluvar calling it a further wealth.
Jainism
“King Śreṇika, ruler of Magadha and owner of horses, elephants, a city and a harem, first mocks a young monk and offers him pleasures, then is so taught by him that he begs forgiveness: “When the lion of kings had thus, with the greatest devotion, praised the lion of houseless monks, he, together with his wives, servants, and relations, became a staunch believer in the Law, with a pure mind.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.2, 20.14–15 and 20.54–59, quoting 20.58 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The richest king of the age bows his head before a monk with no possessions, and the canon presents this humility as his true gain.
The Kural says humility suits everyone but shines as a second wealth in the wealthy; Śreṇika's bow before the penniless sage is a canonical picture of exactly that.
Sikhism
“One who has the Guru on his side - how could he be proud of his wealth?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1399, Savaiye (Bhatt Nal) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhatt Nal, a court poet whose Savaiye are in the Guru Granth Sahib, says that one guided by the Guru does not grow proud of riches.
The Kural says humility in the wealthy shines like a second wealth; the Bhatt's verse names that same freedom from the pride wealth breeds.
Greek Mythology
The fabulously rich King Croesus of Lydia asks the Athenian sage Solon who is the happiest of men, expecting to be named. Solon refuses to call any man happy before his end. Later, defeated and on the pyre, Croesus remembers Solon’s words and calls out his name.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.30–33, 1.86 (paraphrase) [11]
Greek tradition used Croesus as the classic case of a rich man whose pride is humbled by fortune.
Croesus learns too late what the Kural teaches: in the rich, humility is the crowning quality and pride the fatal flaw.
Philosophy
“These reasonings are unconnected: ‘I am richer than you, therefore I am better’; ‘I am more eloquent than you, therefore I am better.’ … But you, after all, are neither property nor style.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 44 [12]
Epictetus points out the logical error in letting wealth inflate one’s sense of personal worth.
Epictetus shows why a rich person has no grounds for pride, which is why humility in the wealthy is the fitting response the Kural praises.

Real Life Example

Princess Elizabeth trains as an army mechanic
February 1945 · Auxiliary Territorial Service training centre, southern England, United Kingdom
Princess Elizabeth, heir to the British throne, turned 18 in April 1944, and in February 1945 she enlisted in the Auxiliary Territorial Service, the women's branch of the British Army, as Second Subaltern Elizabeth Alexandra Mary Windsor. On a driving and vehicle maintenance course she learned to service, maintain and drive heavy army vehicles, and the Imperial War Museums notes that she was treated the same as the rest of her company. She became the first woman in the Royal Family to be a full-time member of the armed services and qualified as a driver before the war ended.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The press nicknamed her Princess Auto Mechanic, and her willingness to do ordinary work alongside other young women was widely admired.
The kural says humility is good in everyone but shines like a second wealth in the wealthy; a future queen in overalls changing tyres displayed exactly that kind of humility.
