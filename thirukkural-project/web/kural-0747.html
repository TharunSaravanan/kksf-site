Kural 747

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

முற்றியும் முற்றா தெறிந்தும் அறைப்படுத்தும் பற்றற் கரியது அரண்.
Mutriyum mutraa therindhum araippaduththum patrar kariyathu aran.

Meaning

A true fort cannot be taken by surrounding it, by attacking it directly, or by tricks from people inside.

Elementary

Imagine your best toy locked in a treasure box. Someone might wait outside your room for hours, or try to grab it quickly, or even trick your little brother into opening the box. A really safe box beats all three tricks. Valluvar says a real fort must be safe from waiting, grabbing and tricking.

University

The couplet lists the three ways a fort can be taken and says a true aran resists all of them: mutriyum (by besieging it all round), mutraathu erindhum (by attacking without a full siege, i.e. storming a weak point), and araippaduththum (by araippadal, which Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read as vanjanai, deceit or treachery, and Pappaiah as turning some of those inside into a fifth column). Pope’s English (‘mine beneath the ground’) follows the other old sense of arai as a hollow or underground passage, so there is a real divergence between treachery and undermining; the Tamil commentarial tradition generally prefers treachery. The sandhi-joined text reads ‘முற்றியு முற்றா தெறிந்து மறைப்படுத்தும்’, where ‘எறிந்து மறைப்படுத்தும்’ is the sandhi form of ‘எறிந்தும் அறைப்படுத்தும்’, which confirms the reading araippaduththum. The inclusion of treachery points ahead to 750: the human element can undo even a physically perfect fort.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Book XIII of the Arthashastra, titled Strategic Means to Capture a Fortress, teaches a conqueror to take forts by sowing dissension among the defenders, using spies and secret agents inside the walls, and only then by siege and assault.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book XIII (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya treats subversion from inside as just as important as siege and storm in taking a fort.
The same three methods (siege, assault and inside treachery) are what the Kural’s fort must resist.
Christianity
“But when a stronger than he shall come upon him, and overcome him, he taketh from him all his armour wherein he trusted, and divideth his spoils.”
— Luke 11:22 (KJV) [4]
Jesus continues his image: an armed man’s stronghold is safe only until someone stronger overpowers it.
It warns of what the Kural’s fort must prevent: being taken by a stronger assault.
Islam
“they thought that their fortresses would protect them from Allah; but [the decree of] Allah came upon them from where they had not expected, and He cast terror into their hearts.”
— Qur’an 59:2 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes a tribe whose strongholds failed because defeat came from a direction they did not expect and their courage collapsed.
It shows why the Kural requires a fort that cannot be taken in any way, including from an unexpected direction or by a collapse inside.
Judaism
“One wise man prevailed over a city of warriors and brought down its mighty stronghold.”
— Proverbs 21:22 (JPS Tanakh 1985, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that one clever strategist can bring down a stronghold that force could not.
It names the danger in the Kural’s third method: stratagem, which a true fort must be proof against.
Buddhism
“Furthermore, the royal frontier fortress has a gate-keeper — wise, experienced, intelligent — to keep out those he doesn’t know and to let in those he does.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha’s fortress has a wise gatekeeper who admits only people he knows.
The gatekeeper guards against the Kural’s third danger, infiltration and treachery.
Jainism
“Though others sleep, be thou awake! Like a wise man, trust nobody, but be always on the alert; for dangerous is the time and weak the body.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's sermon on carelessness tells the monk to keep constant watch and not rely on anyone, because danger can come at any moment and the body is frail.
The Kural's fortress must withstand siege, assault and betrayal from within; the Jain call to stay awake and trust nobody is the same vigilance against an enemy who strikes when the guard is down, applied to the soul.
Sikhism
After months of siege, the besiegers of Anandpur offered the Sikhs safe exit on solemn oaths; but as soon as the Guru and his Sikhs came out, the hill rulers and their Mughal allies fell upon them, and Guru Gobind Singh later rebuked Emperor Aurangzeb in his Zafarnamah for commanders who had perjured their oath and treacherously attacked him once he was outside the safety of his fortification.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entry Gobind Singh, Guru [9]
Anandpur was not taken by blockade or assault; it was emptied by a false promise, and the betrayal is remembered as one of the defining wrongs of Sikh history.
The Kural's true fortress cannot be taken by siege, by storming or by treachery; Anandpur resisted the first two and fell only to the third.
Greek Mythology
After ten years in which neither siege nor battle could take Troy, the Greeks left the wooden horse, filled with hidden warriors, outside the walls. The Trojans drew it into the city themselves, and the men inside opened the gates.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.492-520 [10]
Troy withstood blockade and assault but fell to a trick that got enemies inside the walls.
Troy passed the Kural’s first two tests and failed the third: it was taken by deceit.
Philosophy
“The rule is, not to besiege walled cities if it can possibly be avoided.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 3.4 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu advises against attacking walled cities directly because sieges cost so much.
The strategist’s rule assumes the Kural’s fort: one so hard to take by siege or storm that attackers should avoid it.

Real Life Example

Krak des Chevaliers falls to Sultan Baybars
8 April 1271 · Krak des Chevaliers (Qal'at al-Hosn), Syria
Krak des Chevaliers, the great Hospitaller castle regarded as virtually impregnable, was besieged by the Mamluk sultan Baybars from early 1271. His army pounded the walls with siege engines and used miners to bring down a tower, breaking into the outer ward, but the defenders held out in the stronger inner castle. On 8 April 1271 the remaining garrison surrendered after receiving a letter ordering them to do so, which the French Ministry of Culture's account describes as forged by Baybars and World History Encyclopedia describes as perhaps forged.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Where siege and assault had only partly succeeded against one of the finest castles ever built, deception completed the conquest.
The kural lists the three ways a fort may be taken, by siege, by direct assault and by treachery, and at Krak des Chevaliers all three were used, the last one finishing the work.
