Kural 1328

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

ஊடிப் பெறுகுவம் கொல்லோ நுதல்வெயர்ப்பக் கூடலில் தோன்றிய உப்பு.
Ootip perukuvam kolloa nudhalveyarppak Kootalil thoandriya uppu.

Meaning

By sulking once more, will we win back the sweetness we felt when we came together, when her forehead grew damp with sweat?

Elementary

After a really happy day at the beach, you might wish you could have that same day again. The husband in this poem remembers how happy they were after their last make-up, and wonders whether another small quarrel might bring that happiness back.

University

The husband wonders whether, by sulking again (ūṭi), they will once more peṟukuvam (‘obtain’, first person plural, with the doubting particle kollō) the uppu (literally ‘salt’, figuratively ‘savour, relish’) that arose in kūṭal (reunion), when nutal veyarppa (‘the brow grew moist’). Parimelazhagar and the modern glossators take uppu as the relish or sweetness of love, the salt being the zest that makes food tasty; Mu. Varadarasanar renders it as iṉimai (sweetness). Solomon Pappaiah’s gloss (‘ivaḷuṭaṉ’, with her) confirms the traditional reading of this couplet as the husband’s words. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; thirukkural.net keeps the older spelling veyarppa for viyarppa.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s Meghaduta, the exiled yaksha asks a passing cloud to carry a message to his wife, recalling intimate moments of their life together and promising that their love, made deeper by separation, will be enjoyed again when the curse ends.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Uttaramegha (paraphrase) [3]
The poem turns memory of past happiness into hope of its return.
The yaksha, like the Kural’s husband, remembers a past sweetness and longs to taste it once more.
Christianity
“Draw me, we will run after thee: the king hath brought me into his chambers: we will be glad and rejoice in thee, we will remember thy love more than wine: the upright love thee.”
— Song of Solomon 1:4 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs treats love as something to be remembered and savoured, better than wine.
The Kural’s husband also remembers love’s savour and longs to taste it again.
Islam
The Qur’an names among God’s signs that He created spouses for people so that they may find rest in them, and placed between them affection and mercy.
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International; paraphrase) [5]
Marital affection is presented as a gift renewed between spouses.
The tender reunion the Kural’s husband hopes to taste again is this kind of affection and mercy.
Judaism
“Enjoy life with the wife whom thou lovest all the days of the life of thy vanity, which He hath given thee under the sun, all the days of thy vanity; for that is thy portion in life, and in thy labour wherein thou labourest under the sun.”
— Ecclesiastes 9:9 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Ecclesiastes counts the joy of life with a beloved wife as one of the few lasting goods.
The Kural’s husband wants to keep tasting that joy, even by means of a playful quarrel.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that from craving springs grief and from craving springs fear, and that for one free of craving there is neither grief nor fear.
— Dhammapada 216 (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist teaching sees the wish to repeat a pleasure as craving (taṇhā), a root of suffering.
The Kural’s husband openly hopes to repeat a pleasure, which is just the craving the Dhammapada warns against.
Jainism
“How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain text teaches that sense pleasures never satisfy, so the desire for them only renews itself.
The Kural’s wish to taste a pleasure once more is what Jain teaching calls desire that is never satisfied.
Sikhism
“God unites the separated souls with Himself, awakening their destiny.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 934, Raag Raamkalee (Dakhni Oankaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that God Himself brings back together those who have been separated from Him.
The Kural's husband hopes a little sulking will once more win back the sweetness of reunion; Gurbani likewise trusts that the separated are brought back into union with the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
“As soon as he did so he became inflamed with the same passionate desire for her that he had felt when they had first enjoyed each other’s embraces, and slept with one another without their dear parents knowing anything about it.”
— Homer, Iliad 14 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
When Zeus sees Hera, his love returns as strong as it was at the start of their romance.
The Kural’s husband hopes for the same thing, that an old sweetness can return as fresh as the first time.
Philosophy
In Repetition, Kierkegaard’s pseudonym Constantin Constantius tests whether a past joy can be lived again by returning to the place where he once enjoyed it, and contrasts the backward longing of recollection with the forward hope of repetition.
— Søren Kierkegaard, Repetition (1843) (paraphrase) [11]
Kierkegaard asks whether lost happiness can be truly repeated rather than merely remembered.
The Kural’s husband asks the same question of love: whether another sulk can give back that earlier sweetness.

Real Life Example

A Mewar artist paints Radha Awaiting Reconciliation with Krishna
c. 1680 · Mewar, Rajasthan, India
About 1680 an artist of the Rajput kingdom of Mewar painted a page for a Gita Govinda manuscript, now in the Cleveland Museum of Art, titled Radha Awaiting Reconciliation with Krishna. The painting shows Radha six times as she prepares flowering bowers for their meeting and pleads with a picture of Krishna, while her confidante appeals to him. Such paintings belonged to a wider tradition of Rajasthani miniatures in the 17th and 18th centuries inspired by Jayadeva's poem of estrangement and reunion.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The painting shows how Indian artists turned the longing for a lovers' reconciliation into an image meant to be felt by the viewer.
The kural wonders whether, through a little sulking, the lovers can once more win the sweetness of reunion they knew before, which is exactly the hope Radha acts out in this painting.
