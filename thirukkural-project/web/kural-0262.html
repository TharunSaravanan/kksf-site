Kural 262

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

தவமும் தவமுடையார்க்கு ஆகும் அதனை அஃதிலார் மேற்கொள் வது.
Thavamum thavamudaiyaarkku aagum adhanai Aqdhilaar merkolvadhu.

Meaning

Penance (a life of strict self-control) works only for those who truly have its inner strength. For others, just going through the motions of it is useless.

Elementary

Wearing a doctor’s coat does not make you a doctor. In the same way, putting on the clothes and habits of a holy person does not work unless you really have the patience and goodness inside.

University

The couplet turns on a pun-like repetition: தவம் (tapas) comes only to those who have தவம். Commentators split on what the second ‘tapas’ means. Parimelazhagar and Solomon Pappaiah read it karmically — only those who accumulated tapas in earlier births can undertake it now — while Mu. Varadarasan reads it as a warning against தவக்கோலம், the ascetic costume adopted without ascetic conduct. The second reading anticipates the very next chapter, கூடாவொழுக்கம் (‘Imposture’), and fits Valluvar’s habitual suspicion of form without substance. Either way, the verse asserts that tapas is not a technique anyone can pick up but the outward expression of an already-formed inner capacity.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who undergo severe austerities and penances not recommended in the scriptures, performing them out of pride and egoism, who are impelled by lust and attachment, who are foolish and who torture the material elements of the body as well as the Supersoul dwelling within, are to be known as demons.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.5-6 [3]
Krishna condemns austerity performed by those whose inner disposition is proud and desirous — the outward act is present but the right nature is not.
Both texts insist that tapas without the proper inner quality is worthless or worse.
Christianity
“Moreover when ye fast, be not, as the hypocrites, of a sad countenance: for they disfigure their faces, that they may appear unto men to fast. Verily I say unto you, They have their reward.”
— Matthew 6:16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns against fasting as a public performance; the show earns only human notice, not God’s reward.
Matches Varadarasan’s reading: the ascetic ‘look’ without the ascetic reality is empty.
Islam
“Whoever does not give up forged speech and evil actions, Allah is not in need of his leaving his food and drink.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1903 [5]
A fast that leaves the character unchanged is declared meaningless in God’s sight.
The outward discipline without the inner reality is futile — Valluvar’s exact verdict.
Judaism
“Is it such a fast that I have chosen? a day for a man to afflict his soul? is it to bow down his head as a bulrush, and to spread sackcloth and ashes under him? wilt thou call this a fast, and an acceptable day to the LORD?”
— Isaiah 58:5 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah mocks ritual fasting and sackcloth performed by people whose conduct stays unjust, and redefines the true fast as freeing the oppressed.
Both reject the costume of penance when the substance is missing.
Buddhism
“What is the use of your matted hair, O witless man? What of your garment of antelope’s hide? Within you is the tangle (of passion); only outwardly do you cleanse yourself.”
— Dhammapada 394 [6]
The Buddha ridicules the ascetic whose matted locks and deerskin conceal an untamed mind.
Precisely the ‘tavakkōlam’ critique: the ascetic garb is useless without the inner tapas.
Jainism
“If an ignorant man should eat but a blade of Kusa-grass every month, (the merit of his penance) will not equal the sixteenth part of his who possesses the Law as it has been taught.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
King Nami tells Indra that even the most extreme fasting by one who lacks right understanding is worth almost nothing beside the inner possession of the true Law.
The Kural says outward tapas is futile for those who lack its inner quality; Nami makes the same point by ranking the severest fast of an ignorant man below a sixteenth of true inner discipline.
Sikhism
“Yoga is not the patched coat, Yoga is not the walking stick. Yoga is not smearing the body with ashes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 730, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Raag Soohee Guru Nanak tells the yogis that the ascetic's coat, staff and ash do not make a person one with God; union comes from living purely in the midst of the world.
The Kural says putting on the outward form of penance is futile without the inner quality; Guru Nanak likewise empties the ascetic's outfit of any value.
Greek Mythology
Phaethon begs his father Helios to let him drive the sun-chariot; lacking the strength and skill for it, he loses control, scorches the earth, and is struck down by Zeus.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses Book 2 [9]
A task suited only to one with the proper power ends in ruin when attempted by one without it.
Illustrates the Kural’s principle that a discipline belongs only to those with the capacity for it; others who take it up fail.
Philosophy
Epictetus warns that whoever takes up the Cynic’s cloak, wallet and staff without the inner preparation and divine calling undertakes a great matter in vain and makes himself ridiculous.
— Epictetus, Discourses 3.22 (On the Calling of a Cynic) [10]
For Epictetus the Cynic life is a vocation for the rare person fitted for it, not a costume anyone may wear.
A close philosophical parallel: the ascetic life ‘belongs to’ those who possess its inner qualification.

Real Life Example

Pope Clement VI condemns the flagellant processions
20 October 1349 · Avignon, France (papal court), with the processions spreading across Germany and the Low Countries
During the Black Death, bands of flagellants travelled from town to town in Germany and neighbouring lands, performing public penance by scourging themselves in organised processions. As the movement spread and grew hostile to church authority, the University of Paris appealed to the pope. In letters dated 20 October 1349, Pope Clement VI condemned the movement and forbade the processions, and it soon dwindled into a small, hunted sect.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The church judged that a dramatic outward display of penance, cut loose from inner discipline and authority, had become a danger rather than a virtue.
The kural warns that taking up the outward form of penance is futile for those who lack its inner quality, and the flagellant episode shows outward austerity being rejected as hollow.
