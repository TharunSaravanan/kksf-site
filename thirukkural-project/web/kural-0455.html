Kural 455

Avoiding Mean Associations · Sitrinanjeraamai · சிற்றினஞ்சேராமை

மனந்தூய்மை செய்வினை தூய்மை இரண்டும் இனந்தூய்மை தூவா வரும்.
Mananthooimai seivinai thooimai irandum Inanthooimai thoovaa varum.

Meaning

A clean, pure mind and clean, pure actions both come from keeping company with good, pure people.

Elementary

Having a clean heart and doing clean, honest things are both easier when the people around you are good. Good friends act like a support that keeps your thoughts and actions straight.

University

தூவா (‘support, prop, means’) is the pivot. Purity of mind (மனம்) and of action (செய்வினை), the two pillars of Aram, are made to depend on a third thing, purity of company (இனம்). This goes a step beyond 452’s simile: company is not just an influence on character but its enabling support. The triple repetition of தூய்மை holds the three purities together in one sound-chain, mirroring the argument. Parimelazhagar notes that although mind and deed seem to be individual possessions, they are sustained by one’s associates.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Narada Bhakti Sutra 44 explains why bad company must be shunned: it gives rise to lust, anger and delusion, then loss of memory and of discernment, and finally utter ruin.
— Narada Bhakti Sutra 44 [4]
The sutra traces a chain from bad association to the collapse of inner purity and so of right action.
This is the reverse of the Kural’s claim: impure company corrupts mind and deed, just as pure company supports them.
Christianity
“Make no friendship with an angry man; and with a furious man thou shalt not go: Lest thou learn his ways, and get a snare to thy soul.”
— Proverbs 22:24–25 (KJV) [5]
Friendship with a hot-tempered person teaches you his ways and traps your soul.
The same logic runs through both: the company one keeps shapes one’s inner state (soul) and outer conduct (ways).
Islam
“…the one who is blowing a pair of bellows will either burn your clothes or you will get a bad smell from him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5534 [6]
In the Prophet’s parable, an evil companion is like a blacksmith: you come away either scorched or carrying his foul smell.
In both, impurity passes from companion to person, staining conduct (the burnt clothes) and inner character (the smell).
Judaism
“I have not sat with vain persons, neither will I go in with dissemblers. I have hated the congregation of evil doers; and will not sit with the wicked. I will wash mine hands in innocency.”
— Psalm 26:4–6 (KJV) [5]
The psalmist links his ‘clean hands’ (pure deeds) directly to his refusal to sit among evildoers.
This mirrors the Kural’s logic: purity of action rests on purity of company.
Buddhism
Prince Ajatasattu, won over by the ambitious monk Devadatta, is persuaded to seize the throne from his father, King Bimbisara, who then dies in prison, while Devadatta plots against the Buddha himself.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Cullavagga VII; cf. Digha Nikaya 2 [7]
A royal heir’s mind and deeds are corrupted through one bad association, ending in patricide.
This is the Kural’s claim in its darkest form: impure company brings impurity of mind and of deed.
Jainism
“Living in this (company of the teacher) and protecting (himself or other beings) in the three ways (viz. in thoughts, words, and acts), he (gets) peace and the annihilation (of sins) as they say.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.14.16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga ties the purification of thought, word and deed, and the destruction of sin, to the monk's staying in the company of his teacher.
Valluvar says purity of mind and of deeds both come through purity of company. In the Jain verse, too, guarding thought, word and act and wearing away sin happen within that good company.
Sikhism
“In the Company of the Holy, all filth is removed.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 271, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan teaches that the Saadh Sangat washes away the filth of vice from the mind.
The Kural says purity of mind and of deeds comes through purity of company; Guru Arjan says holy company is what cleanses a person.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus’s scouts eat the lotus offered by the Lotus-eaters, lose all wish to return home, and have to be dragged back to the ships.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 9 [10]
Sharing the ways of a certain people dissolves both their purpose (mind) and their duty (action).
This illustrates how company undermines both of the purities the Kural names.
Philosophy
“I come home more greedy, more ambitious, more voluptuous, and even more cruel and inhuman, — because I have been among human beings.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 7 (trans. Gummere) [11]
Seneca confesses that mixing with the crowd leaves him morally worse in both desire and conduct.
This is a first-person witness to the Kural’s claim that purity of mind and of action depend on one’s company.

Real Life Example

The Clapham Sect campaigns against the slave trade
c. 1790-1833 · Clapham, London, England
A group of evangelical Christians living near one another in Clapham, south London, including William Wilberforce, the banker Henry Thornton, James Stephen and Zachary Macaulay, centred their lives on shared worship and public causes. Many of them sat in Parliament, where they campaigned for decades against the slave trade and for reforms such as prison reform, while funding schools and missionary and Bible societies. They played a large part in the abolition of the British slave trade in 1807 and of slavery in the British Empire in 1833.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The members sustained one another through long years of defeat, showing how a close circle of committed people can keep both conviction and action on course.
The kural says purity of mind and purity of deeds are both supported by the purity of one's company, and the Clapham neighbours kept each other's purpose and conduct steady.
