Kural 1048

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

இன்றும் வருவது கொல்லோ நெருநலும் கொன்றது போலும் நிரப்பு.
Indrum varuvadhu kolloa nerunalum kondradhu poalum nirappu.

Meaning

A poor person asks in fear, 'Will the need that almost killed me yesterday come again today?'

Elementary

Imagine having a very scary day, and waking up the next morning worried: “Will it happen again today?” Valluvar says a very poor person feels like that every single day, afraid that yesterday’s hunger and worry will come back.

University

The couplet is spoken in the voice of the poor man himself, an unusual dramatic touch in the Porutpaal. Nirappu (நிரப்பு, ‘want, need’) is a synonym of nalkuravu, and the particle kolloa (கொல்லோ) marks anxious questioning: ‘will it come today too?’. Nerunal (நெருநல், ‘yesterday’) and kondradhu poalum (‘as though it killed’) describe the daily ordeal as a near-death; Puliyur Kesikan adds the implied cry ‘if it comes, what shall I do?’, while Mu. Varadarasanar frames it as the poor man’s daily trembling. Kalaignar reads it as a longing that it may not return. The present-tense dread contrasts with the general statements of the earlier couplets and leads into the sleeplessness of 1049. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons. They arise from sense perception, O scion of Bharata, and one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that distress comes and goes like the seasons and must be endured without agitation.
Where the Kural voices dread of yesterday’s misery returning, the Gita counsels tolerating such recurring distress with equanimity.
Christianity
“Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.”
— Matthew 6:34 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his hearers not to be consumed by anxiety about tomorrow’s needs.
The Kural records exactly the daily anxiety over provision that this teaching seeks to dissolve.
Islam
“For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease.”
— Qur’an 94:5 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an assures the believer that every hardship is accompanied by relief.
Against the poor man’s fear that yesterday’s hardship will return, the verse promises ease alongside it.
Judaism
“Four are considered as if they were dead: A pauper, and a leper, and a blind person, and one who has no children.”
— Talmud Bavli, Nedarim 64b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
A baraita counts the pauper among those who are regarded as dead, deriving it from Exodus 4:19, where men who had lost their property are called dead.
The Kural says poverty ‘as good as killed’ the poor man yesterday; the Talmud treats the pauper as one already counted among the dead.
Buddhism
“Happy indeed we live, we who possess nothing. Feeders on joy we shall be, like the Radiant Gods.”
— Dhammapada 200 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada celebrates monks who own nothing and yet live in joy.
The Kural’s poor man fears each day’s want; the Buddhist renunciant, owning nothing, has nothing left to fear losing.
Jainism
“‘I get nothing to-day, perhaps I shall get something to-morrow;’ a monk who thinks thus, will not be grieved by his want of success.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira teaches the alms-seeking monk to meet an empty bowl with calm hope instead of distress.
This is the Kural’s day-by-day anxiety over want turned inside out: today’s lack is accepted and tomorrow is faced without dread.
Sikhism
“Why, O mind, do you plot and plan, when the Dear Lord Himself provides for your care?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 10, So Dar (Rehras), Raag Gujri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the evening prayer So Dar (Rehras), Guru Arjan rebukes the anxious mind that keeps scheming about tomorrow's livelihood, reminding it that God has already taken on its care. Sikh commentators stress that this forbids worry, not honest effort.
The Kural gives voice to the poor man's dread that yesterday's near-fatal want will return today; Guru Arjan addresses exactly that fearful planning and answers it with trust in God's provision.
Greek Mythology
“Never dare to taunt a man with deadly poverty which eats out the heart; it is sent by the deathless gods.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 717-718 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod calls poverty ‘deadly’ and ‘heart-eating’, a slow killer that should never be mocked.
Hesiod’s ‘deadly poverty which eats out the heart’ is the Kural’s want that ‘as good as killed’ the poor man yesterday.
Philosophy
“...my means are less to-day than they were yesterday, and to-morrow will rub off something from the little that is left...”
— Juvenal, Satire 3 (tr. G.G. Ramsay, 1918) [11]
Juvenal’s Umbritius explains why he is leaving Rome: each day his small means shrink further.
The yesterday-today-tomorrow arithmetic of want is the very rhythm of the Kural’s anxious question.

Real Life Example

The Dutch Hunger Winter
1944-1945 · Western Netherlands
In September 1944 the German occupiers imposed an embargo on food transport to the western Netherlands, and with an exceptionally harsh winter and poor harvests the region slid into famine. People lived on rations of roughly 400 to 800 calories a day and ate grass and tulip bulbs to survive, and about 20,000 people died before liberation in May 1945.
— Sources: [12] [13]
For months millions of people woke each day not knowing whether they would find enough to eat, and the famine left lasting effects on the health of survivors and their children.
The kural gives the voice of the poor man who asks each morning whether yesterday's crushing want will come again today, and the Hunger Winter was a whole population living that dread day after day.
