Kural 475

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

பீலிபெய் சாகாடும் அச்சிறும் அப்பண்டஞ் சால மிகுத்துப் பெயின்.
Peelipey saakaadum achchirum appandanj Chaala miguththup peyin.

Meaning

Even peacock feathers, which are very light, will break the axle of a cart if you pile on too many.

Elementary

A peacock feather weighs almost nothing. But if you pile far too many onto a cart, even feathers will snap its axle. Lots of small things can add up to too much.

University

This is one of the Kural’s most famous images. The peacock feather (peeli) is the lightest of loads and the cart (saakaadu) a sturdy vehicle, yet when it is loaded chaala miguththu (‘in great excess’), the axle (achchu) breaks. Parimelazhagar applies the verse to kings: even weak enemies can overwhelm a strong ruler if they combine, so a king should not provoke many small foes at once. A broader reading applies it to any capacity. No strength is infinite, and sheer quantity can turn trifles into a fatal burden. The image anticipates the later proverb of the straw that breaks the camel’s back.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who is regulated in his habits of eating, sleeping, recreation and work can mitigate all material pains by practicing the yoga system.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.17 (cf. 6.16) [4]
The verse before it warns that yoga is not for someone who eats too much or too little, or sleeps too much or too little. The body has a measure that excess breaks.
Like the Kural, it treats capacity as having a limit that must not be overloaded, even with ordinary, harmless things.
Christianity
“Neither do men put new wine into old bottles: else the bottles break, and the wine runneth out, and the bottles perish: but they put new wine into new bottles, and both are preserved.”
— Matthew 9:17 (KJV) [5]
Jesus uses the image of wineskins that burst when strained beyond what they can hold.
As with the Kural’s axle, a container or carrier fails when made to bear more than its capacity.
Islam
“Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity. … Our Lord, and lay not upon us a burden like that which You laid upon those before us. Our Lord, and burden us not with that which we have no ability to bear.”
— Qur’an 2:286 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse affirms that every soul has a limit of capacity and prays not to be loaded beyond it.
It matches the Kural’s picture of a burden that must stay within what the bearer can carry.
Judaism
The Talmud repeatedly uses the principle ‘tafasta merubah lo tafasta’: if you grasp too much, you have not grasped anything; if you grasp a little, you have grasped it.
— Babylonian Talmud, Rosh Hashanah 4b (also Yoma 80a, Chagigah 17a) [7]
The sages use this maxim to choose the smaller of two possible measures, reasoning that overreaching loses everything.
Like the Kural, it warns that taking on more than can be held leads to losing it all.
Buddhism
“Do not think lightly of evil, saying, ‘It will not come to me.’ Drop by drop is the water pot filled. Likewise, the fool, gathering it little by little, fills himself with evil.”
— Dhammapada 121 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada warns that small things, each negligible, accumulate until the vessel is full.
It shares the Kural’s insight that light items (drops, feathers) become decisive in quantity.
Jainism
“Do not get into an uneven road like a weak burden-bearer; for you will repent of it afterwards; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 10.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
In his sermon to Gautama, Mahavira uses the image of a weak porter who leaves the smooth road for an uneven one under his load, and will regret it.
The Kural's overloaded cart and the Jain overburdened porter both teach that a load beyond one's capacity ends in breakdown.
Sikhism
“You may read and read loads of books; you may read and study vast multitudes of books. … O Nanak, only one thing is of any account: everything else is useless babbling and idle talk in ego.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 467, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Asa Ki Vaar Guru Nanak imagines learning piled up by the cartload (gadee) and says that without the one thing, the Name, it all counts for nothing.
The Kural pictures a cart overloaded even with feathers; Guru Nanak also pictures a cart piled with light things, though his point is their worthlessness, not the breaking axle.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable, a donkey heavily loaded begs the unburdened horse to take a share. The horse refuses, the donkey collapses and dies under the load, and the master then puts the whole burden, and the donkey’s hide, on the horse.
— Aesop’s Fables, ‘The Horse and the Ass’ (tr. G. F. Townsend) [11]
The fable shows a carrier failing when loaded beyond its strength, and the burden then falling on others.
It is the same picture as the Kural’s overloaded cart: a bearer breaks when the load exceeds its capacity.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that both excessive and deficient exercise destroy bodily strength, and that too much or too little food or drink destroys health, while the right amount produces, increases and preserves them. The virtues, he argues, are destroyed by excess and deficiency in the same way.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.2, 1104a (tr. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle holds that even good and necessary things become destructive past their proper measure.
It gives the philosophical form of the Kural’s point that excess of even a light thing breaks what bears it.

Real Life Example

The cities of the Lombard League defeat Frederick Barbarossa at Legnano
29 May 1176 · Legnano, Lombardy, Italy
Northern Italian cities, among them Milan, Venice, Mantua, Padua and Brescia, formed the Lombard League in 1167 to resist the Holy Roman emperor Frederick I Barbarossa's attempts to curb their self-government. At Legnano in 1176 the League's army, led by Milan, routed the imperial forces. Frederick agreed to a truce and in the Peace of Constance of 1183 granted the cities self-government in return for their allegiance.
— Sources: [13] [14]
No single city could have defeated the emperor, but together they broke his power in northern Italy.
The kural's cart of peacock feathers shows how many light loads together break a strong axle, and the old commentators read it as a warning that small foes combined can overwhelm a great ruler, as the Lombard cities did.
