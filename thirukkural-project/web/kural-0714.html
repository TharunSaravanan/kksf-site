Kural 714

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

ஒளியார்முன் ஒள்ளிய ராதல் வெளியார்முன் வான்சுதை வண்ணம் கொளல்.
Oliyaarmun olliya raadhal veliyaarmun Vaansudhai vannam kolal.

Meaning

Among bright, learned people, show how bright you are. Among dull people, be plain like a white-painted wall and do not show off.

Elementary

With your older brother who loves science, you can talk about planets and black holes. With your little sister you just say “the moon is pretty tonight.” You are just as smart either way. You simply show only as much as your listener can use.

University

The kural depends on a pun: oliyaar (the luminous, the learned) is set against veliyaar (the blank or white, the empty-minded), and before the latter one should take on the colour of vaansudhai, pure white lime plaster. Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read this as appearing unlearned before the unlearned. Solomon Pappaiah softens it to coming down to the hearers’ level so that they can follow, and Kalaignar keeps the stronger sense of appearing ignorant. Plaster is bright but has no lustre of its own, so the image suggests someone who covers his learning without losing his integrity. This is the key verse of the chapter’s three-way scheme (superior, equal, inferior assemblies), and it is the source of the modern Tamil proverbial advice to “be white lime before fools.” The text agrees in all sources; the sandhi text reads “ஒளியார்முன் னொள்ளிய ராதல்” and “வண்ணங் கொளல்”.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Unless one be asked, one must not explain (anything) to anybody, nor (must one answer) a person who asks improperly; let a wise man, though he knows (the answer), behave among men as (if he were) an idiot.”
— Manusmriti 2.110 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
Manu tells the learned not to display knowledge unasked or to answer improper questioners, and to behave, when appropriate, as if he knew nothing.
“Behave … as if he were an idiot” is the Kural’s white plaster: the wise man hides his light among those who cannot receive it.
Christianity
“To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:22 (KJV) [5]
Paul describes adapting himself to every kind of listener so as to reach them.
Paul becoming “weak to the weak” is close to the Kural’s speaker who takes on plain white before the plain.
Islam
“And We did not send any messenger except [speaking] in the language of his people to state clearly for them.”
— Qur’an 14:4 (Saheeh International) [6]
God sends each prophet to speak in his people’s own language, so that the message will be clear to them.
Speaking at the level and in the idiom of your hearers is the Kural’s advice to take on their colour.
Judaism
In the introduction to the Guide for the Perplexed, Maimonides explains that he will present deep matters in scattered hints, so that they reach the prepared reader while remaining hidden from the unprepared multitude. He cites the rabbinic rule that such matters are to be taught only to those fit to receive them.
— Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed, Introduction (trans. M. Friedländer) [7]
Maimonides deliberately veils his learning when speaking to people who are not ready for it, while revealing it to the qualified.
This is the Kural’s double rule: shine before the learned, and wear a plain surface before others.
Buddhism
According to the Dhammapada commentary, the Buddha travelled to Alavi to teach a poor farmer. The man arrived exhausted and hungry after searching for a lost ox, so the Buddha had him fed before saying a word of Dhamma.
— Dhammapada Commentary on verse 203 (story of a lay disciple at Alavi) [8]
The Buddha set aside his teaching until he had met the listener’s actual condition, and remarked that hunger is the greatest of illnesses (Dhammapada 203).
Adjusting what you say and when you say it to where the listener actually is follows the Kural’s advice.
Jainism
“If he is not proud of his learning; if he does not find fault with others”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.10–13 (excerpt) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Among the fifteen qualities of the well-behaved monk, the Uttaradhyayana lists humility about one's learning; being 'proud of his learning' is among the faults of the ill-behaved (11.7).
The Kural's advice to hide one's brilliance like plain white plaster before the dull is close to the Jain ideal of a learned person who does not show off.
Sikhism
“If you are wise, be simple; if you are powerful, be weak; … How rare is one who is known as such a devotee.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 128) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, praises the rare devotee who hides wisdom and strength under simplicity.
The Kural tells the learned to take on the plain colour of white plaster among the dull and keep their brilliance hidden; Farid likewise says the wise should appear simple.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus returns to his own palace disguised by Athena as a ragged beggar. He hides his identity and cleverness among the insolent suitors, and reveals himself only when the moment and the company are right.
— Homer, Odyssey Books 17-22 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
The greatest mind among the Greeks chooses to look like nothing among people who would only abuse what he is.
Odysseus in rags is the Kural’s white plaster: brilliance deliberately covered before the unworthy.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that uneducated speakers are often more persuasive than educated ones before popular audiences, because they argue from familiar, concrete things. He advises speakers to argue from what the hearers already accept, not from far-fetched premises.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.22 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts) [12]
Aristotle sees that sophistication can work against a speaker before a plain audience, and that the effective speaker comes down to the hearers’ level.
It gives the practical logic behind the Kural’s advice to put aside brilliance before the unlearned.

Real Life Example

Faraday gives The Chemical History of a Candle to a young audience
December 1860 - January 1861 · Royal Institution, London, England, United Kingdom
The Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures for young people began in 1825, and Michael Faraday delivered nineteen series of them. His last, given when he was 70 in late December 1860 and early 1861, was a set of six lectures on The Chemical History of a Candle to an audience averaging about 650 young people, using a simple candle and many demonstrations to explain combustion. In the lectures he spoke of himself and his listeners together as fellow philosophers and even as fellow juveniles, and the lectures were published in 1861 and have never been out of print.
— Sources: [13] [14]
One of the greatest scientists of his day set aside the language of the laboratory and met children on their own ground, and the result became a classic.
The kural advises speakers to shine fully among the learned but to take on a plain colour before those without learning, and Faraday wrapped deep science in the plain language of a candle for his young hearers.
