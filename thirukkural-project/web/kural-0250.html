Kural 250

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

வலியார்முன் தன்னை நினைக்கதான் தன்னின் மெலியார்மேல் செல்லு மிடத்து.
Valiyaarmun thannai ninaikkadhaan thannin, Meliyaarmel sellu midaththu.

Meaning

When you go to bully someone weaker than you, remember how you yourself felt standing scared before someone stronger than you.

Elementary

Before you push around a smaller kid, remember how scared you felt when a bigger kid picked on you. That memory is a key that unlocks kindness.

University

The chapter closes with a practical technique rather than a doctrine: a deliberate act of imagination (ninaikka, ‘let him think’) timed for the moment of temptation (sellumidaththu, ‘when he goes against’). Compassion is produced by recalling one’s own vulnerability, so the powerful person is asked to see themselves in the position of the weak. This is the reflective, perspective-taking root of the Golden Rule, and it matches Aristotle’s analysis of pity (eleos) as pain at undeserved suffering that one could imagine befalling oneself. Placing this couplet last grounds the lofty arul of the opening kurals in ordinary moral psychology: universal compassion begins with a remembered fear.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I regard them to be perfect yogis who see the true equality of all living beings and respond to the joys and sorrows of others as if they were their own.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.32 (trans. Mukundananda) [3]
Ātmaupamya, judging others by analogy with oneself, is presented as the mark of the highest yogi.
The same self-analogy the kural recommends: feel another’s fear as your own.
Christianity
“O thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all that debt, because thou desiredst me: Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant, even as I had pity on thee?”
— Matthew 18:32-33 (KJV) [4]
In the parable, a servant forgiven a vast debt by his master then chokes a fellow servant over a small one.
Exactly the kural’s scenario: someone who once stood helpless before a stronger party forgets it when facing someone weaker.
Islam
“None of you will have faith till he wishes for his (Muslim) brother what he likes for himself.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 13 [5]
The hadith makes self-reference the measure of how one should treat others.
Both rely on putting oneself in the other’s place; the kural specifically invokes the memory of one’s own weakness.
Judaism
“Also thou shalt not oppress a stranger: for ye know the heart of a stranger, seeing ye were strangers in the land of Egypt.”
— Exodus 23:9 (KJV) [4]
Israel is told to remember its own powerlessness in Egypt when dealing with the vulnerable.
An almost exact parallel: recall your own weakness before the strong when you are tempted to oppress the weak.
Buddhism
“All tremble at violence; all fear death. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should not kill nor cause another to kill.”
— Dhammapada 129 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha grounds non-violence in the shared fear of all beings and the practice of self-comparison.
The kural’s method exactly: recall your own trembling and so spare the weaker.
Jainism
“As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to tyrannise over. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to torment.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.5.5.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [7]
The Acaranga tells the would-be oppressor to put himself in his victim's place, and concludes that the righteous man therefore neither kills nor causes killing.
Valluvar asks the strong to remember how they once trembled before someone stronger when they turn on the weak; the Acaranga gives the same exercise of imagined reversal at the moment one means to harm or tyrannise.
Sikhism
“But if a powerful tiger attacks a flock of sheep and kills them, then its master must answer for it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 360, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a hymn on Babar's invasion Guru Nanak contrasts fights between equals with the strong falling on the defenceless, which the Lord who owns the flock must answer for.
The Kural asks the powerful to remember their own fear before the stronger when they move against the weak; Guru Nanak likewise singles out the attack of the strong on the defenceless as the wrong that calls for an account.
Greek Mythology
“Fear, O Achilles, the wrath of heaven; think on your own father and have compassion upon me”
— Homer, Iliad 24 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Priam kisses the hands that killed his son, and Achilles weeps as he thinks of his own father, then releases Hector’s body.
Compassion comes through self-reference: Achilles softens when he pictures his own father in Priam’s helpless position.
Philosophy
“Let pity then be a kind of pain excited by the sight of evil, deadly or painful, which befalls one who does not deserve it; an evil which one might expect to come upon himself or one of his friends”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.8 (trans. J. H. Freese) [10]
Aristotle adds that ‘all that men fear in regard to themselves excites their pity when others are the victims.’
Aristotle’s psychology of eleos is the kural’s technique made theory: pity arises from imagining oneself in the sufferer’s place.

Real Life Example

West Germany's Basic Law guarantees asylum to the politically persecuted
23 May 1949 · Bonn, West Germany
The Basic Law of the Federal Republic of Germany, drafted by the Parliamentary Council and proclaimed on 23 May 1949, stated in Article 16 that persons persecuted on political grounds enjoy the right of asylum. Germany's Federal Agency for Civic Education explains that the right was included because many people persecuted for racial or political reasons under the Nazi regime had survived only by finding refuge abroad, while others had been refused such protection. The provision was deliberately worded without restrictions; it was later narrowed by an amendment in 1993.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A new state wrote the memory of its own citizens' flight and vulnerability into a protection for the weak of other nations.
The kural asks the strong to remember how they once stood helpless before the stronger, and the framers of the Basic Law turned the memory of persecution and exile into a guarantee for the persecuted.
