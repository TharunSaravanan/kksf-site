Kural 559

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

முறைகோடி மன்னவன் செய்யின் உறைகோடி ஒல்லாது வானம் பெயல்.
Muraikodi mannavan seyyin uraikodi Ollaathu vaanam peyal.

Meaning

If a king rules unfairly, the seasons go wrong and the clouds will not give rain.

Elementary

Think of a team captain who cheats. Soon nobody plays well, the whole game falls apart, and it feels as if even luck has left. The old poets said that when a king is unfair, even the rain stops coming on time.

University

The couplet repeats கோடி (bending): the king ‘bends’ justice (முறைகோடி) and the rain ‘bends’ from its season (உறைகோடி). Parimelazhagar notes that both forms stand for the infinitive கோட, glosses உறைகோடுதல் as ‘failing to rain at the time for rain’, and says the cause is given in the next couplet. Classical commentators take this as a real cosmic link between royal righteousness and the monsoon, a belief widespread in Indian thought. Kalaignar instead reads உறை as reservoirs and rain-storage works that fall to ruin under misrule, so the rain cannot be stored and used. The text is the same across the sources consulted, apart from sandhi spacing in the older continuous text (செய்யி னுறைகோடி யொல்லாது).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If the deity of the clouds pours rain seasonably and the king acts virtuously, the prosperity that ensues maintain the subjects in felicity.” … “It is the king who constitutes the age.”
— Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva, Section XCI (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
The sage Utathya teaches that whether the age is Krita or Kali, a golden age or a dark one, depends on the king’s conduct, and that seasonable rain goes together with a virtuous king.
It is the same cosmic link the Kural draws between a crooked king and failing rain.
Christianity
“Elias was a man subject to like passions as we are, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months.”
— James 5:17 (KJV), recalling 1 Kings 17-18 [5]
James recalls the drought under King Ahab, whose idolatry and injustice Elijah opposed.
In the biblical story, as in the Kural, an unrighteous king’s reign is marked by the heavens holding back their rain.
Islam
A hadith lists sins that bring collective punishment: when people cheat in weights and measures, they are struck by famine, hardship and the oppression of the ruler, and when they withhold zakat, rain is withheld from the sky.
— Sunan Ibn Majah 4019 [6]
Moral corruption and injustice are linked to drought and hardship.
It shares the Kural’s belief that injustice in a society causes the rain to fail.
Judaism
Moses warns that if Israel turns aside to serve other gods, God will shut up the heaven so that there is no rain and the land will not yield its fruit.
— Deuteronomy 11:13-17 (JPS 1917) [7]
In the Shema, seasonable rain depends on the people’s faithfulness.
It is the same moral link between righteousness and rain, though Deuteronomy applies it to the whole people rather than the king.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that when kings are unprincipled, their officials, the householders and the people follow; then the sun, moon, seasons and winds go awry, the deities are angered, the heavens do not give enough rain, and the crops ripen unevenly.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 4.70, Adhammika Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [8]
The Adhammika Sutta traces a chain from royal injustice all the way to failed rains and failed harvests.
It is almost exactly the Kural’s claim that a crooked king makes the seasons fail.
Jainism
In the Jain account of the first age, the wishing-trees that had fed humanity lost their power ‘through the fault of time’, and only after that did people begin to break the laws and need a king to punish them.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 1, ch. 2, part 8 ‘Coronation as king’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [9]
Jain cosmology puts the decline of nature's bounty in the turning of the cosmic time cycle (avasarpiṇī), not in a ruler's moral failings.
The Kural claims that when a king bends justice the seasons fail and rain stops. Jainism instead attributes natural decline to time itself and makes kings a response to that decline, not its cause.
Sikhism
“Food and wealth are produced in abundance when it rains, if it is the Will of God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1282, Raag Malaar (Malaar ki Vaar), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das teaches that rain and the plenty it brings come by the divine Will (hukam).
The Kural says the seasons fail and rain is withheld when a king rules against justice; Gurbani also links rain to a higher order, but places that order in God's Will rather than in the ruler's conduct.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that on a city whose people practise violence and crooked justice, Zeus sends famine and plague together, the people waste away, women bear no children and houses dwindle. Often a whole city suffers for one wicked man.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 238-247 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [11]
In Hesiod, crooked judgements bring natural disaster on the whole community.
It is the Greek counterpart of the Kural’s idea that injustice from above stops the rain.
Philosophy
“… they appeared glorious and blessed at the very time when they were full of avarice and unrighteous power. Zeus, the god of gods, who rules according to law, and is able to see into such things, perceiving that an honourable race was in a woeful plight, and wanting to inflict punishment on them …”
— Plato, Critias, 121a–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Critias tells how the kings of Atlantis, once just and obedient to law, grew greedy and unjust, so that Zeus resolved to punish them; the dialogue opens by recalling that the island was later sunk by an earthquake.
Plato links the injustice of rulers to a cosmic punishment of their whole land, a distant parallel to the Kural's failed seasons and rain.

Real Life Example

Soviet irrigation schemes dry up the Aral Sea
1960s-1980s · Aral Sea, Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan (then Soviet Union)
Soviet planners diverted almost all the water of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to irrigate cotton fields in Central Asia, and from the 1960s the Aral Sea, which has no outlet, began to shrink rapidly. By the late 1980s it had split into separate northern and southern seas, rising salinity had destroyed its fishery, and dust from the exposed seabed was degrading farmland. NASA reports that the loss of the water made the region's winters colder and its summers hotter.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Decisions made by a distant government without regard for the people of the region changed their very climate and ruined their livelihoods.
The kural says that when a ruler rules against justice the seasons go wrong and the rain fails, and Kalaignar's reading of the neglect of water works fits the Aral Sea's fate.
