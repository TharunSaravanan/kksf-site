Kural 1143

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

உறாஅதோ ஊரறிந்த கௌவை அதனைப் பெறாஅது பெற்றன்ன நீர்த்து.
uRaaadhoa ooraRindha keLavai adhanaip peRaaadhu petranna neerththu

Meaning

Isn't the rumor the whole town knows a gain for me? Hearing it, my heart feels as if we are already together, though we are not yet.

Elementary

Have you ever wanted a puppy so much that, when your friends started saying, ‘You’re getting a puppy!’, you felt almost as happy as if it were already sitting in your lap? The young man in this verse feels that way. The town’s talk about him and his beloved makes him feel as if they were already together.

University

Again ‘itu um atu’ in Parimelazhagar: the hero speaks. Uṟāatō (உறாஅதோ, with the alapeṭai lengthening) is a rhetorical question, ‘does it not come to me?’, and kauvai (கௌவை) is a synonym of alar; peṟāatu peṟṟaṉṉa nīrttu means ‘it has the nature of having got what was not got’. Parimelazhagar supplies maṉam, ‘my mind’, as the subject of nīrttu and glosses the phrase as having a joy like that of actual union. Manakkudavar reads it as the hero telling the tōḻi that the rumour should be welcomed, since through it the family will come to consent. The sources print கெளவை, which is a common encoding of கௌவை (kau) with separate vowel signs; this edition normalises it to கௌவை, as thirukkural.com’s Parimelazhagar text has it.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s Meghaduta, the exiled Yaksha, separated from his wife, consoles himself by picturing her in detail and sending a cloud as his messenger, as though contact could be had through imagination alone.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta (The Cloud Messenger) (paraphrase) [4]
The poem shows a lover living on an imagined closeness when the real one is denied.
The Kural’s hero, too, enjoys through the town’s rumour a union he has not yet obtained.
Christianity
The Letter to the Hebrews defines faith as the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen.
— Hebrews 11:1 (KJV) (paraphrase) [5]
Faith, for the writer, makes what is hoped for present already in a real way.
The Kural’s hero feels something similar: the rumour makes the union he hopes for feel already possessed.
Islam
Ibn Hazm devotes a chapter of The Ring of the Dove to the lover’s contentment, describing how a lover separated from the beloved can be satisfied with very little, such as a glance, a token, or news of her.
— Ibn Hazm, The Ring of the Dove (Tawq al-Hamama), chapter on contentment (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Arberry) [6]
The Andalusian scholar sees that lovers make a feast out of the smallest share of the beloved.
The Kural’s hero is content in just this way: the mere rumour gives him the feeling of having what he does not have.
Judaism
Jacob serves seven years for Rachel, and they seem to him but a few days, because of the love he has for her.
— Genesis 29:20 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [7]
Love for Rachel makes the long wait feel short, as if the goal were already near.
The Kural’s hero likewise feels the union he lacks as if it were already his, though for him it is rumour, not love alone, that does this.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares sensual pleasures to a dream: a man dreams of lovely parks and lakes, but on waking sees nothing of them.
— Majjhima Nikaya 54 (Potaliya Sutta) (paraphrase) [8]
Pleasures enjoyed in the mind feel real but have no substance.
The Kural’s hero enjoys a union that exists only in the town’s talk; Buddhism would call that pleasure dreamlike.
Jainism
“A miracle! O king, you give up those wonderful pleasures, in search of imaginary objects; your very hope will cause your ruin.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.51 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
Indra, testing King Nami, accuses him of giving up real pleasures for imaginary ones; Nami answers that it is worldly pleasure that is the illusion.
The Kural’s hero takes delight in a union that so far exists only in talk; the Jain dialogue asks which pleasures are real and which are imagined.
Sikhism
“If I am slandered, I go to heaven; the wealth of the Naam, the Name of the Lord, abides within my mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 339, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir says that being slandered is pure gain for him, since it brings him nearer to the Lord and settles the Name in his mind.
The Kural's lover asks whether the rumour the whole town knows is not a gain to him, making him feel he has the union he has not yet won; Kabir likewise counts the town's talk as a gain that brings him to the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
Pygmalion falls in love with the ivory statue he has carved, treats it as if it were his living bride, and at last Venus brings it to life.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10 (Pygmalion) (paraphrase) [11]
Ovid’s sculptor lives as though he already had a beloved he does not yet truly have, until the wish is granted.
The Kural’s hero, too, lives as if he already possessed the union that the rumour only promises.
Philosophy
Stendhal’s theory of crystallisation describes how the lover’s mind keeps adorning the beloved and imagining happiness with her, finding new perfections in every small piece of news.
— Stendhal, On Love (De l’amour, 1822), ch. 2 (crystallisation) (paraphrase) [12]
For Stendhal, imagination lets the lover enjoy a happiness that has not yet happened.
The Kural’s hero crystallises around the town’s rumour, enjoying the union as if it were already his.

Real Life Example

Longfellow keeps hoping for six years after Fanny Appleton's refusal
1837-1843 · Boston and Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States
Henry Wadsworth Longfellow fell in love with Frances (Fanny) Appleton after travelling with her family in Switzerland, and was crushed when she rejected his marriage proposal in 1837. He resumed his friendship with her in Cambridge and Boston, and she finally accepted him in the spring of 1843. They married in July 1843, and her father gave them Craigie House as a wedding present.
— Sources: [13] [14]
For years Longfellow lived on the hope of a union he had not yet won, before it finally came true.
The kural's lover says that hearing the town's talk makes his heart feel as though it already has the union it has not yet gained; Longfellow's long hope shows a heart living on a union still to come.
