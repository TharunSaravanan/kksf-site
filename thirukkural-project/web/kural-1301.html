Kural 1301

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

புல்லா திராஅப் புலத்தை அவர்உறும் அல்லல்நோய் காண்கம் சிறிது.
Pullaa thiraaap pulaththai avar-urum allalnoai kaankam siridhu.

Meaning

Hold back your hug and sulk a little. Then we can watch, for a short while, the pain of love that he goes through.

Elementary

Imagine a friend comes home very late after promising to be early. Your sister whispers, ‘Pretend to be a tiny bit upset for a minute, so he sees how much he was missed.’ It is a small, gentle game, not real anger, and it only lasts a moment.

University

The chapter on pulavi (a lovers’ sulk within married love, kaṟpu) opens with the speaker, read as the confidante (tōḻi), urging the wife: pullātu irā (‘remain without embracing’) and pulattai (‘sulk’), so that ‘we’ may see (kāṇkam) for a little (ciṟitu) the allal nōy (‘anguished love-sickness’) he will feel. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar and Puliyur Kesikan all gloss it this way, the first-person plural binding the confidante to the wife’s side, and Pope’s verse likewise asks her to stay reserved and decline his proffered love so as to prove his distress a little while. The limiting word ciṟitu anticipates the next couplet’s warning that the sulk must not be prolonged. ytamizh.com prints அவர்உறும் where thirukkural.net prints the joined form அவருறும், a difference of word division only.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna, wishing to see his queen Rukmini’s face in loving pique, teases her by suggesting she would have done better to marry a king of her own rank; she is so stricken that she faints, and he at once lifts her up and consoles her.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.60 (paraphrase) [3]
The god himself plays a brief game of provocation to see how deeply his beloved feels, then ends it quickly with tenderness.
The Kural’s confidante proposes the same short test from the other side: hold back a little and see how much he cares.
Christianity
“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone: my soul failed when he spake: I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Solomon 5:6 (KJV) [4]
The bride is slow to open the door to her beloved, and by the time she does he has gone, leaving her searching in distress.
The Song shows how a moment of holding back makes the lover’s absence felt sharply, which is the effect the Kural’s confidante wants to produce in the husband.
Islam
In his treatise on love, Ibn Hazm describes a kind of ‘breaking off’ (hajr) that lovers adopt playfully, out of coquetry, and says that such feigned avoidance can be sweeter than many a meeting.
— Ibn Hazm, The Ring of the Dove (Tawq al-Hamama), chapter on Breaking Off (paraphrase; tr. A.J. Arberry) [5]
The Andalusian scholar treats mock coldness between lovers as part of love’s pleasure rather than a real rupture.
The confidante’s advice is exactly this kind of playful, temporary coldness meant to be enjoyed rather than to wound.
Judaism
When his brothers come to Egypt, Joseph hides who he is, speaks roughly to them and tests them, turning aside to weep in private before he finally reveals himself.
— Genesis 42-45 (paraphrase) [6]
Joseph’s concealment lets him see how his brothers really feel, though it costs him tears of his own.
Like the sulking wife, Joseph holds back his embrace for a little while to see the other side’s heart, then ends the test in reunion.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada warns that those who keep thinking ‘he abused me, he wronged me’ never quiet their resentment, while those who let such thoughts go find peace.
— Dhammapada 3-4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhism treats dwelling on a grievance, even a small one, as something that feeds suffering.
The Kural enjoys a little staged grievance; the Dhammapada would gently caution that even play-resentment should be dropped quickly.
Jainism
The Acaranga Sutra teaches that all beings are fond of life, like pleasure and hate pain, so a mendicant should cause pain to none.
— Acaranga Sutra 1.2.3 (paraphrase; tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Jain ethics extends non-harm (ahimsa) even to the small pains we cause others in thought and word.
The Kural takes playful pleasure in a lover’s brief distress, which Jain teaching on non-harm would treat with caution rather than delight.
Sikhism
“It is so long since this rainbird has had even a glimpse of water.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 96, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan compares the soul's long longing for the Guru to the rainbird thirsting for rain.
The Kural's confidante wants the husband to feel the ache of love for a while; Gurbani gives the same ache of longing, though felt by the devotee rather than inflicted on another.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus finally returns, Penelope will not run to embrace him; she holds back and tests him by ordering their bed moved, and only when he protests that it cannot be moved does she weep and embrace him.
— Homer, Odyssey 23 (paraphrase) [10]
Penelope’s cool reserve draws out a burst of feeling from her husband that proves who he is.
Like the Kural’s wife, Penelope withholds her embrace for a little while and watches his distress before yielding.
Philosophy
La Rochefoucauld observes that absence lessens moderate passions and increases great ones, as the wind puts out candles but fans a fire.
— François de La Rochefoucauld, Maxims 276 (paraphrase) [11]
A little distance, he suggests, reveals how strong a love really is.
The confidante’s plan rests on the same insight: a brief coldness will show, and even heighten, how much he loves.

Real Life Example

Martin Luther teases Katharina about her worrying
10 February 1546 · Eisleben, Saxony (now Germany)
In early 1546 Martin Luther was away from home at Eisleben, the town of his birth, while his wife Katharina von Bora stayed in Wittenberg and worried about him. On 10 February he wrote to her with mock solemnity, addressing her as an anxious lady and doctor, and joked that ever since she had started worrying, a fire had nearly broken out at their inn and that her holy worries might make the very earth swallow him up. He told her to pray and leave the worrying to God. Luther died at Eisleben eight days later, on 18 February 1546.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Luther’s teasing turned his wife’s anxious fussing into a shared joke, a playful exchange that only works between two people secure in each other’s love.
The kural advises a small, gentle show of displeasure so that the loved one’s longing can be seen for a moment; Luther’s playful mock-complaint about Katharina’s worrying is the same kind of light, loving game.
