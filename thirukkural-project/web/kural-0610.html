Kural 610

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

மடியிலா மன்னவன் எய்தும் அடியளந்தான் தாஅய தெல்லாம் ஒருங்கு.
Madiyilaa mannavan eydhum adiyalandhaan Thaaaya thellaam orungu.

Meaning

A king who is never lazy will win, all at once, the whole world that the god who measured the worlds with his steps walked across.

Elementary

There is an old story about a god who grew so tall that he crossed the whole world in a few giant steps. This kural says a leader who is never lazy can, by working hard, win everything those giant steps covered. Hard work lets small people take giant steps too.

University

The chapter closes with a Puranic allusion. அடியளந்தான், ‘he who measured with his foot’, is Vishnu as Vāmana-Trivikrama, the dwarf who asked King Mahābali for three paces of land and then grew to cover earth and heaven. தாஅயது (with அளபெடை, vowel lengthening, of தாயது, ‘what he strode across’) is therefore the whole cosmos. ஒருங்கு, ‘together, at once’, suggests that the energetic king gains all of it at once rather than piece by piece. Parimelazhagar and Varadarasanar read it this way; Kalaignar rationalises the image into a king bringing all he traverses under his footprint. The couplet balances 606: there the idle inherit world-rulers’ wealth and gain nothing, here the tireless king gains the world. The older text has the sandhi-joined form அடியளந்தான் றாஅய தெல்லா மொருங்கு, which differs from the ytamizh reading only in how the words are split.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa, the dwarf Vāmana asks King Bali for three steps of land, then expands into Trivikrama, covering the earth with one stride and the heavens with the second, and leaves no room for the third.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.20; cf. Rig Veda 1.154 [3]
This is the episode the Kural alludes to: the whole of creation measured out in Vishnu’s strides, a feat already celebrated in the Rig Veda (1.154).
The Kural promises the tireless king everything Trivikrama’s stride covered, so the myth itself is the measure of his reward.
Christianity
“And he said unto him, Well, thou good servant: because thou hast been faithful in a very little, have thou authority over ten cities.”
— Luke 19:17 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the pounds, the servant who worked hard with what he was given is rewarded with rule over cities.
Diligence rewarded with wide dominion matches the Kural’s tireless king gaining the whole world.
Islam
“Indeed We established him upon the earth, and We gave him to everything a way.”
— Quran 18:84 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran describes Dhul-Qarnayn, a righteous ruler who travels to the far west and far east and is given the means to everything.
A tireless ruler whose reach extends across the earth is the Quranic parallel to the Kural’s unsluggish king who gains all the land Vishnu strode.
Judaism
“Seest thou a man diligent in his business? he shall stand before kings; he shall not stand before mean men.”
— Proverbs 22:29 (KJV) [4]
The proverb promises that diligence raises a person to the highest company and honour.
Diligence lifting a person to royal honour is the Kural’s point, that freedom from sloth brings the greatest possible gain.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that the glory of one who is energetic, mindful, pure in conduct, discerning, self-controlled, righteous and heedful steadily increases.
— Dhammapada 24 [6]
The verse promises ever-growing renown to the energetic and heedful person.
Ever-increasing glory for the energetic is the Buddhist counterpart of the Kural’s tireless king gaining everything.
Jainism
Jain narrative tradition has its own version of the three strides. When the minister Namuci, ruling for a few days, threatened to kill the Jain monks, the ascetic Viṣṇukumāra, brother of King Mahāpadma, came in the form of a dwarf, asked for three paces of ground, and by his ascetic powers grew vast enough to cover the world and defeat Namuci.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra VI.8 (Mahāpadmacakricaritra), vv. 148-203 (tr. Helen M. Johnson, vol. IV, pp. 99-102); also Vividhatīrthakalpa (Hastināpura-kalpa) [7]
In Jain tellings the three strides belong not to a god but to a monk whose powers came from tireless austerity and who used them to protect the community.
The Kural's unsluggish king who gains all that the strider measured has a Jain parallel in the three strides themselves, credited to ascetic striving rather than to Vishnu.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib (Pauri 28), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Japji, Guru Nanak tells the yogis that the real conquest is of one's own mind, and that this is the same as conquering the world.
The Kural promises the tireless king, all at once, the whole world that Vishnu strode across; Guru Nanak also speaks of winning the whole world, but by mastering the mind rather than by conquest.
Greek Mythology
Heracles, after completing his twelve labours and a lifetime of ceaseless toil, is taken up to Olympus at his death and made a god, receiving Hebe as his bride.
— Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.7.7; Hesiod, Theogony 950-955 [9]
In Greek myth, the hero who never rested from labour is the one mortal rewarded with a place among the immortals.
Tireless labour earning the heavens parallels the Kural’s tireless king gaining all that a god’s stride covered.
Philosophy
In The Way to Wealth, Franklin’s Father Abraham quotes Poor Richard that diligence is the mother of good luck and that God gives all things to industry, urging his hearers to plough deep while sluggards sleep.
— Benjamin Franklin, The Way to Wealth (1758) [10]
Franklin presents diligence as the source of fortune itself: what looks like luck is the reward of steady work.
‘God gives all things to industry’ is almost a secular summary of the Kural’s promise to the king without sloth.

Real Life Example

Alexander the Great's campaigns from Macedonia to India
334-323 BCE · From Macedonia across Asia Minor, Egypt and Persia to the Indus valley
Alexander, king of Macedon from 336 BCE, crossed into Asia in 334 BCE and defeated the Persians at the Granicus, at Issus in 333 BCE and decisively at Gaugamela in 331 BCE. Marching almost continuously, he took Egypt and the Persian capitals and reached the Indus valley, where he defeated King Porus at the Hydaspes in 326 BCE. When his exhausted troops refused to go further east he turned back, and he died at Babylon in 323 BCE, ruler of an empire stretching from Greece to northwestern India.
— Sources: [11] [12]
In about eleven years of almost ceaseless campaigning, one king brought a greater share of the known world under his rule than anyone before him.
The kural promises that a king free of sloth will gain all at once the whole world that Vishnu's strides covered, and Alexander's unresting campaigns are history's most famous example of a ruler winning a vast realm in a single lifetime.
