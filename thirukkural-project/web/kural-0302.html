Kural 302

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

செல்லா இடத்துச் சினந்தீது செல்லிடத்தும் இல்அதனின் தீய பிற.
Sellaa idaththuch sinandheedhu sellidaththum Il adhanin theeya pira.

Meaning

Anger at people stronger than you, where it can do nothing, is bad. Anger at people weaker than you, where it can do harm, is worse than anything else.

Elementary

If you get angry at a much bigger kid, you will probably get hurt. If you get angry at a much smaller kid, you might hurt them, and that is even worse. So anger is bad either way: it hurts you, or it makes you hurt someone who can't fight back.

University

This couplet completes the argument of 301 by closing every escape route: anger is bad where it is ineffective (sellaa idam, anger toward the powerful, which rebounds on oneself) and worst of all where it is effective (sellidam, anger toward the weak, which inflicts real injury). Mu. Varadarasanar's gloss makes the two cases explicit as 'stronger' and 'weaker' persons. The ethical weight falls on the second line: power over others magnifies the moral gravity of anger rather than licensing it. The structure anticipates modern concerns about abuse of authority in households, workplaces, and states, where anger combined with power becomes cruelty.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, the ascetic Kaushika burns a crane to death with an angry glance after it soils him; later, when a devoted housewife keeps him waiting, his anger has no effect on her, and she rebukes him with the words that she is not a crane, directing him to learn true dharma from a humble meat-seller.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (Markandeya-Samasya Parva, the tale of Kaushika, the devoted wife, and the Dharmavyadha) [3]
Kaushika's anger kills a helpless bird but is powerless against a righteous woman, and the story treats both as evidence of his spiritual failure.
The two halves of the kural in narrative form: anger at the weak is destructive evil, and anger at the strong is futile and humiliating.
Christianity
“And, ye masters, do the same things unto them, forbearing threatening: knowing that your Master also is in heaven; neither is there respect of persons with him.”
— Ephesians 6:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells those who hold power over servants to give up threats, reminding them that they themselves answer to a higher Master.
Matches the kural's second line: where you have power over the weak, harsh anger and threats are especially wrong.
Islam
“Bear in mind, Abu Mas'ud; verily Allah has more dominance upon you than you have upon your slave.”
— Sahih Muslim 1659a [5]
Abu Mas'ud was whipping his slave in intense anger when the Prophet stopped him with this reminder; he dropped the whip and vowed never to beat a servant again.
Directly illustrates the kural's warning that anger toward those under your power is the worst evil, and that you yourself stand under a greater power.
Judaism
“A person should never impose excessive fear upon the members of his household, as the husband of the concubine of Gibeah imposed excessive fear upon her and this ultimately caused the downfall of many tens of thousands of Jews.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 6b (William Davidson/Koren-Steinsaltz trans., via Sefaria) [6]
Rav Hisda warns that intimidating those under your authority at home can spiral into catastrophe, citing the story in Judges 19–20.
Like the kural, the Talmud singles out anger and intimidation directed at dependents as especially destructive.
Buddhism
“Angered & displeased, she grabbed hold of a rolling pin and gave her a whack over the head, cutting it open.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 21, Kakacupama Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Lady Vedehika was famed for gentleness, but her slave Kali tested her; when provoked, the mistress struck her only slave, and her reputation became 'vicious' and 'violent'.
The story shows that anger vented on someone powerless is the true test of character and the worst of evils, just as the kural says.
Jainism
“He will sink through anger; he will go down through pride; delusion will block up his path; through greed he will incur dangers in both worlds.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.54 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
King Nami tells the disguised Indra that each of the four passions (kaṣāyas) drags down the person who harbours it, and anger comes first.
This matches the Kural's first half: anger harms the one who feels it, even when it cannot touch its target. It does not match the second half, which says anger against the weak is the worst evil of all.
Sikhism
“O anger, you are the root of conflict; compassion never rises up in you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sahaskriti, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Salok Sahaskriti, Guru Arjan addresses anger directly as the source of strife and as wholly without compassion. Anger (krodh) is one of the five thieves in Sikh teaching.
The Kural says anger harms oneself when vented on the strong and is the worst evil when vented on the weak. Guru Arjan names what makes it so evil against the weak: it has no compassion.
Greek Mythology
“A plain man cannot stand against the anger of a king, who if he swallow his displeasure now, will yet nurse revenge till he has wreaked it.”
— Homer, Iliad, Book 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
The seer Calchas fears Agamemnon's anger, and indeed Agamemnon's harsh treatment of the helpless priest Chryses brings a plague on the army, while his quarrel with the stronger Achilles brings disaster on the Greeks.
Iliad Book 1 shows both halves: anger at the powerless (Chryses) is cruelty, and anger at the powerful (Achilles) rebounds on oneself.
Philosophy
“Why should I make my slave atone by stripes and manacles for having answered me too loudly or having put on a stubborn look, or muttered something which I did not catch? Who am I, that it should be a crime to shock my ears?”
— Seneca, On Anger (De Ira), Book III (trans. Aubrey Stewart, 1900) [11]
Seneca argues against anger at dependents, and elsewhere in the same book praises Augustus for stopping Vedius Pollio from feeding a slave to lampreys over a broken cup.
Seneca, like the kural, finds anger most shameful where one has total power over its victim.

Real Life Example

John McEnroe is defaulted from the Australian Open
21 January 1990 · Melbourne, Australia
Playing Mikael Pernfors in the fourth round of the 1990 Australian Open, John McEnroe received a warning for glaring at a lineswoman and then a point penalty for cracking his racket. When he protested and swore at the Grand Slam chief of supervisors, the umpire defaulted him from the match. He became the first player since 1963 to be disqualified from a Grand Slam tournament for misconduct, and afterwards accepted that he had himself to blame.
— Sources: [12] [13]
McEnroe's anger was aimed at officials he had no power to overrule, so it could change nothing except the outcome for himself.
The kural says anger directed where it cannot prevail only harms oneself, and McEnroe's outburst against the officials cost him the match he had been leading.
