Kural 1183

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

சாயலும் நாணும் அவர்கொண்டார் கைம்மாறா நோயும் பசலையும் தந்து.
saayalum naanum avarkondaar kaimmaaraa noayum pasalaiyum thandhu

Meaning

He took away my beauty and my modesty. In return, he gave me love-sickness and this paleness.

Elementary

Imagine trading your shiny new toy for a broken one and a bad cold. Not a fair swap! The wife jokes sadly that her husband made a swap like that: he took her bright looks and her shyness, and left her with sadness and a pale face.

University

The couplet is built on the language of exchange: kaimmāṟā (கைம்மாறா, ‘as a return, in exchange’) the husband took (koṇṭār) her sāyal (சாயல், grace, soft beauty) and nāṇ (நாண், modesty, the sense of shame proper to a woman in Sangam poetics) and gave (tantu) nōy (நோய், the disease of love) and pasalai (பசலை). The loss of nāṇ ties back to chapter 114 (நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல், ‘declaring the abandonment of modesty’). Solomon Pappaiah reads the exchange as happening at the very moment of parting; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar keep the bare trade. Pope renders: ‘Of comeliness and shame he me bereft, / While pain and sickly hue, in recompense, he left.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda Radha’s companion tells Krishna how Radha, pining for him, has wasted away: her ornaments feel like burdens, her beauty fades and she languishes in the bower thinking only of him.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, cantos 4-5 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva describes separation stripping the beloved of her loveliness and leaving only longing.
The Kural’s heroine names the same exchange: her beauty taken, love-sickness left in its place.
Christianity
“To appoint unto them that mourn in Zion, to give unto them beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness …”
— Isaiah 61:3 (KJV) [4]
The prophet promises a divine exchange in which mourners receive beauty in place of ashes.
The Kural’s heroine describes the exact reverse trade: her beauty taken, sorrow and pallor given back.
Islam
In Nizami’s Layla and Majnun, Layla, kept apart from Majnun, slowly loses her bloom; the poet describes her as fading and wasting in secret, hiding her grief from those around her until it consumes her.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (paraphrase) [5]
Separation in the great Persian romance takes the beloved’s beauty and leaves only hidden suffering.
As in the Kural, love’s parting has traded away beauty for illness and pallor.
Judaism
“Call me not Naomi, call me Marah; for the Almighty hath dealt very bitterly with me. I went out full, and the LORD hath brought me back home empty.”
— Ruth 1:20-21 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Naomi (‘pleasant’) says her very name no longer fits: her life has exchanged sweetness for bitterness.
The Kural’s heroine also describes an exchange that has left her changed: grace and modesty gone, pallor and sorrow in their place.
Buddhism
The former courtesan Ambapali, now a nun, describes how her once-celebrated beauty has changed with time: her black hair has turned like hemp bark, her eyes and skin have lost their lustre, and she concludes that the words of the Truth-speaker hold true.
— Therigatha 13.1 (Ambapali’s verses) (paraphrase) [7]
Ambapali’s verses watch beauty being taken away and replaced by its opposite.
The Kural’s heroine also inventories lost beauty, though she blames her husband’s absence, while Ambapali sees the impermanence of all forms.
Jainism
The emperor Sanatkumara, famous for his beauty, invited two gods who had praised it to see him in full splendour at court; they told him that in that short time his body had already been taken over by disease, and on seeing it was so he renounced the world.
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 4 (life of Sanatkumara) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain narrative uses Sanatkumara to show how quickly beauty can be taken and illness given in its place.
The Kural’s heroine experiences the same swift exchange, her beauty for sickness, though she blames her husband’s absence rather than impermanence.
Sikhism
“So many endure distress, deprivation and constant abuse. Even these are Your Gifts, O Great Giver!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib (pauri 25), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib Guru Nanak counts even suffering and want among the gifts of the Giver, since they too come from His hand and can teach the soul.
The Kural's wife says her husband took her beauty and modesty and gave her love-sickness and pallor in exchange; Guru Nanak likewise calls pain and loss gifts of the Beloved Giver.
Greek Mythology
The nymph Echo, spurned by Narcissus, hides in the woods; her love clings to her and grows with grief, sleepless care wastes her body, her skin shrinks and the bloom of her body vanishes into the air until only her voice and bones remain.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.393-399 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid shows unrequited, separated love literally consuming a lover’s beauty and body.
Echo’s fate dramatises the Kural’s complaint: love has taken her beauty and left only sickness and wasting.
Philosophy
In Diotima’s account, Eros, the child of Poverty and Resource, is not delicate and beautiful as people think but always poor, hard and squalid, barefoot and homeless, sharing his mother’s neediness.
— Plato, Symposium 203c-d (paraphrase) [11]
Plato’s Symposium portrays love itself as a state of lack rather than of beauty.
The Kural’s heroine finds the same: love has left her poorer, stripped of beauty and modesty.

Real Life Example

Sappho describes the bodily symptoms of love
c. 600 BCE · Lesbos, Greece
The Greek lyric poet Sappho of Lesbos (c. 620-570 BCE) wrote a famous poem, often numbered fragment 31, in which the speaker watches the person she loves sitting with someone else. The poem lists what love does to her body: her voice fails, a thin fire runs under her skin, her ears roar, she sweats and trembles, and she turns the colour of dried grass. Only about 650 lines of Sappho's poetry survive.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem is the oldest well-known description in Western literature of love as a bodily illness that changes the lover's colour.
The kural's heroine says love took her beauty and gave her sickness and pallor in exchange, and Sappho, centuries earlier and far away, recorded the same pale, fevered body that love leaves behind.
