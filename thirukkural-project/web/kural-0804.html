Kural 804

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

விழைதகையான் வேண்டி இருப்பர் கெழுதகையாற் கேளாது நட்டார் செயின்.
Vizhaidhakaiyaan vendi iruppar kezhudhakaiyaar kelaadhu nattaar seyin.

Meaning

When close friends do something for you without asking, wise people are happy about it, because it came from someone they love.

Elementary

Your friend sees your bike tyre is flat and pumps it up while you are at lunch, without asking. You don’t say, ‘Who gave you permission?’ You say, ‘Thanks!’ That is how old friends treat each other.

University

The key phrase is kelaadhu (கேளாது, ‘without asking’): the friend acts for us kezhudhakaiyaal, ‘by the right of intimacy’. The wise respond vizhaidhakaiyaan (விழைதகையான், ‘because of its desirability’), and vendi iruppar, ‘remain as those who wanted it’. Mu. Varadarasanar emphasises honouring the right itself, while Solomon Pappaiah says the wise accept the act even when they already knew of it, because it was done willingly by a friend. Kalaignar and Puliyur Kesigan make the same point more plainly: a good friend accepts what was done unasked. The couplet assumes a household culture in which a close friend may lawfully use or dispose of what is ours. Both base texts agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Sudama leaves Krishna’s palace without having asked for anything. When he gets home, he finds that Krishna has already, without a word, turned his hut into a mansion and ended his poverty, and Sudama accepts the gift with humility and gratitude.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.81 (story of Sudama) [3]
In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna acts for his old friend without being asked, and the friend receives it as an act of love, not an intrusion.
This is the Kural’s situation: a friend acts unasked out of intimacy, and the other welcomes it.
Christianity
In a parable, Jesus describes a man who goes to his friend at midnight and asks to borrow three loaves for an unexpected guest, sure that friendship gives him the right to ask even at that hour.
— Luke 11:5-8 [4]
The parable assumes that a friend may make demands that would be unthinkable from a stranger.
It reflects the same culture of intimate claims the Kural assumes, though here the friend asks and is grudgingly answered.
Islam
“or [from houses] whose keys you possess or [from the house] of your friend. There is no blame upon you whether you eat together or separately.”
— Qur’an 24:61 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an lists a friend’s house among the places where a believer may eat without blame, alongside the homes of close relatives.
This makes into law the liberty the Kural describes: a close friend may take from what is ours without a formal request.
Judaism
Years after Jonathan’s death, King David asks whether anyone of Saul’s house is left to whom he can show kindness for Jonathan’s sake. He brings Jonathan’s lame son Mephibosheth to his table for life and restores Saul’s lands to him, though no one had asked him to.
— 2 Samuel 9:1-13 [6]
David acts unprompted on the strength of an old friendship.
This is friendship acting without being asked; Mephibosheth’s humble acceptance mirrors the welcome the Kural expects.
Buddhism
The monastic code allows a monk to take an item ‘on trust’ (vissāsa) from a friend when five conditions are met: they are acquainted, intimate, the friend has spoken of it, the friend is alive, and the monk knows the friend will be pleased when the item is taken.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Mahavagga VIII (rule on taking on trust) [7]
Buddhist monastic law recognises that among intimates, taking without asking is not theft if the friend would be pleased.
It is almost the Kural’s exact situation: an act done unasked by right of intimacy, and accepted because the friend wanted it.
Jainism
“In a thing done by strangers, though in itself exceeding bad, what is there fitted to give pain? Considered rightly, it is the acts of those who are affectionately attached; which, O lord of the land where the waterfalls murmur! will be esteemed excellent, abiding in the mind.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 8 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet holds that the acts of those bound to us by affection are, rightly considered, treasured in the mind as excellent, unlike the acts of strangers.
The Kural says the wise welcome what a close friend does unasked as something they wanted, and the Naladiyar similarly says a loving friend's acts are prized for the love behind them.
Sikhism
When Mardana lay dying at Kartarpur in 1534, Guru Nanak asked how he wished his body to be disposed of; Mardana replied that by the Guru's teaching he had overcome pride in the body, and whatever remained should be disposed of as the Guru wished. He declined a tomb, and the Guru consigned his body to the River Ravi.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, ed. Harbans Singh (Punjabi University, Patiala), entry 'Mardana, Bhai (1459-1534)' by Satbir Singh (paraphrase) [9]
Mardana left the most personal decision of all to his old companion without asking for anything, trusting that whatever Guru Nanak chose would be right.
The Kural says the wise welcome what a close friend does for them unasked, as though they had wanted it; Mardana's leaving his own last rites to Guru Nanak shows that complete trust in an old friend's choices.
Greek Mythology
Pirithous raids Theseus’ cattle to test his famous strength. When the two men meet, each is so taken with the other that Pirithous offers to pay whatever penalty Theseus names, and Theseus instead forgives the raid and offers friendship.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 30 [10]
As Plutarch tells it, a liberty taken without leave becomes the start of a lifelong friendship rather than a quarrel.
Theseus welcomes an unasked act instead of resenting it, as the Kural advises, though here the friendship begins rather than having aged.
Philosophy
“it is fitting to go unasked and readily to the aid of those in adversity (for it is characteristic of a friend to render services, and especially to those who are in need and have not demanded them; such action is nobler and pleasanter for both persons)”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.11 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle praises the friend who helps without being asked, and says such help is more pleasant for both sides.
He shares Valluvar’s view that friendship works best through unasked acts that both sides welcome.

Real Life Example

Emerson's friends rebuild his house and send him abroad after a fire
1872-1873 · Concord, Massachusetts, United States
On 24 July 1872 a fire badly damaged Ralph Waldo Emerson's house in Concord, destroying the roof and much of the second floor. Without being asked, Emerson's friends raised money to restore the house; his Harvard classmate Francis Cabot Lowell personally delivered a gift of 5,000 dollars from himself and other friends, and Judge Ebenezer Hoar presented a further sum collected to pay for a journey to Europe and Egypt. Emerson accepted, travelled with his daughter Ellen, and returned on 27 May 1873 to find the house restored and the town waiting to welcome him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode shows a community of old friends acting on its own initiative to rescue a dispirited friend, and Emerson's grateful acceptance of what they had arranged.
The kural says the wise welcome what close friends do for them unasked, as if they had wanted it themselves, and Emerson accepted both the rebuilding and the journey his friends had planned without consulting him.
