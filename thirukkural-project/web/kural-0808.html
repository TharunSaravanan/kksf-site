Kural 808

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

கேளிழுக்கம் கேளாக் கெழுதகைமை வல்லார்க்கு நாளிழுக்கம் நட்டார் செயின்.
Kelizhukkam kelaak kezhudhakaimai vallaarkku naalizhukkam nattaar seyin.

Meaning

Some people trust a close friend so much that they will not listen when others talk about that friend’s faults. For them, the day the friend really does wrong is a good day, because it proves their trust.

Elementary

Someone at school whispers, ‘Your best friend said something mean about you.’ You say, ‘I don’t believe it.’ Later your friend really does make a mistake, and you work it out together. Your trust was strong enough to survive both the gossip and the mistake.

University

The couplet plays on repeated sounds: kel (கேள், ‘friend, kin’) + izhukkam (இழுக்கம், ‘fault’) is ‘a friend’s fault’, and kelaa (கேளா) is ‘not listening’. Its subject is the person vallaar (வல்லார், ‘skilled, strong’) in kezhudhakaimai who refuses to hear of a friend’s faults. The difficulty is naalizhukkam (நாளிழுக்கம்). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah, following the older interpretation, take the day the friend errs as a fruitful or useful day (பயனுள்ள நாள்) for such a person. Kalaignar reverses it: if friends wrong someone of such trust, all the days of that friendship are wasted. Puliyur Kesigan reads it as the friend’s fault seeming to be merely the fault of the day. G. U. Pope (‘The day his friend offends will day of grace to him appear’) follows the first reading. The text is the same in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Bharata arrives at Chitrakuta with an army, Lakshmana suspects he has come to kill Rama and secure the throne, and urges Rama to fight. Rama refuses to believe ill of his brother and rebukes Lakshmana, and Bharata comes in fact to beg Rama to return.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (Bharata’s arrival at Chitrakuta) [3]
In the Ayodhya Kanda, Rama will not listen to a suspicion against someone bound to him by long love.
He shows the Kural’s kelizhukkam kelaa: refusing to hear accusations against an intimate.
Christianity
Paul writes that love thinks no evil, bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things and endures all things, and Peter adds that love covers a multitude of sins.
— 1 Corinthians 13:5-7; 1 Peter 4:8 [4]
The New Testament describes love as slow to suspect and quick to cover a fault.
This is the posture of the Kural’s friend, who will not hear accusations and takes a real fault calmly.
Islam
The Qur’an tells believers that if an untrustworthy person brings them news, they should verify it, lest they harm people in ignorance and come to regret what they did.
— Qur’an 49:6 [5]
Reports against others are not to be acted on until verified.
This is close to the Kural’s refusal to take on trust others’ stories about a friend’s faults.
Judaism
“He that covereth a transgression seeketh love; but he that repeateth a matter separateth very friends.”
— Proverbs 17:9 (King James Version) [4]
Proverbs contrasts the person who covers an offence for love’s sake with the one who keeps repeating it and so divides close friends.
Refusing to repeat or hear of a friend’s fault keeps the friendship, as the Kural teaches.
Buddhism
Describing right speech, the Buddha says the disciple does not repeat elsewhere what he has heard here in order to divide people, but reconciles those who are divided and encourages those who are united.
— Digha Nikaya 1, Brahmajala Sutta [6]
Divisive tale-bearing (pisuna vaca) is listed among the kinds of wrong speech.
The Kural approaches the same evil from the listener’s side, by refusing to hear the tale.
Jainism
“Lord of the cool land where the waters brightly shine! the good will not look upon the faults of others after mixing with them (in friendship), though they act disagreeably. Persons destitute of strength of mind who take up evil things and speak of them after mixing (in friendship), are themselves inferior to those of whom they speak.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 7 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The poet says that good people will not dwell on a friend's faults, and that those who gossip about a friend's failings are lower than the friend they talk about.
The Kural praises the friend so secure in intimacy that he will not even listen to talk of his friend's faults, and the Naladiyar similarly puts the one who talks of a friend's faults below the friend himself.
Sikhism
“Farid, if you have a keen understanding, then do not write black marks against anyone else. Look underneath your own collar instead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid 6 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Farid tells the wise not to keep a record of other people's faults but to examine their own.
The Kural praises the person so secure in friendship that he will not even listen to talk of his friend's faults; Farid's refusal to write black marks against others is the same refusal to dwell on a companion's failings.
Greek Mythology
In the legend of Damon and Pythias (Phintias), Pythias is condemned by the tyrant Dionysius of Syracuse, and Damon stands surety for his life. When others mock him for trusting that Pythias will return, Damon refuses to doubt his friend, and Pythias does come back in time.
— Legend of Damon and Pythias, reported by Cicero and Valerius Maximus [9]
Damon will not listen to the scorn and suspicion of others about his friend’s faithfulness.
His trust is the Kural’s refusal to hear of a friend’s faults.
Philosophy
Plutarch’s essay on telling a flatterer from a friend warns that flatterers often try to slander a man’s true friends to him, and that a wise person guards against such whispering.
— Plutarch, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend (Moralia) [10]
Plutarch treats slander against one’s friends as a typical tactic of the false companion.
This is why the Kural praises the friend who will not even listen.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria defends Abdul Karim against her household
1887-1901 · Windsor and Osborne, England
Abdul Karim arrived from Agra in 1887 to serve Queen Victoria, became her munshi, or teacher, in 1889, taught her Urdu and was later named her Indian Secretary. His rise caused turmoil within the royal household and among government officials, who tried to separate them and accused him of having access to political papers. The Queen supported him in every case, insisted he did not read political papers, and provided for him until her death in 1901.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The episode shows a monarch refusing to let courtiers' complaints undermine a trusted friendship, in a court where many of the objections reflected prejudice.
The kural praises the person so secure in an intimate friendship that they will not even listen to talk of the friend's faults, and Victoria refused to hear her household's charges against Abdul Karim.
