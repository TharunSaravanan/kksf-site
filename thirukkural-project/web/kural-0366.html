Kural 366

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

அஞ்சுவ தோரும் அறனே ஒருவனை வஞ்சிப்ப தோரும் அவா.
Anjuva dhoorum arane oruvanai Vanjippa dhoorum avaa.

Meaning

Being afraid of desire is itself goodness, because it is desire that tricks a person.

Elementary

Some joke toys look like candy but have a spring inside that jumps out at you, so you learn to be careful of them. Valluvar says wanting things is a trick like that. It fools us, so being careful of it is part of being good.

University

The couplet frames the ethical life as vigilance: aran (virtue) here simply is the fearing (anjuvadhu) of avaa, because avaa is what vanjippadhu, what cheats and betrays a person. Commentators such as Mu. Varadarasan add that desire waits for a moment of weakness (sorvu) to strike, much as a thief waits for the watchman to nod off. The repeated expletive oorum gives the line a proverbial, almost incantatory rhythm. This picture of desire as a cunning inner enemy parallels the Gita’s portrait of kama as an “all-devouring” foe and the biblical image of sin crouching at the door.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.37 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna names desire (kama) as the great enemy that drives people into wrongdoing even against their will.
Both portray desire as an inner enemy to be feared and guarded against.
Christianity
“That ye put off concerning the former conversation the old man, which is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts;”
— Ephesians 4:22 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes lusts as “deceitful”: they promise good but corrupt the person who follows them.
This matches the Kural’s key claim that desire is the thing that deceives.
Islam
“But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge.”
— Qur’an 79:40-41 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises the one who, out of fear of God, holds the soul back from its desires (hawa).
Both join fear and the restraint of desire as the heart of righteousness.
Judaism
“If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.”
— Genesis 4:7 (KJV) [4]
God warns Cain that sin crouches at the door like a predator waiting for its chance, and that he must master it.
Both picture a lurking inner force waiting to ambush a person, and both urge vigilance against it.
Buddhism
“Those who are lust-infatuated fall back into the swirling current (of samsara) like a spider on its self-spun web. This, too, the wise cut off. Without any longing, they abandon all suffering and renounce the world.”
— Dhammapada 347 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha compares people trapped by lust to a spider caught in the web it spun itself.
Both show desire as a self-made trap that fools its victim, which is why the wise watch out for it.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The kimpāka fruit, a recurring Jain image, tastes and looks sweet but is poison. Desire works the same way: it attracts at first and destroys afterwards, so the monk must stay on guard against it.
Valluvar says that fearing desire is itself virtue because desire deceives and ruins a person. The kimpāka fruit is a picture of that deception, sweet on the tongue and deadly once swallowed.
Sikhism
“Maya is a serpent, clinging to the world. Whoever serves her, she ultimately devours.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 510, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das calls Maya, the lure of worldly desire, a serpent wrapped around the world that in the end devours those who serve it.
The Kural says that to fear desire is itself virtue because desire deceives and ruins; Guru Amar Das gives that fear its reason, a serpent that destroys its own servants.
Greek Mythology
Warned by Circe that the Sirens’ sweet song lures sailors to their death, Odysseus stops his crew’s ears with wax and has himself tied to the mast. When he begs to be set free, his men only tie him tighter.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.37-54, 153-200 (paraphrase) [9]
Odysseus knows the song of desire will fool him, so he fears it wisely and guards himself ahead of time.
This is the Kural in story form: fear desire, because it deceives even the wise.
Philosophy
“Human infirmity in moderating and checking the emotions I name bondage: for, when a man is a prey to his emotions, he is not his own master, but lies at the mercy of fortune: so much so, that he is often compelled, while seeing that which is better for him, to follow that which is worse.”
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part IV, Preface (trans. R. H. M. Elwes) [10]
Spinoza calls the power of passions over us “bondage”. They mislead us into following the worse even when we can see the better.
Both describe desire as something that tricks a person against their own good, which is why it must be guarded against.

Real Life Example

John Wesley holds his spending fixed as his income rises
1730s onward · Oxford and London, England
As a young fellow at Oxford, John Wesley found he could live on about 28 pounds a year, and as his income rose over the following years from 30 to about 120 pounds, he kept his own expenses at that same level and gave the rest away. In his sermon The Use of Money he urged Methodists to gain all they could, save all they could and give all they could, and he warned them repeatedly about the danger of riches.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Wesley treated rising income not as a chance to live better but as a danger to be guarded against, and built a rule into his life to keep wanting from growing.
The kural says that standing in fear of desire is itself virtue, because desire deceives, and Wesley's fixed allowance was a deliberate guard against the way more money tends to create more wants.
