Kural 903

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

இல்லாள்கண் தாழ்ந்த இயல்பின்மை எஞ்ஞான்றும் நல்லாருள் நாணுத் தரும்.
Illaalkan thaazhndha iyalpinmai egngnaandrum nallaarul naanuth tharum.

Meaning

A man who always gives in and bows low to his wife will feel ashamed whenever he is among good people.

Elementary

If you are always too scared to say what you think, even to someone you love, you may feel awkward around friends who speak up honestly. Love works best when both people can speak freely.

University

Iyalpinmai (இயல்பின்மை) means 'that which is not natural', and Parimelazhagar glosses the whole phrase as the fear (achcham) that makes a man stoop (thaazh) before his wife; within his patriarchal frame, it is 'unnatural' because he expects the wife to be the deferential one. He adds that when a husband is so cowed, no one guides the household and every fault follows, so he is ashamed among the nallaar (நல்லார், the good) at all times (எஞ்ஞான்றும்). Manakkudavar reads it simply as the ruinous habit of stooping to the wife. Kalaignar narrows it to a husband who fails to correct a wife lacking good character, and Solomon Pappaiah stresses fear, showing modern commentators softening the verse toward a critique of timidity. The text is identical in all four sources consulted. Modern readers rightly note that the assumption that deference is natural only for wives is a historical norm, not a universal truth, while the verse's underlying point about a fear-driven partnership remains discussable.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa ends the Raghu dynasty's story with King Agnivarna, who abandons governance for pleasures in the inner palace and dies of his excesses, leaving a pregnant queen to rule.
— Kalidasa, Raghuvamsha, Canto 19 [5]
The epic poet presents the last king's absorption in the women's quarters as the ruin of a glorious line.
The Kural's shame before the good is mirrored in the disgrace of a king who neglected his realm for private pleasure.
Christianity
Proverbs says it is better to live in a corner of the housetop than to share a wide house with a quarrelsome woman.
— Proverbs 21:9 [6]
Wisdom literature acknowledges the misery of a household dominated by strife.
Like the Kural it recognizes an unhealthy domestic dynamic, though it advises distance rather than warning of shame.
Islam
“O you who have believed, indeed, among your spouses and your children are enemies to you, so beware of them. But if you pardon and overlook and forgive - then indeed, Allāh is Forgiving and Merciful.”
— Qur'an 64:14 (Saheeh International) [7]
The Qur'an cautions that family members can pull a believer away from what is right, while urging forgiveness.
The Kural similarly warns that yielding to a spouse against one's better judgment brings harm.
Judaism
When Queen Vashti refuses King Ahasuerus's summons, his counsellors fear that all wives will despise their husbands, and a decree is sent that every man should rule his own house.
— Esther 1:10-22 [6]
The court treats one queen's independence as a threat to every husband's honour.
The same anxiety about a husband's public standing drives the Kural; many readers find the Esther episode deliberately satirical of that anxiety.
Buddhism
The Buddha describes seven kinds of wives to Anathapindika's daughter-in-law, from a 'murderous' wife to a 'mother-like', 'sister-like' or 'friend-like' wife, judging each by conduct.
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.59 (Bhariya Sutta; numbered 7.63 in some editions) [8]
Rather than condemning deference either way, the Buddha evaluates marital relationships by kindness and good character.
This offers a counterpoint: the problem in the Kural is better understood as an unhealthy relationship than as any submission to a wife as such.
Jainism
“When a monk breaks the law, dotes (on a woman), and is absorbed by that passion, she afterwards scolds him, lifts her foot, and tramples on his head.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.2.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The Sutrakritanga shows what doting leads to: the infatuated man ends up humbled and dominated by the woman he gave way to.
It closely matches Kural 903's 'unnatural' stooping in fear before one's wife, which shames a man among good people.
Sikhism
“So why call her bad? From her, kings are born.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak rejects the low view of women common in his time, pointing out that every person, kings included, is born of woman.
The Kural calls a husband's deference to his wife unnatural and shameful; Guru Nanak's verse offers a counterpoint, refusing to treat women as inferior.
Greek Mythology
Xenophon has Socrates explain that he lives with the famously difficult Xanthippe as a horseman trains on a spirited horse, to learn to deal with all people.
— Xenophon, Symposium 2.10 [11]
Socrates treats patience with a strong-willed wife as training in self-mastery rather than as disgrace.
A counterpoint to the Kural: what one tradition calls shameful submission another frames as composure.
Philosophy
Aristotle criticizes Sparta, arguing that much of its affairs were managed by women and that this contributed to the state's love of wealth and decline.
— Aristotle, Politics II.9 (1269b) [12]
Aristotle views women's influence over men in public life as a structural weakness of a polity.
This parallels the Kural's political frame, while modern scholars treat Aristotle's account as a product of his gender theory.

Real Life Example

Empress Alexandra directs Russia's government while Nicholas II is at the front
1915-1916 · Petrograd, Russia
In August-September 1915 Tsar Nicholas II took personal command of the Russian army and left his wife, Empress Alexandra, in charge of internal affairs, with Grigory Rasputin as her closest adviser. Over the following months many capable ministers were dismissed and replaced in rapid succession, a pattern mocked as ministerial leapfrog, and the administration was widely discredited. By early 1917 most Russians had lost faith in the tsarist regime.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
The tsar's deference to his wife in affairs of state damaged his standing among the very people whose support his government needed.
The kural says the habit of yielding to one's wife will make a man ashamed before good people, and Nicholas's reliance on Alexandra badly hurt his reputation among Russia's leaders and public.
