Kural 222

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

நல்லாறு எனினும் கொளல்தீது மேலுலகம் இல்லெனினும் ஈதலே நன்று.
Nallaaru eninum kolal theedhu melulagam Illeninum eedhale nandru.

Meaning

Even if someone says taking from others is a good way, taking is still bad. And even if giving did not get you into heaven, giving would still be good.

Elementary

Imagine someone says, “It’s fine to always ask for other people’s snacks.” It still isn’t a good habit. And imagine someone says, “Sharing won’t win you any prize.” Sharing is still the right thing to do. Giving is good even if nobody rewards you for it.

University

The couplet is built on two concessive clauses (எனினும், ‘even if’) that strip away the usual religious sanctions: receiving is wrong even if called a நல்லாறு (good path), and giving is right even if there is no மேலுலகம் (heaven above). Parimelazhagar reads the first line as rejecting the idea that accepting alms could be meritorious, which touches a live debate in Indian thought where receiving gifts was a recognised livelihood for some groups. The second line is philosophically bolder: it makes the value of giving independent of any afterlife reward, anticipating the distinction between acting from duty and acting from hope of reward. Valluvar thus grounds generosity in its intrinsic goodness rather than in cosmic bookkeeping.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of action. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, and never be attached to not doing your duty.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (Prabhupada translation) [3]
Krishna teaches that right action should be done for its own sake, without attachment to what it will bring you.
The Kural applies the same principle to giving: give because it is good, not because it earns heaven.
Christianity
“Remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he said, It is more blessed to give than to receive.”
— Acts 20:35 (KJV) [4]
Paul quotes Jesus to say that giving is a greater good than receiving.
Mirrors the Kural’s ranking: receiving is lesser, giving is better.
Islam
“The upper hand is better than the lower hand. The upper hand is that of the giver and the lower (hand) is that of the beggar.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1429 [5]
The Prophet, speaking about charity and about refraining from begging, said the hand that gives is better than the hand that asks.
Same judgement as the Kural’s first line: asking and taking are lower; giving is better.
Judaism
“A person should always construct [sic] himself and bear hardship rather than appeal to people at large and make himself a burden on the community.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Matanot Aniyim 10:18 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides urges people to endure hardship rather than depend on others’ charity if they can possibly avoid it.
Echoes the Kural’s first line that taking from others is to be avoided even when it seems acceptable.
Buddhism
“A person gives a gift seeking his own profit, with a mind attached [to the reward], seeking to store up for himself [with the thought], ‘I’ll enjoy this after death.’”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.49, Dana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes this as the lowest motive for giving; higher gifts are given without calculating an afterlife reward.
Supports the Kural’s second line: giving aimed at securing heaven is inferior to giving done because it is good.
Jainism
“Is it, therefore, a reproach to a man if he starve and keep his integrity inviolate, rather than fill his stomach by the disgraceful practice of mendicity?”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 31 ('Dread of mendicity'), st. 2 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
In its chapter on the dread of begging, the Nālaṭiyār says it is better to go hungry with one's integrity intact than to live by asking others for food.
This matches the first half of Valluvar's couplet: taking from others stays bad even when it is called a good path.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself a guru or a spiritual teacher, while he goes around begging - don't ever touch his feet. One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang, Saarang ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In this salok Guru Nanak rejects holy men who live by begging, however spiritual their title, and holds up instead the person who earns honestly and shares part of it.
Like the Kural, which says taking is bad even if called a good path and giving is good in itself, Guru Nanak refuses to honour begging dressed up as religion and praises earning and giving.
Greek Mythology
“Give is a good girl, but Take is bad and she brings death.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 356 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod personifies Giving and Taking as two women: one good, the other deadly.
Same verdict as the Kural: taking is bad, giving is good.
Philosophy
“Be tardy in coming forward to be the objects of their kindness; for it is not noble to be keen to receive benefits.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.11 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says a good person should be slow to accept favours, since eagerness to receive is not noble.
Parallels the Kural’s claim that receiving is ignoble even when socially accepted.

Real Life Example

Banting, Best and Collip sell the insulin patent for a dollar each
1923 · Toronto, Canada
After insulin was developed at the University of Toronto, Frederick Banting, Charles Best and James Collip assigned their patent rights on insulin to the university for a token payment of one dollar each. Their stated purpose in patenting was to prevent anyone else from taking out a patent and claiming a monopoly. The university then licensed drug companies to manufacture insulin and used the royalties to fund further research.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The discoverers of a life-saving treatment declined to take personal profit from it, choosing to place it in public trust instead.
The kural holds that taking is wrong even when it looks respectable and that giving is good without any reward, and the insulin team refused a legitimate-looking fortune in order to give their discovery away.
