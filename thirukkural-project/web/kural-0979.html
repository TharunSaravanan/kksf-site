Kural 979

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

பெருமை பெருமிதம் இன்மை சிறுமை பெருமிதம் ஊர்ந்து விடல்.
Perumai perumidham inmai sirumai perumidham oorndhu vidal.

Meaning

Greatness means not being full of pride. Smallness means riding high on pride and showing off.

Elementary

A real champion says, 'Thanks, my team helped me.' Someone who isn't much of a champion struts around shouting, 'I'm the best!' Valluvar says being great means not puffing yourself up at all.

University

The couplet defines both terms through a single word, perumidham (பெருமிதம், 'swagger, pride'), which etymologically contains the same peru- ('great') as perumai: greatness is its absence (inmai, இன்மை), littleness is perumidham oorndhu vidal (பெருமிதம் ஊர்ந்து விடல்), 'mounting and riding on pride'. Mu. Varadarasanar reads oorndhu vidal as 'pride growing until it reaches its limit and stopping there', while Solomon Pappaiah sharpens the contrast: having cause for pride yet not being proud is greatness, being proud without cause is littleness. Pope's 'Riding on car of vanity supreme' keeps the image of mounting a vehicle. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Pride, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness and ignorance – these qualities belong to those of demoniac nature, O son of Pṛthā.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.4 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna names pride, arrogance and conceit as the first marks of the demoniac nature.
Like the Kural, it defines the lower nature by the presence of conceit.
Christianity
“Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory; but in lowliness of mind let each esteem other better than themselves.”
— Philippians 2:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Philippians to act without vainglory and in humility.
This is the Kural's definition of greatness: the absence of conceit.
Islam
“He who has in his heart the weight of a mustard seed of pride shall not enter Paradise. … Pride is disdaining the truth (out of self-conceit) and contempt for the people.”
— Sahih Muslim 91a (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet condemns even a trace of pride and defines it as self-conceit and contempt for others.
Like the Kural, it treats conceit as the defining mark of the lowly soul.
Judaism
“Now Moses himself was very humble, more so than any other human being on earth.”
— Numbers 12:3 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Torah singles out the greatest prophet for his humility.
Moses is the Kural's greatness defined by the absence of pride.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that conceit about one's own wisdom is the surest sign of folly.
Like the Kural, it makes inflated self-regard the mark of littleness.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra teaches that pride (mana) destroys humility (vinaya), and that pride is to be conquered by softness or humility (mardava), just as anger is conquered by calm, deceit by straightforwardness and greed by contentment.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.37-39 (numbering varies by edition) [8]
In Jain doctrine pride is one of the four passions (kashayas) that bind karma, and its remedy is humility.
The Jain ideal of conquering pride by humility is the Kural's definition of greatness as freedom from conceit.
Sikhism
“The humble beings abide in peace; subduing egotism, they are meek. The very proud and arrogant persons, O Nanak! Are consumed by their own pride.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani, Ashtapadi 12 (opening salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
This salok opens the twelfth ashtapadi of Sukhmani, which goes on to list the pride of power, beauty, wealth and even of good deeds as forms of haumai (ego) that destroy a person. Haumai is the root problem in Sikh teaching.
The Kural defines greatness as the absence of arrogance and littleness as riding on it, and Guru Arjan likewise contrasts the meek who live in peace with the proud who are consumed by their pride.
Greek Mythology
Phaethon, eager to prove his divine parentage, insisted on driving the chariot of his father the Sun; unable to control the horses, he scorched the earth until Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.750-2.328 [10]
The myth shows vanity literally mounting a chariot it cannot manage.
Phaethon is a vivid picture of the Kural's littleness 'riding on pride' to ruin.
Philosophy
In discussing greatness of soul, Aristotle describes the vain man as one who thinks himself worthy of great things when he is not, and who parades his supposed merits in dress and display and is shown up by events.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle separates real greatness of soul from the vanity that claims it falsely.
His vain man is the Kural's littleness riding on pride without any ground for it.

Real Life Example

Pierre and Marie Curie decline the Legion of Honour
Before 1906 (Pierre) and 1910 (Marie) · Paris, France
Pierre and Marie Curie, who shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics for their work on radioactivity, made it a rule to refuse state decorations, while gladly accepting scientific prizes and memberships for their work. Pierre Curie declined France's Legion of Honour in the years before his death in 1906. In 1910, when the minister of education proposed that Marie Curie receive it, she politely declined as well, keeping to the rule she had adopted with her husband.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Two of the most celebrated scientists in the world saw no need for ornaments of rank, valuing only the recognition of their work itself.
The kural says greatness is the absence of arrogance while littleness rides on pride, and the Curies, with every reason for pride, refused the decorations that others coveted.
