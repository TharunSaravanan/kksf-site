Kural 294

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

உள்ளத்தாற் பொய்யா தொழுகின் உலகத்தார் உள்ளத்து ளெல்லாம் உளன்.
Ullaththaal poyyaadhu ozhukin ulagaththaar Ullaththul ellaam ulan.

Meaning

A person who lives without lying, even inside their own heart, will have a place in the hearts of everyone in the world.

Elementary

Think of someone you completely trust — a grandparent or a teacher who never tricks you. You carry them in your heart, don’t you? This kural says that if you are honest even inside your own thoughts, everyone will keep you in their hearts too.

University

The couplet is built on an elegant chiasmus of ullam (heart/mind): one who does not lie ‘with the heart’ (ullaththaal) comes to live ‘in the hearts’ (ullaththul) of all. Truthfulness here extends past speech to conduct (ozhukin, ‘if one conducts oneself’) and even to thought — Kalaignar reads it as not even thinking falsehood. The reward is not wealth or heaven but a permanent place in human regard, which links this chapter to the preceding chapter on fame (புகழ், ch. 24). Pappaiah narrows ‘ulagaththaar’ to the noble (uyarndhavar), following the classical reading of ulagam as ‘the worthy.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, who kept his word at the cost of his kingdom, his family and his freedom, is remembered across India as ‘Satya Harishchandra’ — the very name a byword for truthfulness. Gandhi wrote that the play about him shaped his lifelong devotion to truth.
— Harishchandra legend (Markandeya Purana; popular tradition) [3]
The Puranic king who refused to break his word even in utter ruin became a permanent hero in the popular imagination.
Harishchandra is the Kural’s promise fulfilled: truthfulness made him dwell in the hearts of all people.
Christianity
“Jesus saw Nathanael coming to him, and saith of him, Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile!”
— John 1:47 (KJV) [4]
Jesus singles out Nathanael for praise because there is no deceit in him at all.
A heart without guile wins the highest regard — the Kural’s reward.
Islam
“And grant me a mention [i.e., reputation] of honor among later generations.”
— Qur’an 26:84 (Sahih International) [5]
Abraham prays for a good name among later generations; the Arabic is ‘lisāna ṣidqin,’ literally ‘a tongue of truth.’
The truthful prophet asks for exactly the Kural’s reward: to live on in the regard of all people.
Judaism
“The lip of truth shall be established for ever: but a lying tongue is but for a moment.”
— Proverbs 12:19 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says truthful speech lasts forever, while lies pass away quickly.
Truth gives a lasting place in the world; lies vanish — the same contrast as the Kural.
Buddhism
“People hold dear him who embodies virtue and insight, who is principled, has realized the truth, and who himself does what he ought to be doing.”
— Dhammapada 217 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada says the one people cherish is virtuous and truthful (the Pali saccavādin also means ‘truth-speaker’).
Being held dear by all people is the Kural’s ‘dwelling in their hearts.’
Jainism
“A truthful man is treated as reliable as the mother, as venerable as the guru (preceptor) and as beloved as the one who commands knowledge.”
— Mūlācāra 837 (Vaṭṭakera), English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [7]
Jain monastic teaching holds that truthfulness earns a person the trust, reverence and affection of everyone around them.
The Kural promises that one who never lies even in the heart will live in the hearts of all people. The Jain verse says the truthful man becomes trusted like a mother and beloved by all.
Sikhism
“One knows the Truth only when the Truth is in his heart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 468, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok of Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak says truth is real only when it lives in the heart, not merely on the tongue.
The Kural promises that one who is free of falsehood even in the heart will live in the hearts of all people. Guru Nanak shares the first half: truth must first dwell in one's own heart.
Greek Mythology
“Whoever deliberately lies in his witness and forswears himself… that man’s generation is left obscure thereafter. But the generation of the man who swears truly is better thenceforward.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 280-285 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod teaches his brother that the liar’s family sinks into obscurity, while the truthful man’s line prospers and is remembered.
Truthfulness earns a lasting good name among people; lies bring oblivion.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes the truthful man as one who is true in word and in life even where nothing is at stake, simply because his character is such — and who will therefore be all the more truthful where much is at stake.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.7 (paraphrase of W. D. Ross tr.) [10]
For Aristotle, truthfulness is a settled state of character, not a calculation.
The Kural’s ‘not lying with the heart’ is Aristotle’s truthfulness rooted in character.

Real Life Example

Walter Cronkite is named the most trusted man in America
1972 · United States
Walter Cronkite anchored the CBS Evening News from the early 1960s until 1981 and was known for his adherence to journalistic integrity, reporting events from the Kennedy assassination to the Moon landing. In 1972 an Oliver Quayle poll named him the most trusted man in America, ranking him above the president, the vice-president and members of Congress. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1981.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Cronkite's standing rested on a public belief that he would not knowingly deceive his audience, and that trust gave him lasting affection across a divided country.
The kural says one who lives without falsehood dwells in the hearts of all people, and Cronkite's reputation for truthfulness won him a place in the regard of a whole nation.
