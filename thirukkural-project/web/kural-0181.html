Kural 181

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

அறங்கூறான் அல்ல செயினும் ஒருவன் புறங்கூறான் என்றல் இனிது.
Arangooraan alla seyinum oruvan, Purangooraan endral inidhu.

Meaning

A person may never speak about goodness and may do only wrong. Still, it is good if people can say, “At least they do not talk badly about others behind their backs.”

Elementary

Imagine a kid who forgets his homework, is messy, and gets into trouble a lot. But everyone says, “He never talks badly about anyone when they’re not around.” That one good thing still shines. This kural says not gossiping about people behind their backs is so important that even someone with lots of faults earns a little praise for it.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a deliberately provocative concession: he imagines a man who neither professes aram (virtue) nor practises it, and still finds one thing worth saying in his favour — that he is not a backbiter (puṟaṅkūṟāṉ, literally “one who speaks at the back”). The rhetorical move establishes a hierarchy of vices before any argument is made: slander is placed so low that merely abstaining from it counts as a redeeming trait. The verb “enṟal” (“that it be said”) is significant; the good in question is reputational, located in what the community can truthfully say of the man, which neatly mirrors the fact that backbiting is itself a crime against another’s reputation. Commentators such as Parimelazhagar read the couplet as an argument a fortiori: if non-slander redeems even the unrighteous, how much more is it required of the righteous. The chapter sits in the householder section (Illaraviyal) right after Envy and Covetousness, reflecting a sequence from inner vices to their expression in speech.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…non-violence, truthfulness, absence of anger, renunciation, peacefulness, restraint from fault-finding, compassion toward all living beings…”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1–3 (Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna lists the qualities of a divine nature, and one of them is apaiśunam — literally “non-paiśunya,” refraining from slander and fault-finding about others.
The Gita counts not backbiting as a core mark of a good person; the Kural says it is so basic that even a bad person gets credit for it.
Christianity
“Speak not evil one of another, brethren. He that speaketh evil of his brother, and judgeth his brother, speaketh evil of the law, and judgeth the law”
— James 4:11 (KJV) [4]
James tells the early Christians flatly not to speak against each other, because running someone down makes you a judge in God’s place.
Both texts treat speaking evil of others as a stand-alone wrong serious enough to be named on its own.
Islam
“And do not spy or backbite each other. Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his brother when dead? You would detest it.”
— Qur’an 49:12 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an forbids ghībah (backbiting) and compares it to eating your dead brother’s flesh — attacking someone who cannot defend himself because he is not there.
The Qur’an and the Kural both single out speaking about someone behind his back as a uniquely ugly act.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not go up and down as a talebearer among thy people; neither shalt thou stand idly by the blood of thy neighbour: I am the LORD.”
— Leviticus 19:16 (JPS 1917) [6]
The Torah’s command against being a rakhil (talebearer) is the root of the whole Jewish law of lashon hara, harmful speech about others.
Like Valluvar, the Torah treats carrying tales about others as its own named commandment, set right beside protecting a neighbour’s life.
Buddhism
“And what is right speech? Abstaining from lying, abstaining from divisive speech, abstaining from abusive speech, abstaining from idle chatter: This, monks, is called right speech.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 45.8, Magga-vibhanga Sutta (Thanissaro) [7]
In defining the Eightfold Path, the Buddha makes avoiding divisive speech (pisuṇā vācā, tale-bearing) one of the four parts of Right Speech.
Buddhism, like the Kural, puts refusing to talk others down at the foundation of ethical life.
Jainism
“For eight causes discipline is called virtue, viz.: not to be fond of mirth, to control one's self, not to speak evil of others, not to be without discipline, not to be of wrong discipline, not to be covetous, not to be choleric, to love the truth; for their influence discipline is called virtue.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.4–5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana names refraining from speaking evil of others as one of the eight marks that make a monk's discipline count as virtue.
Valluvar grants that a man who does nothing else right still earns one good word for not backbiting, and the Jain text likewise counts not speaking evil of others as a virtue in its own right.
Sikhism
“It is not good to slander anyone, but the foolish, self-willed manmukhs still do it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 755, Raag Soohee, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das teaches that slandering anyone at all is never good, and that it is the self-willed manmukh who falls into the habit.
The Kural says that even a man with no other virtue earns some credit if he does not backbite; Guru Amar Das likewise singles out slander as a wrong to be avoided toward anyone.
Greek Mythology
“Do not get a name either as lavish or as churlish; as a friend of rogues or as a slanderer of good men.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 715–716 (Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod’s farmer-wisdom warns his brother to guard his reputation, and one of the worst names a man can earn is ‘slanderer of good men.’
Hesiod and Valluvar both frame the issue in terms of what people say of you: being known as a non-slanderer is itself a good name.
Philosophy
“We may, however, enter, though sparingly, into discourse sometimes when occasion calls for it, but not on any of the common subjects … but principally not of men, so as either to blame, or praise, or make comparisons.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion, ch. 33 (tr. Elizabeth Carter (as edited at the Internet Classics Archive)) [11]
In his rules for everyday conduct, Epictetus tells the student that talking about other people, and above all finding fault with them, is the first kind of conversation to give up.
Valluvar picks out the same habit as the one to drop first, so much so that someone who manages only this much has still done something good.

Real Life Example

Young Benjamin Franklin resolves to speak ill of no one
1726 · At sea, on the voyage from London to Philadelphia
In 1726, aged twenty and returning from London to Philadelphia after a period of youthful mistakes he later called errata, Benjamin Franklin drew up a written Plan of Conduct for his future life. Its fourth resolution was to speak ill of no one, even when the criticism would be true, and instead to excuse the faults he heard charged against others and to speak all the good he knew of everybody. The original manuscript is lost, but the text survives in an early printed copy reproduced in The Papers of Benjamin Franklin.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Franklin, who openly admitted his own early failings, singled out refraining from speaking ill of others as one of the few rules he set himself for life.
The kural says that even a man with many faults has something good if it can be said that he does not speak ill of others behind their backs, and Franklin made that one restraint a cornerstone of his self-reform.
