Kural 50

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

வையத்துள் வாழ்வாங்கு வாழ்பவன் வான்உறையும் தெய்வத்துள் வைக்கப் படும்.
Vaiyaththul vaazhvaangu vaazhpavan vaanuraiyum, Theyvaththul vaikkap padum.

Meaning

A person who lives on this earth the way people should live will be counted among the gods in heaven.

Elementary

You don't have to go anywhere special to become great. If you live rightly here — kindly, honestly, taking care of people — the world will think of you the way it thinks of the shining beings in the sky. Being good on the ground is what puts you up there.

University

The chapter's closing couplet is built on a deliberate spatial contrast: வையத்துள் (vaiyaththul, “within the world/earth”) against வான் உறையும் (vaan uraiyum, “sky-dwelling”). The pivot is the untranslatable வாழ்வாங்கு வாழ்பவன் — “one who lives in the manner of living,” i.e. one who lives as living is properly done. The passive வைக்கப்படும் (vaikkap-padum, “will be placed/reckoned”) is carefully non-supernatural: Valluvar does not say such a person *becomes* a god or *goes* to heaven, but that he will be *counted among* them — an evaluative rather than metaphysical claim, which is why the verse has been read comfortably by Saiva, Vaishnava, Jain and secular-humanist commentators alike. It is also the structural answer to kural 1: the book opened by putting God first; it closes this chapter by putting the good householder alongside the gods.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, the sage Kaushika, proud of his ascetic powers, is sent to learn dharma from Dharmavyadha — a butcher in Mithila who supports his aged parents and does his ordinary trade honestly; the householder-butcher then instructs the brahmin ascetic in the highest teaching.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (Vyadha Gita / the story of Dharmavyadha) [4]
The Vyadha Gita makes an ordinary working householder the spiritual superior of a forest ascetic, precisely because he lives his worldly duties as they ought to be lived.
It dramatises the Kural's claim that living rightly in this world is what places a person among the highest, above those who chase higher states.
Christianity
“Whereby are given unto us exceeding great and precious promises: that by these ye might be partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust.”
— 2 Peter 1:4 (KJV) [5]
2 Peter articulates what the Eastern churches call theosis — that human beings living rightly come to share in God's own nature.
This is the Kural's promise in Christian terms: right living on earth results in being counted with the divine.
Islam
“[To the righteous it will be said], 'O reassured soul, Return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing [to Him], And enter among My [righteous] servants And enter My Paradise.'”
— Qur'an 89:27-30, Al-Fajr (Sahih International) [6]
Surah al-Fajr closes by contrasting the greedy who neglect the orphan and the poor with the soul at peace, who is welcomed into the ranks of God's servants.
Both passages describe the reward as being *placed among* an honoured company — the Kural among the sky-dwelling gods, the Qur'an among God's servants.
Judaism
“Rabbi Jacob said: this world is like a vestibule before the world to come; prepare yourself in the vestibule, so that you may enter the banqueting-hall.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 4:16 (trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Pirkei Avot makes present, ordinary life the preparation whose quality determines one's place in the world to come.
Both sayings make how one lives here the sole determinant of the honoured company one joins hereafter.
Buddhism
“Living with Brahma are those families where, in the home, mother & father are revered by the children. Living with the first devas are those families where, in the home, mother & father are revered by the children. … 'Brahma' is a designation for mother & father.”
— Itivuttaka 106 (Iti 4.7), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [8]
The Buddha says that a household which honours its parents is already living in the company of Brahma and the devas — and that the parents themselves *are* what those words name.
This is the Kural's claim almost exactly: rightly ordered domestic life places ordinary people among the gods.
Jainism
“When under such discipline he lives piously even as a householder, he will, on quitting flesh and bones, share the world of the Yakshas.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The Uttarādhyayana promises a heavenly rebirth among the gods to a householder who keeps the lay rules and the fortnightly fast.
Valluvar says the one who lives as life should be lived will be counted among the sky-dwelling gods, and the Jain sūtra gives the same reward to the pious householder.
Sikhism
“In the midst of this world, do seva, and you shall be given a place of honor in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak tells his listeners that selfless service done within ordinary worldly life is what wins honour before God.
Like the Kural's householder who lives on earth as one should and is counted among the gods in heaven, the one who serves within the world is given a place of honour in the divine court.
Greek Mythology
“But after earth had covered this generation -- they are called pure spirits dwelling on the earth, and are kindly, delivering from harm, and guardians of mortal men; for they roam everywhere over the earth, clothed in mist and keep watch on judgements and cruel deeds, givers of wealth”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 121-126 (Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod's golden race, who had lived rightly and died gently, were not simply extinguished but promoted to the status of daimones who guard and watch over the living.
Like the Kural, it says that mortals who lived as one ought are afterwards reckoned among the divine order rather than merely remembered.
Philosophy
“Wherefore we ought to fly away from earth to heaven as quickly as we can; and to fly away is to become like God, as far as this is possible; and to become like him, is to become holy, just, and wise.”
— Plato, Theaetetus 176b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Socrates defines the philosopher's goal as homoiōsis theōi — likeness to God — and immediately glosses it in purely ethical terms: holiness, justice, wisdom.
The Kural makes the same move, defining nearness to the divine as a matter of how one lives rather than where one goes.

Real Life Example

Imhotep, builder of the Step Pyramid, is later worshipped as a god
c. 27th century BCE; worshipped in the Late Period · Saqqara and Memphis, Egypt
Imhotep was a high official of King Djoser of Egypt's Third Dynasty; his name and titles are carved on the base of a statue of the king, and he is credited with designing Djoser's Step Pyramid at Saqqara, Egypt's first monumental structure in stone. His memory lasted for millennia: by the New Kingdom he was listed among the wise men of the past and revered as patron of scribes, and in the Late Period he was worshipped as a god of medicine and son of the god Ptah, later identified by the Greeks with Asklepios.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Imhotep is one of very few people in Egyptian history known to have been raised to divine status after death, purely through the regard in which his life and wisdom were held.
The kural says one who lives on earth as one ought will be counted among the gods in the sky, and Imhotep, a human official remembered for wisdom and achievement, was literally later counted among Egypt's gods.
