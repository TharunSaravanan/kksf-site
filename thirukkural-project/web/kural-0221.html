Kural 221

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

வறியார்க்கொன்று ஈவதே ஈகைமற் றெல்லாம் குறியெதிர்ப்பை நீர துடைத்து.
Variyaarkku ondru eevadhe eekai matru ellaam Kuriyedhirppai neeradhu udaiththu.

Meaning

Only giving to people who have nothing is true giving. Every other gift is like a trade that expects something back.

Elementary

If you give your friend a sticker because you hope they will give you one back, that is really a trade. But if you give your lunch to a kid who has no lunch at all and can never pay you back, that is real giving. This kural says only the second kind counts as true giving.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a definition by exclusion: ஈகை (eekai) is giving to the வறியார் (the destitute), and everything else is குறியெதிர்ப்பை — a gift made with a ‘measured return’ in view, which the old commentators read as something close to a loan or a reciprocal obligation. The move sharply separates philanthropy from the neighbouring virtue of ஒப்புரவு (social beneficence, Chapter 22) and from ordinary gift exchange, which anthropologists since Marcel Mauss have shown to be governed by expectations of return. The criterion is not the size of the gift but the incapacity of the recipient to repay; that incapacity is what guarantees the purity of the motive. The same test appears in Jesus’ advice to invite those who ‘cannot recompense thee’ and in the Gita’s definition of sattvic gifts given ‘without expectation of return’, making this one of the most widely shared ethical intuitions across traditions.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That gift which is given out of duty, at the proper time and place, to a worthy person, and without expectation of return, is considered to be charity in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.20 (Prabhupada translation) [3]
Krishna ranks gifts by motive; the highest (sattvic) gift is given simply because it ought to be given, with no return expected from the recipient.
Same core test as the Kural: a gift that expects a return is not the highest kind of giving. The Gita adds conditions of time, place and a ‘worthy’ recipient, whereas Valluvar makes need itself the qualification.
Christianity
“When thou makest a dinner or a supper, call not thy friends, nor thy brethren, neither thy kinsmen, nor thy rich neighbours; lest they also bid thee again, and a recompence be made thee. But when thou makest a feast, call the poor, the maimed, the lame, the blind: And thou shalt be blessed; for they cannot recompense thee.”
— Luke 14:12-14 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his host that feeding people who can invite you back is just an exchange; feeding the poor, who cannot repay, is what is truly blessed.
Almost a paraphrase of the Kural: giving to those who can return the favour is reciprocity, giving to the needy is real generosity.
Islam
“And they give food in spite of love for it to the needy, the orphan, and the captive, [Saying], ‘We feed you only for the face [i.e., approval] of Allāh. We wish not from you reward or gratitude.’”
— Qur'an 76:8-9 (Sahih International) [5]
The righteous feed the poor, orphans and captives while explicitly refusing any reward or even thanks from them.
Matches the Kural’s two elements: the recipient is the needy, and nothing is expected back from them.
Judaism
“A lower level than this is one who gives charity to the poor without knowing to whom he gave and without the poor person knowing from whom he received. For this is an observance of the mitzvah for its sake alone.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Matanot Aniyim 10:8 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
In his ladder of eight levels of charity, Maimonides ranks anonymous giving very high because no relationship, gratitude or debt can form between giver and receiver.
Like the Kural, Maimonides values the gift that cannot turn into an exchange; anonymity removes any chance of repayment.
Buddhism
“He gives a gift with the thought, ‘I am well-off. These are not well-off. It would not be right for me, being well-off, not to give a gift to those who are not well-off.’”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.49, Dana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Dana Sutta the Buddha ranks motives for giving; giving because others are in need ranks above giving to store up rewards for oneself after death.
Shares the Kural’s focus on need as the reason to give and its low view of gifts made for personal profit.
Jainism
“The effect of giving of the gift–dāna–has distinctions based on the specific manner in which it is given–vidhiviśeṣa, the specific thing given–dravyaviśeṣa, the specific nature of the giver–dātṛviśeṣa, and the specific nature of the recipient–pātraviśeṣa.” … “The presence of qualities which lead to salvation indicates the superiority of the recipient–pātraviśeṣa.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.39, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Umāsvāti's sūtra, with Pūjyapāda's commentary, says that what a gift is worth depends partly on who receives it. For Pūjyapāda the best recipient is one whose qualities lead toward liberation.
Valluvar also defines true giving by its recipient, though for him that recipient is the destitute person who cannot repay. Jain dāna theory ranks recipients by spiritual worth instead, so the two agree on the principle and differ on who should receive.
Sikhism
“In that place where the lowly are cared for-there, the Blessings of Your Glance of Grace rain down.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak closes this Siree Raag hymn by saying that God's grace falls precisely where the lowest and poorest are looked after.
Valluvar defines true giving as giving to those who have nothing, and Guru Nanak locates divine favour exactly where the destitute are cared for.
Greek Mythology
“Give to one who gives, but do not give to one who does not give. A man gives to the free-handed, but no one gives to the close-fisted.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 353-355 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod’s farmer’s wisdom treats giving as a system of reciprocity: you give to those who will give back.
An instructive contrast. Hesiod describes exactly the ‘measured return’ kind of giving that Valluvar says does not count as ஈகை at all.
Philosophy
“Therefore the liberal man, like other virtuous men, will give for the sake of the noble, and rightly; for he will give to the right people, the right amounts, and at the right time.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says the generous person gives because giving well is noble, not to gain something, and gives to the right recipients.
Agrees with the Kural that true giving is not motivated by return; Aristotle leaves ‘the right people’ open, while Valluvar specifies the destitute.

Real Life Example

Thomas Coram wins a royal charter for the Foundling Hospital
17 October 1739 · London, England
Thomas Coram, a retired sea captain of modest background, campaigned for about seventeen years for a home for babies abandoned on London's streets, gathering petitions from aristocrats, artists and scientists. King George II signed the Royal Charter for the Foundling Hospital on 17 October 1739, and the first children were admitted in temporary premises in Hatton Garden in 1741. Coram's memorial at the hospital records that he died in 1751 poor in worldly estate.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Britain's first dedicated children's charity was built for infants who could never repay anyone, and over two centuries it cared for more than 25,000 children.
The kural defines true giving as giving to those who have nothing, and abandoned newborns were the clearest case of recipients who could offer no return.
