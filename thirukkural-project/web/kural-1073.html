Kural 1073

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

தேவர் அனையர் கயவர் அவருந்தாம் மேவன செய்தொழுக லான்.
Thevar anaiyar kayavar avarundhaam mevana seydhozhuga laan.

Meaning

Mean people are like the gods, because they too do whatever they please.

Elementary

Gods in stories can do anything they like, and nobody can tell them off. A spoiled bully acts the same way, doing whatever he wants without thinking of anyone else. The Kural jokes that this makes him 'like a god', but it really means he has no self-control at all.

University

Another ironic comparison: kayavar are thevar anaiyar (தேவர் அனையர், 'like the gods') because they too live mevana seydhu (மேவன செய்து, 'doing what they desire'). Puliyur Kesikan spells out the logic: like the gods, they have no one to restrain them (தம்மை நியமிப்பவர் இல்லாமல்), and Solomon Pappaiah glosses it the same way. Parimelazhagar's reading is that the gods are free because they stand above rules, while the base are 'free' because they ignore them, so the likeness is pure mockery. Kalaignar points to the gods 'of the Puranas' who act on every wish. Pope: 'The base are as the Gods; they too / Do ever what they list to do!' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But he who discards scriptural injunctions and acts according to his own whims attains neither perfection, nor happiness, nor the supreme destination.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.23 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna warns that a person who throws off all guidance and lives by whim reaches no good end.
The Kural's base man who 'does as he pleases' is exactly the whim-driven man the Gita condemns.
Christianity
“For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.”
— Genesis 3:5 (KJV) [4]
The serpent tempts Eve with the promise that by grasping what is forbidden she will become like the gods.
The Kural mocks the same delusion: acting without restraint makes one 'like the gods' only in a false and ruinous sense.
Islam
“Have you seen he who has taken as his god his [own] desire”
— Qur'an 45:23 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the person who makes his own desires his god and so loses guidance.
The Kural's base man treats his wishes as law, as if he were a god; the Qur'an says he has made his wishes his god.
Judaism
“In those days there was no king in Israel; every man did that which was right in his own eyes.”
— Judges 21:25 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The book of Judges ends by describing a time of chaos when everyone did whatever he wanted.
Like the Kural, it treats doing whatever one pleases, with no restraint, as a sign of moral collapse.
Buddhism
“Formerly this mind wandered about as it liked, where it wished and according to its pleasure, but now I shall thoroughly master it with wisdom as a mahout controls with his ankus an elephant in rut.”
— Dhammapada 326 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha contrasts a mind that roams wherever it likes with a mind tamed by wisdom.
The Kural's base man is the untamed mind of this verse, going 'where it wished and according to its pleasure'.
Jainism
“The worthy, though they have attained the wealth of the world, will at no time indulge in haughty speech, but if the mean have acquired the wealth of one cani (1/80 part) added to one muntheri (1/320) they will regard themselves as great as Indra king of heaven.”
— Naladiyar 35.6, chapter 'Meanness' (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Naladiyar mocks the mean man who, with a tiny bit of wealth, thinks himself equal to Indra, king of the gods.
Like the Kural, the Naladiyar sarcastically likens the base to a god, a comparison that exposes their smallness.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, it is written that you shall obey the Hukam of His Command, and walk in the Way of His Will.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib (pauri 1), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
The first pauri of Japji Sahib teaches that the true person lives within hukam, the divine order, rather than by his own will.
The Kural mocks the base as godlike because they too do whatever they please; Guru Nanak sets the opposite ideal, a life that walks in God's will instead of one's own wishes.
Greek Mythology
Salmoneus, a mortal king, set himself up as Zeus, driving a chariot that dragged bronze kettles to imitate thunder and hurling torches as lightning, until Zeus struck him down.
— Apollodorus, The Library 1.9.7 (tr. J. G. Frazer) [10]
Salmoneus believed that doing whatever he liked made him the equal of the king of the gods.
Salmoneus is the Kural's base man who is 'like the gods' only in his unrestrained self-will.
Philosophy
Locke distinguishes liberty from licence: the state of nature is a state of liberty, but not one in which a person may do whatever he pleases, because reason and the law of nature still bind him.
— John Locke, Second Treatise of Government, ch. 2 §6 (1690) [11]
For Locke, true freedom is bounded by moral law; doing anything one likes is licence, not liberty.
The Kural's base man mistakes licence for the freedom of the gods.

Real Life Example

The Tweed Ring plunders New York City
1860s–1871 · New York City, United States
William M. Tweed and his allies in the Tammany Hall political machine controlled New York City government and defrauded it of an estimated 30 to 200 million dollars. In 1871 reporting in The New York Times and the cartoons of Thomas Nast exposed the ring. Tweed was convicted in 1873, escaped to Spain in 1875, was recognised from a Nast cartoon and returned, and died in a New York jail in 1878.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Tweed Ring became a lasting example of what happens when people in power face no checks until the press and the courts finally act.
Like the 'gods' of the kural, the Tweed Ring did whatever it pleased because for years no one could restrain it.
