Kural 323

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

ஒன்றாக நல்லது கொல்லாமை மற்றதன் பின்சாரப் பொய்யாமை நன்று.
Ondraaka nalladhu kollaamai matradhan Pinsaarap poiyaamai nandru.

Meaning

Not killing stands alone as the greatest good. Right behind it, in second place, comes not lying.

Elementary

Think of a race where first prize is gold and second is silver. Valluvar gives the gold medal to “never kill” and the silver medal to “never lie.” Both are winners, but not hurting any living thing comes first.

University

Here Valluvar explicitly ranks two virtues he has treated separately: kollaamai (non-killing) is good “as one” (oṉṟāka) — unique and incomparable — and poyyāmai (truthfulness, the subject of chapter 30) is good “following behind it.” This is notable because in chapter 30 he had praised truth in superlative terms; the commentators reconcile the two by saying truth is supreme among virtues of speech, but non-killing is supreme absolutely. The ordering precisely mirrors Patañjali’s list of yamas, where ahiṃsā precedes satya, and the Buddhist precepts, where abstaining from killing is first and false speech fourth. It also contrasts with Gandhi, who, while treating the two as inseparable, made Truth the end and ahimsa the means.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“ahiṃsā-satya-asteya-brahmacarya-aparigrahā yamāḥ” — non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, continence and non-possessiveness are the restraints.”
— Yoga Sutras of Patañjali 2.30 [4]
Patañjali lists the five yamas (ethical restraints) of yoga, with ahimsa first and satya second.
Identical ranking to the Kural: non-killing first, truthfulness immediately after.
Christianity
“Thou shalt do no murder, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness,”
— Matthew 19:18 (KJV) [5]
When asked which commandments lead to life, Jesus lists them beginning with the prohibition on murder.
Like the Kural, it places not killing at the head of the list, with honesty (no false witness) following.
Islam
“And those who do not invoke with Allāh another deity or kill the soul which Allāh has forbidden [to be killed], except by right, and do not commit unlawful sexual intercourse.”
— Qur’an 25:68 (Saheeh International) [6]
Among the qualities of the servants of the Most Merciful, refraining from unjust killing is named immediately after refusing idolatry.
Shows unjust killing ranked among the very gravest sins, near the top of the moral order as in the Kural.
Judaism
“Keep thee far from a false matter; and the innocent and righteous slay thou not: for I will not justify the wicked.”
— Exodus 23:7 (KJV) [5]
The Torah’s law of justice places the command to shun falsehood and the command not to kill the innocent in a single verse.
Binds together exactly the two virtues the Kural ranks — not lying and not killing.
Buddhism
“Pāṇātipātā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi” — I undertake the training rule to abstain from taking life.”
— Pañcasīla (Five Precepts), Khuddakapāṭha 2 [7]
The first of the five lay precepts is abstaining from killing; abstaining from false speech (musāvādā) is the fourth.
As in the Kural, non-killing is the first and foundational precept, with non-lying following among the core rules.
Jainism
In the Jain list of the five great vows (mahāvratas) taken by monks and the five lesser vows (aṇuvratas) of laypeople, the vow of non-killing (ahiṃsā) always stands first and the vow of truthfulness (satya) second, as in the account of Mahāvīra's five great vows and their clauses.
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.15 (the five great vows; tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
Jainism orders its vows with non-injury at the head and abstention from falsehood immediately after it.
Valluvar ranks non-killing as the peerless good with not-lying following close behind; the Jain sequence of vows gives precisely this order.
Sikhism
“Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 62, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak places truth, and above all a life lived in truth, at the very top of the spiritual life.
The Kural ranks non-killing first and truthfulness second; Guru Nanak's ranking differs, placing truthful living above everything, so the parallel lies in the shared high esteem for truth rather than in the order.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod names Horkos (Oath), who most afflicts men who knowingly swear falsely, while the avenging Erinyes, born from the blood of the wounded Ouranos, punish bloodshed.
— Hesiod, Theogony 185, 231–232 [10]
Early Greek myth assigns divine punishers to both the perjurer and the shedder of blood.
Shows falsehood and bloodshed as twin cosmic offences, though without the Kural’s explicit ranking.
Philosophy
“Ahimsa and Truth are so intertwined that it is practically impossible to disentangle and separate them.”
— M. K. Gandhi, From Yeravda Mandir, ch. 2 “Ahimsa or Love” [11]
Gandhi likens the two to two sides of a coin, but calls ahimsa the means and Truth the end.
Like Valluvar, Gandhi ranks and pairs non-violence and truth; interestingly he inverts the Kural’s order, placing Truth highest.

Real Life Example

Miep Gies hides the Frank family and saves Anne Frank's diary
July 1942 - August 1944 · Amsterdam, Netherlands
In July 1942 Otto Frank asked his employee Miep Gies to help hide his family from deportation, and she agreed. For more than two years she, her husband Jan and several colleagues secretly brought food, supplies and news to the eight people hiding in the Secret Annex, concealing their presence from the authorities. After the hiding place was raided on 4 August 1944, Gies gathered Anne Frank's diary and papers, kept them in her desk, and later gave them to Otto Frank, the only survivor.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Gies and her colleagues risked their own lives and lived a long concealment to protect others, and her rescue of the diary preserved one of the most widely read books of the twentieth century.
The kural ranks not killing first and not lying second, and Gies's two years of necessary concealment were in the service of protecting life.
