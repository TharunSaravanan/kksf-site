Kural 719

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

புல்லவையுள் பொச்சாந்தும் சொல்லற்க நல்லவையுள் நன்கு செலச்சொல்லு வார்.
Pullavaiyul pochchaandhum sollarka nallavaiyul Nanku selach chollu vaar.

Meaning

People who can speak good things well to a good group should never, even by mistake, speak about them to a low-minded group.

Elementary

You wouldn’t show your best drawing to kids who you know will just scribble on it and laugh. Save it for people who will really look at it. Good words should be shared with good listeners.

University

Pullavai (a low or mean assembly, from pul, grass, meaning petty or base) is set against nallavai (a good assembly). Pochchaandhum (even through forgetfulness) uses the root of ch. 54, Pochchaavaamai (Unforgetfulness): the speaker is not to slip, even for a moment, into speaking before an unworthy audience. Nanku sela sollu (to speak so that it goes in well) describes speech that enters and settles in the hearers’ minds. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as speech that is “fixed in the mind” (manadhil padhiyumaaru). The ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net texts print “நன்குசலச்”, apparently a corruption. The sandhi text “நன்கு செலச்சொல்லு வார்” and P. A. Muthukrishnan’s musical setting (“நன்கு செலச் சொல்லுவார்”, quoted on thirukkural.net) support the reading செல, which Parimelazhagar’s gloss (to cause to enter) also requires.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“This confidential knowledge may not be explained to those who are not austere, or devoted, or engaged in devotional service, nor to one who is envious of Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.67 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
At the end of the Gita, Krishna forbids Arjuna to pass on its teaching to the undisciplined, the uninterested, or the hostile.
Krishna’s ban on teaching the unfit hearer is the Kural’s refusal to speak in the pullavai.
Christianity
“And whosoever shall not receive you, nor hear your words, when ye depart out of that house or city, shake off the dust of your feet.”
— Matthew 10:14 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells the disciples not to keep pressing their message on those who refuse to hear it, but to move on.
Like the Kural, it advises withholding speech from an unreceptive audience.
Islam
“And when they hear ill speech, they turn away from it and say, ‘For us are our deeds, and for you are your deeds. Peace will be upon you; we seek not the ignorant.’”
— Qur’an 28:55 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an praises believers who withdraw courteously from vain and hostile talk instead of engaging with it.
“We seek not the ignorant” is the Kural’s refusal to hold forth in the mean assembly.
Judaism
“Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest thou also be like unto him.”
— Proverbs 26:4 (KJV) [5]
The proverb warns that engaging a fool on his own terms lowers the wise to his level.
Speaking in the pullavai risks this lowering, which is why the Kural forbids it.
Buddhism
When the wanderer Vacchagotta asks the Buddha whether there is a self or not, the Buddha stays silent. He later explains to Ananda that either answer would only have confused Vacchagotta further or confirmed a wrong view.
— Ananda Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 44.10 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha holds back even true teaching when the listener is not in a state to receive it properly.
His silence before an unready hearer is the Kural’s rule: do not speak good words where they cannot take root.
Jainism
“When reprimanded a wise man should not be angry, but he should be of a forbearing mood; he should not associate, laugh, and play with mean men.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Jain disciple is told to keep away from low company and not to take part in its idle talk and amusement.
The Kural warns the good speaker never, even by forgetting himself, to hold forth before a mean assembly, and the Jain verse likewise keeps the disciplined person out of mean men's company.
Sikhism
“Don't argue with fools.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (pauri 19), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's brief counsel in Asa ki Vaar is not to waste words in dispute with the foolish.
The Kural tells those who speak well in good assemblies never, even by forgetfulness, to speak in a mean one; Guru Nanak likewise says not to spend words on fools.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable, a cock scratching for food finds a precious jewel. He says that it may be valuable to someone who prizes it, but he would rather have a single grain of barley.
— Aesop’s Fables, ‘The Cock and the Jewel’ (trans. G. F. Townsend) [10]
The fable shows a treasure wasted on a finder who cannot value it.
Good words spoken in a mean assembly are the jewel before the cock, a treasure the hearers will not value.
Philosophy
“Never call yourself a philosopher, nor talk a great deal among the unlearned about theorems, but act conformably to them. … Thus, therefore, do you likewise not show theorems to the unlearned, but the actions produced by them after they have been digested.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion, ch. 46 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus tells the student not to lay out philosophical principles before people who cannot receive them, but to keep silent among them and show the fruit of those principles in conduct.
Both texts hold that worthy teaching should not be spoken before an audience unfit to receive it.

Real Life Example

Pablo Casals refuses to perform in countries that recognised Franco's regime
1946-1950 · Prades, France
The Catalan cellist Pablo Casals, an outspoken opponent of fascism, settled in Prades in French Catalonia and refused to return to Spain after the Spanish Civil War. In 1946 he announced his retirement from public performance in protest at worldwide recognition of the Franco regime, extending his boycott to countries such as the United States and the United Kingdom that maintained relations with Spain. He resumed public performance in 1950 only in Prades, where the Casals Festival was founded, and later made rare exceptions such as his 1961 performance at the White House for President Kennedy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One of the world's greatest musicians withheld his art from stages he considered compromised, offering it only where it could serve his convictions.
The kural says those who can speak good things well in a worthy assembly should never offer them in an unworthy one, and Casals kept his music back from audiences he would not dignify with it.
