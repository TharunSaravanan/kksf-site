Kural 162

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

விழுப்பேற்றின் அஃதொப்பது இல்லையார் மாட்டும் அழுக்காற்றின் அன்மை பெறின்.
Vizhuppetrin aqdhoppadhu illaiyaar maattum Azhukkaatrin anmai perin.

Meaning

If you can stop being jealous of anyone at all, no other good thing you could ever gain is equal to that.

Elementary

Imagine a treasure chest full of prizes: gold, trophies, toys. This kural says the best prize is a heart that is never jealous of anyone. Better than all the rest.

University

The key phrase is யார் மாட்டும் (“toward anyone at all”): Valluvar asks for complete freedom from envy, not selective freedom. Envy usually targets equals, as Aristotle observed, so envying no one means refusing to compare one's own standing with others'. By calling this the highest of all விழுப்பேறு (excellent attainments), the couplet puts an inner state above wealth, fame, and learning. The point anticipates the Stoic claim that goods in our control are the only true goods, and that once we see this, “there will be no room for envy.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who is satisfied with gain which comes of its own accord, who is free from duality and does not envy, who is steady both in success and failure, is never entangled, although performing actions.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.22 [3]
Krishna describes the free person as vimatsara, one without envy, content with whatever comes and steady in both success and failure.
Both texts present freedom from envy as a supreme attainment that frees a person from bondage.
Christianity
“Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up,”
— 1 Corinthians 13:4 (KJV) [4]
In the hymn to love, Paul says love “envieth not,” and closes the chapter by calling love the greatest of the three abiding gifts (13:13).
The Kural calls an envy-free heart the greatest attainment; Paul makes the love that “envieth not” the greatest gift.
Islam
“None of you will have faith till he wishes for his (Muslim) brother what he likes for himself.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 13 [5]
The Prophet makes complete faith depend on wanting for others the same good one wants for oneself, which is the opposite of envy.
Both texts treat a heart that wishes others well, without envy, as the highest attainment of the religious life.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines wealth as contentment with one's own portion, not comparison with others.
Both texts put a heart that does not measure itself against others above any outward prize.
Buddhism
“A monk who does not despise what he has received, even though it be little, who is pure in livelihood and unremitting in effort — him even the gods praise.”
— Dhammapada 366 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The verse after the warning against envying others' gains (Dhp 365) praises the monk who is content with his own.
Both texts rank contentment free from envy as an attainment worth more than material gain.
Jainism
“I forgive all living beings and may all living beings forgive me; I cherish feelings of friendship towards all and I harbour enmity towards none.”
— Saman Suttam, verse 86 (English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [8]
This is the Prakrit formula (khāmemi savva jīve) recited at the annual Paryuṣaṇa confession, in which the Jain declares friendship with every being and ill-will towards none.
Valluvar prizes freedom from envy towards anyone at all (yār māṭṭum) above every other gain, and the Jain formula likewise refuses any exception: friendship towards all, enmity towards none.
Sikhism
“I have totally forgotten my jealousy of others, … No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that in the company of the holy (the Saadh Sangat) all jealousy of others fell away, so that no one remained an enemy or a stranger to him.
The Kural prizes being free of envy toward anyone at all above every other gain; Guru Arjan describes exactly that universal release, with envy gone toward everyone.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor old couple in Phrygia, share their meager meal with disguised gods whom richer neighbours turned away; they are rewarded while the neighbourhood is drowned.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 [10]
The couple are content with their humble lot and give freely, with no resentment of wealthier neighbours.
Their contentment, free of envy, turns out to be worth more than their neighbours' wealth, echoing the Kural's ranking.
Philosophy
“For, if the essence of good consists in things in our own control, there will be no room for envy or emulation.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 19 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus says that once you see real goods as inner ones, no one else's honors or power can make you envious. The only thing worth wanting is to be free.
Both thinkers see freedom from envy as the sign of the highest good a person can reach.

Real Life Example

Nishida and Oe make the Medals of Friendship
1936 · Berlin, Germany, and Japan
At the Berlin Olympic Games, the Japanese pole vaulters Shuhei Nishida and Sueo Oe both cleared 4.25 metres and tied for second place after a final lasting more than five hours. The placings were settled by the Japanese side, with the older Nishida given the silver medal and Oe the bronze. After returning to Japan, the two friends had both medals cut in half and joined, so that each man kept a medal that was half silver and half bronze, which became known in Japan as the Medals of Friendship.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rather than let a ranking divide them, two rivals in the same event chose to share the honour equally, and Nishida's medal is now displayed by Waseda University.
The kural says no gain equals freedom from envy toward anyone at all, and Nishida and Oe valued their friendship above the difference between silver and bronze.
