Kural 543

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

அந்தணர் நூற்கும் அறத்திற்கும் ஆதியாய் நின்றது மன்னவன் கோல்.
Andhanar Noorkum Araththirkum Aadhiyaai Nindradhu Mannavan Kol.

Meaning

A king’s fair rule is what holds up the holy books of the learned priests and the dharma (the right way of living) that those books teach.

Elementary

Think of a town library. The books are wonderful, but if nobody keeps the doors safe and the roof mended, the books get ruined and people stop reading. A fair ruler is like the person who keeps the library safe, so that good teachings and good behaviour can last.

University

Parimelazhagar takes அந்தணர் நூல் as the Vedas and அறம் as the dharma they lay down, and he explains ஆதியாய் நின்றது (‘stood as the origin’) causally: if the sceptre fails, the Veda is forgotten and dharma lapses, so the sceptre is their support, not their source in time. Varadarasan follows him (‘the scripture revered by the Brahmins’). Modern commentators widen the terms: Solomon Pappaiah reads அந்தணர் as ‘those who cultivate wisdom’ and நூல் as their books of knowledge, Kalaignar speaks of ‘the books of the virtuous’, and Sivayogi Sivakumar reads அந்தணர் as ‘those who know the end’. The couplet sets out a characteristic Kural view: learning and morality are not self-sustaining; they need a just political order to protect them. Pope’s ‘Learning and virtue of the sages spring / From all-controlling sceptre of the king’ overstates the idea of origin. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That sceptre on which the well-being and progress of the sciences of Anvikshaki, the triple Vedas, and Varta depend is known as Danda (punishment).”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.4 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya says the well-being of philosophy, the three Vedas and economic life all depend on the king’s sceptre of just punishment.
Almost the Kural’s thesis word for word: the Vedas and the order of life rest on the king’s கோல்.
Christianity
“I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty.”
— 1 Timothy 2:1–2 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks Christians to pray for rulers because stable government is what allows a godly and honest life.
Religion and virtue flourish under the ruler’s protection, which is the Kural’s point.
Islam
“We have already sent Our messengers with clear evidences and sent down with them the Scripture and the balance that the people may maintain [their affairs] in justice. And We sent down iron, wherein is great military might and benefits for the people,”
— Qur’an 57:25 (Sahih International) [5]
The verse puts scripture, the balance of justice and ‘iron’ (coercive power) side by side as the gifts that keep human society just.
Scripture needs the balance and the iron, just as the sages’ books and dharma need the sceptre.
Judaism
“In all matters, his deeds shall be for the sake of heaven. His purpose and intent shall be to elevate the true faith and fill the world with justice, destroying the power of the wicked and waging the wars of God.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Kings and Wars 4:10 (trans. E. Touger) [6]
Maimonides defines the purpose of Jewish kingship as raising up the true faith and filling the world with justice.
The king’s power exists to uphold religion and righteousness, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta, the Buddha describes the duty of a wheel-turning monarch: relying on the Dhamma and honouring it, he is to provide righteous protection for his household, troops, nobles, brahmins and householders, town and country people, ascetics and brahmins, and even animals and birds, and to allow no wrongdoing in his realm.
— Digha Nikaya 26, Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta [7]
The ideal king’s protective rule makes room for recluses and brahmins to live out their spiritual life.
Like the Kural, it makes the righteous ruler the guardian under whom teachers of dharma can flourish.
Jainism
“The example and advice of Samprati induced his vassals to embrace and patronize his creed, so that not only in his own kingdom, but also in the adjacent countries, the monks could practice their religion.”
— Hemacandra, Pariśiṣṭaparvan (Sthavirāvalī) XI, as summarised by Hermann Jacobi; quoted in ‘The Jain Church After Mahavira’, jainworld.com [8]
Hemacandra's history of the Jain elders credits the Mauryan king Samprati with protection and patronage that let the mendicant order and its teaching flourish across and beyond his realm.
The Kural says the just sceptre is what keeps the sages' scriptures and dharma standing, and the Samprati story makes the same claim from the Jain side: the monks could practise their dharma because a righteous king supported them.
Sikhism
In 1609 Guru Hargobind built the Akal Takht at Amritsar, a temple and assembly hall where both the spiritual and the temporal affairs of the Sikh community could be settled, and he bore two swords for his twin authority, temporal (miri) and spiritual (piri).
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Guru Hargobind” (paraphrase) [9]
From Hargobind onward Sikh tradition joins temporal authority to the spiritual, with temporal power set to guard the community's faith.
The Kural says the king's sceptre is the foundation that keeps the sages' scriptures and dharma standing; in Sikh tradition the seat of temporal authority stands to protect the life of the Guru's word.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Achilles swears by the sceptre that the sons of the Achaeans carry in their hands when they give judgment, the men who guard the ordinances (themistes) that come from Zeus.
— Homer, Iliad 1.234–239 [10]
In Homer the sceptre is the visible sign that the judge-king holds and protects the god-given laws.
The sceptre as guardian of sacred law is close to the Kural’s கோல் upholding scripture and dharma.
Philosophy
“But justice is the bond of men in states, for the administration of justice, which is the determination of what is just, is the principle of order in political society.”
— Aristotle, Politics I.2, 1253a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle argues that people without law and justice are the most savage of animals, and that the administration of justice is what holds a state together.
For both writers, justice administered by the ruler is the foundation on which the rest of human goodness stands.

Real Life Example

Goryeo carves the Tripitaka Koreana
1237-1248 · Goryeo Korea (the woodblocks are now at Haeinsa Temple, South Korea)
During the Mongol invasions of Korea, which began in 1231, the Goryeo court under King Gojong undertook to carve the entire Buddhist canon onto wooden printing blocks, in the belief that the Buddha's protection could help drive out the invaders. The work, carried out between 1237 and 1248, produced more than 81,000 blocks. The woodblocks are still kept at Haeinsa Temple, where they are inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register and their storage halls are a World Heritage Site.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
In a time of invasion, royal authority and resources preserved a complete body of sacred learning that has survived for nearly eight centuries.
The kural says the king's rule is the foundation that keeps the scriptures of the sages and the dharma they teach standing, and the Goryeo court used its power to preserve the whole Buddhist canon.
