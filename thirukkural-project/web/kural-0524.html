Kural 524

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

சுற்றத்தால் சுற்றப் படஒழுகல் செல்வந்தான் பெற்றத்தால் பெற்ற பயன்.
Sutraththaal sutrap pataozhukal selvandhaan Petraththaal petra payan.

Meaning

The real reward of becoming rich is living in a way that keeps your relatives gathered around you.

Elementary

Suppose you have a big box of snacks. If you eat them alone, it’s just snacks. If you share them, your cousins come and sit around you, laughing and talking. That circle of family is the real prize, not the snacks.

University

The couplet repeats sounds on purpose (சுற்றத்தால் சுற்றப்பட, பெற்றத்தால் பெற்ற) so that the verse itself seems to ‘surround’ its subject. Parimelazhagar glosses it as: the fruit a man gains from having gained wealth is to conduct himself, embracing his kin, so that he is encircled by them. He notes that in பெற்ற the final அ, and in அதனால் the particle அன், have dropped out for the sake of the rhyme. He explains that such conduct counts as the ‘fruit’ of wealth because it lets a king rule without enemies, and he treats it as the third term of the sequence cause–fortress–fruit (kurals 522-524). Pappaiah and Karunanidhi read it the same way. The gloss attributed to Mu. Varadarasan for this kural on thirukkural.net actually belongs to kural 468 and is evidently misplaced there.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The riches of the liberal never waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.1 (trans. R. T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Vedic hymn on liberality teaches that shared wealth lasts and wins companions, while hoarded wealth leaves its owner alone.
Wealth that gathers people round its owner is the Kural’s definition of what wealth is for.
Christianity
“But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.”
— 1 Timothy 5:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul makes providing for one’s own relatives a basic duty of faith.
Resources are meant to sustain one’s kin, which the Kural calls the very fruit of wealth.
Islam
“They ask you, [O Muhammad], what they should spend. Say, ‘Whatever you spend of good is [to be] for parents and relatives and orphans and the needy and the traveler.’”
— Qur’an 2:215 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an lists parents and relatives first among those on whom wealth should be spent.
Kin come first in the proper use of wealth, as the Kural says.
Judaism
The Torah commands that when a brother becomes poor and his hand fails, his kinsman must uphold him and let him live alongside him, lending without interest.
— Leviticus 25:35-37 [6]
The law treats supporting an impoverished relative as a duty of those who have means.
The Kural too sees the proper use of wealth as keeping one’s kin close and supported.
Buddhism
“performs the five oblations: to relatives, guests, the dead, kings, & devas. This is the fourth benefit that can be obtained from wealth.”
— Adiya Sutta, AN 5.41 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells Anathapindika the benefits to be gained from wealth, and offerings to relatives are one of them.
Both texts define the benefit of wealth partly by what it does for one’s kin.
Jainism
After the birth of Mahavira, King Siddhartha and Queen Trisala, whose silver, gold and riches had kept increasing from the time the child was conceived, invite their friends, relations, kinsmen, agnates and cognates, feed them, honour them with flowers, clothes, perfumes and ornaments, and before them name the boy Vardhamana, 'the increasing'.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §§ 104-107 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Kalpa Sutra shows a prosperous Jain royal household using its increase to gather and honour the whole circle of its kin.
Valluvar says the fruit of wealth is to be surrounded by one's kin; in the Kalpa Sutra the family's growing wealth is spent precisely on gathering and honouring the kindred.
Sikhism
Every Sikh gurdwara has its langar, a communal refectory, where all who come sit together in one straight line, none placed ahead to claim higher status or behind to mark inferiority; the distinctive Sikh langar arose as a protest against caste.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Sikhism: Sikh practice” by W. H. McLeod (paraphrase) [9]
The community's resources are spent on a common kitchen that gathers everyone, kin and stranger alike, around a shared meal.
The Kural says the real fruit of wealth is to live surrounded by one's people; the langar turns shared resources into a gathered community, though Sikhism widens that circle to everyone.
Greek Mythology
When Telemachus reaches Pylos, he finds old King Nestor seated among his sons and people at a great sacrifice on the shore. His wealth is spent on feasting, and he is surrounded by a loyal household.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 3 [10]
Nestor is the Homeric picture of a prosperous old age: a rich king surrounded by devoted sons.
His wealth takes the form of kin gathered round him, the image the Kural holds up.
Philosophy
“even rich men and those in possession of office and of dominating power are thought to need friends most of all; for what is the use of such prosperity without the opportunity of beneficence, which is exercised chiefly and in its most laudable form towards friends?”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that prosperity is pointless without people on whom to use it for good.
The value of wealth lies in the people it gathers and serves, which is what the Kural teaches about kin.

Real Life Example

Christian IX and Queen Louise gather their family at Fredensborg each summer
late 19th century · Fredensborg Palace, Denmark
King Christian IX of Denmark and Queen Louise saw their children marry into many of Europe's royal houses: their daughters included Alexandra, later Queen of the United Kingdom, and Dagmar, Empress of Russia, and their son George became King George I of Greece. Every summer the couple, known as Europe's parents-in-law, gathered their children, their children's spouses and their many grandchildren at Fredensborg Palace. The painter Laurits Tuxen recorded these Fredensborg Days in a group portrait about five by seven metres, made in 1883-1886, which now hangs in Christiansborg Palace in Copenhagen.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The king of a small country found the true reward of his position in a family circle that stretched across the continent and returned to him year after year.
The kural says the real fruit of gaining wealth and standing is to live surrounded by one's kin, and Christian IX's summers at Fredensborg were exactly such a life.
