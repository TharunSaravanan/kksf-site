Kural 1041

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

இன்மையின் இன்னாதது யாதெனின் இன்மையின் இன்மையே இன்னா தது.
Inmaiyin innaadhadhu yaadhenin inmaiyin inmaiyae innaa thadhu.

Meaning

If you ask what hurts more than being poor, the only answer is being poor itself. Nothing else hurts as much.

Elementary

Imagine someone asking, “What hurts more than a really bad toothache?” and the answer is, “Only a really bad toothache.” Valluvar says being so poor that you have nothing is like that: there is nothing else to compare it with, because it is the worst hurt of its kind.

University

The chapter opens with a deliberately circular definition built on repetition of inmai (இன்மை, ‘not-having, want’): inmaiyin innaadhadhu (‘what is more grievous than want’) is answered by inmaiyae (‘want itself’). Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar both gloss inmaiyin as a comparative (‘like poverty’), so the sense is that poverty is its own only standard of comparison; Kalaignar makes the same point by saying no other sorrow can serve as an upamai (simile) for it. The figure is a classic Tamil ananvaya-like device in which the subject is compared to itself to mark it as incomparable, and Pope’s ‘Nothing pains more than poverty, save poverty alone’ preserves the tautology. Nalkuravu (நல்குரவு) in the chapter title is the standard classical word for destitution; placed in the Kudiyiyal right after Uzhavu (ch. 104, agriculture, the remedy for want) and before Iravu (ch. 106, begging), the chapter traces the social consequences of losing one’s means. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net apart from spacing.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The man that is fallen, O king, grieveth, as also he that is poor. I do not see the difference between a fallen man and a poor man.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna, urging Yudhishthira not to renounce his kingdom, argues that poverty is a misery equal to moral or social ruin.
Like the Kural, Arjuna treats poverty as a sorrow that has no lesser comparison than utter downfall.
Christianity
“The rich man’s wealth is his strong city: the destruction of the poor is their poverty.”
— Proverbs 10:15 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs observes that the poor man’s ruin is nothing other than his poverty itself, while wealth is a fortress.
The proverb’s tautology, the poor are destroyed by their poverty, mirrors the Kural’s ‘poverty is worse than anything but poverty’.
Islam
“O Allah, I seek refuge in Thee from poverty”, lack and abasement, and I seek refuge in Thee lest I cause or suffer wrong.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 1544 (graded sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet regularly sought God’s protection from poverty, naming it alongside want and humiliation as evils to be shielded from.
Seeking refuge from poverty itself shows it was regarded, as in the Kural, as among the gravest afflictions.
Judaism
“There is nothing in the world as harsh as poverty, as it is harsher than all the suffering in the world. Our Rabbis said: All forms of suffering on one side, and poverty on the other.”
— Shemot Rabbah 31:12 (The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah, 2022) [6]
The Midrash weighs every other suffering on one side of the scale and poverty alone on the other, and finds poverty heavier.
This is almost exactly Valluvar’s claim that nothing is as painful as poverty.
Buddhism
“Hunger is the worst disease, conditioned things the worst suffering.”
— Dhammapada 203 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada names hunger, the most immediate face of want, as the worst of illnesses, before turning to the deeper suffering of conditioned existence.
Both texts single out want of food and means as a misery beyond comparison.
Jainism
“Though emaciated like the joint of a crow’s (leg) and covered with a network of veins, he should know the permitted measure of food and drink, and wander about with a cheerful mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Among the troubles (parishaha) a monk must bear, Mahavira lists hunger, and asks the monk to accept even extreme emaciation cheerfully.
Where the Kural calls want the worst pain, Jain discipline treats chosen want as a trial to be borne with a calm mind, an instructive contrast.
Sikhism
“Says Kabeer, he alone is poor,”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1159, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Closing a hymn about how the world despises the penniless, Bhagat Kabir turns the question around: the truly poor person is the one whose heart lacks the Lord's Name. Gurbani thus admits the pain of material want while locating the deepest poverty elsewhere.
The Kural says nothing is more painful than poverty except poverty itself; Kabir agrees that poverty is the great affliction but redefines the worst poverty as spiritual emptiness.
Greek Mythology
“...he left Aeolian Cyme and fled, not from riches and substance, but from wretched poverty which Zeus lays upon men...”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 633-640 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod recalls that his father abandoned his homeland and crossed the sea simply to escape poverty, which he presents as a burden laid on men by Zeus.
The flight shows poverty as an affliction so heavy that a man will give up his home to escape it, echoing the Kural’s estimate of its pain.
Philosophy
“But if you are averse to sickness, or death, or poverty, you will be wretched.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 2 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus warns that fearing things outside our control, poverty among them, is what makes people miserable.
The Stoic answers the Kural from the other side: poverty is painful above all to the one who judges it an evil.

Real Life Example

Dadabhai Naoroji publishes Poverty and Un-British Rule in India
1901 · London, United Kingdom
Dadabhai Naoroji, the Indian nationalist who had served as a British MP, published Poverty and Un-British Rule in India in 1901 while living at Anerley Park in Penge, London. The book was the fullest statement of his drain theory, which held that India had been impoverished by high taxation and by an expensive foreign administration whose costs drained wealth to Britain, and that British policies had contributed to deadly famines.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Naoroji made Indian poverty itself the measure by which colonial rule was judged, and his drain theory became the basis of the classic Indian nationalist critique of British rule.
The kural says nothing is more grievous than poverty except poverty itself, and Naoroji built a lifetime's political argument on the conviction that India's poverty was the affliction beside which all others had to be measured.
