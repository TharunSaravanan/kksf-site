Kural 799

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

கெடுங்காலைக் கைவிடுவார் கேண்மை அடுங்காலை உள்ளினும் உள்ளஞ் சுடும்.
Kedungaalaik kaividuvaar kenmai adungaalai ullinum ullanj chudum.

Meaning

Friends who left you when you were in trouble will burn your heart, even if you only think of them at the hour of your death.

Elementary

Have you ever been left alone by a friend when you really needed help? That hurt can stay with you for a very long time, even when you are grown up. Valluvar says this hurt is so strong it can burn even at the very end of life.

University

Kedungaalai (கெடுங்காலை) is ‘at the time when one is being ruined’, and adungaalai (அடுங்காலை) is ‘at the time when one is being slain’, the hour of death; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as the time when Yama comes to kill, and Kalaignar reads it as the moment one is being put to death. Ullinum (உள்ளினும், ‘even if one recalls’) and ullam sudum (உள்ளம் சுடும், ‘the heart burns’) make a play on ul- (‘think’) and ullam (‘mind’). The force of the couplet is psychological: the wound of desertion outlasts every other pain, so one should never form such a friendship in the first place, which ties it back to the chapter’s theme of investigation. The sandhi-joined text reads கேண்மை யடுங்காலை யுள்ளினு முள்ளஞ் சுடும், the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Drona and Drupada were boyhood friends, and Drupada promised to share his kingdom one day; when the impoverished Drona later came to him, King Drupada scorned him, saying friendship is impossible between a king and a poor man. The humiliation burned in Drona for years, until he had his pupils, the Kuru princes, capture Drupada and paid him back by taking half his kingdom.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Sambhava Parva (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
In the Adi Parva, a friend’s rejection in a time of need leaves a wound that shapes Drona’s whole life and feeds the feud behind the great war.
It is the Kural’s burning memory of a friend who abandoned you when you were down.
Christianity
“To him that is afflicted pity should be shewed from his friend; but he forsaketh the fear of the Almighty. My brethren have dealt deceitfully as a brook, and as the stream of brooks they pass away;”
— Job 6:14-15 (KJV) [5]
In his suffering, Job complains that his friends have failed him like seasonal streams that dry up just when they are needed.
Job voices the burning pain of friends who fail in the time of ruin.
Islam
“Close friends, that Day, will be enemies to each other, except for the righteous”
— Qur’an 43:67 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse says that on the Day of Judgment friendships not built on righteousness will turn into hostility.
Like the Kural, it looks ahead to the final hour, when unworthy friendships are remembered with bitterness.
Judaism
“All love that depends on a something, [when the] thing ceases, [the] love ceases; and [all love] that does not depend on anything, will never cease. What is an example of love that depended on a something? Such was the love of Amnon for Tamar. And what is an example of love that did not depend on anything? Such was the love of David and Jonathan.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:16 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
The Mishnah explains that love based on some benefit vanishes when the benefit does, while unconditional love lasts.
It explains why the deserting friend left: his love depended on your good fortune, and when that ended, so did he.
Buddhism
“One is a bottle friend; one says, ‘friend, friend’ only to one’s face; one is a friend and an associate only when it is advantageous.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
The Buddha describes friends who are present only for drinking, flattery or advantage.
These are the friends who leave when ruin comes, whom the Kural says will burn in memory.
Jainism
“if friends from whom it is difficult to separate, possess not virtuous dispositions, they are as a fire kindled to burn our hearts.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 4 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The stanza says close friends who lack good character become a fire that burns the heart, because the bond is too close to break easily.
The Kural says the memory of friends who deserted you in ruin burns the heart even at the hour of death, and the Naladiyar uses the same image of the heart burning because of unworthy friends.
Sikhism
“My teeth, feet, eyes and ears have stopped working. My body cries out, “Those whom I knew have left me!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Salok Sheikh Farid 77 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In this salok Sheikh Farid, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, speaks in the voice of an old man near death whose body laments that the people close to him have gone.
The Kural says the memory of friends who left you as you were falling burns the heart even at the hour of death; Farid's dying body likewise cries out over those who have left it.
Greek Mythology
On the way to Troy, the Greek leaders abandon the wounded archer Philoctetes alone on the island of Lemnos because his festering wound is unbearable to them; in Sophocles’ play, ten years later, his bitterness toward Odysseus and the sons of Atreus is still so fierce that he would rather die than help them.
— Sophocles, Philoctetes [11]
Sophocles’ Philoctetes is an extended study of the lasting resentment of a man deserted by his comrades in his worst hour.
His undying anger is the Kural’s burning heart, kindled by friends who left him when he was falling.
Philosophy
“it is in these two ways that most people betray their untrustworthiness and inconstancy, by looking down on friends when they are themselves prosperous, or deserting them in their distress.”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia §17 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [12]
Laelius names deserting a friend in distress as one of the two commonest betrayals of friendship.
Cicero names the very betrayal whose memory, the Kural says, burns at the last.

Real Life Example

Napoleon names Marmont's betrayal in his last will
15 April 1821 · Longwood, St Helena
Auguste Marmont had been one of Napoleon's closest companions since meeting him at the siege of Toulon in 1793, serving as his aide-de-camp and later becoming a marshal. In April 1814, with Napoleon's position collapsing, Marmont went over to the Allies with his army corps, a defection that helped force Napoleon's abdication and made the word ragusade, from Marmont's title of duke of Ragusa, a byword for treachery. In exile on St Helena, as his strength was failing, Napoleon wrote out his will on 15 April 1821, and in it he named Marmont first among those whose treason he blamed for the invasions of France, while declaring that he forgave them.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15] [16]
Even in his final weeks, Napoleon's mind returned to the old friend who had abandoned him at his fall.
The kural says the memory of friends who abandoned you when you were falling will burn the heart even at the hour of death, and Napoleon named Marmont's desertion in his last will.
