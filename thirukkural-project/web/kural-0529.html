Kural 529

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

தமராகித் தற்றுறந்தார் சுற்றம் அமராமைக் காரணம் இன்றி வரும்.
Thamaraakith thatrurandhaar sutram amaraamaik Kaaranam indri varum.

Meaning

Relatives who were once close but left because of some hurt will come back on their own once the cause of the fight is gone.

Elementary

Suppose your cousin stopped coming over because you kept teasing him. If you stop teasing, he’ll start coming back all by himself. When you fix the reason someone left, the friendship often heals itself.

University

Parimelazhagar construes the couplet as: when those who were once one’s own (தமர்) have left because of some cause, their becoming kin again ‘comes’ (வரும்) of itself once that cause of discord (அமராமைக் காரணம்) no longer exists in oneself. He infers from the phrase that the cause really did exist before, and names it as the king’s own conduct, such as straying from the right path or doing things that displease. Because the kin were naturally affectionate and the estrangement was artificial, it ends when its cause ends; the ruler need not court them again, since they ‘return’ to their old nature. Pappaiah reads the kural in modern political terms, as members returning to a movement once the leader’s fault is gone. Karunanidhi reads it differently: those who left on some pretext come back once they see the pretext does not hold. Thirukkural.net prints தமராகிக் in the first word, but Parimelazhagar’s text on Wikisource and the standard editions read தமராகித், which is the reading adopted here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let, therefore, thy sons cherish the Pandavas, and the Pandavas also cherish thy sons. O king, let the Kurus and the Pandavas, both having same friends and same foes, live together in happiness and prosperity.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 36 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura urges Dhritarashtra to remove the cause of the family rift, his sons’ injustice, so that the estranged cousins can be reunited.
Remove the cause of the estrangement and the kin come back, as the Kural says.
Christianity
“And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.”
— Luke 15:20 (KJV) [4]
The estranged son returns home and is welcomed without conditions.
A kinsman who has left comes back and the old bond resumes at once, as the Kural predicts.
Islam
“Al-Wasil is not the one who recompenses the good done to him by his relatives, but Al-Wasil is the one who keeps good relations with those relatives who had severed the bond of kinship with him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5991 (Book of Adab) [5]
The Prophet defines the true maintainer of kinship as the one who restores ties with relatives who have broken them off.
Keeping the way open for estranged kin to return is the spirit of the Kural.
Judaism
Twenty years after Jacob fled Esau’s anger over the stolen blessing, the brothers meet again, and Esau runs to meet Jacob, embraces him, falls on his neck and kisses him, and they weep together.
— Genesis 33:4 [6]
Once the old grievance has faded, the estranged brother comes back and the kinship bond is restored.
When the cause of estrangement is gone, kin return, as the Kural says.
Buddhism
When the Sakyas and Koliyas, kin connected by marriage, are about to go to war over the waters of the Rohini river, the Buddha removes the cause of the quarrel by asking whether water is worth more than the lives of princes. The kinsmen are reconciled.
— Dhammapada Commentary on verses 197-199; Kunala Jataka (Jataka No. 536), introduction [7]
Once the cause of the dispute is set aside, the two kin clans return to peace.
Remove the cause and estranged kin come back together, as the Kural says.
Jainism
The brothers Bharata and Bahubali, sons of Rishabhanatha, fall out when Bharata demands his brother's submission and they fight; Bahubali renounces the world, but his omniscience comes only once he lets go of the pride that kept him from bowing to his brothers, and Bharata comes to honour him.
— Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra of Hemachandra, Book 1 (Bharata and Bahubali episode) (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS) [8]
In the Jain account the rift between brothers is mended once its cause, pride and possessiveness, has gone.
The Kural says kin who left over a grievance return of themselves once the cause of the rift is removed; the Bharata-Bahubali story shows estranged brothers reconciled once the pride between them has gone.
Sikhism
During the siege of Anandpur forty Sikhs signed a disclaimer (bedava) renouncing Guru Gobind Singh and went home; shamed by Mai Bhag Kaur (Mai Bhago), they returned to fight beside him at Khidrana in 1705, and the Guru, finding their dying leader Maha Singh, forgave them and tore up the disclaimer. The place was renamed Muktsar, the Pool of Liberation.
— Wikipedia, “Battle of Muktsar” (Forty Liberated Ones), paraphrase of traditional accounts [9]
Those who had broken away came back of their own accord, and the Guru received them as his own again.
The Kural says kin who left over some grievance return by themselves once the cause of the rift is gone; the Forty Liberated Ones returned and were fully restored to their Guru.
Greek Mythology
Achilles withdraws from the Greek army because Agamemnon has insulted him by taking his prize Briseis. Once Agamemnon admits his fault and the grievance is set right in Book 19, Achilles returns to the fight.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 1, 9 and 19 [10]
An estranged comrade returns once the leader’s wrong that caused his withdrawal has been removed.
Remove the leader’s own fault and those who left come back, which is Parimelazhagar’s reading of the Kural.
Philosophy
“so to those who have been our friends we ought to make some allowance for our former friendship, when the breach has not been due to excess of wickedness.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle holds that a former bond still has a claim on us after a rift, provided the rift did not come from real wickedness.
An old bond whose rift has a removable cause can be restored, which is the Kural’s point.

Real Life Example

Hungary is reconciled with the Habsburg crown in the Compromise of 1867
1867 · Vienna, Austria, and Budapest, Hungary
After the Hungarian revolution of 1848-49 was put down, Hungary was ruled directly from Vienna, and many Hungarians answered with stubborn passive resistance, boycotting the imperial parliament set up in 1861. Led by the moderate Ferenc Deak, who insisted on Hungary's constitutional rights, Hungary negotiated the Compromise (Ausgleich) concluded on 8 February 1867, under which Emperor Franz Joseph gave Hungary full internal self-government with its own responsible ministry, while Hungary accepted that the empire remain a single state for war and foreign affairs. On 8 June 1867 Franz Joseph was crowned King of Hungary in Buda.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Once the grievance that had driven Hungary away, rule without its constitution, was removed, the estranged kingdom returned to its allegiance to the dynasty.
The kural says those who left because of a grievance come back of their own accord once its cause is gone, and Hungary returned to its crowned king when its constitution was restored.
