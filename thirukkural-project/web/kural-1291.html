Kural 1291

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

அவர்நெஞ்சு அவர்க்காதல் கண்டும் எவன்நெஞ்சே நீஎமக்கு ஆகா தது.
Avarnenju avarkkaadhal kandum evannenjae nee-emakku aakaa thadhu.

Meaning

My heart, you have seen how his heart stays on his side alone. So why do you not stay on my side?

Elementary

Imagine two friends on a team. One friend’s helper always sticks by him, but your own helper keeps running over to the other side. You would ask, ‘Why aren’t you on my side?’ The wife in this poem asks her own heart that same question.

University

The chapter title neñcoṭu pulattal means ‘sulking with (one’s own) heart’: in the kaṟpu (wedded love) section, the husband has returned, the wife wishes to stage a lovers’ quarrel (ūṭal/pulavi) over his long absence, but her heart refuses to cooperate and rushes toward him, so she turns her reproach on the heart itself. Here she observes that avar neñcu avarkku ātal (‘his heart being his own’, i.e. siding with him and not thinking of her) is plain to see, yet her heart (neñcē) will not be emakku (‘ours’, on her side). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read ātal as ‘becoming a companion/ally (tuṇai)’, and Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar add that her heart keeps melting toward him instead; Pope renders it ‘You see his heart is his alone; O heart, why not be all my own?’ The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the chapter title is printed நெஞ்சொடுபுலத்தல் on ytamizh and நெஞ்சோடு புலத்தல் on thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Katha Upanishad pictures the self as the rider in a chariot, the body as the chariot, intelligence as the charioteer and the mind as the reins, with the senses as horses; for one without understanding, the senses run wild like vicious horses that will not obey their driver.
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.3-6 (paraphrase) [3]
The image shows the mind as something meant to serve the self but able to pull away from it.
The wife’s heart behaves like reins slipping from the driver’s hand, running toward the husband instead of obeying her.
Christianity
“For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do.”
— Romans 7:19 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes a self divided against itself, wanting one thing while doing another.
The wife meets the same split: she wills to hold back from her husband, yet her heart goes the other way.
Islam
“And I do not acquit myself. Indeed, the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon which my Lord has mercy. Indeed, my Lord is Forgiving and Merciful.”
— Qur’an 12:53 (Saheeh International) [5]
In the story of Yusuf, this confession admits that one’s own soul (nafs) pushes toward what the person knows she should resist.
Like the Kural’s speaker, the confessor admits that her own inner self has not been on her side.
Judaism
“Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why moanest thou within me? Hope thou in God; for I shall yet praise Him for the salvation of His countenance.”
— Psalms 42:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The psalmist speaks to his own soul as if it were a second person, questioning and rebuking it.
The Kural uses the same form, a person addressing her own heart and asking why it acts as it does.
Buddhism
“Whatever harm an enemy may do to an enemy, or a hater to a hater, an ill-directed mind inflicts on oneself a greater harm.”
— Dhammapada 42 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that one’s own badly aimed mind can hurt a person more than any outside enemy.
The wife finds that her own heart, not her husband, is the one working against her.
Jainism
“My own Self is the doer and undoer of misery and happiness; my own Self, friend and foe, according as I act well or badly.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Anathi teaches King Srenika that a person’s own self can be either friend or enemy.
The wife complains that her heart, which ought to be her friend, has gone over to the other side.
Sikhism
“My mind longs for the Blessed Vision of the Guru's Darshan.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 96, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes a mind that runs of its own accord toward the one it loves, longing only to see him.
The Kural's heart has already gone over to the beloved and will not stay with the speaker; Guru Arjan's mind likewise belongs wholly to the one it longs for.
Greek Mythology
In a short fragment Sappho says that she does not know what to do, because her mind is split in two.
— Sappho, fragment 51 (paraphrase; cf. tr. D.A. Campbell, Greek Lyric I) [10]
The poet describes love leaving her inner self divided and unable to settle.
The Kural’s wife feels the same split: one part of her wants to sulk while her heart wants to run to him.
Philosophy
Pascal writes that the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées 277 (Brunschvicg numbering; paraphrase) [11]
Pascal holds that the heart moves by a logic of its own that the reasoning mind cannot overrule.
The wife’s reason says to hold back; her heart, following its own reasons, goes to him anyway.

Real Life Example

Nietzsche’s marriage proposal to Lou Salomé is refused
1882 · Rome, Italy, and Leipzig, Germany
In 1882, at the writer Malwida von Meysenbug’s salon in Rome, the philosophers Friedrich Nietzsche and Paul Rée met Lou Salomé, a brilliant young Russian student. Both men fell in love with her, but she turned down their proposals of marriage and suggested instead that the three live and study together; apart from three weeks sharing an apartment in Leipzig, the plan came to nothing. After Nietzsche’s unsuccessful proposal, Salomé and Rée left together for Berlin, and the friendship ended painfully for him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nietzsche gave his heart completely while Salomé kept hers firmly her own.
The kural’s speaker complains that the beloved’s heart stays loyal to him alone while her own heart runs off to him; Nietzsche’s heart went to Salomé while hers remained entirely her own.
