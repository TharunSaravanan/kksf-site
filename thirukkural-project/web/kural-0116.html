Kural 116

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

கெடுவல்யான் என்பது அறிகதன் நெஞ்சம் நடுவொரீஇ அல்ல செயின்.
Keduval yaan enbadhu arika than nenjam Naduvoreei alla seyin.

Meaning

If your heart leaves fairness and turns to doing wrong, take it as a sure sign: ‘I am going to be ruined.’

Elementary

When a car starts drifting out of its lane, a warning beeps. This kural says that when your heart starts wanting to be unfair, that is your warning beep: trouble is coming if you don’t steer back.

University

The couplet is written as an internal monologue: கெடுவல் யான் (“I shall perish”) is what one should say to oneself the moment the heart நடு ஒரீஇ (“having abandoned the middle”) turns toward அல்ல (“what is not [right]”). Commentators differ on emphasis. Varadarasanar and Karunanidhi stress the intention itself as the omen (“if the heart even thinks of it”), while Pope renders it “let man impending ruin’s sign discern.” Either way, injustice is presented as self-destructive, not merely harmful to others. This matches the Manusmriti’s maxim that violated justice destroys the violator, the Qur’anic warning to David that following desire in judgment leads astray, and Plato’s argument that injustice is a disease of the soul.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Justice, being violated, destroys; justice, being preserved, preserves: therefore justice must not be violated, lest violated justice destroy us.”
— Manusmriti 8.15 (trans. G. Bühler, Sacred Books of the East vol. 25) [4]
The Laws of Manu state the principle dharmo rakṣati rakṣitaḥ: dharma (justice) destroys whoever violates it and protects whoever protects it.
An almost exact counterpart: whoever abandons justice should know it will destroy them.
Christianity
“Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap.”
— Galatians 6:7 (KJV) [5]
Paul warns that a person inevitably reaps the consequences of what they sow.
Both warn that wrongdoing carries its own ruin back to the doer.
Islam
“O David, indeed We have made you a successor upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth and do not follow [your own] desire, as it will lead you astray from the way of Allah.”
— Qur’an 38:26 (Saheeh International) [6]
God tells King David to judge by truth and not by personal inclination, warning that following desire leads astray and to punishment.
The same inner movement as the Kural: when the heart leaves fairness for desire, ruin follows.
Judaism
“He that soweth iniquity shall reap vanity: and the rod of his anger shall fail.”
— Proverbs 22:8 (KJV) [5]
The proverb says whoever sows injustice will harvest emptiness, and his power will fail.
Injustice leads to the doer’s own ruin, the self-warning of the Kural.
Buddhism
“Whoever through desire, hate or fear, Or ignorance should transgress the Dhamma, All his glory fades away Like the moon during the waning half.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha names the four biases (agati: desire, hatred, fear, delusion) that lead people to act wrongly, and says whoever is swayed by them declines like the waning moon.
The agati are exactly the forces that pull a heart away from the “middle.” The Kural and the Sutta both predict decline.
Jainism
“A cut-throat enemy will not do him such harm as his own perversity will do him; the man without pity will feel repentance in the hour of death.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The sermon to King Śreṇika warns that a person's own inner corruption ruins them more surely than any outside foe.
Valluvar asks one whose heart turns from fairness to recognise it as the sign of their own ruin, and the Uttarādhyayana likewise names the perverted heart as the real destroyer.
Sikhism
“Do not harbor evil intentions against others in your mind, and you shall not be troubled, O Siblings of Destiny, O friends.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 386, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan ties freedom from suffering to keeping ill will toward others out of one's heart.
The Kural tells the person whose heart turns to wrong to read it as a sign of coming ruin; Gurbani teaches the converse: keep the heart free of ill intent and trouble will not come.
Greek Mythology
“And there is virgin Justice, the daughter of Zeus, who is honoured and reverenced among the gods who dwell on Olympus, and whenever anyone hurts her with lying slander, she sits beside her father, Zeus the son of Cronos, and tells him of men’s wicked heart, until the people pay for the mad folly …”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 256–262 (trans. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod portrays Dike (Justice) as a goddess who reports every injury done to her to Zeus, who then punishes the wrongdoers.
Both warn that turning away from justice brings certain ruin, and Hesiod even locates the problem in the “wicked heart.”
Philosophy
“Then virtue is the health and beauty and well-being of the soul, and vice the disease and weakness and deformity of the same?”
— Plato, Republic IV, 444d–e (trans. Jowett) [11]
Socrates argues that injustice is to the soul what disease is to the body: it harms the unjust person first.
Like the Kural, it treats departure from justice as self-destruction.

Real Life Example

Former Prime Minister Tanaka and the Lockheed bribery scandal
1976 - 1983 · Tokyo, Japan
Tanaka Kakuei, prime minister of Japan from 1972 to 1974, was accused of having accepted, while in office, the equivalent of about 2 million US dollars from the American aircraft maker Lockheed to influence All Nippon Airways to buy Lockheed airliners. He was arrested on 27 July 1976, and in 1983 the Tokyo District Court found him guilty and sentenced him to four years in prison and a fine of 500 million yen. He remained free while appealing, suffered a serious stroke in 1985 after which his power and influence waned, and died in 1993 before the appeal was decided.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A leader at the height of power who turned public office toward private gain saw his reputation and influence destroyed, and the case became a lasting symbol of political corruption in Japan.
The kural warns that when the heart turns from fairness to wrongdoing, ruin is near; Tanaka's fall followed from a decision to sell the influence of his office.
