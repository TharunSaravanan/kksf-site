Kural 959

Lineage · Kudimai · குடிமை

நிலத்தில் கிடந்தமை கால்காட்டும் காட்டும் குலத்தில் பிறந்தார்வாய்ச் சொல்.
Nilaththil kidandhamai kaal kaattum kaattum kulaththil pirandhaarvaaich chol.

Meaning

A sprout shows what kind of soil it grew in. In the same way, a person's words show what kind of family they come from.

Elementary

A healthy green plant tells you the soil is good. In the same way, when someone speaks kindly and wisely, you can tell they grew up in a good home.

University

The image is agricultural: kaal (கால்) is read by Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan as mulai (முளை), the sprout or shoot, which reveals (kaattum, காட்டும்) what lay hidden in the soil (nilaththil kidandhamai, நிலத்தில் கிடந்தமை). Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar widen it to the crop that grows there. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads kaal in its common sense of 'foot', so that the soil clinging to one's feet shows where one has walked; this is a minority reading that the standard commentators do not follow. The repetition kaattum kaattum (காட்டும் காட்டும்) closes the simile and opens its application: so too the speech (vaaich chol, வாய்ச்சொல்) of those born in a family reveals that family. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the boy Satyakama asks to become a pupil, he honestly tells the sage Gautama that he does not know his father's name or his lineage, since his mother does not know it either; Gautama replies that no one but a Brahmin could speak so truthfully, and accepts him.
— Chandogya Upanishad 4.4 (tr. F. Max Müller, SBE 1) [3]
In the Chandogya Upanishad, the boy's truthful words reveal his worth when no family record can.
This is the Kural's claim in story form: a person's speech shows the lineage behind them.
Christianity
“Either make the tree good, and his fruit good; or else make the tree corrupt, and his fruit corrupt: for the tree is known by his fruit. O generation of vipers, how can ye, being evil, speak good things? for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”
— Matthew 12:33-34 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says a tree is known by what it produces, and a person by the words that come from the heart.
The same two steps as the Kural: what grows shows the source, and speech shows the person.
Islam
“Have you not considered how Allah presents an example, [making] a good word like a good tree, whose root is firmly fixed and its branches [high] in the sky?”
— Qur'an 14:24 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an compares a good word to a good tree, rooted firmly and reaching high.
Like the Kural, it joins speech and plant growth, with roots below and fruit above.
Judaism
“When two people quarrel with each other, they observe which of them becomes silent first. Then they say: This silent party is of finer lineage.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 71b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
The Talmud reports that in the Land of Israel people tested a family's lineage by how its members spoke in a quarrel.
Like the Kural, the rabbis read a person's ancestry from the way they use their mouth.
Buddhism
The Buddha says a person's discernment can be known through discussion, if one pays attention over a long time, just as integrity is known by living together and purity by dealings.
— Thana Sutta, AN 4.192 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The sutta teaches that a person's inner quality is revealed in how they speak and reason.
Like the Kural, it treats speech as the outward shoot that shows the inner soil.
Jainism
The monk Jayaghosha tells the priest Vijayaghosha that one does not become a Brahmana by uttering the sacred syllable om nor a Sramana by being tonsured, but by one's actions one becomes a Brahmana, a Kshatriya, a Vaisya or a Sudra.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 25 (The True Sacrifice), tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45 [8]
The Uttaradhyayana says that outward signs, including ritual words, do not prove one's class; conduct does.
It agrees with the Kural that what comes out of a person reveals what they are, but it denies that ritual speech alone proves birth.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, speaking insipid words, the body and mind become insipid.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar, salok of Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak teaches that harsh, loveless speech both shows and shapes the state of a person's whole being.
The Kural says a person's words reveal what they come from as a sprout reveals its soil; Guru Nanak says one's words reveal, and even make, what one is.
Greek Mythology
“It is plain you take after your father. One can soon see when a man is son to one whom heaven has blessed both as regards wife and offspring.”
— Homer, Odyssey 4 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
After young Pisistratus speaks with tact at the dinner table, Menelaus recognises Nestor's wisdom in his words.
This is the Kural's claim in Homer: a young man's speech shows the family he comes from.
Philosophy
Seneca quotes a Greek proverb that a man's speech is just like his life, and argues that the style of a person's words reflects the state of their character.
— Seneca, Moral Letters 114 (tr. R. M. Gummere) [11]
For Seneca, the way someone speaks is an index of their inner life.
Like the Kural's sprout, speech shows what lies beneath.

Real Life Example

Shaw's Pygmalion has its premiere in Vienna
16 October 1913 · Hofburg Theatre, Vienna, Austria
George Bernard Shaw's play Pygmalion was first performed at the Hofburg Theatre in Vienna on 16 October 1913, in a German translation by Siegfried Trebitsch, and opened in London in April 1914. Its hero, the phonetics professor Henry Higgins, wagers that by changing the speech of a Cockney flower seller he can pass her off as a duchess. Shaw described it as a play about phonetics, and it became a classic comedy about language and the English class system.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Pygmalion made famous the idea that the way a person speaks immediately reveals their background to others.
The kural says a person's words show the family they come from as surely as a sprout shows the soil, which is the observation on which Shaw built his whole play.
