Kural 418

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

கேட்பினும் கேளாத் தகையவே கேள்வியால் தோட்கப் படாத செவி.
Ketpinum kelaath thagaiyave kelviyaal Thotkap padaadha sevi.

Meaning

Ears that have not been pierced (opened up) by good teaching are no better than deaf ears, even though they hear sounds.

Elementary

Your ears can hear noises all day, but if you never really listen to wise lessons, it’s almost like your ears don’t work. Real hearing means letting good lessons get inside.

University

The pun turns on கேள் (‘hear’): ears may கேட்பினும் (hear sounds) yet be கேளாத் தகைய (of the nature of not hearing, i.e., deaf) if not தோட்கப்பட்ட — ‘bored, pierced’ — by கேள்வி. The piercing alludes to the ear-boring ritual (and earrings) of Tamil custom: the true ornament and opening of the ear is instruction. The distinction between physiological hearing and receptive understanding is the heart of the kural and finds close echoes in prophetic and philosophical literature on ‘hearing without hearing’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Katha Upanishad says of the Self: many do not even get to hear of it, and many, even hearing of it, do not comprehend it; wonderful is the teacher and wonderful the one who learns it.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.7 [4]
Mere hearing is not enough — receptive understanding is rare.
The Kural’s ‘ears that hear yet are deaf’ in Upanishadic form.
Christianity
“Therefore speak I to them in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand.”
— Matthew 13:13 (KJV) [5]
Jesus distinguishes physical hearing from receptive understanding.
Almost verbatim the Kural’s paradox: hearing, yet not hearing.
Islam
“Deaf, dumb and blind — so they will not return [to the right path].”
— Qur’an 2:18 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an describes those who reject guidance as deaf though their physical hearing is intact.
Ears untouched by guidance are counted as deaf — as in the Kural.
Judaism
“Hear now this, O foolish people, and without understanding; which have eyes, and see not; which have ears, and hear not:”
— Jeremiah 5:21 (KJV) [5]
Jeremiah rebukes a people whose senses function but whose understanding is closed.
Same paradox as the Kural: working ears, no real hearing.
Buddhism
“Though all his life a fool associates with a wise man, he no more comprehends the Truth than a spoon tastes the flavor of the soup.”
— Dhammapada 64 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
Proximity to teaching does not guarantee reception; the fool is like a spoon that touches soup without tasting it.
The fool’s ears, like the spoon, contact wisdom without taking it in — the Kural’s ‘unpierced ear’.
Jainism
“Though men destitute of rectitude listen to those who are destitute of envy, when they declare the way of virtue, yet they give no heed, just as the chakler's dog, which seizes and devours leather, knows not how to appreciate the taste of rice and milk.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 33 'Defective knowledge', stanza 2 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar describes people who physically hear the teaching of virtue yet take none of it in, like a dog used to leather that cannot taste good food.
Valluvar says ears never pierced by instruction are as good as deaf, even though they hear sounds. The Naladiyar shows the same kind of hearing, where words arrive and nothing enters.
Sikhism
“O my ears, you were created only to hear the Truth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 922, Raag Raamkalee, Anand Sahib (pauri 37), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Anand Sahib Guru Amar Das tells his ears that their purpose is to hear the Truth and the Name.
The Kural says ears not opened by instruction are as good as deaf; Guru Amar Das says ears exist to hear the Truth, so ears that do not are failing their purpose.
Greek Mythology
Cassandra, cursed by Apollo, prophesies truly the fall of Troy and Agamemnon’s murder, but those who hear her words do not take them in; in Aeschylus the chorus hears her plainly yet cannot understand.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1072–1330 [10]
The Trojans and later the Argive elders hear the truth but remain effectively deaf to it.
A mythic image of ears that hear yet do not hear.
Philosophy
Heraclitus: those who do not understand, even when they have heard, are like the deaf; the saying bears witness of them — ‘present, they are absent’.
— Heraclitus, fragment DK 22 B34 [11]
Heraclitus distinguishes hearing the logos from understanding it; the unreceptive are functionally deaf.
Nearly identical to the Kural: hearing without instruction is deafness.

Real Life Example

Titanic's ice warnings go unheeded
14-15 April 1912 · North Atlantic Ocean
On 14 April 1912, during her maiden voyage, RMS Titanic received a series of wireless warnings from other ships about ice ahead; most were passed to the bridge, but a late message from the Mesaba reporting heavy pack ice and many large icebergs was never delivered because the operator was busy sending passengers' messages. The ship did not slow down, struck an iceberg shortly before midnight, and sank early on 15 April with the loss of more than 1,500 lives. The United States inquiry criticised the captain for failing to reduce speed after receiving ice warnings, and the disaster led to round-the-clock radio watches and the International Ice Patrol.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The disaster showed that warnings which are heard but not heeded give no protection.
The kural says ears that have not been opened by instruction are as good as deaf even though they hear sounds, and the Titanic received warning after warning without letting them change its course or speed.
