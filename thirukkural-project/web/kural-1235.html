Kural 1235

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

கொடியார் கொடுமை உரைக்கும் தொடியொடு தொல்கவின் வாடிய தோள்.
Kotiyaar kodumai uraikkum thotiyodu tholkavin vaadiya thoal.

Meaning

My thin shoulders, their old beauty gone and their bangles slipping off, tell everyone how cruel the hard-hearted one was to me.

Elementary

Sometimes our bodies tell a story we don’t say out loud, like a droopy face when we’re upset. Here the girl’s thin arms and falling bangles seem to tell the whole town, “Look how much he has hurt her by staying away so long.”

University

Koṭiyār koṭumai uraikkum (‘they speak the cruelty of the cruel one’) is an emphatic figura etymologica: koṭiyār (‘the cruel/crooked one’, honorific plural used of the husband) and koṭumai (‘cruelty’) share the root koṭu, ‘bent, harsh’. The second line repeats 1234’s tolkaviṉ vāṭiya tōḷ verbatim, a deliberate echo, while toṭiyoṭu (‘together with the bangles’) makes the slipping bracelets co-witnesses. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses koṭiyār as ‘the cruel one who does not feel my suffering’; Puliyur Kesikan adds that the shoulders say it ‘for the town to know’ (ūraṟiya). The accusation is ventured here only to be withdrawn in 1236, where the heroine is pained to hear him so called. Pope: ‘These wasted arms, the bracelet with their wonted beauty gone, / The cruelty declare of that most cruel one.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, when Krishna finally comes to Radha at dawn bearing the marks of a night spent with another, she sends him away in reproach, calling him faithless and telling him to go back to the one he chose.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 8 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Barbara Stoler Miller) [3]
Jayadeva gives the wronged heroine a sharp, public voice of reproach against her lover.
The Kural’s heroine lets her wasted shoulders make the same charge of cruelty on her behalf.
Christianity
“Look not upon me, because I am black, because the sun hath looked upon me: my mother’s children were angry with me; they made me the keeper of the vineyards; but mine own vineyard have I not kept.”
— Song of Solomon 1:6 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song explains her changed looks as the visible result of others’ harshness.
The Kural’s heroine likewise points to her altered body as proof of the harsh treatment she has suffered.
Islam
“And if a woman fears from her husband contempt or evasion, there is no sin upon them if they make terms of settlement between them - and settlement is best.”
— Qur’an 4:128 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an names a husband’s neglect or turning away as a real wrong to a wife and urges reconciliation.
The Kural’s heroine suffers exactly such ‘evasion’, and her body bears witness to it.
Judaism
“For the LORD hath called thee as a wife forsaken and grieved in spirit; and a wife of youth, can she be rejected? saith thy God.”
— Isaiah 54:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Isaiah uses the figure of a young wife left grieving to describe Israel, and protests that such a wife cannot rightly be cast off.
The Kural’s heroine is just such a ‘wife forsaken and grieved in spirit’, whose body cries out against the one who left.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha lists a husband’s duties to his wife: to honour her, not to disparage her, to be faithful, to give her authority in the home and to provide her with adornments.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Narada Thera) [7]
Buddhist lay ethics treats a husband’s neglect of his wife as a failure of duty.
The heroine’s bare, wasted arms indict a husband who has not fulfilled these duties, including the gift of ornaments that now fall away.
Jainism
In the Jain Ramayana, Prince Pavananjaya, offended on his wedding day, refuses even to look at his bride Anjana and neglects her for many years; she wastes away in grief, while those around her see the injustice of her lot.
— Hemachandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 7, story of Anjana and Pavananjaya (paraphrase; cf. tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
The story is the Jain tradition’s classic portrait of a faithful wife wasting under a husband’s cruelty.
Anjana’s wasted body makes the same silent accusation as the Kural’s shoulders and slipping bangles.
Sikhism
“If her Husband Lord does not return home, how can the soul-bride find peace? Her body is wasting away with the sorrow of separation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1108, Raag Tukhari, Barah Maha, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes the soul-bride whose Husband has not come home, her body wasting away from the grief of separation.
In the Kural the wife's faded shoulders and slipping bangles proclaim to everyone the cruelty of the one who left; Guru Nanak too makes the bride's wasting body the visible sign of the Husband's absence.
Greek Mythology
In Catullus’ telling, Ariadne, waking on Naxos to see Theseus’ ship sailing away, calls him faithless and forgetful, and cries out against the cruelty of the man for whom she gave up her home.
— Catullus, Poem 64.52-201 (paraphrase) [10]
The deserted Ariadne is the Roman poets’ model of a woman denouncing a cruel lover.
The Kural’s heroine also names her lover ‘the cruel one’ (koṭiyār), though she lets her body speak the charge.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Pausanias says the common, bodily lover is not constant, because he loves what is itself inconstant; when the bloom of youth he desired has faded, he takes wing and flies away, breaking all his promises.
— Plato, Symposium 183d-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Pausanias explains a lover’s desertion as the natural result of loving only the body’s bloom.
The Kural’s heroine fears this cruelty, her bloom gone, her lover away; Pausanias offers the philosophical diagnosis of such a lover.

Real Life Example

Octavia's loyalty turns Rome against Mark Antony
35-32 BCE · Rome, Italy
Octavia, sister of Octavian, the future Emperor Augustus, married Mark Antony in 40 BCE to seal the alliance between the two men. After Antony returned to Cleopatra in the East and sent Octavia back to Rome, she went on living in his house as if he were at home, raising her own children and his children by his earlier marriage. Plutarch records that her loyal conduct, without her intending it, damaged Antony, because people hated him for wronging such a woman; he divorced her in 32 BCE.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Octavia's faithful conduct did more to expose Antony's treatment of her than any accusation could have done.
The kural's wasted shoulders and slipping bangles proclaim the cruelty of the one who left, and Octavia's visible, faithful waiting proclaimed Antony's cruelty to all of Rome.
