Kural 875

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

தன்துணை இன்றால் பகையிரண்டால் தான்ஒருவன் இன்துணையாக் கொள்கவற்றின் ஒன்று.
Thandhunai indraal pakaiyirantaal thaanoruvan indhunaiyaak kolkavatrin ondru.

Meaning

If you have no one to help you and you face two enemies alone, make one of them your friend.

Elementary

Suppose two bigger kids are both upset with you and nobody is on your side. Instead of fighting both, you make peace with one of them, and suddenly it is two against one in your favour. That is the smart move the poet recommends.

University

This is the most explicitly strategic couplet of the chapter. Three conditions are stacked in clipped clauses: taṉtuṇai iṉṟāl (‘if one has no support’), pakai iraṇṭāl (‘if the enemies are two’), tāṉ oruvaṉ (‘and one is alone’). The remedy is to take one of the two as iṉtuṇai (இன்துணை), a ‘sweet’ or agreeable ally. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it as a general maxim, while Solomon Pappaiah again frames it for a ruler; Sivayogi Sivakumar keeps it close to the text. It complements 874: where that couplet praised turning enemies into friends as a virtue, this one treats it as a necessity of statecraft, in line with the Indian political tradition of alliances among neighbouring powers. The text is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthashastra treats foreign policy as a circle of kings and recommends that a weaker king make peace or alliance (sandhi) with one power when he cannot stand against several.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Books 6-7 [3]
The classical Indian manual of statecraft makes alliance with one party a standard response to weakness.
The Kural gives the same advice in miniature: alone against two, ally with one.
Christianity
“Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace.”
— Luke 14:32 (KJV) [4]
A king who sees he is outmatched sends envoys to make peace before battle.
The Kural likewise advises turning a foe into a friend when one cannot win alone.
Islam
“And if they incline to peace, then incline to it [also] and rely upon Allāh. Indeed, it is He who is the Hearing, the Knowing.”
— Qur’an 8:61 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an tells the Prophet to accept peace when an adversary offers it.
Both texts endorse turning an adversary into a partner in peace when possible.
Judaism
Hunted by King Saul and without support in Israel, David went over to Achish, the Philistine king of Gath, and lived under his protection.
— 1 Samuel 27 [4]
Facing a deadly pursuer, David found safety by allying himself with another of Israel’s enemies.
He acts out the Kural’s advice: alone and threatened, take one hostile power as an ally.
Buddhism
In the account of the Buddha’s last days, King Ajatasattu’s minister Vassakara is sent to ask about attacking the Vajjians; the Buddha explains they cannot be defeated while united, and the tradition later relates that they were overcome after being divided.
— Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 16 [6]
The story shows how dividing a set of opponents changes the balance of power.
It is the reverse side of the Kural’s strategy: separating two hostile parties so one can be won over.
Jainism
“Knowing the thoughts of their pupils, both Keśi and Gautama made up their minds to meet each other. Gautama, knowing what is proper and what is due to the older section (of the church), went to the Tinduka park, accompanied by the crowd, his pupils. When Keśi, the young monk, saw Gautama approach, he received him with all becoming attention.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23.14–16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The followers of Pārśva and of Mahāvīra were uneasy about their differences, so the two leaders met and turned a possible rivalry into union, and Keśi in the end adopted Mahāvīra's five vows.
The Kural advises a man facing two adversaries to make one of them a friendly ally, and Gautama's courteous approach to Keśi turns a potential rival community into an ally.
Sikhism
Britannica records that the Mughal emperor Jahangir, who had imprisoned Guru Hargobind in Gwalior fort, finally released him, apparently seeking the Sikhs' favour as possible allies against the Indian states still defying Mughal rule.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Hargobind (paraphrase) [8]
Facing several opponents at once, the emperor chose to turn one adversary, the Sikhs, into a potential ally rather than keep fighting on every front.
The Kural advises one who faces two enemies alone to make one of them an ally; Jahangir's release of Guru Hargobind is an instance of that same calculation.
Greek Mythology
In the war against the Titans, Zeus freed the Hundred-Handers whom their father had imprisoned, and with them as allies he defeated the Titans.
— Hesiod, Theogony 617-735 [9]
Zeus won a war he could not win alone by bringing estranged powers to his side.
It reflects the Kural’s strategy of gaining an ally to tip the balance.
Philosophy
Machiavelli argues that a prince gains respect by declaring himself openly for one side, and warns that neutrality usually leaves one prey to the winner.
— Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 21 [10]
He advises taking an ally rather than standing alone between rival powers.
Like the Kural, he sees lone neutrality between two threats as dangerous and alliance as the remedy.

Real Life Example

President Nixon visits China
21-28 February 1972 · Beijing, China
Facing a Cold War rivalry with the Soviet Union and two decades of hostility with the People's Republic of China, the Nixon administration opened a secret channel to Beijing through Pakistan. Nixon travelled to China from 21 to 28 February 1972, becoming the first US president to visit the mainland while in office, and the visit ended with the Shanghai Communique. The State Department's historians record that the administration saw the Sino-Soviet split as an opportunity to build a relationship with Beijing that would counterweigh Moscow, and a US-Soviet summit followed in May 1972.
— Sources: [11] [12]
By reaching out to one of its two great adversaries, the United States changed the balance of the Cold War and gained leverage with the other.
The kural advises that one facing two enemies should make one of them an ally, which is exactly the strategy of the opening to China.
