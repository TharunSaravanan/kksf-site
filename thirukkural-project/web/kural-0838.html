Kural 838

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

மையல் ஒருவன் களித்தற்றால் பேதைதன் கையொன்று உடைமை பெறின்.
Maiyal oruvan kaliththatraal pedhaidhan kaiyondru udaimai perin.

Meaning

When a fool gets hold of something valuable, they are like a person who is already out of their mind and then gets drunk too.

Elementary

Picture a kid who is already running wild at a party and then gets a big bag of sugar. Things go from silly to much worse! Giving power or money to someone who doesn’t think clearly only makes their foolishness bigger.

University

The simile doubles the condition: maiyal (மையல், bewilderment, madness) is a man already deranged, and kaliththal (களித்தல்) is intoxication by toddy, so the fool who gains udaimai (உடைமை, possession) is a madman who is also drunk. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both gloss maiyal as piththu (பித்து, madness) and kaliththal as being dazed by kal (கள், toddy), and Puliyur Kesigan adds that such a man drinks more and more and strays from his course. Kai ondru (கை ஒன்று) is read as ‘anything in his hand’, so the point holds for any wealth or authority, not only great fortune. Pope (‘as when a mad man raves in drunken glee’) and Drew follow the same reading, and the two base sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The demoniac person thinks: ‘So much wealth do I have today, and I will gain more according to my schemes. So much is mine now, and it will increase in the future, more and more…’ In this way, such persons are deluded by ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.13-15 (As It Is; excerpt) [3]
The Gita shows wealth swelling the deluded person’s arrogance into a kind of intoxication.
Valluvar likewise says possession makes an already confused mind drunk.
Christianity
“And I will say to my soul, Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry. But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided?”
— Luke 12:19-20 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the rich fool, abundance makes a man complacent and heedless of what matters.
Both texts show possessions intoxicating the fool rather than steadying him.
Islam
“And do not give the weak-minded your property, which Allāh has made a means of sustenance for you, but provide for them with it and clothe them and speak to them words of appropriate kindness.”
— Qur’an 4:5 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an forbids handing control of property to those who lack sound judgment, while still providing for them.
It rests on the same insight as the Kural: wealth in a fool’s hands does harm.
Judaism
“For three things the earth is disquieted, and for four which it cannot bear: For a servant when he reigneth; and a fool when he is filled with meat;”
— Proverbs 30:21-22 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Proverbs lists a fool who has plenty among the things the world cannot bear.
Valluvar likewise says a fool with possessions becomes worse, like a madman made drunk.
Buddhism
“Riches ruin only the foolish, not those in quest of the Beyond. By craving for riches the witless man ruins himself as well as others.”
— Dhammapada 355 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada says wealth is dangerous only in the hands of the foolish.
Both texts say possession magnifies the fool’s folly into ruin.
Jainism
“Wealth will not protect a careless man in this world and the next. Though he had seen the right way, he does not see it, even as one in the dark whose lamp has suddenly been put out.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana says possessions do not save the heedless person; his judgment goes dark, as when a lamp is suddenly put out.
Valluvar compares a fool who gets possessions to a madman who is also drunk; the Uttarādhyayana likewise shows wealth failing the heedless man and his sight of the right way vanishing.
Sikhism
“One person brings a full bottle, and another fills his cup. Drinking the wine, his intelligence departs, and madness enters his mind; he cannot distinguish between his own and others, and he is struck down by his Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 554, Raag Bihaagraa, Bihaagre Ki Vaar (salok 1 before pauri 16), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das describes how wine drives out intelligence and brings in madness, so a man can no longer tell his own from others; Sikh teaching rejects intoxicants.
The Kural likens a fool who gains something to a madman who is also drunk; Guru Amar Das's picture of drink bringing madness gives the same image of reason lost.
Greek Mythology
King Midas, granted any wish by Dionysus, foolishly asked that everything he touched turn to gold, and soon found he could not eat or drink.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 [9]
Ovid shows a foolish man given power turning a gift into a curse.
Like the Kural’s fool, Midas is made worse, not better, by what comes into his hands.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes the character that wealth produces as insolent and arrogant, as if possessed by their riches, behaving like people who think they have every good thing; he calls this a prosperous fool.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.16 [10]
Aristotle observes that sudden wealth distorts character, especially in those without judgment.
Valluvar says the same: possession intoxicates the foolish.

Real Life Example

Ceausescu builds the House of the People
1984-1989 · Bucharest, Romania
From 1984 the Romanian leader Nicolae Ceausescu had an enormous government palace, the House of the People, built in Bucharest, and the project required clearing large areas of the historic city centre. Thousands of workers and hundreds of architects worked on it during the 1980s, a time when Romania was struggling to feed its own people and the costs were enormous. The building was still unfinished when Ceausescu fell in December 1989, and it now houses Romania's Parliament.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Absolute power let one man pour a struggling nation's resources into a monument to himself.
The kural compares a fool who comes into possession of something to a madman who is also drunk, and unchecked control of a country led Ceausescu into one of the most extravagant building projects of the century.
