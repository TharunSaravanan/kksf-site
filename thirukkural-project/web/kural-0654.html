Kural 654

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

இடுக்கண் படினும் இளிவந்த செய்யார் நடுக்கற்ற காட்சி யவர்.
Idukkan padinum ilivandha seyyaar Nadukkatra kaatchi yavar.

Meaning

People who see clearly and are not shaken will never do anything shameful, even when they are in deep trouble.

Elementary

Imagine you lost your lunch money and saw someone else’s wallet on the floor. A person with a steady, clear mind would still hand it in, even though they’re hungry. Being in trouble doesn’t make a wrong thing right.

University

Iṭukkaṇ is ‘distress, affliction’ (compare the chapter iṭukkaṇ aḻiyāmai, ch. 63), and iḷivanta means ‘what brings disgrace’. Parimelazhagar takes the past-tense participle literally as ‘acts that have (in the past) brought disgrace’, which is why the prose explanation printed with Pope says ‘acts which have brought disgrace (on former ministers)’. The key phrase naṭukku-aṟṟa kāṭci, ‘vision free of trembling’, describes firm, clear discernment that does not waver under pressure, and Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as ‘motionless, clear knowledge’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar add that such people will not act basely even to escape their distress, which prepares for the stronger case of 656. ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net read இடுக்கண் படினும்; thirukkural-thamizh.org shows the sandhi form இடுக்கட் படினு மிளிவந்த.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, tested by the sage Vishvamitra, loses his kingdom, sells his wife and son, and works as a servant at a cremation ground, yet never breaks his word or his truthfulness.
— Markandeya Purana, Harishchandra episode (chs. 7-8) [4]
However deep his suffering, the king refuses to buy relief with a dishonourable act, and in the end the gods restore him.
Harishchandra is the classic Indian example of the ‘unshaken vision’ that will not stoop even in great distress.
Christianity
“Choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season;”
— Hebrews 11:25 (KJV) [5]
Hebrews praises Moses for choosing hardship with his people over the comforts of Pharaoh’s court.
Like the Kural’s steady-minded person, Moses accepts affliction rather than take a dishonourable way out.
Islam
“He said, ‘My Lord, prison is more to my liking than that to which they invite me. And if You do not avert from me their plan, I might incline toward them and [thus] be of the ignorant.’”
— Quran 12:33 (Sahih International) [6]
The prophet Joseph (Yusuf), threatened with prison unless he gives in to wrongdoing, prefers prison.
Yusuf is a precise image of the Kural’s ideal: facing real hardship, he still refuses the disgraceful act.
Judaism
Joseph, a slave in Potiphar’s house, refuses the repeated advances of his master’s wife, asking how he could do so great a wickedness and sin against God, and he is thrown into prison on her false charge.
— Genesis 39:7-20 (JPS 1917) [7]
Joseph’s refusal costs him his position and freedom, yet the Torah presents it as the root of his later rise.
Joseph shows the Kural’s unshaken discernment: danger does not move him to a dishonourable act.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada compares the wise to a rock that the winds of fortune cannot move.
The unshaken rock is a close image for the Kural’s naṭukku-aṟṟa kāṭci, vision that does not tremble.
Jainism
“Though overcome by thirst, he should drink no cold water, restrained by shame and aversion (from forbidden things); he should try to get distilled water.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Among the hardships a monk must bear, the Uttaradhyayana says that even when tormented by thirst he must not do what is forbidden, held back by shame and aversion.
Valluvar says people of steady vision will not do shameful deeds even in deep distress; the Jain monk likewise holds to his rule under thirst, restrained by the same sense of shame.
Sikhism
Guru Tegh Bahadur, the ninth Guru, took up the cause of those facing forced conversion under Aurangzeb and, refusing to convert himself, was publicly beheaded in Delhi in 1675; Gurdwara Sis Ganj marks the place.
— The Pluralism Project, Harvard University, The Development of the Sikh Community [10]
In the greatest personal danger the Guru chose death over an act he held to be dishonourable, and Sikhs remember him for that steadfastness.
The Kural says people of clear understanding will not do anything shameful even in serious trouble; Guru Tegh Bahadur's refusal to renounce his faith at the cost of his life is a supreme Sikh example of that resolve.
Greek Mythology
Bellerophon, a guest at the court of King Proetus, rejects the queen Anteia’s advances; she falsely accuses him, and he is sent to Lycia carrying a sealed message asking for his death, and made to face the Chimera and other perils.
— Homer, Iliad 6.155-195 [11]
Bellerophon keeps his integrity even though it brings him into mortal danger, and he survives his trials.
Like the Kural’s steady-minded person, he will not do the disgraceful thing even when refusing puts him in peril.
Philosophy
Socrates tells the jury that when the Thirty Tyrants ordered him and four others to arrest Leon of Salamis for execution, he alone went home and refused, though it might have cost him his life.
— Plato, Apology 32c-d [12]
Socrates says he cared nothing for death but everything about not doing anything unjust or unholy.
This is a model case of the Kural: under real threat, a clear mind will not take part in a disgraceful act.

Real Life Example

Mark Twain pays his creditors in full after bankruptcy
1894 to the late 1890s · United States and a world lecture tour
Mark Twain's publishing firm, Charles L. Webster and Company, failed in 1894, leaving debts of about 80,000 dollars beyond its assets, and Twain declared personal bankruptcy. Although he was not legally required to repay them in full, he set out from Cleveland on 15 July 1895 on a lecture tour around the world and wrote further books. With the earnings he repaid his creditors every cent, with interest, about a year sooner than he had expected.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Twain was in serious financial trouble and had a legal way out, but he chose the honourable course of paying in full.
The kural says people of clear, unshaken understanding will not do anything shameful even when in serious trouble, and Twain refused to let bankruptcy become an excuse for leaving his creditors unpaid.
