Kural 145

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

எளிதென இல்லிறப்பான் எய்துமெஞ் ஞான்றும் விளியாது நிற்கும் பழி.
Elidhena illirappaan eydhum enjnjaandrum, Viliyaadhu nirkum pazhi.

Meaning

A man who goes after another man’s wife, thinking it is easy, earns a shame that never dies and stays with him forever.

Elementary

Some mistakes are like writing on sand: they wash away. Others are like carving into stone: they stay forever. This kural says that going after someone else's husband or wife might seem easy at the time, but the shame is carved in stone and never goes away.

University

Valluvar contrasts the ease of the act (எளிது, 'easy') with the permanence of its consequence (விளியாது நிற்கும் பழி, 'blame that stands undying'). The verb இல்லிறப்பான் combines இல் ('home') and இறப்பு ('crossing over, transgressing'), so the offender is literally 'one who oversteps a home'. The couplet reflects a culture where reputation (புகழ்) was treated as the only thing that outlives a person, a theme developed in the Kural's later chapter on fame; adultery produces the exact opposite, a lasting infamy. The asymmetry between a moment's ease and an eternity of blame is a common moral argument, found in scriptural warnings about reproach that 'shall not be wiped away'.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, Indra, king of the gods, disguises himself as the sage Gautama to approach Gautama's wife Ahalya. The sage discovers him and curses him, and the episode is remembered ever after as a lasting stain on Indra's name.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda (narrative summary) [3]
Even the king of the gods, who thought the deception would be easy, could not escape a disgrace that followed him permanently.
This is the Kural's 'undying blame': an act taken lightly leaves a mark that never fades.
Christianity
“Marriage is honourable in all, and the bed undefiled: but whoremongers and adulterers God will judge.”
— Hebrews 13:4 (KJV) [4]
The letter to the Hebrews says marriage must be kept pure, and that God himself will judge adulterers.
Both texts say what seems easy and hidden will not simply pass: the Kural speaks of lasting blame, Hebrews of divine judgement.
Islam
“And those who do not invoke with Allah another deity or kill the soul which Allah has forbidden [to be killed], except by right, and do not commit unlawful sexual intercourse. And whoever should do that will meet a penalty. Multiplied for him is the punishment on the Day of Resurrection, and he will abide therein humiliated.”
— Qur'an 25:68-69 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says whoever commits these grave sins, including unlawful intercourse, will face a multiplied penalty and remain in humiliation.
The Qur'an's 'abide therein humiliated' echoes the Kural's disgrace that 'never dies'.
Judaism
“Anyone who descends to Gehenna ultimately ascends, except for three who descend and do not ascend, and these are they: One who engages in intercourse with a married woman ...; and one who humiliates another in public; and one who calls another a derogatory name.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 58b (William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz translation) [6]
The Talmud teaches that most sinners are eventually released from punishment, but someone who commits adultery with a married woman is among the few who never come back out.
Both texts stress permanence: the Kural's blame 'never dies', and in the Talmud the adulterer 'does not ascend'.
Buddhism
“An evil deed is better left undone, for such a deed torments one afterwards. But a good deed is better done, doing which one repents not later.”
— Dhammapada 314 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says evil deeds keep tormenting the doer long after they are done.
Both texts say that a wrong that seems small at the moment leaves a lasting burden behind.
Jainism
“If any one see, disgrace lights on the house; if any hand should seize, leg's maimed; in the doing of the shameful deed is dread; it yields as fruit vast hell's affliction: tell me, O profligate, what measure of delight is thine?”
— Nālaṭiyār 84 (ch. 9, 'The Not Desiring Other Men's Wives'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [8]
The Nālaṭiyār sets the adulterer's small pleasure against a lasting price: disgrace on the family, bodily punishment, constant dread, and the long suffering of hell as karmic fruit.
Valluvar says the man who thinks it easy to cross into another's home earns a disgrace that never dies, and the Nālaṭiyār weighs that 'easy' pleasure against disgrace and suffering that follow him into the next life.
Sikhism
“Those men who go out to enjoy other men's women shall suffer in shame.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1362, Phunhay Mahala 5, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says plainly that men who pursue other men's wives will be put to shame.
The Kural warns that the man who thinks it easy to cross into another's home earns a disgrace that never dies; Guru Arjan names the same shame as the certain outcome.
Greek Mythology
Ixion, a king who was a guest at Zeus's own table, tried to seduce Hera, Zeus's wife. As punishment he was bound to a fiery wheel that turns forever, and the poet Pindar says his fate is a lasting warning to all mortals.
— Pindar, Pythian Ode 2 (narrative summary) [10]
Ixion thought he could wrong his host's wife and get away with it; instead his punishment became eternal and his name a byword.
Ixion embodies the Kural's 'blame that stands undying': a moment's reckless desire, an eternity of consequence.
Philosophy
“When you have been overcome in sexual intercourse with a person, do not reckon this single defeat only, but reckon that you have also nurtured, increased your incontinence.”
— Epictetus, Discourses II.18 (trans. George Long) [11]
Epictetus warns that giving in once is never a single event: it strengthens the bad habit and leaves a lasting mark on the character.
The Kural says the 'easy' act leaves a permanent stain on one's name; Epictetus says it leaves a lasting mark on one's soul.

Real Life Example

Georges d'Anthes and the duel that killed Pushkin
1836-1837 · St Petersburg, Russia
Georges d'Anthes, a French officer serving in Russia, openly courted Natalya Pushkina, the wife of the poet Alexander Pushkin, and in 1836 anonymous letters mocking Pushkin over his wife circulated in St Petersburg. D'Anthes married Natalya's sister Ekaterina in January 1837, but the gossip continued, and later that month the two men fought a duel in which Pushkin was mortally wounded; he died two days later. The tsar stripped d'Anthes of his military rank and expelled him from Russia.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Russia lost its greatest poet, and in Russian memory d'Anthes has been remembered ever since chiefly as the man who killed Pushkin.
The kural warns that the man who thinks it easy to cross into another's household earns a disgrace that never dies; d'Anthes' pursuit of Pushkin's wife left his name permanently tied to that loss.
