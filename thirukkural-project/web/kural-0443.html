Kural 443

Seeking the Aid of Great Men · Periyaaraith Thunaikkodal · பெரியாரைத் துணைக்கோடல்

அரியவற்று ளெல்லாம் அரிதே பெரியாரைப் பேணித் தமராக் கொளல்.
Ariyavatru lellaam aridhe periyaaraip Penith thamaraak kolal.

Meaning

Of all rare things, the rarest is to respect great people and make them your own family.

Elementary

Many things in life are hard to get. But this kural says the hardest and most precious thing of all is to have truly great people treat you like family, because you have honored and cared for them.

University

The superlative construction ‘ariyavatrul ellaam aridhe’ (rarest among all rare things) places the friendship of the great above wealth, armies, and territory, all of which are also ‘rare’ in the Porutpaal. The key word is ‘thamar’ — one’s own people, kin. Valluvar is not describing hiring an expert but transforming a great person into someone bound to you by affection and loyalty; ‘peni’ (having cherished) marks the reciprocity required. The rarity has two sources: the great are few, and they cannot be commanded — they must be won.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Satsaṅgatve nissaṅgatvaṁ, nissaṅgatve nirmohatvam; nirmohatve niścalatattvaṁ, niścalatattve jīvanmuktiḥ.”
— Adi Shankara (attr.), Bhaja Govindam (Moha Mudgara), verse 9 [4]
Shankara’s verse says that from the company of the good comes non-attachment, from that freedom from delusion, from that a steady mind, and from that liberation in this life.
Both treat association with the good (satsang) as the rarest and most transformative possession, the root of every higher gain.
Christianity
“A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly: and there is a friend that sticketh closer than a brother.”
— Proverbs 18:24 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs says friendship must be cultivated by one’s own conduct, and that the best friend becomes closer than family.
Matches the Kural’s ‘peni’ (cherishing) and ‘thamar’ (making them kin).
Islam
“And keep yourself patient [by being] with those who call upon their Lord in the morning and the evening, seeking His countenance.”
— Quran 18:28 (Sahih International) [6]
God tells the Prophet to hold himself firmly among the devout and not turn his eyes away from them for worldly glamour.
Like the Kural, it values keeping close to the virtuous above worldly advantages.
Judaism
Yose ben Yoezer taught that one’s house should be a meeting place for the sages, that one should sit in the dust of their feet and drink in their words thirstily.
— Pirkei Avot 1:4 [7]
Hosting and humbly attending the wise is treated as the way to fill one’s home with blessing.
Parallels the Kural’s ideal of honoring the great and drawing them into one’s own household circle (thamar).
Buddhism
“Admirable friendship, admirable companionship, admirable camaraderie is actually the whole of the holy life.”
— Upaddha Sutta, SN 45.2 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
When Ananda calls good friendship ‘half’ the holy life, the Buddha corrects him: it is the whole of it.
The most emphatic parallel to the Kural’s claim that such friendship is the rarest of all things.
Jainism
“If one can obtain the privilege of living always with men of good disposition in friendship, who constantly perform acts of virtue through a succession of births, though that succession is affliction, no one will despise it when they have considered the matter.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 18 'Good Society', stanza 4 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
This Tamil Jain quatrain says that the friendship of the virtuous is so precious that it would make even the round of rebirth, which Jainism counts as suffering, worth accepting.
Valluvar calls honouring the great and making them one's own the rarest of rare things. The Naladiyar values that same friendship above even release from the misery of rebirth.
Sikhism
“They alone are relatives, and they alone are friends, who, as Gurmukh, join together in love.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 643, Raag Sorath, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das redefines kinship: one's true relatives and friends are those who live as Gurmukhs, turned towards the Guru, and meet in love.
The Kural calls it the rarest of rare things to honour the great and make them one's own kin; Guru Amar Das likewise counts the God-oriented as one's real family.
Greek Mythology
In Odyssey 3–4, Telemachus travels to Pylos and Sparta to seek out the aged Nestor and the king Menelaus, who receive him as a guest-friend and give him counsel and news of his father.
— Homer, Odyssey 3–4 [11]
The young prince must journey to win the counsel and friendship of great elders, and is received as if family.
Reflects the rarity and value of gaining great men as one’s own.
Philosophy
“Cherish some man of high character, and keep him ever before your eyes, living as if he were watching you, and ordering all your actions as if he beheld them.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 11 (tr. Gummere), quoting Epicurus [12]
Seneca, quoting Epicurus, advises taking a person of high character as a constant inner companion and model.
Same verb of cherishing (‘peni’) applied to a great person held close.

Real Life Example

Emperor Rudolf II welcomes Tycho Brahe to Prague
1599 · Prague and Benatky nad Jizerou, Bohemia (now Czech Republic)
After the great Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe left Denmark, the Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II made him imperial mathematician in 1599, gave him a generous salary and provided the castle of Benatky near Prague for his observatory. In 1600 Tycho invited the young Johannes Kepler to work with him there. When Tycho died in 1601, Rudolf appointed Kepler to succeed him, and Kepler's planetary tables were later named the Rudolphine Tables in the emperor's honour.
— Sources: [13] [14]
By honouring and providing for great scholars, Rudolf made his court a centre of the astronomy that transformed European science.
The kural calls it the rarest of all things to honour great people and make them one's own, and Rudolf drew two of the greatest astronomers of the age into his household.
