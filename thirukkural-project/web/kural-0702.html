Kural 702

The Knowledge of Signals · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

ஐயப் படாஅது அகத்தது உணர்வானைத் தெய்வத்தோ டொப்பக் கொளல்.
Aiyap padaaadhu akaththadhu unarvaanaith Theyvaththodu oppak kolal.

Meaning

A person who can know without any doubt what is inside someone else's mind should be thought of as equal to a god.

Elementary

Some grown-ups seem to know exactly what you are feeling before you say it, and they are always right. It can feel almost magical. Valluvar says a person who can truly do that deserves to be honoured almost like a god.

University

Aiyap padaaadhu uses alapedai (metrical vowel lengthening, படாஅது for படாது) to stretch ‘without doubting’, stressing certainty rather than guesswork. Akaththadhu is ‘that which is within’ (akam = interior, mind), and unarvaan ‘one who perceives’. Parimelazhagar explains the comparison theyvaththodu oppa (as equal to a deity): to know the heart directly is a faculty proper to the divine, so a man who has it without error shares a divine attribute, though he remains human (Mu. Varadarasanar adds ‘even though he is a man’). Kalaignar makes the logic explicit: if such knowledge belongs to God, the man who has it may be likened to God. The couplet should be read with 701 as a rising scale: ornament to the world, then peer of the gods. No significant textual variants; the sandhi text reads படாஅ தகத்த துணர்வானைத்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna tells Arjuna that he knows all beings, past, present and to come, while no one truly knows him.
— Bhagavad Gita 7.26 [3]
Complete knowledge of every being’s inner life is presented as a mark of the divine.
If knowing hearts fully is God’s own trait, the Kural’s claim that such a person is like a god follows naturally.
Christianity
“for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart.”
— 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV) [4]
God tells Samuel that humans judge by looks, but God sees the heart directly.
Seeing into the heart is God’s way of seeing, so a person who does it without doubt comes close to the divine, as the Kural says.
Islam
“He knows that which deceives the eyes and what the breasts conceal.”
— Qur’an 40:19 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an names knowledge of hidden glances and concealed thoughts as one of God’s attributes.
Knowing what the breast conceals is described as God’s knowledge, which is why the Kural compares its human possessor to a deity.
Judaism
The Talmud reads Isaiah 11:3 as saying the Messiah will ‘smell and judge’, knowing the truth without evidence, and reports that Bar Koziba was rejected when he could not do this.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 93b [6]
Knowing guilt or innocence without witnesses is treated as a sign of divinely endowed, messianic status.
As in the Kural, knowing what is inside without doubt raises a person above the ordinary toward the divine.
Buddhism
Among the Tathagata’s powers the Buddha lists knowing, as it really is, the dispositions and faculties of other beings.
— Majjhima Nikaya 12, Mahasihanada Sutta [7]
Accurate knowledge of others’ inner tendencies is counted among the powers that mark a fully awakened one.
Buddhism, like the Kural, places flawless knowledge of other minds among the highest, near-superhuman attainments.
Jainism
“He reached the knowledge called Manaḥparyāya, by which he knew the thoughts of all sentient beings, with five organs, which are not defective, and possess a developed intellect, (living) in the two and a half continents and the two oceans.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.15.23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org) [8]
When Mahavira took up the ascetic life he gained manaḥparyāya jñāna, the direct knowledge of other beings' minds, which Jainism counts among the higher, supersensory kinds of knowledge.
The Kural sets the one who knows another's mind without doubt beside the divine; Jain scripture likewise treats certain knowledge of other minds as a mark of the most advanced souls.
Sikhism
“You are permeating each and every heart, O Lord, Inner-knower, Searcher of Hearts.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 96, Raag Maajh, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Antarjami, the Inner-knower who sees what lies within every heart, is one of Gurbani's most frequent names for God.
The Kural calls the one who perceives another's mind without doubt equal to a god; in Gurbani, knowing the heart from within is precisely the mark of the divine.
Greek Mythology
The Delphic oracle tells Croesus’ envoys that Apollo knows the number of the sands and the measure of the sea, understands the mute and hears those who do not speak.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.47 (trans. A. D. Godley) [10]
The god’s power shows in knowing what is unspoken; Croesus’ test of the oracles turns on it.
In Greek myth, hearing the voiceless is exactly what makes a god a god, which is the Kural’s comparison.
Philosophy
Aquinas argues that the secret thoughts of the heart, as they are in themselves, are known to God alone; angels and men can only conjecture them from bodily signs such as changes of face.
— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 57, a. 4 [11]
He separates certain knowledge of hearts, which belongs to God, from inference by signs, which is open to creatures.
Aquinas sharpens the Kural’s point: to read signs without doubt is to come as close as a creature can to what belongs to God.

Real Life Example

George Kennan sends the Long Telegram from Moscow
22 February 1946 · U.S. Embassy, Moscow, Soviet Union
After Stalin's speech of 9 February 1946 alarmed American officials, the State Department asked George F. Kennan, its foremost Soviet expert and charge d'affaires in Moscow, to explain Soviet conduct. Kennan wired back a reply of more than 5,000 words, arguing that powerful internal dynamics, not Western incentives, drove Moscow's behaviour. The telegram circulated widely in Washington, and its ideas, developed in his 1947 X article, became the basis of the American policy of containment.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kennan read the hidden intentions of a closed regime with such assurance that his analysis guided American policy for decades.
The kural honours one who perceives without doubt what lies inside another's mind, and Kennan's confident reading of the Soviet leadership's purposes earned him almost oracular standing in Washington.
