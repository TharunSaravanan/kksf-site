Kural 699

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

கொளப்பட்டேம் என்றெண்ணிக் கொள்ளாத செய்யார் துளக்கற்ற காட்சி யவர்.
Kolappattem endru ennik kollaadha seyyaar Thulakkatra kaatchiyavar.

Meaning

People with steady judgment never think 'the king likes us' and then do things the king would not accept.

Elementary

Being the teacher’s favourite does not mean you can break the rules. A sensible student knows that being liked is not a free pass, and keeps behaving well so that the trust lasts.

University

The couplet pivots on two forms of kol (to take, to accept). Kolappattem means ‘we have been accepted or esteemed [by the king]’, and kollaadha means ‘things [he] would not accept’. The echo makes the point that acceptance by the king does not make the unacceptable acceptable. Thulakkatra kaatchi, ‘vision without wavering’, marks the wise courtier as one whose judgement does not drift once favour arrives. Parimelazhagar reads the error as a specific psychology: favour breeds presumption, and presumption breeds offence. That links the couplet forward to 700, where old familiarity replaces present favour as the source of self-deception. The couplet also refines 692: not coveting what is the king’s includes not coveting the licence that favour seems to give.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna promises to pardon a hundred offences from his kinsman Shishupala. Relying on that forbearance, Shishupala heaps insults on Krishna at Yudhishthira’s royal consecration, until the hundredth is passed and he is struck down.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Shishupala-vadha), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Shishupala treats his protected status as permission to offend, and it ruins him.
He is the clearest mythic case of the person who thinks ‘I am accepted’ and so does what cannot be accepted.
Christianity
“So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.”
— Luke 17:10 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that a servant, however faithful, should claim no special standing from his service.
The Kural’s steady-minded servant likewise never turns favour into a claim to licence.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet or be loud to him in speech like the loudness of some of you to others, lest your deeds become worthless while you perceive not.”
— Quran 49:2 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran warns the Prophet’s own companions, the people closest to him, not to become loud or casual in his presence, or their good deeds could be lost without their noticing.
Being close to the leader is no licence for improper conduct, which is the Kural’s point about favoured courtiers.
Judaism
“So Haman came in. And the king said unto him, What shall be done unto the man whom the king delighteth to honour? Now Haman thought in his heart, To whom would the king delight to do honour more than to myself?”
— Esther 6:6 (KJV) [4]
Haman, the Persian king’s favourite, is so sure of his standing that he misreads the king’s question, and his presumption leads to his downfall.
Haman is the Kural’s foolish favourite, whose certainty that he is esteemed blinds his judgement.
Buddhism
“Let him not seat himself on the king’s chair or couch or seat or elephant or chariot; as thinking himself a privileged person, such a one may dwell in a king’s court.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 153 [6]
Vidhura forbids a courtier from taking royal privileges because he believes himself specially favoured.
‘Thinking himself a privileged person’ is precisely the Kural’s kolappattem endru enni, thinking ‘we are esteemed’.
Jainism
“He should never do anything disagreeable to the wise, neither in words nor deeds, neither openly nor secretly.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The sutra says the disciple must never do what displeases his superiors, in word or deed, openly or in secret.
The Kural warns those who think 'the king favours us' against doing what he would not accept; the Jain rule simply allows no exception, so favour gives no licence for conduct the superior would disapprove.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, one who claims to know everything shall not be decorated in the world hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Jap (Japji Sahib, Pauri 21), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
At the end of Japji Pauri 21 Guru Nanak warns that the self-important person who presumes on his own greatness is not honoured in the Lord's Court.
The Kural says that people of steady judgement never presume on the king's favour to do what he would not accept; Guru Nanak likewise warns that presumption (haumai) loses honour in the Court one thinks one has a claim on.
Greek Mythology
Ixion is invited by Zeus to live among the gods on Olympus and is even purified by him. Presuming on this favour, he tries to seduce Hera, and is bound for ever to a turning wheel.
— Pindar, Pythian Ode 2.21-48 (Ixion) [9]
Pindar tells the story as the warning of a guest who could not bear his great good fortune.
Ixion is the Kural’s favourite who, sure that he is accepted, does the unacceptable.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that in friendships between unequals, such as ruler and subject, the lesser party must give more honour and love in proportion. The friendship breaks down when the lesser party forgets the difference.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.7 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
For Aristotle, a friendship between unequals lasts only if the lower party keeps the proper ratio of honour.
The favoured courtier who stops honouring the difference because he is ‘accepted’ breaks exactly this proportion.

Real Life Example

The Earl of Essex deserts his post in Ireland and loses Elizabeth's favour
1599-1601 · Ireland and London, England, United Kingdom
Robert Devereux, 2nd Earl of Essex, was Queen Elizabeth I's favourite, and for years she put up with his rashness, even after he turned his back on her during a dispute. Sent to Ireland as lord lieutenant in 1599, he concluded an unfavourable truce and, defying the queen's instructions, suddenly returned to England to justify himself to her privately. She placed him under house arrest and deprived him of his offices in June 1600, and after a failed attempt to raise London in revolt in February 1601 he was tried and executed for treason.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Essex mistook years of indulgence for licence and did what the queen had forbidden, trusting her affection to cover it.
The kural says people of steady judgement never think the king favours them and so do what the king would not accept, the very error that ruined Essex.
