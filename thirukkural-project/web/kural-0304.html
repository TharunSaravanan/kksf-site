Kural 304

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

நகையும் உவகையும் கொல்லும் சினத்தின் பகையும் உளவோ பிற.
Nagaiyum uvagaiyum kollum sinaththin Pagaiyum ulavo pira.

Meaning

Anger kills the smile on your face and the joy in your heart. Is there any enemy worse than that?

Elementary

When you are really angry, you can't laugh and you can't feel happy. It's like a thief that steals your smile. The kural asks: what enemy could be worse than one that takes away all your happiness?

University

Valluvar pairs nagai (laughter, the outward cheerfulness of the face) with uvagai (inner delight), a pairing the commentators render as 'face-blossoming and heart-blossoming'. Anger 'kills' both, so the damage is not only social but inward. The rhetorical question reframes anger as a pagai (enemy) that lives within, which is more dangerous than any external foe because it cannot be escaped. The idea of anger as an internal enemy runs through Indian thought (the 'six inner enemies', including krodha) and has close parallels in the Buddhist teaching that an angry person does to himself what an enemy would wish on him.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.37 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna identifies desire turned into wrath as the 'all-devouring' enemy that must be known and conquered.
Like the kural, the Gita names anger as the greatest enemy, one that consumes a person from within.
Christianity
“Idolatry, witchcraft, hatred, variance, emulations, wrath, strife ... But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith,”
— Galatians 5:20-22 (KJV) [4]
Paul sets wrath among the 'works of the flesh' and sets joy and peace among the fruits of the Spirit.
The contrast shows wrath and joy as opposites, as in the kural, where anger kills joy.
Islam
“Anger is an ember in the heart of the son of Adam, as you see it in the redness of his eyes and the bulge of his jugular veins. So whoever senses something from that, then let him cling to the ground.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2191 (graded da'if by Darussalam) [5]
The Prophet describes anger as a burning coal that visibly disfigures the face and body.
Like the kural, this shows anger taking over the face and heart, replacing calm cheer with burning.
Judaism
When God accepted Abel's offering and not his, Cain became very angry and his face fell; God asked him why he was angry and why his face had fallen, warning that sin was crouching at the door.
— Genesis 4:5-8 [6]
In the Torah's first account of anger, the emotion shows immediately on Cain's face and leads him to kill his brother.
Cain's 'fallen countenance' is the kural's lost smile: anger strips the face of light before it does any other harm.
Buddhism
“Having killed anger you sleep in ease. Having killed anger you do not grieve. The noble ones praise the slaying of anger — with its honeyed crest & poison root —”
— Samyutta Nikaya 1.71, Ghatva Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Asked what one thing should be killed, the Buddha answers: anger, because killing it brings ease and ends grief.
The reverse of the kural: anger kills joy, and killing anger restores it.
Jainism
“Self is the one invincible foe, (together with the four) cardinal passions, (viz. anger, pride, deceit, and greed, they are five) and the (five) senses (make ten). These (foes), O great sage, I have regularly vanquished.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Asked by Keśi who the real enemies are, Gautama names the unconquered self and the four passions, with anger listed first, as the foes to be defeated.
Valluvar asks whether there is any enemy greater than anger. Gautama's answer places anger among the very few enemies that count, ranked above any outward foe.
Sikhism
“Emotional attachment to Maya, sexual desire, anger and egotism are demons.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 513, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gujri, Guru Amar Das calls anger and the other passions demons that possess a person from within.
The Kural asks whether there is any enemy greater than anger, which kills both the smile and the joy within. Guru Amar Das likewise counts anger among the inner demons that are a person's real foes.
Greek Mythology
“But Achilles abode at his ships and nursed his anger. He went not to the honourable assembly, and sallied not forth to fight, but gnawed at his own heart, pining for battle and the war-cry.”
— Homer, Iliad, Book 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
After his quarrel, Achilles withdraws from everything he loves and consumes himself with resentment.
Achilles 'gnawing at his own heart' is anger killing the angry man's own joy, as the kural says.
Philosophy
“Eighth, consider how much more pain is brought on us by the anger and vexation caused by such acts than by the acts themselves, at which we are angry and vexed.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.18 (trans. George Long) [11]
Among his rules for handling offence, Marcus observes that our own anger hurts us more than the offence did.
Like the kural, Marcus treats anger as the real enemy of our peace of mind, worse than any outside wrong.

Real Life Example

Seneca writes On Anger for his brother Novatus
c. 41-49 CE · Rome, Roman Empire
The Stoic philosopher Lucius Annaeus Seneca wrote the treatise De Ira (On Anger), addressed to his elder brother Novatus, who had asked how anger might be soothed. In it Seneca described anger as a kind of brief madness, more conspicuous than other passions because it shows in the face and boils forth the more plainly the greater it is. He argued that anger cannot be moderated and must be eliminated rather than managed.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Seneca's treatise is one of the earliest sustained arguments that anger disfigures the person who feels it and is an enemy to be removed entirely.
The kural says anger kills both the smile on the face and the joy in the heart and asks whether there is any greater enemy, and Seneca likewise portrayed anger as visibly ruining the face and overthrowing the mind.
