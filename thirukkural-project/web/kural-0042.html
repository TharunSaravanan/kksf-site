Kural 42

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

துறந்தார்க்கும் துவ்வாதவர்க்கும் இறந்தார்க்கும் இல்வாழ்வான் என்பான் துணை.
Thurandhaarkkum thuvvaadhavarkkum irandhaarkkum, Ilvaazhvaan enpaan thunai.

Meaning

A family person supports those who have given up worldly life, those who are poor and hungry, and those who have died.

Elementary

Imagine one family in a village that keeps a pot of rice always ready. The travelling monk who owns nothing eats from it. The hungry neighbour eats from it. And a share is set aside in memory of grandparents who have died. That one household quietly feeds three groups who cannot feed themselves.

University

Valluvar names three classes with three neat participial nouns: துறந்தார் (thurandhaar, “those who have let go”), துவ்வாதவர் (thuvvaadhavar, literally “those who do not eat” — the destitute), and இறந்தார் (irandhaar, “those who have passed”). The last is the most striking to a modern reader: the dead are treated as an ongoing claim on the living, through the memorial offerings (தென்புலத்தார் rites) that the next couplet will name explicitly. The verse is a deliberate argumentative move within a Tamil intellectual landscape in which Jain and Buddhist renunciant ideals were culturally prestigious: Valluvar concedes the renunciant's standing and then points out that the renunciant literally eats because a householder cooked. Economic dependency becomes a moral argument for the dignity of domestic life.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Charity given out of duty, without expectation of return, at the proper time and place, and to a worthy person is considered to be in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.20 [4]
Krishna classifies charity by motive: sattvic giving is given because it ought to be given, to those who genuinely merit it, with no thought of return.
The Kural's householder gives to renunciants, the destitute and the dead — three classes that by definition can never repay him.
Christianity
“Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world.”
— James 1:27 (KJV) [5]
James relocates the essence of religion from ritual to the concrete care of society's most defenceless members.
Both verses define true piety as practical support for those who cannot support themselves, rather than as withdrawal or ceremony.
Islam
“…but [true] righteousness is [in] one who believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask [for help], and for freeing slaves…”
— Qur'an 2:177, Al-Baqarah (Sahih International) [6]
The famous 'verse of righteousness' lists the specific categories of dependent people whose support constitutes real piety, against merely outward observance.
Like the Kural, it measures a person's virtue by the list of unrepaying dependants he sustains out of his own household wealth.
Judaism
“Charity is given to the living, while acts of kindness are performed both for the living and for the dead.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 49b (Koren-Steinsaltz / William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Talmud ranks gemilut chasadim above tzedakah partly because kindness extends to the dead — burial and mourning rites are called chesed shel emet, 'true kindness', since the recipient can never repay.
This is an unusually exact parallel: the Kural likewise lists the dead alongside the poor as recipients of the householder's support.
Buddhism
“Householders & the homeless / in mutual dependence / both reach the true Dhamma: / the unsurpassed safety from bondage. / From householders, the homeless / receive requisites: robes, lodgings, / protection from inclemencies.”
— Itivuttaka 107 (Iti 4.8), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [8]
The Buddha describes the lay-monastic relationship as reciprocal: householders supply material requisites, monastics supply the Dhamma, and neither reaches the goal without the other.
This states the Kural's first clause outright — the renunciant (துறந்தார்) survives because the householder supports him.
Jainism
“Yathavat atithau sadhau dine cha pratipatti-krt : Diligent in succoring the ascetics, the righteous and the needy.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra 1.47–56 (the thirty-five householder qualities, no. 19), English as given in V. A. Sangave, Ethics of Jainism [9]
Hemacandra counts care for ascetics and the destitute among the defining qualities of a good Jain layman, and the lay vow of atithi-saṃvibhāga commits the householder to eat only after feeding mendicants.
Two of the Kural's three dependants, those who have renounced and those who have nothing to eat, are exactly the people Jain lay ethics charges the householder to support.
Sikhism
Sent by his father with money to start trading, the young Nanak met a band of holy men who had not eaten for days, gave them the money, and bought them food, saying he had spent it to the best advantage.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter III, pp. 30-31 [10]
Macauliffe retells this Janamsakhi episode, remembered by Sikhs as the sacha sauda or true bargain: the first act of the future Guru's working life is to feed renunciants and the hungry from household money.
It enacts the Kural's claim that the renunciant and the destitute eat because a householder provides, here literally with a householder's trading capital.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles' Antigone, Antigone defies Creon's edict and performs burial rites for her brother Polyneices, insisting that the unwritten laws owed to the dead outrank a ruler's decree, and accepts death rather than leave him unburied.
— Sophocles, Antigone (c. 441 BCE) [11]
Greek religion held that the dead had an enforceable claim on their kin for burial and grave offerings, and that failing them polluted the whole household and city.
It matches the Kural's most distinctive element — that the dead (இறந்தார்) remain dependants of the living household.
Philosophy
Cicero argues in De Officiis that we are not born for ourselves alone — our country, our parents, our friends and our fellow human beings each claim a share of our existence — and that this is why generosity and beneficence are duties of justice rather than optional favours.
— Cicero, De Officiis 1.22 [12]
Stoic-influenced Roman ethics grounds the obligation to support others in the claim that a person's life is partly owned by the communities that made it possible.
Both texts derive the householder's obligation to support non-reciprocating others from the simple fact of belonging to a community.

Real Life Example

Emperor Harsha's five-yearly gift-giving at Prayag
7th century CE (reign 606-647) · Prayag (Prayagraj), Uttar Pradesh, India
Harsha, who ruled much of northern India from 606 to 647, held a great gathering every five years at Prayag, where the Ganges and Yamuna meet. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang, who travelled in India between 629 and 645, recorded the king's generosity there, describing how he gave gifts and donations to scholars and ascetics. At these ceremonies of giving, most of the wealth gathered over the previous five years was given away in charity.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Harsha's assemblies became one of the most celebrated examples of royal charity in early Indian history, preserved by an eyewitness from another land.
The kural names the householder as the support of those who have renounced the world and of the destitute, and Harsha's gifts to ascetics and to the needy at Prayag show such support given on a grand scale.
