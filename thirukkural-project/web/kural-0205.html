Kural 205

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

இலன்என்று தீயவை செய்யற்க செய்யின் இலனாகும் மற்றும் பெயர்த்து.
Ilan endru theeyavai seyyarka seyyin, Ilanaagum matrum peyarththu.

Meaning

Do not do bad things because you are poor. If you do, you will only end up even poorer.

Elementary

Suppose you have no snacks and you take someone else’s lunch. Now nobody trusts you, nobody shares with you, and you might get in big trouble. You end up with even less than before! Being poor is never a good reason to do bad things — it just makes things worse.

University

Valluvar anticipates the most sympathetic excuse for wrongdoing — need — and refuses it. இலன் (‘one who has not’) is echoed in the second line (இலனாகும், ‘becomes one who has not’) with பெயர்த்து (‘again, once more’), producing a circular structure that mirrors the poverty trap itself: evil committed to escape want returns one to want. Most commentators (Mu. Varadarasan, Pappaiah, Kalaignar) read இலன் as ‘poor’; Pulavar Kesigan offers an alternative — ‘do not harm someone because he is without support’ — which shifts the focus to exploiting the weak. On the dominant reading, the couplet makes a hard-headed point: ill-gotten means destroy trust, reputation and future prosperity, and so perpetuate the very lack they were meant to cure.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Being bound by hundreds and thousands of desires, by lust and anger, they secure money by illegal means for sense gratification.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.12 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [4]
In describing the ‘demoniac’ nature, the Gita names acquiring wealth by unjust means as a mark of those who then sink into ever-lower states.
Condemns the unjust pursuit of wealth and links it to downfall; does not address poverty as an excuse.
Christianity
“Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth.”
— Ephesians 4:28 (KJV) [5]
Paul redirects the thief — often driven by want — to honest labour, which not only meets need but produces surplus to give away.
Rejects wrongdoing as a remedy for need; honest work, not theft, is the way out of lack.
Islam
“Satan threatens you with poverty and orders you to immorality, while Allah promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty.”
— Qur’an 2:268 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an identifies the fear of poverty as a tool Satan uses to push people into wrongdoing, while God’s promise is abundance.
Exposes poverty-as-excuse for evil as a deception; the path of evil does not lead to plenty.
Judaism
“Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: Lest I be full, and deny thee… or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain.”
— Proverbs 30:8–9 (KJV) [5]
Agur prays to be spared extreme poverty precisely because he knows it tempts a person to steal and so to dishonour God.
Recognizes poverty’s pull toward wrongdoing — the very temptation the Kural forbids yielding to.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta, a king neglects to give to the needy; poverty spreads, a poor man steals, and the king’s response sets off a cascade — theft, violence, lying — in which society grows ever poorer and human life-span declines.
— Digha Nikaya 26, Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha narrates a spiral in which poverty leads to theft, and theft leads to further degradation and deprivation.
Illustrates the Kural’s trap on a social scale: wrongdoing born of want produces more want.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana warns that wealth gained through evil deeds does not last: the one who gains it loses it and is trapped by his own passions.
Valluvar refuses poverty as an excuse for evil because the evil-doer only ends up poorer; the Jain text likewise says wealth won by evil is lost.
Sikhism
“It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Raag Aasaa from the time of Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak says of the riches that ruined their owners that such wealth is never gathered without sin and never goes with anyone at death.
The Kural forbids doing evil to escape poverty, warning that it only makes one poorer still; Guru Nanak likewise shows wealth won through sin as a loss in the end.
Greek Mythology
“Wealth should not be seized: god-given wealth is much better; for if a man take great wealth violently and perforce, or if he steal it through his tongue… the gods soon blot him out and make that man’s house low, and wealth attends him only for a little time.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 320–326 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod warns his brother that riches got by force or fraud are short-lived; the gods reduce such a man’s household.
Same economic moral: unjust gain ends in greater loss.
Philosophy
In the Gorgias, Polus cites Archelaus, who rose from a slave woman’s son to king of Macedon through murder and treachery, as proof that injustice pays. Socrates replies that Archelaus, far from being enviable, is the most wretched of men.
— Plato, Gorgias 470d–479e (paraphrase) [11]
Socrates rejects the idea that wrongdoing is a rational escape from low estate: the unjust man gains power but loses the true good of his soul.
Next-best: rejects the excuse of lowly circumstance for evil, and argues the wrongdoer ends up poorer in what matters.

Real Life Example

Nick Leeson's hidden losses break Barings Bank
1992 - February 1995 · Singapore
Nick Leeson, a trader for Britain's Barings Bank in Singapore, used a secret error account numbered 88888 to hide his trading losses, which had reached hundreds of millions of Singapore dollars by December 1994. After the Kobe earthquake in January 1995 sent the Tokyo market sharply lower, he kept making larger bets in an attempt to recover, and he fled Singapore on 23 February 1995, after which auditors found losses of about 2.2 billion Singapore dollars. Barings, one of Britain's oldest merchant banks, collapsed and was sold to the Dutch group ING for a token one pound, and Leeson was sentenced in Singapore to six and a half years in prison.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Each attempt to escape a hole by concealment and further risk made the hole deeper, until it swallowed the bank.
The kural warns that evil done to escape want only makes a person poorer still, and Leeson's hidden trades, made to recover losses, turned a loss into the ruin of the whole bank and of himself.
