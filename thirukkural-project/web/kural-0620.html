Kural 620

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

ஊழையும் உப்பக்கம் காண்பர் உலைவின்றித் தாழாது உஞற்று பவர்.
Oozhaiyum uppakkam kaanbar ulaivindrith Thaazhaadhu unjatru bavar.

Meaning

People who keep working hard without giving up or slowing down will even see fate turn around and run away from them.

Elementary

Think of a tiny stream running into a big rock. The rock seems stronger, but the water keeps flowing, day after day, until it carves a path around or even through. People who keep going without giving up can get past even the biggest ‘it’s just meant to be’.

University

The chapter ends with its boldest claim, the one most often quoted: tireless strivers will காண்பர் (see) the உப்பக்கம் (back, rear side) of ஊழ் (fate) itself, a battlefield image of an enemy turning its back in flight (Solomon Pappaiah: விதியையும் புறமுதுகு காட்டக் காண்பர்). The conditions are exacting: உலைவின்றி (without wavering), தாழாது (without delay or slackening) and உஞற்றுபவர் (those who exert themselves, an old verb for effort). Because chapter 38 insists on ஊழின் பெருவலி (fate’s great strength, kural 380), commentators have laboured to reconcile the two; Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar soften the claim so that strivers defeat the fate that obstructs a particular undertaking ‘in time’, while Kalaignar reads it as an outright rejection of fatalism. The couplet stands in the same Indian debate as the Mahabharata’s daiva-versus-puruṣakāra discussions and the Yoga Vasishtha’s praise of paurusha; the text agrees in both editions (sandhi: ஊழையு முப்பக்கங் காண்ப ருலைவின்றித் / தாழா துஞற்று பவர்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Yoga Vasishtha, Vasishtha tells Prince Rama that what people call fate (daiva) is nothing but the fruit of their own past effort, and that vigorous present effort (paurusha) can overcome it, as the stronger of two fighting rams prevails.
— Yoga Vasishtha, Mumukshu Prakarana (Book 2), chs. 4-9 [3]
The Mumukshu section devotes several chapters to arguing that self-effort, not destiny, is the decisive force in human life.
This is the fullest Sanskrit statement of the Kural’s claim that relentless effort can put fate to flight.
Christianity
“forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus.”
— Philippians 3:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes his life as an unrelenting forward effort that refuses to be defined by the past.
Paul’s tireless ‘pressing on’ is the Kural’s உலைவின்றித் தாழாது, striving without faltering or slackening.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves.”
— Qur’an 13:11 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an makes a people’s own inner change the condition on which their fortunes are transformed.
Human effort as the hinge of changed destiny is the Kural’s point, though framed within divine decree.
Judaism
“Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel; for thou hast striven with God and with men, and hast prevailed.”
— Genesis 32:29 (JPS 1917) [6]
After wrestling all night with a mysterious being and refusing to let go, Jacob is renamed for his striving.
Jacob’s victory through unrelenting struggle with a superhuman power is the Kural’s striver who makes fate turn its back.
Buddhism
“By effort and heedfulness, discipline and self-mastery, let the wise one make for himself an island which no flood can overwhelm.”
— Dhammapada 25 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that effort builds a refuge that the flood of circumstance cannot sweep away.
An island built by effort that no flood can overwhelm matches the Kural’s effort that stands firm against fate.
Jainism
“As a large tank, when its supply of water has been stopped, gradually dries up by the consumption of the water and by evaporation, so the Karman of a monk, which he acquired in millions of births, is annihilated by austerities, if there is no influx of bad Karman.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.5-6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jainism teaches that persistent, untiring austerity can wear away even karma built up over countless lives, so no 'destiny' is beyond the reach of effort.
The Kural's tireless strivers who see fate turn its back match the Jain teaching that unflagging exertion drains away the accumulated karma that makes up one's fate.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Bhagat Kabir in Raag Maaroo, often recited by Sikhs, the true hero is the one who never gives up the fight, whatever it costs him.
The Kural promises that those who strive without faltering will see even fate turn its back; Kabir praises the one who never leaves the field, even when cut to pieces.
Greek Mythology
Although Poseidon pursues him with storms and shipwreck for ten years, Odysseus keeps striving through every disaster and finally reaches Ithaca, winning his homecoming in the teeth of a god’s hostility.
— Homer, Odyssey, esp. Books 5 and 13 [10]
The Odyssey shows sustained human endurance prevailing against a hostile divine power.
Odysseus is the Greek image of the Kural’s striver who outlasts adverse fate.
Philosophy
Machiavelli judges that fortune may be arbiter of half our actions but leaves the other half to us, and compares her to a flooding river against which prudent men build dykes and embankments in calm times.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 25 [11]
In The Prince, ch. 25, he rejects fatalism and argues that energetic foresight (virtù) can check fortune.
Machiavelli’s dyke-building against fortune is a Renaissance version of the Kural’s effort that routs fate.

Real Life Example

Lis Hartel wins an Olympic dressage medal after polio
1944-1952 · Denmark and Helsinki, Finland
In 1944 the Danish rider Lis Hartel, then twenty-three and pregnant, contracted polio and was left paralysed below the knees. After years of rehabilitation she returned to competition, finishing second at the 1947 Scandinavian championships, and at the 1952 Helsinki Olympics, the first Games at which women could compete against men in dressage, she won the silver medal on her horse Jubilee. She needed help to mount and dismount, and the gold medallist Henri Saint Cyr helped her onto the podium; she won silver again in 1956.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A rider whom illness seemed to have shut out of her sport became the first woman to win an Olympic equestrian medal.
The kural says those who strive without faltering or slackening will see even fate turn its back, and Hartel's unbroken effort carried her past what polio seemed to have decided for her.
