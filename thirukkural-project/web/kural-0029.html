Kural 29

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

குணமென்னும் குன்றேறி நின்றார் வெகுளி கணமேயும் காத்தல் அரிது.
Gunamennum kundreri nindraar veguli, Kanameyum kaaththal aridhu.

Meaning

If people who stand on the high hill of good character ever get angry, the person who angered them can hardly be protected, even for a moment.

Elementary

Some people almost never get angry — so if one of them finally does, you know something has gone very wrong. This kural says the anger of a truly good person is like that: rare, and so serious that nobody can protect whoever caused it, not even for a second.

University

The couplet works by compounding two improbabilities. “Gunam-ennum kundru” — the hill called character — puts the sage at an elevation, and “nindraar,” having stood, stresses that he has held the position; such a person's “veguli,” hot anger, is by definition rare. Against that rarity Valluvar sets “kanam,” the blink of an eye, the smallest unit of Tamil time-reckoning, and the verb “kaaththal,” to guard or protect: even for a kanam the offender cannot be guarded. Commentators read the verse in two compatible ways — as a warning about the objective force of an ascetic's curse, a commonplace of Indian narrative, and as a moral observation that the anger of the habitually gentle is uniquely devastating because it can only mean a real wrong has been done. The verse is also a fine example of Valluvar's economy with sound: gunam / kundru and kanam / kaaththal set up internal echoes that make the couplet memorable in recitation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Indra, riding his elephant, treats a garland given to him by the sage Durvasa with contempt and lets it be trampled, the sage curses him on the spot; the gods immediately lose their splendour and their sovereignty, and only the vast labour of churning the ocean for the nectar restores them.
— Bhagavata Purana 8.5-8 (the curse of Durvasa and the churning of the ocean) [4]
A single moment's disrespect toward an ascetic costs the king of the gods his kingdom, and no power in the three worlds can shield him from the consequence once the word is spoken.
This is the Kural's warning in narrative form: no one, not even Indra, can be protected for an instant from a sage's roused anger.
Christianity
Ananias and his wife Sapphira sell property, secretly hold back part of the price, and lie about it to the apostles; Peter confronts each of them in turn, and each falls down dead at his words, so that great fear comes on the whole church.
— Acts 5:1-11 (KJV) [5]
The narrative gives no interval for appeal or protection — the apostle's rebuke and the consequence arrive together, and the community's reaction is fear rather than dispute.
As in the Kural, the offender against a holy man's integrity cannot be shielded even briefly once the sentence is spoken.
Islam
“Allah said, ‘I will declare war against him who shows hostility to a pious worshipper of Mine.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6502 (Book of Ar-Riqaq, chapter on humility) [6]
This hadith qudsi opens by putting God Himself on the side of the devoted servant, framing hostility toward such a person not as a private quarrel but as a declaration of war with heaven.
It states the Kural's warning at maximum force: whoever provokes a person of realised piety is beyond anyone's protection.
Judaism
“And [he also said:] warm yourself before the fire of the wise, but beware of being singed by their glowing coals, for their bite is the bite of a fox, and their sting is the sting of a scorpion, and their hiss is the hiss of a serpent, and all their words are like coals of fire.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:10 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Rabbi Eliezer urges closeness to the sages and in the same breath warns that the same heat which warms can burn, cataloguing the danger in escalating animal images.
This is the Kural's exact caution — the roused anger of a person of high character is a thing from which no one can be safely protected.
Buddhism
“One should not strike a holy man, nor should a holy man, when struck, give way to anger. Shame on him who strikes a holy man, and more shame on him who gives way to anger.”
— Dhammapada 389, Brahmanavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada protects the holy man from assault and in the same verse denies him the right to retaliate in anger, holding him to the higher standard precisely because of his attainment.
It addresses the Kural's situation and deliberately takes the other side: offending the holy is wrong, but the holy person's anger is a fault rather than a force.
Jainism
“‘do not injure him who cannot be injured, lest he consume you all by the fire (of his virtue).’ … ‘for in his wrath he might reduce the world to ashes.’ … “There is not the least hatred in me, neither now, nor before, nor in future.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.19-33, esp. 12.23, 12.28, 12.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
When Brahmin youths beat the outcaste-born monk Harikesa, the lady Bhadra warns them that such an ascetic's power could burn the world, and his attendant Yakshas strike the attackers down. The monk himself then says he bears no hatred at all.
Like the Kural, the story warns that whoever provokes one who stands on the hill of virtue cannot be shielded even for a moment. Jainism, though, takes the anger away from the monk: the danger lies in his power and in his divine protectors, while he himself stays free of wrath.
Sikhism
“Slandering the Saints, one’s life is cut short... One who is cursed by a Saint cannot be saved... But if the Compassionate Saint shows His Kindness, O Nanak! In the Company of the Saints, the slanderer may still be saved.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 13, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan warns that wronging a saint brings ruin, and that someone cursed by a saint cannot be saved. He ends by leaving room for the saint's mercy.
The Kural warns that no one can be shielded from the wrath of those who stand on the hill of character. Sukhmani gives the same warning and adds the saint's power to forgive.
Greek Mythology
Chryses, priest of Apollo, is insulted and driven off by Agamemnon when he comes to ransom his daughter; he walks along the shore, prays once to his god, and Apollo comes down in anger and shoots a plague into the Greek camp that burns for nine days until the army is forced to submit.
— Homer, Iliad 1.8-52 [11]
The Iliad opens with this episode precisely to show that slighting a holy man is not a social misstep but a catastrophe, and that the whole army cannot be shielded from the consequence of one man's arrogance.
The Kural's claim, dramatised: the offender and everyone near him are defenceless the moment a holy man's anger is provoked.
Philosophy
Seneca argues at length in De Ira that anger is not a useful tool that a good person may occasionally deploy but a temporary madness that no one of genuine virtue would consent to entertain even briefly, and that the truly wise man therefore does not become angry at all.
— Seneca, De Ira (On Anger), esp. I.1 and III.5 [12]
Stoicism treats freedom from anger as constitutive of the sage, so the notion of a virtuous person's wrath carrying moral authority is ruled out from the start.
It engages the Kural's subject directly and rejects its premise: for Seneca, a person standing on the hill of character does not get angry, so there is nothing to be shielded from.

Real Life Example

Emile Zola publishes J'accuse
13 January 1898 · Paris, France
The celebrated novelist Emile Zola published an open letter to the President of France, Felix Faure, in the newspaper L'Aurore, defending Captain Alfred Dreyfus and accusing the army of covering up his wrongful conviction for treason. About 200,000 copies were sold that day, and the letter became the turning point of the Dreyfus affair. Zola was convicted of libel, but the case was reopened and Dreyfus was fully cleared in 1906.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The public anger of a respected figure turned a buried injustice into a national cause that the guilty could no longer hide.
The kural warns that when those of high character are roused to anger the offender cannot be shielded, and Zola's letter stripped away the protection around those who had wronged Dreyfus.
