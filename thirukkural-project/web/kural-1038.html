Kural 1038

Agriculture · Uzhavu · உழவு

ஏரினும் நன்றால் எருவிடுதல் கட்டபின் நீரினும் நன்றதன் காப்பு.
Erinum nandraal eruvidudhal kattapin neerinum nandradhan kaappu.

Meaning

Adding manure is better than ploughing. After weeding, guarding the crop is even better than watering it.

Elementary

Planting a garden is not the end of the job. You feed the soil, pull out the weeds, and then you have to protect the plants from birds, goats and trampling feet. Watching over what you grew matters even more than watering it.

University

The couplet ranks the farmer's tasks: eruviduthal (எருவிடுதல், 'applying manure') is better than Er (ஏர், 'ploughing'), and after katta (கட்ட, 'weeding'; Kesikan and Varadarasanar gloss kattapin as 'after removing weeds') kaappu (காப்பு, 'guarding, protection') is better than neer (நீர், 'watering'). Puliyur Kesikan, following Parimelazhagar, reads the sequence as plough, manure, weed, water, guard, with each later step more decisive than the one before it; Kalaignar's paraphrase ranks weeding and watering as 'very good' and protection as best. The point is that the work ends with vigilant protection of what has been produced, a principle the Porutpal applies to wealth as well as crops. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“May the Field's Lord for us be full of sweetness, and may we follow after him uninjured.”
— Rig Veda 4.57.3 (tr. Ralph T.H. Griffith, 1896, sacred-texts.com) [3]
The Vedic farmers invoke Kshetrapati, the Lord or guardian of the Field, to keep the field and its workers safe and fruitful.
The hymn appeals to a guardian of the field, a religious form of the Kural's stress on protecting the crop.
Christianity
“The kingdom of heaven is likened unto a man which sowed good seed in his field: But while men slept, his enemy came and sowed tares among the wheat, and went his way.”
— Matthew 13:24-25 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus' parable the field is spoiled because no one watched it while the workers slept.
Unguarded fields are ruined, so watching over a crop matters as much as sowing it, as the Kural says.
Islam
“Whoever keeps a dog, one Qirat of the reward of his good deeds is deducted daily, unless the dog is used for guarding a farm or cattle.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2322 (sunnah.com) [5]
Islamic law makes a specific exception for dogs that guard crops and livestock, recognising the need to protect a farm.
Guarding the crop is important enough to override a general rule, which matches the Kural's high ranking of kaappu.
Judaism
“Catch us the foxes, The little foxes That ruin the vineyards— For our vineyard is in blossom.”
— Song of Songs 2:15 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The lovers' song calls for the vineyard to be protected from the small animals that damage it just when it blooms.
Guarding the growing crop at its most vulnerable stage is the Kural's kaappu.
Buddhism
“whatsoever wealth a householder is in possession of, obtained by dint of effort, collected by strength of arm, by the sweat of his brow, justly acquired by right means — such he husbands well by guarding and watching so that kings would not seize it, thieves would not steal it, fire would not burn it, water would not carry it away”
— Dighajanu (Vyagghapajja) Sutta, AN 8.54 (tr. Narada Thera, accesstoinsight.org) [7]
The Buddha names arakkha-sampada, the accomplishment of watchfulness, as essential for keeping what effort has produced.
Guarding what one's labour has produced is the Kural's kaappu, which it ranks above further inputs.
Jainism
“If you do not give me what I ask for, I who observe the Samitis, who am protected by the Guptis, who subdue my senses, what benefit, then, will you gain by your sacrifices?”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Jain monk is 'protected by the guptis', the three guards over mind, speech and body that keep new karma from flowing in.
The Jain guptis apply the Kural's lesson inwardly: after cultivating virtue, one must guard it.
Sikhism
“Make your mind the farmer, good deeds the farm, modesty the water, and your body the field. Let the Lord's Name be the seed, contentment the plow, and your humble dress the fence.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 595, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sorath, Guru Nanak lays out a full farming routine in spiritual terms, assigning a virtue to each task: tilling, watering, sowing, levelling and fencing the crop against harm.
The Kural ranks the farmer's tasks, placing manuring above ploughing and guarding above watering; Guru Nanak likewise lists each stage of farm work, ending with the fence that protects the crop.
Greek Mythology
“Let a slave follow a little behind with a mattock and make trouble for the birds by hiding the seed; for good management is the best for mortal men as bad management is the worst.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 469-471 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, 1914, sacred-texts.com) [10]
Hesiod advises posting a helper with a mattock behind the sower to keep the birds from the seed.
Hesiod's guard behind the plough is the Kural's kaappu, which it values above more inputs.
Philosophy
“If you want to make men fit to take charge, you must supervise their work and examine it, and be ready to reward work well carried through, and not shrink from punishing carelessness as it deserves.”
— Xenophon, Oeconomicus 12.19 (tr. E.C. Marchant, Loeb 1923, Perseus Digital Library) [11]
Ischomachus insists that a farm prospers only under constant supervision and careful attention.
Constant watchfulness over the work, rather than more inputs, is what secures the yield, which is close to the Kural's ranking.

Real Life Example

Peru's guano becomes the world's most prized fertiliser
1840s-1870s · Chincha Islands, Peru
From the early 1840s seabird guano from Peru's Chincha Islands was mined on a large scale and shipped to farms in Britain, Europe and the United States, where demand grew into what contemporaries called a guano mania. U.S. imports peaked at about 176,000 tons in 1854, and for decades guano supplied a major share of the Peruvian government's revenue.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Farmers on several continents were willing to pay heavily for manure, showing how decisive fertilising had become to harvests.
The kural ranks manuring above ploughing, and the nineteenth-century world paid for Peruvian manure as though it were treasure.
