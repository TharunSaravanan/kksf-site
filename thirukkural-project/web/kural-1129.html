Kural 1129

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

இமைப்பின் கரப்பாக்கு அறிவல் அனைத்திற்கே ஏதிலர் என்னும்இவ் வூர்.
Imaippin karappaakku arival anaiththirkae aedhilar ennumiv voor.

Meaning

I know that if I blink he will be hidden, so I keep my eyes open. For that reason alone, this town says he does not love me.

Elementary

Suppose you stayed up late because you were thinking about someone you love, and the next day people said, 'That person must be unkind to keep you awake!' The girl in this poem says the town blames her lover, though really it is just her love that keeps her eyes open.

University

The heroine explains her sleeplessness: she will not close her eyes because she knows that on blinking (imaippin) he would be hidden (karappaakku). Seeing her wakeful and pale, the ur (ஊர், 'town') concludes that he is aedhilar (ஏதிலர், 'a stranger, one without love') who has abandoned her, and she protests the injustice. Anaiththirkae ('for just that much') marks the town's verdict as unfair. This introduces alar (அலர், gossip), the theme of ch. 115. Mu. Varadarasanar and Pulavar Kesikan agree; Kalaignar adds that the town blames him for the 'pain of sleeplessness'. Pope: 'I fear his form to hide, nor close my eyes: / "Her love estranged is gone!" the village cries.' The second line is shared verbatim with 1130, a deliberate refrain. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“even when we can eagerly look upon Your beautiful face, so lovely with its adornment of curly locks, our pleasure is hindered by our eyelids, which were fashioned by the foolish creator.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.31.15 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, vedabase.io) [3]
The gopis complain to Krishna that even blinking costs them a glimpse of him, and blame the creator for making eyelids at all.
The Kural's heroine also refuses to blink for fear of losing sight of her lover; the gopis wish their eyelids had never been made.
Christianity
“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh”
— Song of Solomon 5:2 (KJV) [4]
The woman in the Song of Songs lies down to rest but her heart stays awake, alert for her beloved.
The Kural's heroine likewise stays awake for her lover, even if in her case it is her eyes that will not close.
Islam
“My eyes sleep, but my heart does not sleep.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3569 [5]
Asked whether he sleeps before the night prayer, the Prophet answers that only his eyes rest while his heart stays awake.
The Kural's heroine keeps her eyes open for the sake of the one in her heart; the Prophet's heart stays awake while his eyes sleep.
Judaism
“I will not give sleep to mine eyes, Nor slumber to mine eyelids;”
— Psalm 132:4 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalm recalls David's vow not to rest until he had found a dwelling place for God.
The Kural's heroine also refuses to let her eyelids close for the sake of the one she loves.
Buddhism
“Those who are ever vigilant, who discipline themselves day and night, and are ever intent upon Nibbana — their defilements fade away.”
— Dhammapada 226 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada praises wakefulness and vigilance, day and night, as the path to liberation.
The Kural's heroine is wakeful for love; Buddhism prizes the same unsleeping vigilance for the opposite goal of freedom from attachment.
Jainism
“The Venerable One, exerting himself, did not seek sleep for the sake of pleasure; he waked up himself, and slept only a little, free from desires.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.8.2.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
The Acaranga describes Mahavira staying almost sleepless during his years of austerity, out of vigilance rather than desire.
The Kural's heroine also barely sleeps, but out of love; Mahavira's wakefulness is the ascetic counterpart.
Sikhism
“Night and day, I utter, "Pri-a, Pri-a - Beloved, Beloved" with my mouth. I cannot sleep, even for an instant; I remain awake and aware.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1203, Raag Saarang, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan voices the bride-soul who stays wakeful through the night, calling out to the Beloved and refusing the sleep that would take Him from her.
The Kural's heroine will not close her eyes lest her lover be hidden; the Sikh bride-soul likewise stays awake without blinking into sleep, held by her Beloved.
Greek Mythology
Hera sets the hundred-eyed Argus to guard Io; he never closes all his eyes at once, so that some are always watching.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.622-688 (paraphrase) [10]
Argus is the Greek image of a watcher who never fully sleeps.
The Kural's heroine also refuses to close her eyes, though she watches for love, not on a goddess's orders.
Philosophy
Pascal observes that the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées 277 (Brunschvicg numbering; Lafuma 423) (paraphrase) [11]
For Pascal, love follows a logic that outsiders reasoning coolly cannot see.
The town judges the heroine's sleeplessness by outward signs and blames her lover; it cannot see the heart's real reason.

Real Life Example

Chopin and George Sand's winter in Majorca
1838–1839 · Palma and Valldemossa, Majorca, Spain
In November 1838 the composer Frédéric Chopin and the novelist George Sand travelled to Majorca, and from mid-December they lived in rented cells in the former Carthusian monastery at Valldemossa. Chopin fell seriously ill with a cough, and local people, fearing his illness was contagious, turned against the couple: one landlord made them leave, and villagers refused to sell them supplies. Sand cared for him while he completed his 24 Preludes, and they left in February 1839; she later described the stay in A Winter in Majorca (1842).
— Sources: [12] [13]
The winter produced some of Chopin's greatest music, even while the local community judged the couple harshly from the outside.
The kural's heroine protests that the town wrongly judges her lover from what it sees on the surface; the Majorcans likewise judged Chopin and Sand only by appearances, while the couple's devotion went on unseen inside the monastery.
