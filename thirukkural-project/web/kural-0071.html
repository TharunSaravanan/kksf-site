Kural 71

The Possession of Love · Anbudaimai · அன்புடைமை

அன்பிற்கும் உண்டோ அடைக்குந்தாழ் ஆர்வலர் புன்கணீர் பூசல் தரும்.
Anbirkum undo adaikkundhaazh aarvalar, Punkaneer poosal tharum.

Meaning

Is there any lock that can keep love hidden? When someone dear is suffering, the tears of sorrow of those who love them will give it away.

Elementary

Imagine trying to keep a puppy shut inside a box — it will still wiggle and whine and everyone will know it's there. Love is like that. Even if you never say "I love you," the moment someone you care about gets hurt, your eyes fill up and everybody can see exactly how you feel.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter on அன்பு (anbu, love) with an argument from evidence rather than exhortation. தாழ் (thaazh) is the wooden bolt or latch of a door, and the rhetorical question அடைக்குந்தாழ் உண்டோ — "is there a latch that shuts it in?" — treats love as a physical presence that leaks past every attempt at concealment. The proof offered is deliberately small: புன்கணீர் (punkaneer), literally "mean" or "slight" eye-water, a single involuntary tear shed for an ஆர்வலர் (aarvalar, one who is loved/desired). The commentator Parimelalhagar notes that love may not be available to காட்சி அளவை (perception) directly, yet it is established by அனுமான அளவை (inference) — a striking use of the technical vocabulary of Indian epistemology to argue that inner states are publicly readable through their effects. பூசல் (poosal) means a public outcry or uproar, so the couplet's paradox is that the quietest possible sign makes the loudest possible announcement.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A devotee whose speech is sometimes choked up, whose heart melts, who cries continually and sometimes laughs, who feels ashamed and cries out loudly and then dances — a devotee thus fixed in loving service to Me purifies the entire universe.”
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa (Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam) 11.14.24, trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda [3]
Krishna tells Uddhava that genuine love for God cannot stay inside a person; it breaks out involuntarily as a choked voice, uncontrollable weeping, and even dancing.
Exactly the Kural's point: love overflows the body's locks, and tears are the first thing to escape.
Christianity
“Jesus wept. Then said the Jews, Behold how he loved him!”
— Gospel of John 11:35–36, King James Version [4]
At the tomb of his friend Lazarus, Jesus breaks down in tears, and the bystanders immediately read those tears as evidence of how deeply he had loved the dead man.
This is the Kural's mechanism acted out literally: the tear is what tells the crowd about the love.
Islam
“The eyes are shedding tears and the heart is grieved, and we will not say except what pleases our Lord, O Ibrahim!”
— Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1303 (Book of Funerals), narrated by Anas ibn Mālik [5]
When his infant son Ibrahim died, the Prophet Muhammad wept; challenged by Ibn ʿAwf for weeping, he answered that this was mercy (raḥma), then spoke these words.
The Prophet does not try to latch his love away — he names the tears as the visible overflow of an inward bond.
Judaism
“Then Joseph could not refrain himself before all them that stood by him… And he wept aloud: and the Egyptians and the house of Pharaoh heard.”
— Genesis 45:1–2, King James Version [4]
Joseph has spent the whole scene disguised and composed before his brothers; the love he has been suppressing finally bursts out as weeping so loud that the Egyptian court hears it.
A textbook case of the Kural's claim — the bolt fails, and the tears become a public announcement (பூசல்).
Buddhism
In the account of the Buddha's final days, the attendant Ānanda withdraws to a doorpost and weeps at the news that his teacher will soon pass away; the Buddha sends for him and gently acknowledges his long devotion before teaching him about impermanence.
— Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, Dīgha Nikāya 16 (Ānanda's grief) [6]
Ānanda tries to go off alone precisely so no one will see him cry, but his grief is discovered and read by the community as the measure of his attachment and love.
As in the Kural, the attempt to shut love away behind a door fails, and tears betray it.
Jainism
“O great king, my loving and faithful wife moistened my breast with the tears of her eyes. The poor lady did not eat, nor drink, nor bathe, nor use perfumes, wreaths, and anointment, with my knowledge or without it.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.28–29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
The monk Anâthin tells King Srenika how, when he lay gravely ill, his wife's love showed itself in tears and in her refusal of food and adornment, though her love could not cure him.
The kural says love cannot be locked away and shows itself in tears at a loved one's pain; this wife's tears on her sick husband's breast are exactly that.
Sikhism
“I cannot survive without seeing my Beloved. My eyes are welling up with tears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 94, Raag Maajh, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a Maajh hymn of longing, Guru Ram Das describes love for the Beloved Lord that cannot be held in silence: separation shows itself at once in tears.
Like Valluvar's single tear, the welling eyes are the outward proof that betrays a love the heart could not lock inside.
Greek Mythology
“Odysseus concealed the tears he shed from all the rest, but Alcinous alone marked him and took heed, for he sat by him, and heard him groaning heavily.”
— Homer, Odyssey 8.93–95, trans. A. T. Murray (Loeb, 1919) [9]
At the Phaeacian feast, Odysseus pulls his cloak over his head to hide his weeping as the bard sings of Troy, but the king sitting beside him notices anyway.
The Kural's image exactly — a man bolts his feelings behind a cloak, and the tears still give him away.
Philosophy
“How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it except the pleasure of seeing it.”
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), Part I, Section I, Chapter I [10]
Adam Smith opens his moral philosophy by arguing that fellow-feeling is not chosen or calculated but built into human nature and triggered automatically by the sight of another's condition.
Smith gives the Kural's claim a philosophical form: the response to another's suffering is involuntary, which is precisely why it cannot be bolted in.

Real Life Example

Jim Redmond helps his injured son Derek finish the Olympic 400 metres
August 1992 · Barcelona, Spain
In the 400 metres semi-final at the Barcelona Olympics, the British runner Derek Redmond tore his hamstring on the back straight and began hobbling towards the finish alone. His father, Jim Redmond, came down from the stands, got past the stewards, put his arm around his son and helped him along the track. Just before the line his father let go, and Derek finished the race as a crowd of about 65,000 stood to applaud.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
The father's love could not be held back by rules, officials or the setting of an Olympic stadium; it showed itself openly and at once when his son was suffering.
The kural asks whether any bolt can lock love in, and a father pushing past security to hold his weeping son shows love breaking out the moment a loved one is in pain.
