Kural 993

Courtesy · Panpudaimai · பண்புடைமை

உறுப்பொத்தல் மக்களொப்பு அன்றால் வெறுத்தக்க பண்பொத்தல் ஒப்பதாம் ஒப்பு.
Uruppoththal makkaloppu andraal veruththakka panpoththal oppadhaam oppu.

Meaning

Having the same body parts does not make people truly alike. Being alike in good, pleasing character is the real likeness.

Elementary

Two dolls can look exactly the same on the outside, but one might be stuffed with cotton and the other with sharp pins. People are like that too: having two eyes and two hands like someone does not make you like them. Being kind like them does.

University

Uruppu-oththal (உறுப்பொத்தல், 'sameness of limbs') is denied the status of makkal-oppu (மக்களொப்பு, 'likeness among humans'); true oppu is panb-oththal (பண்பொத்தல்), likeness in qualities. The crux is veruththakka (வெறுத்தக்க): Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as 'fit to be joined' (பொருந்தத்தக்க), Solomon Pappaiah as qualities that unite heart with heart, and Pope as 'kindred quality' ('Similitude of kindred quality makes likeness true'), reflecting the older commentarial reading of வெறு as 'to be close, to cohere'. Sivayogi Sivakumar instead reads it in the modern sense 'hateful', yielding 'likeness in shunning what deserves shunning', a minority interpretation. Kalaignar draws the strong conclusion that only those alike in good qualities deserve to be counted as human at all, which anticipates kural 997. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Pṛthā, in this world there are two kinds of created beings. One is called divine and the other demoniac. I have already explained to you at length the divine qualities. Now hear from Me of the demoniac.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.6 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna divides human beings not by appearance or birth but by whether they bear divine or demonic qualities.
Like the Kural, it classes people by their qualities rather than their bodily sameness.
Christianity
“But the LORD said unto Samuel, Look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; because I have refused him: for the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart.”
— 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV) [4]
When Samuel is impressed by Eliab's looks, God tells him that the true measure of a person is the heart, not the body.
This sets aside outward bodily form in favour of inner character, as the Kural does.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth but He looks to your heart and to your deeds.”
— Sahih Muslim 2564c (Book of Virtue, Enjoining Good Manners) [5]
The Prophet teaches that appearance and possessions count for nothing before God compared with the heart and actions.
As in the Kural, a person's real identity lies in qualities and deeds, not in the shape of the face or body.
Judaism
“Rabbi said: don’t look at the container but at that which is in it: there is a new container full of old wine, and an old [container] in which there is not even new [wine].”
— Pirkei Avot 4:20 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Judah the Prince warns that outward vessels deceive and that worth lies in what they hold.
The Kural's point is the same: the body is a container, and true likeness lies in the qualities within.
Buddhism
In the Vasettha Sutta the Buddha explains that grasses, insects, animals, fish and birds show distinct marks of species by birth, but among human beings there are no such distinguishing bodily marks; distinctions among humans are only by name, and a person is what his deeds make him.
— Vasettha Sutta, Sutta Nipata 3.9 (also Majjhima Nikaya 98) [7]
The Buddha argues that human bodies are basically alike, so the real differences between people must lie in conduct.
This is close to the Kural's argument: bodily sameness is shared by all, and what truly distinguishes or unites people is their qualities.
Jainism
“One does not become a Sramana by the tonsure, nor a Brâhmana by the sacred syllable ôm, nor a Muni by living in the woods, nor a Tâpasa by wearing (clothes of) Kusa-grass and bark. One becomes a Sramana by equanimity, a Brâhmana by chastity, a Muni by knowledge, and a Tâpasa by penance.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.31–32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Gayaghosha teaches that outward marks such as a shaven head or bark clothing do not make a monk or sage; only the inner qualities do.
Like the Kural, it rejects outward resemblance as the measure of a person and locates true likeness in character.
Sikhism
“If you are indeed a Brahmin, born of a Brahmin mother, then why didn't you come by some other way?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 324, Raag Gauri, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir mocks the claim of birth-superiority by pointing out that every human body comes into the world in the same way. Bodily sameness proves nobody is higher, and what distinguishes people has to lie elsewhere.
The Kural says sameness of limbs does not make people truly alike and that real likeness is likeness of character, and Kabir likewise treats the shared body as settling nothing about a person's worth.
Greek Mythology
When Circe turns Odysseus' companions into swine, they take on the heads, voices and bristles of pigs, yet their minds remain as before, and they weep in their sties.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.237–243 [10]
Homer imagines bodies changed into beasts while the human mind stays intact.
The myth makes the Kural's point from the other side: what makes someone human is not the shape of the limbs but what is within.
Philosophy
“He lit a lamp in broad daylight and said, as he went about, ‘I am looking for a man.’”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 6.41 (tr. R. D. Hicks, via Wikisource) [11]
Diogenes the Cynic mocked the crowds of Athens by implying that among all the human bodies around him he could not find a true human being.
Diogenes dramatizes the Kural's claim that sharing the human form does not make one truly human.

Real Life Example

Albert Einstein gives Marian Anderson a room when a hotel refuses her
16 April 1937 · Princeton, New Jersey, United States
After the African American contralto Marian Anderson sang at the McCarter Theatre in Princeton on 16 April 1937, the town's Nassau Inn refused her a room because of her race. Albert Einstein, who had attended the concert, invited her to stay at his home instead. The two became friends, and on later visits to Princeton she stayed with Einstein until his death in 1955.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Einstein saw in Anderson a kindred spirit and an artist he admired, and treated the colour line that the hotel enforced as meaningless.
The kural says that real likeness between people lies in shared qualities of character rather than in bodily sameness, and the friendship between the physicist and the singer rested on exactly such kinship.
