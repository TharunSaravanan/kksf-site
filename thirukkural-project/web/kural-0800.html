Kural 800

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

மருவுக மாசற்றார் கேண்மைஒன் றீத்தும் ஒருவுக ஒப்பிலார் நட்பு.
Maruvuka maasatraar kenmaion reeththum oruvuka oppilaar natpu.

Meaning

Stay close to good people who have no faults. Leave the friendship of unworthy people, even if it costs you something to get away.

Elementary

Think of friends like food. Keep eating the fresh fruit. If something in your lunchbox has gone bad, throw it away, even if you paid for it. Keep good friends close, and let the bad ones go even if that costs you something.

University

The closing couplet balances two imperatives in parallel: maruvuka (மருவுக, ‘cling to, embrace’) and oruvuka (ஒருவுக, ‘leave, shun’). Maasatraar (மாசற்றார்) are ‘those without stain’, while oppilaar (ஒப்பிலார்) is ambiguous: Parimelazhagar takes it as those who do not accord with the world’s good ways, Solomon Pappaiah follows with ‘those who do not fit with the world’, and Mu. Varadarasanar reads ‘those who lack matching virtue’. Onru eeththum (ஒன்று ஈத்தும், ‘even by giving something’) mirrors koduththum in kural 794: just as a good friend is worth buying, a bad one is worth paying to be rid of. The ytamizh and thirukkural.net texts read ஈத்தும், while the sandhi-joined edition divides the line as கேண்மையொன் றீத்து மொருவுக, which reads the same once the sandhi is undone.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Old age killeth beauty; patience, hope; death, life; the practice of virtue, worldly enjoyments; lust, modesty; companionship with the wicked, good behaviour; anger, prosperity; and pride, everything.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXVII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura lists things that destroy other things, including bad company destroying good conduct.
It gives the reason the Kural says to leave unsuitable friends at any cost: bad company eats away at good conduct.
Christianity
“Be not deceived: evil communications corrupt good manners.”
— 1 Corinthians 15:33 (KJV) [5]
Paul quotes a proverb, found earlier in the Greek poet Menander, warning that bad company corrupts good character.
Paul and Valluvar both warn that the wrong friends corrupt, so they must be left behind.
Islam
“And keep yourself patient [by being] with those who call upon their Lord in the morning and the evening, seeking His countenance … and do not obey one whose heart We have made heedless of Our remembrance and who follows his desire and whose affair is ever [in] neglect.”
— Qur’an 18:28 (Sahih International) [6]
The Prophet is told to stay with the sincere worshippers and not to follow the heedless.
The verse has the same two halves as the Kural: hold fast to the pure, turn from the unworthy.
Judaism
“Nittai the Arbelite used to say: keep a distance from an evil neighbor, do not become attached to the wicked, and do not abandon faith in [divine] retribution.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:7 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Nittai of Arbel tells his students to keep away from bad neighbours and not to bind themselves to the wicked.
This is the Kural’s second imperative, ‘shun the unsuitable’, in rabbinic form.
Buddhism
“Do not associate with evil companions; do not seek the fellowship of the vile. Associate with the good friends; seek the fellowship of noble men.”
— Dhammapada 78 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The verse sets avoidance of evil companions directly beside the seeking of noble friends.
Its two halves mirror the Kural’s maruvuka and oruvuka almost exactly.
Jainism
“If he does not meet with a clever companion who surpasses or equals him in virtue, he should live by himself, abstaining from sins and not devoted to pleasures.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahāvīra's teaching sets a high bar for companionship: a companion equal or superior in virtue, and otherwise solitude rather than company with someone unworthy.
The Kural tells us to cling to the friendship of the blameless and to leave unfit friends whatever it costs, and the Jain canon likewise accepts only virtuous companions and prefers solitude to unworthy company.
Sikhism
“Kabir! I will remain in the Saadh Sangat, the Company of the Holy, even if I have only coarse bread to eat. Whatever will be, will be. I will not associate with the faithless cynics.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir 99 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir resolves to hold to good company at any material cost, even a diet of coarse bread, and to refuse the company of faithless people whatever the consequences.
The Kural says to cling to the friendship of the blameless and leave the unsuitable even if it costs you something; Kabir accepts poverty as the price of keeping good company and shunning bad.
Greek Mythology
“A bad neighbour is as great a plague as a good one is a great blessing; he who enjoys a good neighbour has a precious possession. Not even an ox would die but for a bad neighbour.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 346-348 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod tells Perses that the people living closest to you are either a blessing or a plague.
Like the Kural, Hesiod puts the good associate and the bad one side by side and counts the first as a treasure.
Philosophy
“Perfect friendship is the friendship of men who are good, and alike in virtue; for these wish well alike to each other qua good, and they are good themselves.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle says the highest friendship exists only between good people who are alike in virtue.
Aristotle’s ‘alike in virtue’ is the opposite of the Kural’s oppilaar, the unlike or unfit whose friendship should be left.

Real Life Example

Eisenhower accepts the resignation of Sherman Adams
22 September 1958 · Washington, D.C., United States
In 1958 a congressional subcommittee revealed that Sherman Adams, President Dwight D. Eisenhower's chief of staff, had accepted gifts, including a vicuna coat and an oriental rug, as well as hotel bills from his old friend Bernard Goldfine, a New England textile manufacturer in trouble with federal regulators, and that Adams had contacted those agencies about Goldfine's cases. Eisenhower at first defended Adams publicly, but as pressure from his own party mounted before the 1958 elections, Adams resigned, and Eisenhower accepted the resignation with deep regret on 22 September 1958.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Adams's career was lost to an unwise friendship, and Eisenhower had to give up his most valued aide to protect his administration.
The kural urges us to cling to the blameless and to let go of unsuitable associations even at a cost, and Eisenhower paid the price of losing an aide he greatly valued because of Adams's ties to Goldfine.
