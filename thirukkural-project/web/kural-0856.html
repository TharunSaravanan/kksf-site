Kural 856

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இகலின் மிகலினிது என்பவன் வாழ்க்கை தவலும் கெடலும் நணித்து.
Ikalin mikalinidhu enbavan vaazhkkai thavalum kedalum naniththu.

Meaning

A person who thinks it is sweet to get ahead by fighting with others will soon see their life fail and fall apart.

Elementary

Some people enjoy picking fights because winning feels good. But someone who always looks for a fight soon has no friends and lots of enemies, and things start to go wrong fast. The poet warns that loving fights leads to falling down.

University

Ikalin mikal inidhu (இகலின் மிகல் இனிது): ‘to excel (mikal) by means of hostility is sweet’ is the boast of the quarrelsome man. Valluvar answers that for him thavalum (தவலும், failing, slipping) and kedalum (கெடலும், perishing) are naniththu (நணித்து, near). Puliyur Kesigan reads mikal as ‘exceeding others in hostility’, Mu. Varadarasanar as ‘victory through hostility’, and Kalaignar as ‘thinking victory easy through opposition’. Pappaiah gives a distinctive reading: such a life’s survival is brief, and its ruin also comes within a brief time. The couplet turns from the inner disease to its social consequence. Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Sabha Parva of the Mahabharata, Shishupala heaps insult after insult on Krishna at Yudhishthira’s Rajasuya sacrifice, delighting in his hostility, until Krishna, having forborne a hundred offences, slays him in the assembly.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Shishupala-vadha) [3]
A king who revelled in hostility met swift destruction.
He is the Kural’s man who found hostility sweet and found ruin near.
Christianity
“But if ye bite and devour one another, take heed that ye be not consumed one of another.”
— Galatians 5:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that a community that feeds on infighting will consume itself.
Both texts warn that relish for hostility ends in self-destruction.
Islam
“And obey Allah and His Messenger, and do not dispute and [thus] lose courage and [then] your strength would depart; and be patient.”
— Qur’an 8:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that quarrelling saps courage and makes strength vanish.
The Kural similarly says hostility brings swift failure.
Judaism
The Talmud tells of a host who, out of personal hatred, threw a man named Bar Kamsa out of his feast; the humiliated guest’s revenge set in motion events the sages connect with the destruction of Jerusalem.
— Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 55b-56a [6]
A private grudge, enjoyed and indulged, escalated into national catastrophe.
This shows how near ruin is to those who indulge hostility.
Buddhism
“Victory begets enmity; the defeated dwell in pain. Happily the peaceful live, discarding both victory and defeat.”
— Dhammapada 201 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that winning by conflict only breeds more enmity.
The Kural likewise exposes the sweetness of victory through hostility as a trap.
Jainism
“He stumbled and fell into a pit that was like the mouth of hell. As he fell, the sharp axe fell on him and split his head in two. For there was maturing of bad karma.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X (Mahāvīracaritra), ch. III, part 10 'Episode of Caṇḍakauśika' (tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In a former birth, the hot-tempered ascetic Kauśika rushes with an axe at men breaking into his woods. His own fury throws him into a pit, where his own axe kills him, and the text adds that anger carries on into the next birth.
The Kural warns that for whoever thinks it sweet to get ahead through hostility, failure and ruin are close at hand, and Kauśika's charge ends in his destruction within moments.
Sikhism
“Do not speak ill of others, or get involved in arguments.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 566, Raag Vadhans, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a chhant in Raag Vadhans Guru Nanak warns against calling others bad and stirring up quarrels.
The Kural warns that one who finds it sweet to get ahead through hostility is close to ruin; Guru Nanak likewise steers the seeker away from picking quarrels.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad 18, after Patroclus dies because of his quarrel with Agamemnon, Achilles wishes that strife would perish from gods and men, along with the anger that grows sweeter than honey in a man’s heart.
— Homer, Iliad 18.107-110 [10]
Achilles recognises too late that the sweetness of his anger brought ruin on those he loved.
This is the Kural’s warning: hostility tastes sweet, but ruin is near.
Philosophy
Thucydides describes how, during the civil strife at Corcyra, factions came to prize revenge over self-preservation and admired the violent, until the whole Greek world was convulsed and its character corrupted.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 3.82-83 [11]
The historian shows that a culture which comes to love conflict quickly destroys itself.
The Kural states the same law for a single life.

Real Life Example

Charles the Bold's wars end in his death at Nancy
1474-1477 · Grandson and Morat, Switzerland; Nancy, Lorraine, France
Charles the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, pursued constant wars of expansion, and after a failed siege of Neuss he went to war against the Swiss confederates and the Duke of Lorraine. In 1476 the Swiss defeated him at Grandson and again at Morat. On 5 January 1477 he was killed in battle at Nancy against Rene II of Lorraine and his Swiss allies, and his Burgundian state was then divided between the King of France and the Habsburgs.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Within three years of his greatest power, Charles's appetite for war had destroyed both himself and the independent Burgundian state he had sought to enlarge.
The kural warns that for one who thinks it sweet to get ahead through hostility, failure and ruin are near, and Charles's relentless warring brought defeat, death and the break-up of his realm within a few years.
