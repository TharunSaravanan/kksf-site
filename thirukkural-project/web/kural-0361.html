Kural 361

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

அவாஎன்ப எல்லா உயிர்க்கும் எஞ்ஞான்றும் தவாஅப் பிறப்பீனும் வித்து.
Avaa enba ellaa uyirkkum egnaandrum Thavaaap pirappeenum viththu.

Meaning

People say that desire is the seed that, for every living being and at all times, never fails to cause one birth after another.

Elementary

A seed falls into the ground and grows into a plant, and that plant drops more seeds that grow again and again. Valluvar says wanting things is like that seed: as long as we keep wanting, we keep getting planted into new lives, over and over.

University

The chapter that closes the ascetic section (Thuravaraviyal) opens by naming avaa (craving) as the vittu, the seed, of an unbroken chain of births (thavaap pirappu). The agricultural metaphor is exact: a seed holds the whole future plant, and craving holds the whole future of embodied existence, so destroying the seed leaves the cycle nothing to grow from. The word “enba” (“they say”) shows Valluvar reporting a consensus of the wise rather than inventing a doctrine, and the idea is indeed shared across Indian soteriologies: the Upanishadic teaching that desire determines rebirth, the Buddhist second noble truth that craving leads to renewed existence, and the Jain account of passions binding karma. Placed right after “Knowledge of Truth” (Meyyunarthal) and just before “Destiny” (Oozh), the chapter that ends Book 1, this chapter makes the uprooting of desire the practical summit of the renunciant’s path.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who forms desires in his mind, is born again through his desires here and there. But to him whose desires are fulfilled and who is conscious of the true Self (within himself) all desires vanish, even here on earth.”
— Mundaka Upanishad 3.2.2 (trans. Max Müller, SBE 15) [3]
The Upanishad teaches that it is desire itself that pulls a person back into birth again and again, and that desire falls away only when the true Self is known.
Both texts name desire as the thing that keeps producing new births. The verse just before it in the Mundaka even calls this the “seed” that the wise go beyond.
Christianity
“But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed. Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death.”
— James 1:14-15 (KJV) [4]
James describes desire as something that conceives and gives birth: lust gives birth to sin, and sin grows up into death.
Like the Kural’s ‘seed’, James treats desire as the thing everything else grows from. Here it produces sin and death, not rebirth.
Islam
“And I do not acquit myself. Indeed, the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon which my Lord has mercy.”
— Qur’an 12:53 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the lower self (nafs) as constantly urging a person toward wrongdoing unless God’s mercy restrains it.
Both locate the root of trouble inside a person’s own urges. Islam sees the result as sin, not a cycle of rebirth.
Judaism
“Rabbi Elazar Ha-kappar said: envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit) [6]
The Mishnah names three forms of craving that drive a person out of a good life in this world and out of the world to come.
Both treat desire as the root of a person’s ruin. The Kural says desire keeps producing births, while the Mishnah says it drives a person out of the world.
Buddhism
“The origin of suffering, as a noble truth, is this: It is the craving that produces renewal of being accompanied by enjoyment and lust, and enjoying this and that; in other words, craving for sensual desires, craving for being, craving for non-being.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (trans. Ñanamoli Thera) [7]
In his first sermon, the Buddha teaches that craving (tanha) is what produces renewed existence, which is rebirth.
This is almost the same statement as the Kural: craving is the seed that keeps producing new births.
Jainism
“As the crane is produced from an egg, and the egg is produced from a crane, so they call desire the origin of delusion, and delusion the origin of desire. Love and hatred are caused by Karman, and they say that Karman has its origin in delusion; Karman is the root of birth and death, and birth and death they call misery.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.6–7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana sets out a chain in which desire (Jacobi notes the original word is tṛṣṇā, craving) and delusion breed each other, delusion binds karmic matter to the soul, and that karma is the root of repeated birth and death.
Valluvar calls desire the seed that unfailingly yields birth after birth for every being, and the Jain text traces the same line from craving through karma to the 'root of birth and death'.
Sikhism
“Some are happy, and some are sad. Caught in the desires of the mind, they perish.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 152, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that people, whether happy or sad, are destroyed by being caught in the desires and attachments of the mind.
The Kural calls desire the seed that unfailingly produces birth after birth; Guru Nanak says desire is what destroys people, though he does not here call it the seed of rebirth.
Greek Mythology
“Eros (Love), fairest among the deathless gods, who unnerves the limbs and overcomes the mind and wise counsels of all gods and all men within them.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 120-122 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
In Hesiod’s creation story, Eros (Desire) is one of the very first powers to exist, and it drives all the later pairings and births of the gods.
In both texts desire comes first and generations grow out of it. Hesiod honors that power, while Valluvar wants it removed.
Philosophy
Schopenhauer argues that the world is driven by a blind “will-to-live”, a restless striving. All willing springs from lack and so from suffering. Each satisfied wish only makes room for new ones, and the only real deliverance is for the will to deny itself.
— Arthur Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Representation, Vol. 1, §§ 56-57, 68 (paraphrase) [11]
For Schopenhauer, endless wanting is the engine that keeps life and suffering going. Peace comes only when wanting itself is given up.
This is close to the Kural’s claim that desire is the seed of ceaseless existence. Schopenhauer himself pointed to Indian thought as a parallel.

Real Life Example

The Klondike Gold Rush
1896-1899 · Klondike region and Dawson City, Yukon, Canada
Gold was found on Bonanza Creek, a tributary of the Klondike River, in August 1896, and when news reached the United States in July 1897 it set off a stampede. More than 100,000 people set out for the Klondike, but only some 30,000 to 40,000 actually reached Dawson City, many after brutal journeys over the Chilkoot and White passes. According to the US National Park Service, only a few of those who arrived obtained the wealth they had dreamed of along the way.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single report of riches drew tens of thousands into hardship, and the hope of gain kept pulling new waves of people north long after the best claims had been taken.
The kural calls desire the seed that unfailingly produces birth after birth, and the gold rush shows on a human scale how one seed of craving keeps generating new journeys, new hardships and new disappointments.
