Kural 64

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

அமிழ்தினும் ஆற்ற இனிதேதம் மக்கள் சிறுகை அளாவிய கூழ்.
Amizhdhinum Aatra Inidhedham Makkal, Sirukai Alaaviya Koozh.

Meaning

Plain porridge that your own children have stirred with their small hands tastes far sweeter than amrita (the drink of the gods).

Elementary

Imagine the most delicious dessert in the world — the kind a king would eat. Now imagine ordinary rice that your little brother or sister has squished and messed up with their tiny fingers. This kural says the messy rice tastes better. Not because of the rice — because of whose hands were in it.

University

This is perhaps the most quoted couplet in the Kural's domestic chapters, and its power lies in a calculated collision of registers. அமிழ்து (amiḻtu, Sanskrit amṛta) is the immortalising nectar of the gods, the top of the classical scale of sweetness; கூழ் (kūḻ) is at the bottom — a thin boiled gruel or mash, the plainest food in the Tamil larder. Between them Valluvar inserts the word that does all the work: அளாவிய (aḷāviya), "stirred, mixed about, dabbled in" by a சிறுகை, a "small hand." Parimēlaḻakar glosses அளாவல் by quoting a Puṟanāṉūṟu line about a child "dropping and touching and mouthing and churning" the ghee-rice until it is smeared everywhere — that is, he understands the verse to be describing genuine mess, not a decorous gesture. The theological move is quietly radical: the transcendent standard of sweetness is beaten not by a better food but by a relationship, and the sensory language of the next two couplets (touch, hearing) shows Valluvar building a deliberate small phenomenology of parenthood through the bodily senses.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Bhāgavata Purāṇa's Kṛṣṇa cycle dwells on the infant god raiding the butter pots, smearing curds over himself and the household, and being caught with his hands in the food — and on Yaśodā's helpless delight at exactly this, which becomes the devotional mode called vātsalya, love in the manner of a parent for a small child.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.8–10.9 (the butter-thief episodes) [5]
An entire branch of Hindu devotion is built on the claim that God is most lovable in the form of a messy toddler with food on his hands.
Both traditions locate supreme sweetness not in the food but in the small hands that have been in it.
Christianity
“There is a lad here, which hath five barley loaves, and two small fishes: but what are they among so many?”
— John 6:9 (King James Version) [6]
Andrew points out a boy's plain, inadequate packed lunch — barley was the cheap grain — and that humble food from a child's hands becomes the meal that feeds five thousand.
Both texts take the poorest imaginable food, pass it through a child's hands, and find it has become more than the finest fare.
Islam
“O Allah, shower blessings upon us in our city, and in our fruits, in our mudd and in our sa', blessings upon blessings,” — and he would then give that fruit to the youngest of the children present.”
— Sahih Muslim 1373b, Book of Pilgrimage (narrated by Abū Hurayrah) [7]
When the season's first fruit was brought to the Prophet, he would pray over it and then hand the choicest first taste not to an elder or a guest but to the smallest child in the room.
Like the Kural, it treats food as something whose value is completed by a child's hands rather than by its own quality.
Judaism
“And it shall come to pass, when your children shall say unto you, What mean ye by this service? That ye shall say, It is the sacrifice of the LORD's passover.”
— Exodus 12:26–27 (King James Version) [6]
The Passover meal is legislated from the outset around the child's question, so that the family's central ritual dinner is structured by and for the children at the table.
Both traditions make the presence of children the thing that transforms an ordinary family meal into the sweetest one there is.
Buddhism
In the Ashokāvadāna a small boy named Jaya, playing in the dirt, sees the Buddha passing on alms round and, having nothing else, drops a handful of dust into his begging bowl as if it were food; the Buddha accepts it, and predicts the child will be reborn as the great emperor Aśoka.
— Ashokāvadāna, the gift of dust (Divyāvadāna, ch. 26) [8]
The offering is literally worthless and literally dirty, yet it is received as the most consequential gift in the story because of the hands and heart it came from.
As in the Kural, what a child's small hand has put into the food is worth incomparably more than the food itself.
Jainism
On the twelfth day after Mahâvîra's birth his parents prepared plenty of food, drink, spices and sweetmeats, invited friends, relatives and the Jñâtrika Kshatriyas, and sat down with them to eat, taste and share the meal before announcing the child's name, Vardhamâna.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahâvîra §§104–107 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The Kalpa Sutra makes a shared family meal the centre of the celebration of a newborn son.
The scene links food and the joy of a child, but nothing in it matches Valluvar's image of a meal made sweeter than nectar by a small child's hands.
Sikhism
“As many mistakes as the son commits, his mother does not hold them against him in her mind.” … “If the son, in anger, runs away, even then, his mother does not hold it against him in her mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 478, Raag Asa, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir uses a parent's indulgent love, which overlooks whatever mess or mischief a child makes, as the image for God's forgiveness of the devotee.
It reflects the same parental tenderness the Kural celebrates, in which food a child has messed with becomes sweeter than nectar instead of spoiled.
Greek Mythology
“for by day rich-crowned Demeter would anoint him with ambrosia as if he were the offspring of a god and breathe sweetly upon him as she held him in her bosom”
— Homeric Hymn 2 (To Demeter), 235–237 (trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White, 1914) [11]
Disguised as a nurse in the house of Celeus, the grieving goddess Demeter secretly feeds the infant Demophoön the food of the immortals, trying to make a mortal child divine.
The Greek scene runs the Kural's image in reverse — there a goddess gives ambrosia to a child, while Valluvar says a child's hands make plain gruel beat ambrosia outright.
Philosophy
“And so plain savours bring us a pleasure equal to a luxurious diet, when all the pain due to want is removed; and bread and water produce the highest pleasure, when one who needs them puts them to his lips.”
— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus (trans. Cyril Bailey, 1926) [12]
Epicurus argues that the pleasure of eating peaks with simple food properly met, and that expensive fare adds nothing to the actual quality of the experience.
Both texts dethrone luxury food, though Valluvar's plain gruel wins by affection where Epicurus's bread wins by the analysis of desire.

Real Life Example

Victor Hugo publishes The Art of Being a Grandfather
May 1877 · Paris, France
After his son Charles died in 1871, Victor Hugo took charge of the upbringing of his two young grandchildren, Georges and Jeanne. In May 1877 he published L'Art d'etre grand-pere (The Art of Being a Grandfather), a collection of poems devoted to the two children and their everyday play. The book was a great success, and Georges and Jeanne became famous through it.
— Sources: [13] [14]
France's most celebrated poet, near the end of a career spent on epic and political themes, chose small children and their ordinary doings as the subject of one of his last collections.
The kural says the plain food a child's little hands have stirred is sweeter than the nectar of the gods, and Hugo set the small daily doings of his grandchildren above the grand themes of his earlier work.
