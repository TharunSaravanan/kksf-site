Kural 293

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

தன்நெஞ் சறிவது பொய்யற்க பொய்த்தபின் தன்நெஞ்சே தன்னைச் சுடும்.
Than nenju arivadhu poyyarka poyththapin Than nenjae thannaich chudum.

Meaning

Do not lie about what your own heart knows is true. Once you lie, your own heart will burn you with guilt.

Elementary

Have you ever told a fib and then felt a hot, uncomfortable feeling inside, even if no one found out? That feeling is your own heart “burning” you. This kural says: don’t lie against what you know is true, because your conscience will punish you.

University

The couplet moves truthfulness from the social sphere into the interior. ‘Than nenju arivadhu’ — what one’s own heart knows — specifies a deliberate lie, one told against one’s own knowledge. The punishment is not external: ‘than nenjae thannaich chudum,’ the self’s own heart scorches the self. The repetition of than (self) three times across two lines enacts the reflexive structure of conscience. Kalaignar explicitly glosses nenju as manachaatchi, ‘conscience,’ and Pappaiah adds that the burning intensifies when the world discovers the lie — combining inner guilt with outer shame.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Soul itself is the witness of the Soul, and the Soul is the refuge of the Soul; despise not thy own Soul, the supreme witness of men.”
— Manusmriti 8.84 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu warns witnesses in court not to lie, because their own inner self is the highest witness; the next verse says the wicked think no one sees them, but the gods and ‘the male within their own breasts’ see them.
Both texts make the inner self the witness and judge of every lie.
Christianity
“For if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things. Beloved, if our heart condemn us not, then have we confidence toward God.”
— 1 John 3:20-21 (KJV) [4]
John describes the heart as an inner court that condemns or acquits us, with God knowing even more than our heart.
The self-accusing heart is the Kural’s burning heart.
Islam
“Leave what makes you in doubt for what does not make you in doubt. The truth brings tranquility while falsehood sows doubt.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2518 (graded Sahih) [5]
The Prophet taught that truth settles the heart while lying leaves it restless and troubled.
The Kural’s ‘burning heart’ after a lie is Islam’s ‘doubt’ that falsehood sows within.
Judaism
“LORD, who shall abide in thy tabernacle? who shall dwell in thy holy hill? He that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart.”
— Psalm 15:1-2 (KJV) [4]
The Psalmist says the one fit to stand before God speaks truth ‘in his heart’ — inner words agreeing with outer ones.
Both demand that speech never betray what the heart knows.
Buddhism
“When anyone feels no shame in telling a deliberate lie, there is no evil, I tell you, he will not do.”
— Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 61 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha teaches his young son Rahula that shamelessness about deliberate lies empties a person of spiritual worth, and tells him to reflect on every action like looking in a mirror.
The Kural’s burning heart is exactly the healthy shame the Buddha wants Rahula to keep.
Jainism
“After and before he has lied, and when he is on the point of lying, he feels infinitely unhappy.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana describes the inner torment that surrounds a lie: before it, while it is being told, and after it.
Valluvar warns that once you lie against what your own heart knows, your heart will burn you. The Jain verse likewise places the punishment for lying inside the liar, as 'infinite' unhappiness.
Sikhism
“Speaking lies, one eats poison; the self-willed manmukh departs, crying out in pain.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 948, Raag Raamkalee (Ramkali ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok of the Vaar in Raag Raamkalee, Guru Amar Das says that the liar feeds himself poison. The self-willed person (manmukh) who lives by falsehood leaves the world in anguish.
The Kural warns that a lie told against what one's own heart knows will burn that heart. Guru Amar Das likewise makes the lie a poison taken by the liar himself.
Greek Mythology
“For hateful in my eyes, even as the gates of Hades, is that man that hideth one thing in his mind and sayeth another.”
— Homer, Iliad 9.312-313 (tr. A. T. Murray) [9]
Achilles tells Odysseus he despises anyone whose words differ from what is in his mind.
Achilles condemns the same split the Kural forbids: saying what the heart knows is false.
Philosophy
In the Republic (382a-c), Socrates distinguishes the ‘true lie’ — falsehood lodged in the soul about the most important things — which gods and men hate above all, from the mere lie in words.
— Plato, Republic 382a-c (paraphrase) [10]
For Plato, the worst harm of falsehood is to the liar’s own soul.
Both thinkers locate the deepest damage of lying inside the liar.

Real Life Example

Thomas More refuses to swear the oath his conscience rejected
13 April 1534 - 6 July 1535 · Lambeth Palace and the Tower of London, London, England
On 13 April 1534 Sir Thomas More, the former Lord Chancellor, appeared before the king's commissioners at Lambeth and said he would accept the royal succession but refused to swear the Oath of Succession, whose preamble renounced papal authority and affirmed Henry VIII's supremacy over the Church. He was imprisoned in the Tower of London, where he was interrogated several times and continued to refuse to affirm what he did not believe. He was convicted of treason on 1 July 1535 and beheaded on Tower Hill on 6 July 1535.
— Sources: [11] [12]
More chose imprisonment and death rather than state on oath something his own conscience held to be false.
The kural warns never to lie about what one's own heart knows to be true, and More would not utter such a lie even to save his life.
