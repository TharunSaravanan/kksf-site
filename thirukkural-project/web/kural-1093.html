Kural 1093

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

நோக்கினாள் நோக்கி இறைஞ்சினாள் அஃதவள் யாப்பினுள் அட்டிய நீர்.
Nokkinaal nokki irainjinaal aqdhaval yaappinul attiya neer.

Meaning

She looked at me, and after looking, she lowered her head. That was like water poured on the growing plant of our love.

Elementary

Imagine planting a seed with a friend. Every time you water it, it grows a little. When someone shyly looks at you and then looks down with a little smile, it is like giving water to the seed of a new friendship.

University

Iraiñcināḷ (இறைஞ்சினாள்) means ‘she bowed, lowered her head’, the classic sign of nāṇam (நாணம், maidenly modesty) that the hero reads as consent. The key image is yāppu (யாப்பு), which the commentators take as the bond or bed of love: Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as the love she is nurturing, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar as the ‘crop’ (payir) of love, and Puliyur Kesikan as the anpup-payir watered by her sign. Aṭṭiya nīr (அட்டிய நீர்) is water poured into a channel or plot, an agricultural metaphor in keeping with the marutam landscape of settled love. Pope: ‘She looked, and looking drooped her head: / On springing shoot of love ’tis water shed!’ The text is the same on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“(SHAKUNTALA darts a glance at him, then looks down.)”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III, stage direction (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
In Act III, when the king speaks tenderly to her, Shakuntala glances at him and then lowers her eyes.
The glance followed by the lowered head is exactly the sequence the Kural describes as watering love.
Christianity
“O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock, in the secret places of the stairs, let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.”
— Song of Solomon 2:14 (KJV) [4]
The lover calls the shy beloved, hidden like a dove, to show her face.
The beloved’s shy hiding, which only deepens the lover’s longing, is close to the Kural’s modest bowed head.
Islam
“Then one of the two women came to him walking with shyness.”
— Qur’an 28:25 (Sahih International) [5]
The daughter of the old man of Midian approaches Moses with modesty, and the meeting leads to their marriage.
Her shyness on meeting Moses is the same nāṇam that the Kural reads as a hopeful sign of love.
Judaism
“Raising her eyes, Rebekah saw Isaac. She alighted from the camel and said to the servant, ‘Who is that man walking in the field toward us?’ And the servant said, ‘That is my master.’ So she took her veil and covered herself.”
— Genesis 24:64-65 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Rebekah lifts her eyes, sees her future husband, and then modestly covers herself with her veil.
She looks, then veils herself: the same pairing of a glance and modest withdrawal that the Kural describes.
Buddhism
In the Buddhist story of the past life of Gotama, the young ascetic Sumedha meets the maiden Sumitta, who gives him lotuses for the Buddha Dipankara on condition that she may be his companion in future lives.
— Jataka Nidanakatha (story of Sumedha and Dipankara) [7]
Their bond begins in one small exchange at a meeting and grows across lifetimes.
Like the Kural’s watered shoot, a single modest gesture becomes the seed of a lasting love.
Jainism
“As the crane is produced from an egg, and the egg is produced from a crane, so they call desire the origin of delusion, and delusion the origin of desire.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain scripture says that desire and delusion keep feeding each other in a cycle.
Jainism sees the same self-feeding growth the Kural praises in watered love, but it judges it as a cycle to be broken.
Sikhism
“Make your mind the farmer, good deeds the farm, modesty the water, and your body the field.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 595, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak describes the spiritual life as a farm, with saram (modesty, or by another reading, effort) as the water that makes the crop of Naam grow.
In the Kural the girl's modest bowing of the head is water poured on the young plant of love; Guru Nanak too names modesty as the water that makes a crop grow.
Greek Mythology
“And ever she kept darting bright glances straight up at Aeson’s son, and within her breast her heart panted fast through anguish, all remembrance left her, and her soul melted with the sweet pain.”
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.286-289 (tr. R. C. Seaton, 1912) [10]
After Eros’ arrow strikes her, Medea keeps glancing at Jason as love takes root in her.
Her repeated glances show love taking hold and growing, as the Kural’s watered shoot does.
Philosophy
Stendhal compares the growth of love to a bare branch left in a Salzburg salt mine, which comes out covered in sparkling crystals; each small sign from the beloved adds new crystals to the lover’s idea of her.
— Stendhal, On Love (De l’amour, 1822), Book I, ch. 2 [11]
His theory of ‘crystallization’ explains how love grows from small signs and impressions.
The Kural’s image of a look as water on a young plant describes the same growth of love from a small sign.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria proposes to Prince Albert
15 October 1839 · Windsor Castle, England
Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha arrived at Windsor in October 1839, and five days later, on 15 October, Queen Victoria proposed to him. As the reigning monarch she could not traditionally be proposed to, so she had to take the first step. They married at the Chapel Royal, St James's Palace, on 10 February 1840.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Their marriage became one of the most famous royal love matches, and Victoria's journal records her joy at being loved.
The kural describes the moment the beloved shows her consent, which waters the growing plant of love; Victoria herself gave that sign, and the courtship at once became a marriage.
