Kural 613

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

தாளாண்மை என்னும் தகைமைக்கண் தங்கிற்றே வேளாண்மை என்னுஞ் செருக்கு.
Thaalaanmai ennum thagaimaikkan thangitre Velaanmai ennum serukku.

Meaning

Being proud that you can help others depends on one thing only: the good habit of working hard.

Elementary

You can only share cookies with your friends if somebody baked them first. People who work hard are the ones who end up with something to give, and giving feels great. So working is what makes kindness possible.

University

தாளாண்மை, literally ‘the rule of the foot’, is Valluvar’s image for vigorous exertion, and வேளாண்மை here means beneficence or generosity to others (Parimelazhagar: உபகாரம்), not ‘agriculture’, its commonest modern sense. செருக்கு, usually ‘arrogance’, is used positively for the lofty eminence or noble pride of a benefactor, and the emphatic particle ஏ in தங்கிற்றே (‘it abides only there’) makes effort the exclusive seat of that eminence. The logic, which Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both preserve, links this chapter to the Arathuppal chapters on ஒப்புரவறிதல் and ஈகை: generosity is a virtue, but it presupposes the means that only labour produces. Kalaignar reads the couplet as describing people of ‘unceasing effort’ in whom the pride of helping others takes root. Both editions consulted agree on the text, apart from sandhi (தகைமைக்கட் டங்கிற்றே).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Riches and other objects of enjoyment do not follow the stingy, nor the impotent, nor the idler.”
— Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Section VI (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
In Bhishma’s discourse on destiny and exertion, Brahma declares that prosperity eludes both the miser and the idle.
Linking the idler with the miser echoes the Kural’s claim that the power to give depends on the habit of effort.
Christianity
“Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth.”
— Ephesians 4:28 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells converts that honest labour has a purpose beyond self-support: it makes giving to the needy possible.
Labour ‘that he may have to give’ is exactly the Kural’s claim that beneficence rests on effort.
Islam
“O you who have believed, spend from the good things which you have earned”
— Qur’an 2:267 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an asks believers to give in charity from their own honest earnings and from the produce of the earth.
Charity is spoken of as flowing from what one has earned, as the Kural roots beneficence in labour.
Judaism
The ‘woman of valour’ of Proverbs 31 rises before dawn, works wool and flax with willing hands, buys a field and plants a vineyard from her earnings, and also opens her hand to the poor and needy.
— Proverbs 31:10-31 [6]
The acrostic poem portrays a household sustained by tireless industry that overflows into generosity.
Her open hand to the poor is made possible by her busy hands, as the Kural says generosity lives in effort.
Buddhism
“He who acquires his wealth in harmless ways like to a bee that honey gathers, riches mount up for him like ant hill’s rapid growth.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha tells the householder Sigala to gather wealth industriously and then divide it, keeping portions for use, business and need, and so win friends by liberality.
Industrious gathering followed by generous sharing mirrors the Kural’s chain from effort to beneficence.
Jainism
“doing service consists in giving one's assistance as well as one is able.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Vaiyāvṛttya, active service to teachers, the sick, the aged and the community, is one of the internal austerities in Jainism; benefiting others is defined as giving one's own exertion.
The Kural roots the ability to help others in hard work; Jain vaiyāvṛttya likewise defines service as giving active effort as far as one is able.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in Saarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak links giving to honest labour: the person who earns his bread by his own work and shares part of it is the one who has found the right way.
The Kural rests the power to help others on hard work alone; Guru Nanak likewise makes earned livelihood (kirat karni) the ground of sharing (vand chhakna).
Greek Mythology
Heracles’ labours, from killing the Nemean lion to clearing the Stymphalian birds, rid Greece of monsters and earned him cult as Alexikakos, ‘averter of evil’, a benefactor of humankind.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5 (the Twelve Labours) [10]
Greek tradition made Heracles’ glory inseparable from the gruelling toil through which he served others.
His honour as benefactor rests on his labours, as the Kural says the pride of helping others dwells in effort.
Philosophy
Aristotle says the liberal man will not neglect his own property, since he wishes by means of it to help others.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (1120b) [11]
In his account of liberality, Aristotle insists that true generosity requires caring for and maintaining one’s means.
Both thinkers see generosity as dependent on the diligent management that produces the means to give.

Real Life Example

Tan Kah Kee builds a business empire and founds Xiamen University
1900s-1937 · Singapore and Xiamen, Fujian, China
Tan Kah Kee, a Chinese-born businessman in Singapore with little formal education, built a large business on pineapple canning, rice milling and above all rubber, which by the 1920s employed thousands of people. He used his fortune to found or support many schools in Singapore and China, including Nanyang Chinese High School in 1918. In 1921 he founded Xiamen University, the first university in China established by an overseas Chinese, and he funded it until 1937, when the government took it over.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Tan Kah Kee's generosity on this scale was possible only because of the fortune his years of hard work had produced, and he spent much of it on education.
The kural says the proud distinction of helping others rests on hard work alone, and Tan Kah Kee's schools and university were built from the earnings of a lifetime of labour.
