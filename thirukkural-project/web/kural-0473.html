Kural 473

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

உடைத்தம் வலியறியார் ஊக்கத்தின் ஊக்கி இடைக்கண் முரிந்தார் பலர்.
Udaiththam valiyariyaar ookkaththin ookki Idaikkan murindhaar palar.

Meaning

Many people did not know how strong they really were. They rushed into a task, driven only by eagerness, and broke down halfway.

Elementary

Lots of people get very excited and jump into something big without checking whether they’re strong enough. Halfway through they run out of strength, and it all falls apart.

University

Udaiththam vali, ‘the strength they themselves possess’, is exactly what these people fail to know. Ookkam (zeal, drive) is praised elsewhere in the Kural, with a whole chapter on it (ch. 60, Ookkam Udaimai), so Valluvar is not condemning enthusiasm itself. He condemns enthusiasm cut loose from measurement. The verb murindhaar (‘they snapped’) suggests a branch or axle giving way under strain and looks ahead to the cart-axle image of 475. Palar (‘many’) gives the verse an empirical, historical tone: this is not a hypothetical but a pattern seen across many careers and kingdoms.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Gita calls an action tamasic (of the mode of ignorance) when it is undertaken out of delusion, without regard to consequences, loss, harm to others, or one’s own ability.
— Bhagavad Gita 18.25 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [4]
Among the marks of deluded action, Krishna names disregard of one’s own capacity (pauruṣam anavekṣya).
This is the Kural’s diagnosis in doctrinal form: action begun without knowing one’s strength belongs to delusion and ends badly.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it? Lest haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all that behold it begin to mock him, Saying, This man began to build, and was not able to finish.”
— Luke 14:28–30 (KJV) [5]
Jesus describes a builder who starts without reckoning his resources and is left with an unfinished tower and public ridicule.
‘Began to build, and was not able to finish’ closely matches the Kural’s idaikkan murindhaar, ‘broke down midway’.
Islam
The Prophet taught that religion is easy, and that whoever overburdens himself in it will be overpowered and unable to continue. He advised believers to avoid extremes, to aim at what is right and come close to it, and to take heart.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 39 (Book of Belief) [6]
The hadith warns that zeal which ignores one’s real capacity cannot be sustained, and the zealous person gives up.
It matches the Kural’s picture of zeal without self-knowledge collapsing before it reaches the end.
Judaism
“Better is it that thou shouldest not vow, than that thou shouldest vow and not pay.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:5 (KJV; 5:4 in Hebrew numbering) [5]
Ecclesiastes warns against eagerly undertaking obligations one cannot fulfil. It is wiser not to begin than to begin and fail.
Like the Kural, it condemns commitments made in enthusiasm without regard to capacity, which end unfinished.
Buddhism
Before his awakening, the Bodhisatta pushed ascetic practice to the extreme, holding his breath and reducing food until his body wasted and he was near death. He found that these austerities did not lead to awakening, recalled a calmer state of absorption from his childhood, and took up the middle way.
— Majjhima Nikāya 36, Mahāsaccaka Sutta [7]
The Buddha’s own story is one of overreaching zeal that nearly destroyed him partway along the path, until he adopted a practice suited to what a human body and mind can bear.
It shows the Kural’s warning that fervour beyond one’s real strength breaks down before reaching the goal.
Jainism
“A man believes himself a hero as long as he does not behold the foe, as did Śiśupāla (before he beheld) the valorously-fighting, great warrior.” … “So a novice, who as yet has not suffered pains and is not yet used to a mendicant's life, believes himself a hero till he practises austerities.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.3.1.1 and 1.3.1.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Sutrakritanga's lecture on hardships pictures eager novices who rush into the monastic life sure of their courage, then lose heart once cold, heat, hunger and insult test them.
This is the Kural's warning almost exactly: many who did not know their real strength set out in a burst of zeal and broke down halfway.
Sikhism
“My strength is exhausted, and I am in bondage; I cannot do anything at all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1429, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 53), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks Guru Tegh Bahadur gives voice to someone whose strength has run out and who is left helpless and bound.
The Kural speaks of those who misjudged their strength and broke down midway; Guru Tegh Bahadur describes that state of spent strength and helplessness.
Greek Mythology
Phaethon begs his father Helios to let him drive the chariot of the Sun. Helios warns him that not even Zeus could control those horses, but Phaethon insists. Partway across the sky he loses control, the horses bolt and scorch the earth, and Zeus strikes him down with a thunderbolt.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.750–2.328 [10]
Phaethon’s eagerness overrides his father’s warning about the limits of his strength, and he fails in mid-course.
This is a mythic version of the Kural: zeal without knowing one’s strength, then collapse midway.
Philosophy
“In every affair consider what precedes and follows, and then undertake it.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 29 (tr. T. W. Higginson) [11]
Epictetus continues that someone who does not do this will begin eagerly, without thinking of the consequences, and then give up in disgrace when difficulties appear. He adds: consider first what the matter is and what your own nature can bear.
It is almost a commentary on the Kural: eager starts without self-assessment end in shameful abandonment midway.

Real Life Example

The Burke and Wills expedition breaks down on the way back
1860-1861 · Victoria to the Gulf of Carpentaria, Australia
The Victorian Exploring Expedition under Robert O'Hara Burke set out to cross Australia from south to north. Burke split his party, pushed ahead from Cooper Creek with a small group and inadequate provisions, and came near the Gulf of Carpentaria in February 1861, but on the return journey the men weakened, missed the depot party at Cooper Creek by hours, and Burke and William Wills died at the end of June 1861. A royal commission later censured Burke for dividing his party and setting out with inadequate supplies.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The expedition reached its goal but collapsed on the way back, because its leader had not matched the pace of his plans to the strength of his men and supplies.
The kural says many who did not know the measure of their strength have broken down midway, and Burke's party gave out on the homeward stretch of its journey.
