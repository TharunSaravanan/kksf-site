Kural 891

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

ஆற்றுவார் ஆற்றல் இகழாமை போற்றுவார் போற்றலுள் எல்லாம் தலை.
Aatruvaar aatral ikazhaamai potruvaar potraluL ellaam thalai.

Meaning

Of all the ways a careful person can protect themselves, the first is never to look down on people who have the power to do whatever they set out to do.

Elementary

Imagine a small puppy barking and nipping at a big, calm elephant. The safest thing the puppy can do is simply not tease the elephant. Being respectful to people who are strong and capable is the best way to keep yourself out of trouble.

University

The opening couplet frames the whole adhikaram as a matter of self-protection: poatruvaar (போற்றுவார், those who guard themselves) have many kinds of poatral (போற்றல், guarding, watchfulness), but the thalai (தலை, head, foremost) of them all is ikazhaamai (இகழாமை, not despising) the aatral (ஆற்றல், power, capability) of aatruvaar (ஆற்றுவார், those able to carry out what they undertake). Pope’s explanation, following the traditional commentary associated with Parimelazhagar, reads the chapter in the setting of statecraft: having discussed internal enmity (Ch. 89), Valluvar now warns the king and his men against offending two classes of the great, the mighty in worldly power and the great in spiritual power (ascetics). Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both gloss aatruvaar as one able to complete a chosen task, while Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as those who lead and guide. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Udyoga Parva, when Krishna comes to the Kaurava court as an envoy of peace, Duryodhana and his allies scheme to seize him, underestimating him; Krishna reveals his overwhelming divine form and the plot collapses.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Bhagavat-yana sub-parva), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Duryodhana treated a being of immense power as an ordinary messenger who could be bound, and the episode foreshadows the ruin that followed his contempt.
The Kural’s first safeguard is exactly what Duryodhana neglected: never despise the power of those able to act.
Christianity
“And we beseech you, brethren, to know them which labour among you, and are over you in the Lord, and admonish you; And to esteem them very highly in love for their work’s sake.”
— 1 Thessalonians 5:12-13 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks the Thessalonian church to recognize and highly esteem those who labour and lead among them, precisely because of the work they do.
Like the Kural, it ties respect to the capacity and accomplishment of those who do the work, though without the Kural’s warning of danger.
Islam
“O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you.”
— Qur’an 4:59 (Saheeh International) [5]
The verse commands believers to give due obedience and regard to the Prophet and to those holding rightful authority.
The Kural similarly counsels not to slight those who hold real power, though it frames this as wise caution rather than obedience.
Judaism
“Let the honor of your student be as dear to you as your own, and the honor of your colleague as the reverence for your teacher, and the reverence for your teacher as the reverence of heaven.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 4:12 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ben Shammua sets up a ladder of honor in which reverence for one’s teacher approaches reverence for God.
The Kural’s chapter likewise places due regard for the great among the highest of duties.
Buddhism
“To one ever eager to revere and serve the elders, these four blessing accrue: long life and beauty, happiness and power.”
— Dhammapada 109 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that steady reverence for elders brings concrete benefits to the one who shows it.
Both texts present respect for the great as something that protects and benefits the respectful person.
Jainism
“He should never do anything disagreeable to the wise, neither in words nor deeds, neither openly nor secretly.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahāvīra's first lecture on discipline (vinaya) makes giving no offence to the wise, in word, deed, public or private, a basic rule for anyone who wishes to prosper.
The Kural makes not belittling the great the first of all self-protections, and the Uttarādhyayana likewise puts care never to offend the wise at the foundation of right conduct.
Sikhism
“Fareed, do not slander the dust”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Fareed (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses are in the Guru Granth Sahib, warns against looking down on the dust underfoot, since in the end it covers everyone; traditional commentary reads the dust as the humble saint.
The Kural's first safeguard is never to belittle those who have real strength, and Farid likewise warns that what seems low and easy to despise is greater than it looks.
Greek Mythology
Arachne boasts that her weaving surpasses that of Athena, refuses the goddess’s warning, and after the contest is transformed into a spider.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.1-145 (Arachne) [10]
A mortal who scorned the skill and power of a goddess lost her own human form as a result.
Arachne did exactly what the Kural warns against: she made light of the power of one far more capable.
Philosophy
“Now slighting is the actively entertained opinion of something as obviously of no importance. … clearly you do not think that he can do you harm, for then you would be afraid of him instead of slighting him …”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric, II.2, 1378b (tr. W. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle defines anger as a painful impulse to revenge for an unjustified slight, and observes that we slight only those we judge unable to harm us; if we thought them capable, we would fear them instead.
To belittle someone is to misjudge his power, and the Kural's first safeguard is never to make that error about people who are able to act.

Real Life Example

Japan destroys the Russian fleet at Tsushima
27-28 May 1905 · Tsushima Strait, between Korea and Japan
During the Russo-Japanese War, Russia sent its Baltic Fleet on a voyage of nearly 20,000 nautical miles to the Far East. In the Tsushima Strait the Japanese fleet under Admiral Togo Heihachiro crossed the Russian line of advance and destroyed or captured almost the entire Russian force. Britannica describes it as the first occasion in the modern era on which an Asian power defeated a European nation, and Russia soon accepted peace in the Treaty of Portsmouth.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A great European empire that had treated Japan as a minor power was decisively defeated by it, changing how the world saw Japan.
The kural says the first safeguard is never to belittle the strength of those able to carry out what they undertake, a lesson Russia learned at Tsushima.
