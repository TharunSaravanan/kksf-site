Kural 62

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

எழுபிறப்பும் தீயவை தீண்டா பழிபிறங்காப் பண்புடை மக்கட் பெறின்.
Ezhupirappum Theeyavai Theendaa Pazhipirangaap, Panbudai Makkat Perin.

Meaning

If you have children of good character whom no one can blame, no harm will come near you in all seven births (all your lives to come).

Elementary

Think of a family like a long relay race, with each generation passing the baton on. This kural says that if you raise children who do good and never give anyone a reason to blame them, the whole race stays clean — trouble never catches up with your family, not for a very, very long time.

University

The phrase எழுபிறப்பு (ezhu-piṟappu, "seven births") invokes the classical Indian reckoning of the sevenfold cycle of embodied existence, and Parimēlaḻakar's gloss here is explicitly transmigrational: the parent's accumulated evil karma is worn away by the meritorious acts a virtuous child performs on their behalf. The key qualifier is பழிபிறங்காப் பண்புடை — character (paṇpu) so ordered that பழி (paḻi, public blame or reproach) never rises around it. Valluvar thus binds an other-worldly promise to an entirely this-worldly test: the proof of the child's virtue is that no one can speak ill of them. Whether "seven births" is read literally as rebirths (the Jain and Hindu reading) or idiomatically as "seven generations" — which is how the modern commentator M. Karunanidhi renders it — the structural claim is the same: a child's good conduct is retroactively protective of those who produced them. That inversion of the usual direction of moral influence, from child upward to parent, is what makes the couplet distinctive.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Because a son delivers (trāyate) his father from the hell called Put, he was therefore called put-tra (a deliverer from Put) by the Self-existent (Svayambhū) himself.”
— Manusmṛti 9.138 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East, 1886) [5]
Manu offers a folk etymology in which the very Sanskrit word for son, putra, encodes the son's function: to pull his father out of a particular hell after death.
This is the same claim as the Kural — that a child's existence and virtue shield the parent from evil across the cycle of births.
Christianity
“The just man walketh in his integrity: his children are blessed after him.”
— Proverbs 20:7 (King James Version) [6]
The proverb promises that a righteous person's integrity spills forward onto the generation that follows them.
It shares the Kural's intuition that moral standing flows along a family line, but it runs in the opposite direction — parent blessing child, rather than child protecting parent.
Islam
“When a man dies, his acts come to an end, but three, recurring charity, or knowledge (by which people) benefit, or a pious child, who prays for him (for the deceased).”
— Sahih Muslim 1631, Book of Wills (narrated by Abū Hurayrah) [7]
The hadith names three things that keep generating reward for a person after death, and one of them is a righteous child whose prayers continue to reach the parent.
Exactly the Kural's mechanism: the parent's post-mortem condition is improved by the child's goodness.
Judaism
The Talmud states the principle that a son can confer merit on his father, but a father cannot confer merit on his son — illustrated by the wicked king Amon, who is held to have retained a share in the World to Come on account of his righteous son Josiah.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 104a [8]
Merit in this rabbinic discussion flows upward only: a child's righteousness can rescue a parent's standing, but not the reverse.
This is the Kural's exact claim — that a blameless child's character reaches backward to shield the one who bore them.
Buddhism
“He will maintain his heritage, / And practice according to wealth, / Also he will make offerings / To relatives who pass away.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.39, Puttasutta, v. 507 (trans. Ānandajoti Bhikkhu, Buddhist Wisdom Verses) [9]
Among the reasons the Buddha says wise householders want children is that a child will make merit-offerings on behalf of dead relatives — a transfer of benefit to those who can no longer act for themselves.
Like the Kural, it makes the child an active agent of relief for the parent beyond this life.
Jainism
“The study of the Vêdas will not save you; the feeding of Brâhmanas will lead you from darkness to darkness, and the birth of sons will not save you. Who will assent to what you said?”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.8 and 14.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
When their Brahmin father argues that there is no better world for men without sons, the two sons of Bhrigu reply that sons cannot save anyone; only one's own conduct under the Law frees the soul.
Jainism agrees with Valluvar that the seven births matter, but it rejects the idea that a child's goodness can shield a parent's soul: in Jain doctrine each soul bears its own karma, which is the opposite of what this kural claims.
Sikhism
“Those, within whose minds the Lord abides, are robed with honor in the Court of the Lord. They are carried across, along with all their family, and the whole world is saved along with them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 310, Raag Gauri, Gauri ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 4, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
Guru Ram Das teaches that one person in whom God dwells brings blessing beyond themselves, carrying their whole family across the world-ocean with them.
Like Valluvar's virtuous child whose good character keeps evil away from the family through seven births, the God-filled person here becomes the means of the family's release.
Greek Mythology
When the oxen were not ready, the brothers Cleobis and Biton yoked themselves to the cart and drew their mother, priestess of Hera, all the way to the temple; the watching Argives praised her for such sons, and she prayed the goddess grant them the best thing a human can receive — whereupon they fell asleep in the temple and never woke.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.31 [12]
Herodotus tells the story as Solon's example of true human happiness: sons whose conduct honours their mother so visibly that the community credits her for them, and the goddess seals it.
As in the Kural, blameless children are what keeps evil from reaching the parent — here the mother's happiness is made secure precisely by her sons' virtue.
Philosophy
Plato argues in the Laws that the human race is by its nature a partner of all time, and that a person attains to immortality not by outliving death but by leaving children, and children's children, so that the line never breaks.
— Plato, Laws IV.721b–c [13]
Marriage law in Plato's ideal city is grounded on this: procreation is how a mortal being participates in something that does not end.
Both texts make offspring the mechanism by which a person's fate extends safely across spans of time they will not personally live through.

Real Life Example

Suor Maria Celeste takes on her father Galileo's penance
1633 · Arcetri, near Florence, Italy
In June 1633 the Roman Inquisition condemned Galileo Galilei over his Dialogue on the two chief world systems and imposed a penance that included the repeated recitation of the penitential psalms. His eldest daughter, Suor Maria Celeste, a Poor Clare nun at the convent of San Matteo in Arcetri, took on that part of his penance herself. Throughout his trial she wrote to him constantly and looked after his household affairs while he was away in Rome.
— Sources: [14] [15]
A daughter of good character stood in for her father and shouldered the burden laid on him, one of the best-documented acts of filial devotion in the history of science.
Parimelalagar reads this kural as saying that a virtuous child's good acts lift the burden from the parent, and Maria Celeste literally performed her father's penance in his place.
