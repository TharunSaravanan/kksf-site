Kural 910

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

எண்சேர்ந்த நெஞ்சத் திடனுடையார்க்கு எஞ்ஞான்றும் பெண்சேர்ந்தாம் பேதைமை இல்.
Enserndha nenjath thitanutaiyaarkku egngnaandrum penserndhaam pedhaimai il.

Meaning

People whose minds are full of careful thought and who are strong inside will never fall into the foolishness of being ruled by a woman.

Elementary

When you think things through and stay steady inside, you do not get swept away by any one wish or person. A calm, thoughtful mind keeps you in charge of your own choices.

University

En-serndha nenjam (எண்சேர்ந்த நெஞ்சம்) is 'a mind joined to deliberation'; Parimelazhagar takes it as a mind engaged in planning affairs of state, and reads idan (இடன்) not as 'firmness' (as some modern glosses do) but as 'wealth/means', citing Kural 218 ('இடனில் பருவத்தும்') and Kalittokai to establish that sense. Ytamizh's 'thitan' transliteration reflects the sandhi 'நெஞ்சத் திடன்', which modern commentators such as Kalaignar and Mu. Varadarasanar also read as thidan (திடன், firmness of mind), so both readings circulate. Pen-serndhaam pedhaimai (பெண்சேர்ந்தாம் பேதைமை) is the folly arising from attachment to the wife; Parimelazhagar says this folly is the root of all three faults treated earlier, namely desiring, fearing and obeying, so the closing verse sums up the chapter negatively. Manakkudavar explains en-serdhal as discerning the faults that arise from such attachment. Read today, the chapter's enduring core is this closing ideal of deliberative self-command, while its gendered framing belongs to its historical setting.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Katha Upanishad compares the self to the rider of a chariot, the body to the chariot, the intellect to the charioteer, the mind to the reins and the senses to the horses.
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.3-9 [5]
One whose intellect holds the reins firmly reaches the goal, while one with unruly senses does not.
The Kural's thoughtful, steady-minded person is the well-reined charioteer who is not dragged off course.
Christianity
Joseph, in Egypt, refuses the repeated advances of his master Potiphar's wife and flees, leaving his garment behind.
— Genesis 39 [6]
Joseph's steadfast integrity keeps him from yielding, at great personal cost.
He embodies the Kural's firm-minded person who does not fall into folly.
Islam
Yusuf resists the governor's wife, seeking refuge in God, and later says prison is dearer to him than what he is invited to.
— Qur'an 12:23-33 [7]
The Qur'anic Joseph shows steadfast resolve rooted in faith.
His firmness mirrors the Kural's steady-minded person free of folly.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city.’”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [8]
Ben Zoma defines true strength as mastery over one's own impulses.
The Kural likewise places real strength in a mind governed by deliberation.
Buddhism
“He who delights in subduing evil thoughts, who meditates on the impurities and is ever mindful — it is he who will make an end of craving and rend asunder Mara's fetter.”
— Dhammapada 350 (Buddharakkhita) [9]
Mindful, disciplined thought breaks the bonds of craving.
The Kural's deliberative mind similarly stays free of attachment's folly.
Jainism
“Restraining himself by the thought that these dangerous (pleasures) will not be to his benefit, a monk should abstain from women.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.2.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [10]
The Sutrakritanga's remedy is reflection: a man who thinks clearly about what harms him keeps himself from falling under a woman's sway.
Kural 910 likewise says that people whose minds are joined to deliberation (en-serndha nenjam) never fall into the folly of being ruled by a woman.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib (Pauri 28), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak teaches that mastering one's own mind is the real victory.
The Kural says a mind given to careful thought will never be wholly ruled by attachment, and Japji likewise makes self-mastery the foundation of every other success.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus has himself bound to the mast so he can hear the Sirens' song without steering his ship to ruin.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 12 [12]
Foresight lets the hero experience temptation without being ruled by it.
This is the Kural's ideal: forethought that keeps one from folly.
Philosophy
Plato's Socrates likens the soul to a charioteer driving two horses, one noble and one unruly, and says the well-ordered soul is one in which reason guides the team.
— Plato, Phaedrus 246a-254e [13]
Reason must govern appetite for a soul to rise to the good.
The Kural's 'mind joined to deliberation' is the charioteer in command.

Real Life Example

Titus sends Queen Berenice away from Rome
79 CE · Rome, Italy
Titus, son of the emperor Vespasian, lived openly with Berenice, sister of the Judean king Agrippa II, who hoped to become his wife, but the relationship was unpopular in Rome. Not daring to marry her, he sent her away, probably on his accession as emperor in 79 CE. The Roman biographer Suetonius records that he sent her from Rome at once, against her will and his own.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
On taking power, Titus placed the duties of his office and public confidence above his personal attachment.
The kural says those whose minds are joined to careful thought will not fall into the folly of being ruled by attachment to a woman, and Titus made exactly that choice when he became emperor.
