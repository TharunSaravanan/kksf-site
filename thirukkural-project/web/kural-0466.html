Kural 466

Acting after Due Consideration · Therindhu Seyalvakai · தெரிந்து செயல்வகை

செய்தக்க அல்ல செயக்கெடும் செய்தக்க செய்யாமை யானுங் கெடும்.
Seydhakka alla seyak ketum seydhakka, Seyyaamai yaanung ketum.

Meaning

Doing what you should not do ruins you. Not doing what you should do ruins you just as much.

Elementary

You can get into trouble in two ways: by doing something you shouldn’t, and by not doing something you should. Both can hurt you just as much.

University

The couplet is built on a chiastic repetition of செய்தக்க (‘what is fit to be done’). It balances sins of commission against sins of omission and treats both as equally ruinous (கெடும் … கெடும்). This keeps the chapter from turning into a counsel of timidity: deliberation is meant to discern the right action, and paralysis is as fatal as rashness. In a political reading, a ruler who neglects necessary measures (defence, justice, relief) falls as surely as one who pursues reckless ones. The same double structure is familiar from moral and legal traditions that separate positive duties from prohibitions.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Laws of Manu state that a man who omits a prescribed act, or performs a blameworthy act, or clings to sensual objects, must perform a penance.
— Manusmriti 11.44 (tr. G. Bühler) [4]
In the Hindu law books you can go wrong both by skipping your duties and by doing forbidden things, and both need to be put right.
Same two-sided account of wrongdoing as the Kural.
Christianity
The General Confession: “We have left undone those things which we ought to have done; And we have done those things which we ought not to have done.
— Book of Common Prayer (1662), Morning Prayer, General Confession [5]
Anglican worshippers confess both kinds of failure: what they neglected and what they wrongly did.
An almost word-for-word counterpart to the Kural’s two clauses.
Islam
The Qur’an calls for a community “inviting to [all that is] good, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong,” and says those are the successful.
— Qur’an 3:104 (Sahih International) [6]
Muslims are asked both to promote what is good and to stop what is wrong. Doing only one isn’t enough.
The paired duties of doing right and avoiding wrong echo the Kural’s two-sided rule.
Judaism
Rabbi Simlai taught that 613 commandments were given to Moses: 365 prohibitions, matching the days of the year, and 248 positive commands, matching the limbs of the body.
— Babylonian Talmud, Makkot 23b [7]
Jewish law is built from both ‘do this’ and ‘don’t do that’ commandments, and a person can fail by breaking either kind.
The positive/negative split mirrors the Kural’s ‘what should be done’ and ‘what should not be done’.
Buddhism
To avoid all evil, to cultivate good, and to cleanse one’s mind — this is the teaching of the Buddhas.
— Dhammapada 183 [8]
The Buddha’s teaching in one line: stop doing bad things and start doing good things, and keep your mind pure.
Avoiding the wrong and doing the right, the Kural’s two clauses in the same order.
Jainism
“One should desist from one thing, and practise another: desist from neglect of self-control, and practise self-control.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 31.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The lecture on the monk's mode of life opens with a double rule: some things must be given up and others actively practised.
The Kural says ruin comes both from doing what should not be done and from failing to do what should; the Jain verse likewise demands both abstaining and acting.
Sikhism
“You have not done what you should have done; you are entangled in the web of greed. Nanak, your time is past and gone; why are you crying now, you blind fool?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1428, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 36), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Tegh Bahadur rebukes the person who, caught up in greed, leaves undone what ought to have been done, until the time for it has gone.
The Kural says one is ruined as much by failing to do what ought to be done as by doing what ought not; Guru Tegh Bahadur lays out the ruin of omission.
Greek Mythology
Achilles withdraws from the fighting after his quarrel with Agamemnon and refuses to return even when the Greeks are being routed. His inaction leads to the death of Patroclus.
— Homer, Iliad Books 1, 9 and 16 [11]
Achilles’ refusal to act when he was needed brought disaster on his own side and on his best friend.
Shows the second half of the Kural: ruin from failing to do what ought to be done.
Philosophy
Mill writes that a person may cause evil to others not only by his actions but by his inaction, and in either case is justly accountable to them for the injury.
— John Stuart Mill, On Liberty (1859), ch. 1 [12]
Mill says you can harm people by what you fail to do as well as by what you do, and you are responsible either way.
A modern philosophical statement of the Kural’s parity between commission and omission.

Real Life Example

The Quebec Bridge collapses during construction
29 August 1907 · Near Quebec City, Quebec, Canada
The design of the Quebec Bridge's long cantilever span had been enlarged, but its dead load had been calculated using weights for the original, shorter span. In August 1907 the inspector Norman McLure reported that key compression members were bending and the bend was growing, yet the chief engineer insisted that work continue; McLure's urgent advice that no more load be placed on the bridge was relayed only on 27 August, and the south arm collapsed two days later, killing 75 workers. A royal commission found that faulty design and inadequate engineering supervision had caused the disaster.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The failure came both from what was done wrongly, a miscalculated design, and from what was left undone, halting the work when the warnings appeared.
The kural says ruin comes both from doing what should not be done and from failing to do what should be done, and the Quebec Bridge failed through both kinds of error at once.
