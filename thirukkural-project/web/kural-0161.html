Kural 161

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

ஒழுக்காறாக் கொள்க ஒருவன்தன் நெஞ்சத்து அழுக்காறு இலாத இயல்பு.
Ozhukkaaraak kolga oruvanthan nenjathu Azhukkaaru ilaadha iyalbu.

Meaning

Make it a rule of your life to keep your heart free of jealousy.

Elementary

Some families have house rules, like “always say please.” This kural says: make “never be jealous of anyone” one of your own rules, as important as any other rule for being good.

University

Valluvar builds the chapter on a pun: ஒழுக்காறு (ozhukkaaru, “the path of right conduct”) and அழுக்காறு (azhukkaaru, “the path of impurity,” i.e., envy) differ by a single sound, so the couplet sets the right road against the soiled road. He places the absence of envy inside the heart (நெஞ்சத்து), not just in outward behavior, which treats envy as a flaw of character rather than a wrong act. Commentators from Parimelazhagar to Mu. Varadarasanar read the couplet as saying freedom from envy should be held as seriously as the formal rules of ozhukkam (Chapter 14). The view is close to Aristotle's, who counted envy among the passions whose very names imply badness, and to the Buddhist idea that a disposition must be trained, not merely a deed avoided.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not envious but who is a kind friend to all living entities, who does not think himself a proprietor, who is free from false ego and equal both in happiness and distress, who is always satisfied and engaged in devotional service with determination and whose mind and intelligence are in agreement with Me-he is very dear to Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.13-14 [3]
Krishna lists the character traits of the ideal devotee, and “not envious” (adveṣṭā) comes first.
Both texts make freedom from envy a settled trait of character, the foundation of a good life rather than one rule among many.
Christianity
“Let us not be desirous of vain glory, provoking one another, envying one another.”
— Galatians 5:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul closes his list of the “fruit of the Spirit” by telling the community that people who live by the Spirit should not envy each other.
Like the Kural, Paul makes not envying a standing rule of conduct for anyone who wants to live rightly.
Islam
“Do not hate one another, and do not be jealous of one another, and do not desert each other, and O, Allah's worshipers! Be brothers.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6065 [5]
The Prophet lists envy (tahāsud) among the things believers must never do to each other if they are to live as brothers.
Both texts turn “do not envy” into a basic rule of how a good person relates to others.
Judaism
“A good eye, a humble spirit and a moderate appetite he is of the disciples of Abraham, our father. An evil eye, a haughty spirit and a limitless appetite he is of the disciples of Balaam, the wicked.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:19 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah marks the followers of Abraham by a “good eye” (ayin tovah), a generous way of seeing others' good, and the followers of Balaam by an “evil eye,” a grudging one.
Like the Kural, Avot treats a heart without envy as the sign of a whole way of life.
Buddhism
Asked why beings who wish to live without hostility still live in hostility, the Buddha answers that devas, humans, and all other beings “are fettered with envy & stinginess.
— Digha Nikaya 21 (Sakka-pañha Sutta), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
In the Sakka-pañha Sutta, envy (issā) and stinginess (macchariya) are named as the two fetters behind all conflict between beings.
Both texts see envy as a basic flaw of the heart that must be removed for a good life, not a minor fault.
Jainism
“Benevolence (maitrī) towards all living-beings (sattva), joy (pramoda) at the sight of the virtuous (guṇādhika), compassion and sympathy (kāruṇya) for the afflicted (kliśyamāna), and tolerance (mādhyasthya) towards the insolent and ill-behaved (avineya) are the other observances.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.11, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [8]
Umāsvāti names four standing attitudes a vow-holder must cultivate, and the second, pramoda, is gladness at another's excellence, which is the exact inner opposite of envy; Pūjyapāda's commentary glosses it as fervent affection and veneration in the presence of the virtuous.
Valluvar asks that an envy-free heart be adopted as the very rule of conduct, and the Tattvārtha makes joy at others' virtue a fixed observance (bhāvanā) that supports every vow.
Sikhism
“The Gurmukh eliminates hate and envy.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 942, Raag Raamkalee, Sidh Gosht, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sidh Gosht, his dialogue with the yogis, Guru Nanak describes the Gurmukh, the person oriented to the Guru, as one whose way of life clears hatred and envy out of the heart.
The Kural asks us to make a heart free of envy the very rule of conduct; Guru Nanak makes freedom from envy a defining mark of the Gurmukh's way of living.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says there are two Strifes on earth: one “a man would praise,” which spurs neighbour to compete with neighbour in honest work, and one that is “blameworthy,” fostering war and cruelty.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 11-24 (trans. Evelyn-White) [10]
The poet separates healthy rivalry, which pushes a person to work harder, from the destructive strife that wants only to harm.
Valluvar's choice between the “right path” and the “impure path” matches Hesiod's choice between the good Strife and the bad one.
Philosophy
“It is therefore a good feeling felt by good persons, whereas envy is a bad feeling felt by bad persons.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.11 (trans. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle compares emulation (wanting to earn what another has) with envy (wanting the other not to have it), and calls envy a mark of a bad character.
Like Valluvar, Aristotle treats the presence or absence of envy as a test of the kind of person one is.

Real Life Example

Robert Schumann hails the young Chopin and the young Brahms
1831 and 1853 · Leipzig and Dusseldorf, Germany
In 1831 the young Robert Schumann, himself an aspiring composer, published his first piece of music criticism in the Leipzig Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung, a review that greeted the unknown Frederic Chopin as a genius. In 1853, after the twenty-year-old Johannes Brahms visited the Schumanns in Dusseldorf and played them his unpublished works, Schumann wrote the article New Paths in the Neue Zeitschrift fur Musik, presenting Brahms to the musical world. Yale University Library notes that his two best-remembered reviews, the first and last of his critical career, each hailed the arrival of a young genius.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Schumann, a leading composer and the most influential music critic of his day, repeatedly used his position to lift up younger rivals rather than guard his own standing.
The kural asks that a heart free of envy be made the settled rule of one's conduct, and Schumann's whole critical career, from first article to last, was built on celebrating others' gifts.
