Kural 915

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

பொதுநலத்தார் புன்னலம் தோயார் மதிநலத்தின் மாண்ட அறிவி னவர்.
Podhunalaththaar punnalam thoyaar madhinalaththin maanda arivi navar.

Meaning

People whose wisdom has been made strong by good sense will not go after the cheap pleasures of women who give themselves to anyone who pays.

Elementary

A smart kid who knows what real friendship is will not chase someone who is ‘best friends’ with whoever gives the most presents that day. Good sense helps you see that kind of friendship is not really for you at all.

University

Podhu-nalaththaar (பொதுநலத்தார்) means ‘those whose charms (nalam) are common (podhu)’, available to all who pay. Valluvar sets this against mathi-nalam (மதிநலம்), ‘the excellence of natural intelligence’, which makes knowledge maandu (மாண்ட, perfected, excellent). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesigan distinguish iyarkai arivu (inborn sense) from acquired learning, and Kalaignar explicitly names both ‘natural intelligence and learned knowledge’, following Parimelazhagar’s distinction between mathi (native wit) and arivu (learning). The couplet is built parallel to 914 (punnalam thoyaar repeats), so the pair should be read together: 914 names the motive of the wise (arul), 915 the faculty (mathi). Both base sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is able to withdraw the senses from their objects, just as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, is established in divine wisdom.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.58 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna describes the steady-minded person who can pull the senses back from their objects.
Valluvar’s person of perfected understanding likewise draws back from common, purchased pleasure.
Christianity
“Flee also youthful lusts: but follow righteousness, faith, charity, peace, with them that call on the Lord out of a pure heart.”
— 2 Timothy 2:22 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Timothy to turn away from youthful passions and pursue virtue.
The wise of Kural 915 likewise turn from low pleasure toward what is excellent.
Islam
“Beautified for people is the love of that which they desire - of women and sons, heaped-up sums of gold and silver... That is the enjoyment of worldly life, but Allah has with Him the best return.”
— Qur’an 3:14 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an notes that worldly desires are made attractive, but that a better return lies with God.
Kural 915’s wise man sees past what is made attractive, toward what is truly better.
Judaism
“Lust not after her beauty in thy heart; Neither let her captivate thee with her eyelids.”
— Proverbs 6:25-26 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs warns the young man not to let outward beauty capture him, and adds that such a woman reduces a man to a loaf of bread.
Valluvar likewise tells the wise not to sink into podhu-nalam, beauty offered to all.
Buddhism
“Weeds are the bane of fields, lust is the bane of mankind. Therefore, what is offered to those free of lust yields abundant fruit.”
— Dhammapada 356 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada compares lust to weeds choking a field.
Valluvar’s wise keep their field clear by refusing common, purchased pleasure.
Jainism
“...cohabitation with a harlot–itvarikā aparigṛhītāgamana... are the five transgressions of the fourth minor vow of contentment with one’s wife (svadārasantoṣa aṇuvrata).”
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.28 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018, wisdomlib.org) [8]
Umasvati's Tattvartha Sutra makes going to a prostitute a formal breach of the Jain householder's vow of sexual restraint.
Kural 915 says those of mature understanding will not seek the 'common' charms (podhu-nalam) of paid women, and Jain lay ethics makes this a vow.
Sikhism
“You gaze upon the wives of others, O my soul; you steal and commit evil deeds.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 155, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak speaks to his own soul and lists its faults: a wandering eye for other men’s wives, theft and wrongdoing. Self-examination of this kind, done with the Guru’s understanding, is how Sikhism expects a person to check desire before it acts.
The Kural says a mind perfected by good sense will not seek low pleasures, and Guru Nanak shows that understanding at work by naming the craving and rebuking it.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus, held for years by the nymph Calypso, refuses her offer to make him immortal and ageless, choosing instead to return home to Ithaca and Penelope.
— Homer, Odyssey 5 [10]
Homer shows his hero choosing what is truly his own over an easy, enchanting comfort.
Kural 915’s discerning man likewise refuses tempting pleasure in favor of what his good sense values.
Philosophy
“The Love who is the offspring of the common Aphrodite is essentially common, and has no discrimination, being such as the meaner sort of men feel...”
— Plato, Symposium 181b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Pausanias separates common (Pandemos) love, which is indiscriminate and bodily, from heavenly love, which looks to the soul.
Plato’s ‘common’ love echoes Valluvar’s podhu-nalam, and both say the wise prefer the higher kind.

Real Life Example

Scipio returns a captive young woman to her father at New Carthage
209 BCE · New Carthage (Cartagena, Spain)
After the Roman general Publius Cornelius Scipio captured New Carthage during the Second Punic War, some young soldiers brought him a captive young woman of great beauty. The Greek historian Polybius records that Scipio thanked them, summoned the young woman's father and handed her over to him to be married to whomever he chose. Polybius adds that this display of self-restraint won Scipio the warm respect of his men, and the episode later became the famous artistic subject known as the Continence of Scipio.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A young commander at the height of victory chose restraint, and was admired for it by his own soldiers and by later generations.
The kural says those whose understanding has been perfected by good sense will not seek pleasures that are merely offered to them, and Scipio's restraint at New Carthage became a lasting model of that judgment.
