Kural 621

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

இடுக்கண் வருங்கால் நகுக அதனை அடுத்தூர்வது அஃதொப்ப தில்.
Itukkan varungaal nakuka adhanai Atuththoorvadhu aqdhoppa thil.

Meaning

When trouble comes, smile at it. Nothing beats trouble as well as staying cheerful and calm.

Elementary

Imagine your sandcastle gets knocked over by a wave. You can cry, or you can laugh and start building again. The Kural says the laugh is the strongest tool you have: a cheerful heart beats trouble better than anything else.

University

The chapter opens with its most famous imperative, nakuka (laugh, smile), which the commentators do not read as mockery but as the outward sign of a mind that refuses to be shaken; Solomon Pappaiah paraphrases it as rejoicing inwardly, Mu. Varadarasanar as not being agitated. The second line turns on aṭuttūrvatu, literally ‘that which presses close and rides over’, a verbal noun describing what can close in on sorrow and subdue it, and aḥtoppatu il says nothing equals ‘that’ (the laughter or the unperturbed state it expresses). Parimelazhagar, whose commentary frames Idukkan Azhiyaamai as the natural sequel to the preceding chapter on sustained effort (Aalvinaiyudaimai), understands the troubles here as those that arise in the course of undertaking great works, so the king or doer must not lose heart midway. The couplet text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; ytamizh writes the chapter title as one word, இடுக்கணழியாமை, and thirukkural.net renders it ‘Not despairing in afflictive times’, while Pope’s title is ‘Hopefulness in Trouble’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Dhritarashtra, thereafter, in the midst of both the armies, Shree Krishna smilingly spoke the following words to the grief-stricken Arjun.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.10 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
At the darkest moment of the Mahabharata, with Arjuna collapsed in despair between two armies, Krishna answers him first with a smile (prahasann iva) before teaching him anything.
The Kural’s first move against trouble is exactly Krishna’s: meet the crisis with a smile, and the smile itself begins the victory.
Christianity
“My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience.”
— James 1:2-3 (KJV) [4]
James tells believers to greet their trials with joy, because hardship builds steadfast endurance in them.
Like the Kural, James makes joy the proper response to trouble and treats that joy as the force that wins through it.
Islam
“For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease. Indeed, with hardship [will be] ease.”
— Qur’an 94:5-6 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an twice assures the Prophet, in a time of pressure, that relief comes together with every difficulty.
The Kural’s cheerful face toward trouble rests on the same conviction the sura repeats: hardship never comes alone and never has the last word.
Judaism
The Talmud tells of the sage Nachum Ish Gamzu, who met every misfortune that befell him with the words gam zu le-tovah, ‘this too is for the good’; when a gift he carried to the emperor was stolen and replaced with dirt, he still said it, and the dirt turned out to be miraculous.
— Babylonian Talmud, Taanit 21a [6]
Nachum became a byword for greeting each blow of fortune with serene confidence instead of lament, and his calm is shown to be vindicated.
His habitual ‘this too is for the good’ is a Jewish form of the Kural’s smile at trouble, an inner stance that disarms misfortune.
Buddhism
“A mind unruffled by the vagaries of fortune, from sorrow freed, from defilements cleansed, from fear liberated — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Maha-mangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.4 (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
In the Mangala Sutta the Buddha names a mind that stays untroubled through life’s ups and downs as one of the highest blessings a person can have.
The Kural says an unshaken, cheerful mind is the strongest weapon against trouble; the Buddha calls that same mind the greatest blessing.
Jainism
“If any misfortune happens and he suffers pain, he should cheerfully steady his mind, and bear the ills that attack him.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In Mahavira's teaching on the twenty-two troubles (parishaha) a monk is to meet illness and misfortune with a cheerful, steady mind instead of distress.
The Kural's call to smile when trouble comes is matched by the Jain instruction to meet misfortune cheerfully and with a steadied mind.
Sikhism
“Your actions seem so sweet to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 394, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan says that whatever God does tastes sweet to him. Sikh tradition links this acceptance of God's will (bhana manna) with his calm in the face of torture and martyrdom at Lahore in 1606.
The Kural tells us to meet trouble with a smile; Guru Arjan meets whatever comes as something sweet, which is the same calm, cheerful acceptance.
Greek Mythology
“Heart, be still, you had worse than this to bear on the day when the terrible Cyclops ate your brave companions; yet you bore it in silence till your cunning got you safe out of the cave, though you made sure of being killed.”
— Homer, Odyssey 20 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Back in his own house disguised as a beggar and surrounded by insults, Odysseus steadies himself by reminding his heart of worse troubles it has already survived.
Odysseus shows the Kural’s point that the mind’s own steadiness, not outward force, is what finally masters trouble.
Philosophy
“for the mind converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid; and so that which is a hindrance is made a furtherance to an act; and that which is an obstacle on the road helps us on this road.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.20 (tr. George Long) [11]
Marcus Aurelius argues that a well-ordered mind can turn every obstacle into material for its own progress.
Both texts hold that the right inner response does not just endure trouble but overpowers it and turns it to use.

Real Life Example

Ronald Reagan jokes with his surgeons after being shot
30 March 1981 · Washington, D.C., United States
On 30 March 1981 President Ronald Reagan was shot outside a Washington hotel; a bullet struck his left lung near his heart and he was rushed to George Washington University Hospital. According to accounts from people who were there, he lifted his oxygen mask moments before surgery and joked to the doctors that he hoped they were all Republicans, and one surgeon replied that today they all were. Reagan recovered and returned to the White House less than four weeks later.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A light-hearted remark at a moment of real danger steadied the people around him, and the Reagan Foundation has since presented it as a defining example of his calm and optimism.
The kural advises meeting trouble with a smile because nothing overpowers sorrow so well, and Reagan's joke on the way into surgery is a vivid example of cheerful calm pressing back against a grave crisis.
