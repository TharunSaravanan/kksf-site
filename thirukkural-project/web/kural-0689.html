Kural 689

The Envoy · Thoodhu · தூது

விடுமாற்றம் வேந்தர்க்கு உரைப்பான் வடுமாற்றம் வாய்சேரா வன்க ணவன்.
Vidumaatram vendharkku uraippaan vadumaatram Vaaysera vankanavan.

Meaning

Only a person strong enough never to let a shameful word slip out should carry their king's message to other kings.

Elementary

Suppose you are sent to tell a bigger kid that your friend wants his bike back. If the big kid scares you, it’s tempting to say ‘my friend is silly, never mind’. A good messenger stays strong and never says something that makes the person who sent him look bad.

University

Viṭu māṟṟam is ‘the word sent’, the king’s message. Vaṭu māṟṟam is ‘a word that scars’. Parimelazhagar explains this as words that lower one’s own king, which the envoy might say out of fear of harm to himself. Vaṉkaṇ is the firmness never to let such words out, even by a slip of the tongue (vāy cōrntum). He says ‘vaṭu’ (a scar) was chosen because such lowering goes against the duty of one’s station (cāti tarumam). He also notes that ‘vāy cērā’ expresses the result (not speaking) through its cause (firmness). Manakkudavar calls the quality añcāmai, fearlessness. Pappaiah and Kalaignar stress not saying anything wrong or demeaning when danger threatens. On the text: ytamizh.com prints the couplet without a final stop; thirukkural.net and the sandhi-joined text have வன்க ணவன். in the metrical division; thirukkuralvilakkam.blogspot.com joins it as வன்கணவன். Some editions give வாய்சோரா for வாய்சேரா (Parimelazhagar’s gloss uses ‘வாய்சோர்ந்தும்’), but the sources consulted read வாய்சேரா.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya tells the envoy to deliver his king’s message exactly as entrusted, even if weapons are raised against him, and to bear whatever the enemy says without betraying his master. Because envoys are the mouthpieces of kings, they are not to be killed.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.16 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [5]
The Arthashastra makes faithful, unflinching delivery the envoy’s first duty under threat.
This is the Kural’s vaṉkaṇ: the firmness that keeps a message intact and free of any disgrace to the king under pressure.
Christianity
Frightened in the high priest’s courtyard, Peter denies three times that he knows Jesus. When the cock crows he remembers Jesus’ words and weeps bitterly.
— Matthew 26:69-75 [6]
Under fear, the chief disciple says words that dishonour the master he represents, and he bitterly regrets it.
It is a warning example of the Kural’s vaṭu māṟṟam, the shameful word that slips out when firmness fails.
Islam
“What do you believe yourselves? They said: We believe as he believes. He said: I swear by Allah that were it not that messengers are not killed, I would cut off your heads.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 2761 [7]
Musaylimah’s envoys stand by their master’s claim even in front of the Prophet, and the Prophet spares them because envoys are not to be killed.
They do not back away from their sender’s message under pressure. This is the Kural’s firmness, though the cause they served was rejected.
Judaism
Avtalyon warns scholars to be careful with their words. A careless phrase could lead students into error, and in the end the name of Heaven would be profaned.
— Pirkei Avot 1:11 [8]
The Mishnah teaches that a representative’s careless words bring disgrace on the one he represents.
The Kural’s envoy must likewise never let slip a word that stains the name of the king who sent him.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells Prince Abhaya that he never speaks words that are untrue or unbeneficial. Even true and beneficial words he speaks only when he knows the time is right, whether or not they are welcome.
— Majjhima Nikāya 58, Abhayarājakumāra Sutta [9]
The Buddha keeps strict control over what leaves his mouth, weighing truth, benefit and timing.
This kind of self-mastery in speech is what keeps the Kural’s envoy from letting a harmful word slip.
Jainism
“Well done! You alone, messenger, are the first of the eloquent since you are able to utter such a speech before me.”
— Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra 1.5, Part 1, 'Negotiations with Bahubali' (Hemachandra, tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS 1931) [10]
Bahubali, angered by Bharata's demand, still admits that Suvega delivered his master's message in full and with force, without softening it out of fear.
The Kural asks for an envoy firm enough never to let slip a word that lowers his king; Suvega speaks for Bharata's supremacy in a hostile court, and even his opponent grants his firmness.
Sikhism
Summoned by Aurangzeb, Guru Har Rai sent his son Ram Rai to represent him; when the emperor objected to a line of the Adi Granth as disparaging to Muslims, Ram Rai tried to appease him by claiming it had been wrongly transcribed, and Guru Har Rai excommunicated him for it and passed him over for the succession.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Har Rai [11]
Sikh history remembers Ram Rai as the envoy who, under pressure at a foreign court, let slip words that lowered his own Guru's scripture, and was cast off for it.
The Kural says only one firm enough never to let a shameful word slip out under pressure is fit to carry his master's message to other kings; Ram Rai is the exact counter-example, and the Guru's response shows how seriously the fault was judged.
Greek Mythology
The Trojan herald Idaeus brings Paris’s offer to the Greek camp. While delivering it, he adds his own wish that Paris had died before causing all this.
— Homer, Iliad 7.381-397 [12]
Idaeus lets words slip that shame the side he represents while he is delivering their official message.
He is the envoy the Kural rules out: one who lets a vaṭu māṟṟam, a disgraceful word, pass his lips.
Philosophy
“But I thought that I ought not to do anything common or mean in the hour of danger: nor do I now repent of the manner of my defence, and I would rather die having spoken after my manner, than speak in your manner and live.”
— Plato, Apology, 38d–e (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [13]
After being condemned, Socrates explains why he would not weep, plead or bring his children into court to win pity, saying that he would rather die than save himself with words unworthy of him.
Like the Kural's envoy, Socrates holds that no pressure, even the threat of death, should force a shameful word from a man of firm character.

Real Life Example

Busbecq holds firm through years of confinement at Suleiman's court
1556 to 1562 · Constantinople (Istanbul), Ottoman Empire
Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, envoy of the Habsburg ruler Ferdinand I to Sultan Suleiman, presented demands at his first audience that he described as full of dignity and independence, and the Sultan received them coldly. On his second mission he and his staff were confined to their lodgings and treated almost as prisoners, and when his colleagues were allowed to leave in 1557 he chose to stay, judging that his departure would signal the end of talks and make war more likely. In 1562 he concluded a truce acceptable to both sides and returned to Vienna.
— Sources: [14] [15]
Under years of pressure and confinement, Busbecq neither abandoned his post nor gave way on his master's position, and his patience finally produced peace terms.
The kural says only an envoy firm enough never to let a word that lowers his king slip out under pressure is fit to carry his message, and Busbecq's steadiness in Constantinople fits that demand.
