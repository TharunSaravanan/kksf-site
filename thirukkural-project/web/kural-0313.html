Kural 313

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

செய்யாமல் செற்றார்க்கும் இன்னாத செய்தபின் உய்யா விழுமந் தரும்.
Seyyaamal setraarkkum innaadha seydhapin Uyyaa vizhumam tharum.

Meaning

Even if people hate and hurt you when you did nothing to them, hurting them back will later bring you sorrow you cannot escape.

Elementary

Suppose someone is mean to you for no reason at all, and you get them back. It might feel fair for a minute, but later you end up feeling awful and stuck in a big fight. The kural says hitting back, even when you didn’t start it, brings trouble you can’t get out of.

University

This couplet sharpens 312 by removing every excuse: the victim did nothing (seyyaamal) to provoke the enmity (setraar, those who hate). Even so, retaliating yields uyyaa vizhumam, a grief from which there is no escape. Because the chapter belongs to the section on the renunciant, older commentators and Pope’s translation read it as addressed to the ascetic, for whom retaliation undoes the discipline of years; the ‘inescapable sorrow’ is at once moral self-damage and the karmic consequence of the act. The logic is prudential as well as ethical: revenge draws the avenger into the cycle of harm. Greek tragedy dramatises exactly this spiral in the house of Atreus, and the Gospel compresses it into the saying that those who take the sword perish by it.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If (the punishment falls) not on (the offender) himself, (it falls) on his sons, if not on the sons, (at least) on his grandsons; but an iniquity (once) committed, never fails to produce fruit to him who wrought it.”
— Manusmriti 4.173 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu teaches that a wrong, once done, always bears fruit for the one who did it, even if the result is delayed.
The Kural’s ‘inescapable sorrow’ is this law applied to retaliation: harm done in revenge is still harm, and its fruit cannot be avoided.
Christianity
“Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword.”
— Matthew 26:52 (KJV) [4]
When Peter strikes one of the men arresting Jesus, Jesus orders him to stop, warning that violence returns on those who use it.
Peter is retaliating against men who came against an innocent Jesus, the Kural’s exact situation, and Jesus warns that such retaliation brings ruin on the one who strikes.
Islam
“The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah.”
— Qur’an 42:40 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The Qur’an allows proportionate response to a wrong, but holds up pardon and reconciliation as the better and more rewarded path.
Both texts steer the wronged person away from retaliation. The Kural warns of the sorrow revenge brings; the Qur’an promises reward for forgiving instead.
Judaism
“Say not thou, I will recompense evil; but wait on the LORD, and he shall save thee.”
— Proverbs 20:22 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs tells the wronged person not to plan revenge but to leave the matter to God.
Both texts tell the victim of unprovoked harm not to repay it; Proverbs adds that deliverance lies in restraint, not in revenge.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that answering hatred with hatred only keeps it alive; only non-hatred ends it.
The Kural says retaliation traps the avenger in lasting sorrow; the Dhammapada explains why: hatred returned only feeds the cycle.
Jainism
“When he who is free from desires approached the village, the inhabitants met him on the outside, and attacked him, saying, ‘Get away from here.’ He was struck with a stick, the fist, a lance, hit with a fruit, a clod, a potsherd. … abandoning the care of his body, the Venerable One humbled himself and bore pain, free from desire.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I, Lecture 8 ('The Pillow of Righteousness'), Lesson 3, vv. 7–12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org numbering) [7]
During his wanderings in the wild Lāḍha country, Mahāvīra, who had done the villagers no wrong, was beaten, cut and set upon by dogs. He bore it without striking back, 'ceasing to use the stick against living beings.'
Valluvar warns against retaliating even against people who hate you without any provocation. Mahāvīra's conduct in Lāḍha is the Jain model of exactly that restraint toward unprovoked enemies.
Sikhism
“No one is my enemy, and I am no one's enemy.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 671, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes the state reached in the company of the holy: enmity has gone from both sides, because he no longer answers hostility with hostility.
The Kural warns that striking back even at those who hated us without cause brings sorrow; Guru Arjan describes the freedom of one who has stopped being anyone's enemy.
Greek Mythology
In the myth behind Aeschylus’ Agamemnon, Thyestes wrongs his brother Atreus, and Atreus takes revenge by serving Thyestes his own children at a feast. The revenge does not end the feud; it plants a curse that brings murder upon murder through the next generations of the house.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1583–1602 (Aegisthus’ account of the feast of Thyestes) [9]
Atreus answers a real wrong with revenge, and the revenge brings disaster on his own family for generations.
This is the Kural’s ‘inescapable sorrow’ made into a family history: harm returned for harm becomes a curse on the avenger’s own line.
Philosophy
“The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.6 (trans. George Long) [10]
Marcus Aurelius advises that the real victory over someone who wrongs you is refusing to become like them.
Both see revenge as a trap: the Kural warns that it brings inescapable sorrow, and Marcus says it turns you into the very wrongdoer you resent.

Real Life Example

Henry II does public penance at Canterbury after Becket's murder
29 December 1170 - July 1174 · Canterbury, England
King Henry II and Archbishop Thomas Becket, once close friends, quarrelled bitterly for years over the rights of the Church, and Becket spent six years in exile. Soon after Becket's return, four of Henry's knights, reportedly acting on the king's angry words although there is no evidence he ordered it, killed the archbishop in Canterbury Cathedral on 29 December 1170. Becket was declared a saint in 1173, and in 1174 Henry walked barefoot through Canterbury, submitted to beatings by bishops and monks, and kept an overnight vigil at the shrine as penance.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The king's anger at an opponent who had defied him led to a crime that shocked Europe and left a stain on his reign that he could not escape.
The kural warns that striking back with harm at those who opposed and hated you brings sorrow that cannot be escaped, and Henry's angry outburst against his adversary ended in public humiliation and lasting disgrace.
