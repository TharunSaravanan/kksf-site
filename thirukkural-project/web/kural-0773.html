Kural 773

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

பேராண்மை என்ப தறுகண்ஒன் றுற்றக்கால் ஊராண்மை மற்றதன் எஃகு.
Peraanmai enba tharukan ondru utrakkaal Ooraanmai matradhan eqku.

Meaning

Having no fear in battle is called great bravery. But helping your enemy when he is in trouble is the sharpest edge of that bravery.

Elementary

A good football player plays hard to win. But if a player on the other team falls and gets hurt, the best player stops and helps him up. Being brave is good; being brave and kind is even better.

University

This is the moral centre of the chapter: tharukan (fierceness, unflinching daring) is what people call peraanmai (great manliness or valour), but ooraanmai, helping or showing grace when ondru utrakkaal (“a calamity befalls,” understood as befalling the foe), is its eqku, the keen cutting edge. Parimelazhagar glosses ooraanmai as upakaram (benevolent aid) and the traditional reading takes the misfortune as the enemy’s, so magnanimity to a fallen foe is the sharpest point of heroism. Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar both read it this way; Sivayogi Sivakumar paraphrases more broadly as relieving others’ suffering, even enemies’. The metaphor of eqku (steel, blade-edge) is apt: valour without mercy is a blunt weapon.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Yuddha Kanda, when Ravana is exhausted and disarmed in his first duel with Rama, Rama does not kill him but sends him back to Lanka to rest and return to fight another day.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (Sarga 59 in common numbering; paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki shows Rama refusing to strike a spent and weaponless opponent, a classic image of dharmic restraint in war.
Rama’s mercy toward a foe in distress is exactly what the Kural calls the sharp edge of valour.
Christianity
“Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink: for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head. Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.”
— Romans 12:20-21 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to answer an enemy’s need with practical help rather than revenge.
Helping an enemy in trouble is the Kural’s highest form of strength, and Paul calls it overcoming evil with good.
Islam
“And if any one of the polytheists seeks your protection, then grant him protection so that he may hear the words of Allah. Then deliver him to his place of safety.”
— Qur’an 9:6 (Saheeh International) [5]
Even in a context of hostilities, an enemy who asks for protection must be granted it and escorted to safety.
Protecting a vulnerable enemy is the same magnanimity the Kural calls the edge of valour.
Judaism
“If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat; If he is thirsty, give him water to drink. You will be heaping live coals on his head, And GOD will reward you.”
— Proverbs 25:21-22 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs teaches that an enemy’s need is an occasion for kindness, not gloating.
This is the Kural’s ooraanmai: answering the foe’s misfortune with help.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that enmity ends only when one side answers it with non-hatred.
Kindness toward a stricken foe is non-hatred in action, the Kural’s sharp edge of valour.
Jainism
In Hemacandra's account of the duel between the brothers Bharata and Bahubali, Bahubali raises his fist for a blow that would have crushed Bharata. He then stops himself, turns the same hand to pluck out his own hair in the ascetic's renunciation, and gives up the kingdom instead of striking his defeated brother.
— Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 1.5 (Ādīśvaracaritra, 'The fight between Bharata and Bāhubali'), tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org [8]
Jain tradition makes this moment of restraint toward a beaten rival, not the victory, the climax of Bahubali's heroism; he goes on to become a revered kevalin.
The Kural calls kindness to an enemy in misfortune the 'sharp edge' of valour, and Bahubali's mercy to the brother at his feet is the best-known Jain example of exactly that.
Sikhism
During the siege of Anandpur (1704-05), Bhai Kanhaiya carried water to wounded soldiers of both armies without distinction. When Sikhs complained to Guru Gobind Singh that he was helping the enemy, Kanhaiya said he saw only the Guru's face in every man; the Guru approved and gave him ointment to treat the wounds as well.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, 'Kanhaiya, Bhai (1648-1718)' (paraphrase) [9]
Bhai Kanhaiya became the founder of the Sevapanthi order, and his service to wounded enemies is a central Sikh example of seva without enmity.
The Kural calls help given to an enemy in his misfortune the sharp edge of valour; Bhai Kanhaiya, with the Guru's blessing, gave exactly that help on the battlefield.
Greek Mythology
When the aged Priam comes alone to Achilles’ tent to ransom Hector’s body, Achilles weeps with him, raises him up, and grants a truce for the funeral.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 24 (paraphrase) [10]
The fiercest Greek warrior, faced with his enemy’s grieving father, chooses compassion over rage.
Achilles’ mercy to a defeated foe is the moment the Iliad itself treats as the height of his greatness, as the Kural would.
Philosophy
Seneca argues to the young Nero that mercy (clementia) is the virtue that most becomes the powerful, because only the strong are in a position to spare.
— Seneca, De Clementia, Book I (paraphrase) [11]
For Seneca, restraint toward those one could crush is a sign of greatness, not weakness.
Seneca’s claim matches the Kural’s ranking of mercy as the keenest edge of strength.

Real Life Example

The captain of the Admiral Hipper rescues Glowworm's crew and praises her commander
8 April 1940 · Norwegian Sea, off the coast of Norway
The British destroyer HMS Glowworm, under Lieutenant Commander Gerard Roope, met the much larger German heavy cruiser Admiral Hipper, engaged her and rammed her before Glowworm sank. Hipper's captain, Hellmuth Heye, stopped for over an hour to pick up survivors from the sea. He later sent word to the British through the Red Cross praising Roope's courage in closing with a far stronger ship, and Roope was posthumously awarded the Victoria Cross, gazetted in 1945.
— Sources: [12] [13]
In the midst of a battle he had just won, an enemy commander used his ship to save his opponents and then made sure their captain's bravery would be honoured.
The kural calls fearlessness valour but says helping the enemy when misfortune strikes him is its sharp edge, and Captain Heye's rescue of Glowworm's crew is that edge in action.
