Kural 632

Ministry of State · Amaichu · அமைச்சு

வன்கண் குடிகாத்தல் கற்றறிதல் ஆள்வினையோடு ஐந்துடன் மாண்டது அமைச்சு.
Vankan kudikaaththal katrarithal aalvinaiyodu Aindhudan maandadhu amaichchu.

Meaning

A good minister is also excellent in five more things: firm courage, protecting the people, learning, understanding, and hard work.

Elementary

A good class monitor is brave enough to speak up, looks after classmates, keeps learning, and does not give up when a job gets hard. This kural says a good adviser needs all of these together, not just one.

University

The couplet names further qualities: vankan (firmness or fearlessness, literally ‘hard eye’), kudikaaththal (protecting the subjects), katrarithal (knowing through study), and aalvinai (sustained effort, the subject of chapter 62). The phrase aindhudan (‘with the five’) is read in more than one way. In the Parimelazhagar tradition it refers back to the qualities already listed, so the minister has these as well as those. Mu. Varadarasanar splits kudi as noble birth (kudippirappu) and kaaththal as protection so that the verse itself lists five. Puliyur Kesikan instead takes the five as the purity of the five senses. Solomon Pappaiah divides katru and arithal into learning from books and learning by listening to the learned. The older-orthography edition prints ‘katrarithalaalvinaiyo / daindhudan’, which is the same text with a different word division.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…possessed of foresight, wise, of strong memory, bold, eloquent, skilful, intelligent, possessed of enthusiasm, dignity and endurance…”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.9 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [4]
Kautilya gives a long list of qualities for a minister, including wisdom, boldness, learning and staying power.
Boldness, learning and endurance match the Kural’s vankan, katrarithal and aalvinai almost one for one.
Christianity
“Wherefore, brethren, look ye out among you seven men of honest report, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, whom we may appoint over this business.”
— Acts 6:3 (KJV) [5]
The apostles pick administrators to care for the community’s needy, requiring good character and wisdom.
Like the Kural, it chooses officials by character and wisdom so that they can look after the people.
Islam
“One of the women said, ‘O my father, hire him. Indeed, the best one you can hire is the strong and the trustworthy.’”
— Qur’an 28:26 (Sahih International) [6]
A daughter of Madyan urges her father to employ Moses because he is both strong and trustworthy.
Strength and reliability match the Kural’s firmness and dependable protection of those in one’s care.
Judaism
“Moreover thou shalt provide out of all the people able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating unjust gain; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens.”
— Exodus 18:21 (JPS 1917) [7]
Jethro advises Moses to share his burden by appointing capable, honest men to govern and judge the people.
Like the Kural, Jethro sets out the qualities of those who help a leader rule and protect the people.
Buddhism
In the Kutadanta Sutta, the Buddha tells of a wise royal chaplain who advises a king to deal with banditry by giving seed to farmers, capital to traders, and proper wages to officials, rather than by punishment. The kingdom becomes prosperous and safe.
— Kutadanta Sutta, Digha Nikaya 5 [8]
The ideal counsellor here protects the people by fixing the economic causes of disorder.
This is kudikaaththal, protecting the people, carried out through wise and learned counsel.
Jainism
“Thus I became the protector of myself and of others besides, of all living beings, whether they move or not.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.35 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The monk Anathi explains to King Shrenika that by renouncing and keeping the vows he became a true protector of all beings, unlike the king with all his wealth and army.
The Kural lists protecting the people among a minister's qualities, with firmness, learning and effort; the Jain verse recasts protection as the ahimsa of the self-controlled person toward all living beings.
Sikhism
In May 1675 a group of Kashmiri Pandits, persecuted under the Mughal governor of Kashmir, came to Guru Tegh Bahadur at Anandpur for help. According to the Gurbilas account cited in the Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, the Guru said that the earth's burden would be lifted only if a worthy person gave his head, and the young Gobind Das replied that none was worthier than the Guru himself. Guru Tegh Bahadur took up their cause and was executed in Delhi later that year.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (Punjabi University, Patiala, ed. Harbans Singh), 'Tegh Bahadur, Guru', via The Sikh Encyclopedia (paraphrase) [10]
Sikhs remember Guru Tegh Bahadur as one who gave his life to protect the freedom of conscience of people who were not even of his own faith.
Among a minister's qualities the Kural lists firm courage and the protection of the people; Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom is the Sikh model of fearless protection of the vulnerable.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus leaves for Troy he puts his old friend Mentor in charge of his household. In the Ithacan assembly, Mentor boldly rebukes the people for allowing the suitors to waste the absent king’s estate.
— Homer, Odyssey 2 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Mentor is the trusted steward who guards what belongs to his lord and speaks out bravely.
He shows two of the Kural’s qualities, courage (vankan) and protection of those in his charge.
Philosophy
Cicero reminds those who take charge of public affairs of two rules from Plato: to keep the good of the citizens so clearly in view that everything they do serves it, and to care for the whole body politic rather than one part of it.
— Cicero, De Officiis 1.85 [12]
For Cicero, the first duty of a statesman is to protect the welfare of all citizens, not a faction.
This is the Kural’s kudikaaththal, protecting the people, as a basic mark of a minister.

Real Life Example

Sardar Patel brings the princely states into the Indian Union
1947-1949 · India
At independence in 1947, rulers of the princely states, covering about two fifths of India's territory, were free to join India or Pakistan or to stay out of both. Vallabhbhai Patel, as deputy prime minister and minister in charge of the new States Department, worked with its secretary V. P. Menon to bring them in one by one. Over 560 princely states were brought into the Indian Union within a short span of time.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The work called for firmness with reluctant rulers, steady concern for the security of the people, a knowledge of law and administration, and unbroken effort over many months.
The kural adds firmness, protection of the people, learning and persevering effort to the minister's qualities, and Patel's integration of the princely states displayed all of them in a single task.
