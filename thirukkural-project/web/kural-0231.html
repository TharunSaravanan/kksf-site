Kural 231

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

ஈதல் இசைபட வாழ்தல் அதுவல்லது ஊதியம் இல்லை உயிர்க்கு.
Eedhal isaipada vaazhdhal adhuvalladhu Oodhiyam illai uyirkku.

Meaning

Give to people in need, and live so that people speak well of you. There is no other real gain a person can get from life.

Elementary

Imagine a piggy bank you can’t take with you when you leave. The only things you really keep from life are what you gave away and the kind words people say about you because of it. Giving, and being remembered well for it, is the real prize of being alive.

University

The chapter on Renown opens by fusing it with the preceding chapter on Giving (ஈகை): ஈதல் (giving) and இசைபட வாழ்தல் (living so that isai, praise, arises) are presented as one achievement, gathered by the demonstrative அது (‘that’). The pivotal word is ஊதியம் (wages, income, profit), a commercial term deliberately applied to the உயிர் (the living soul): life is framed as a venture whose only true yield is renown generated by generosity. Classical commentary (Parimelazhagan, echoed by Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah) specifies giving to the poor, which anchors புகழ் in ethical action rather than mere celebrity. The couplet thus redefines ‘gain’: wealth hoarded is not profit; wealth converted into others’ welfare, and the esteem that follows, is. The same link between liberality and lasting worth appears in the Vedic hymn to Liberality and in Aristotle’s remark that the liberal are loved precisely because they give.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The riches of the liberal never waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.1 (trans. R.T.H. Griffith) [3]
The Rig Vedic hymn titled ‘Liberality’ says wealth that is shared is never really lost, while the person who refuses to give ends up alone and friendless.
Like the Kural, it treats giving as the one ‘income’ that does not evaporate: the giver’s wealth survives by being given.
Christianity
“Remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how he said, It is more blessed to give than to receive.”
— Acts 20:35 (KJV) [4]
Paul, taking leave of the Ephesian elders, tells them to support the weak and cites Jesus’ saying that giving is more blessed than receiving.
Both texts relocate life’s real gain from what one takes in to what one gives out.
Islam
“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allāh is like a seed [of grain] which grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains.”
— Qur’an 2:261 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an pictures charity as a seed that multiplies many hundredfold, so that what is given is not lost but increased.
The Kural calls giving the soul’s only profit; the Qur’an likewise frames spending on others as the most fruitful investment a person can make.
Judaism
“A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches, and loving favour rather than silver and gold.”
— Proverbs 22:1 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
Hebrew wisdom literature ranks a good reputation and people’s goodwill above any amount of money.
Valluvar says renown is the only real profit; Proverbs says a good name outranks riches. Both place reputation above wealth.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha contrasts the miser, who gains nothing lasting, with the wise giver, whose generosity alone secures future happiness.
Both texts make giving the single act that yields a real, lasting return for a life.
Jainism
“Three merchants set out on their travels, each with his capital; one of them gained there much, the second returned with his capital, and the third merchant came home after having lost his capital. … The capital is human life, the gain is heaven; through the loss of that capital man must be born as a denizen of hell or a brute animal.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.14–16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Mahāvīra's parable of the three merchants treats human life as capital: virtue earns a profit, mere decency only preserves the capital, and sin loses it.
Valluvar says giving and a praiseworthy life are the only real profit a person can earn from living. The Jain parable likewise treats a human life as an investment whose only true gain is a good life's reward, not wealth.
Sikhism
“Those who serve are content. They meditate on the Truest of the True. They do not place their feet in sin, but do good deeds and live righteously in Dharma.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 466-467, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar (pauri), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
This pauri of Asa di Vaar describes those whose lives consist of service, remembrance of God and good deeds.
Valluvar says giving and living so that praise gathers round one's name is the only real profit of life; the Sikh Gurus likewise define a well-spent life as service and good deeds, though without making praise the goal.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor elderly couple, alone in their Phrygian village welcome the disguised Jupiter and Mercury and share their meagre food. The gods flood the inhospitable neighbourhood, turn the couple’s cottage into a temple, and at death change them into an oak and a linden that later visitors honour with garlands.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Baucis and Philemon) [9]
Their small act of hospitality is the only thing that distinguishes them, and it becomes the reason they are remembered and honoured ever after.
As in the Kural, humble giving is what converts an ordinary life into an honoured name.
Philosophy
“The liberal are almost the most loved of all virtuous characters, since they are useful; and this depends on their giving.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (trans. W.D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle observes that generous people win exceptional affection from others, and that this esteem springs directly from their giving.
Aristotle and Valluvar both trace a person’s good standing among others back to the act of giving.

Real Life Example

Sir Thomas Bodley reopens Oxford's university library
8 November 1602 · Oxford, England
Oxford's old university library had been stripped of its books and furniture in the mid-sixteenth century. The retired diplomat Thomas Bodley offered to restore it, personally paying for and directing its refurbishment from 1598 and giving books of his own, and the library opened on 8 November 1602. In 1610 he secured an agreement under which the Stationers' Company would give the library a copy of every book printed in England.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A private fortune given to a public institution made Bodley's name permanent: the library has been called the Bodleian ever since.
The kural says the only real profit of life is to give and to live so that praise gathers around one's name, and Bodley's gift turned his wealth into a name that has lasted four centuries.
