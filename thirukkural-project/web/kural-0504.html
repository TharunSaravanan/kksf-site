Kural 504

Selection and Confidence · Therindhu Thelithal · தெரிந்து தெளிதல்

குணம்நாடிக் குற்றமும் நாடி அவற்றுள் மிகைநாடி மிக்க கொளல்.
Kunamnaadik kutramum naadi avatrul Migainaadi mikka kolal.

Meaning

Look at a person’s good points, then at their faults. See which side is bigger, and judge them by that.

Elementary

Imagine a friend who sometimes forgets your birthday but always shares, always helps, and always tells the truth. If you put the good things on one side of a see-saw and the forgetful thing on the other, the good side sinks way down. That’s how the Kural says to judge people: by which side is heavier.

University

The verb நாடு (to seek, look into) occurs three times, giving a three-step procedure: examine குணம் (good qualities), then குற்றம் (faults), then மிகை (which is greater), and finally கொளல், ‘take’ the person according to what is மிக்க (predominant). Following 503’s point that no one is flawless, this is a rule of proportion rather than perfection. Pappaiah narrows it to appointment (choose by the excess of virtues), while Kalaignar keeps it general: a clear judgment about someone comes only after this weighing. Some commentators note the ambiguity of மிக்க கொளல்: judge the person by what predominates, or keep the one in whom virtues predominate; either way the method is the same. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Sometimes the mode of goodness becomes prominent, defeating the modes of passion and ignorance, O son of Bharata. Sometimes the mode of passion defeats goodness and ignorance, and at other times ignorance defeats goodness and passion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 14.10 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that everyone has all three guṇas, and a person’s character is known by which one is dominant.
Judging someone by the quality that prevails is the Kural’s rule of மிகை.
Christianity
“And above all things have fervent charity among yourselves: for charity shall cover the multitude of sins.”
— 1 Peter 4:8 (KJV) [4]
Peter teaches that great love outweighs and covers many faults.
The idea that a strong virtue can outweigh faults is close to the Kural’s weighing.
Islam
“Then as for one whose scales are heavy [with good deeds], … But as for one whose scales are light, …”
— Qur’an 101:6-9 (Sahih International) [5]
On the Day of Judgment each person’s deeds are weighed; the fate of each depends on which side is heavier.
The Kural uses the same image of a balance: judge by which side tips.
Judaism
“Everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted, And the world is judged with goodness; And everything is in accordance with the preponderance of works.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:15 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Rabbi Akiva teaches that judgment follows the preponderance of a person’s deeds.
‘Preponderance’ is precisely the Kural’s மிகை.
Buddhism
In the Salt Crystal discourse the Buddha explains that a small bad deed by someone of great, developed goodness is like a pinch of salt in the Ganges, while the same deed by someone with little goodness is like salt in a cup of water.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 3.100 (Loṇakapalla / Salt Crystal Sutta) [7]
The effect of a fault depends on how much good surrounds it.
The Kural likewise judges faults in proportion to a person’s virtues.
Jainism
Jain epistemology teaches that a thing is known through valid knowledge (pramana) and through partial standpoints (nayas), so that any single viewpoint captures only one aspect of a many-sided reality (anekantavada).
— Tattvartha Sutra 1.6 (pramana and naya), with the anekantavada doctrine as generally expounded [8]
Because reality is many-sided, Jain thinkers insist that judgment weigh several aspects together rather than fix on one feature alone.
Valluvar asks the examiner to look at a man's virtues and then his faults and judge by what prevails; the Jain doctrine of standpoints similarly warns against a verdict drawn from one side only.
Sikhism
“Good deeds and bad deeds-the record is read out in the Presence of the Lord of Dharma.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 8, Japji Sahib (closing salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the closing salok of Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak pictures a person's good and bad actions both being read out and weighed before the divine judge.
The Kural says to look into a person's virtues, then his faults, and judge him by whichever prevails; Japji Sahib likewise has both columns of the record read before judgement.
Greek Mythology
Before Achilles and Hector fight, Zeus lifts his golden scales with the fates of both warriors; Hector’s side sinks toward Hades, and Apollo leaves him.
— Homer, Iliad 22.208-213 [10]
The gods decide by weighing and seeing which side is heavier.
The Kural’s method is a moral version of these scales.
Philosophy
Aristotle says that someone who strays only a little from the right, whether toward excess or deficiency, is not blamed; only the one who strays far is, and how far is a matter for perception in particular cases.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2.9 (1109b18-23) [11]
Moral judgment of a person allows for small deviations and looks at the overall pattern.
The Kural too tolerates lesser faults when virtues clearly prevail.

Real Life Example

General Groves chooses Oppenheimer to lead Los Alamos
October 1942 · Washington, D.C. and Los Alamos, New Mexico, United States
General Leslie Groves, head of the Manhattan Project, chose the physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer to direct its new weapons laboratory even though Oppenheimer had almost no administrative experience, had not won a Nobel Prize, and faced security objections over past left-wing associations. Groves ordered that his clearance be issued, calling him essential to the project. Groves later wrote that he had never felt it was a mistake to select and clear Oppenheimer for the post.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Groves openly listed the candidate's drawbacks and still judged that his breadth of knowledge and drive outweighed them.
The kural tells us to weigh a person's virtues against his faults and judge by whichever prevails, which is what Groves did.
