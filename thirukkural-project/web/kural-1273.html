Kural 1273

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

மணியில் திகழ்தரு நூல்போல் மடந்தை அணியில் திகழ்வதொன்று உண்டு.
Maniyil thikazhdharu noolpol madandhai aniyil thikazhvadhondru undu.

Meaning

Just as the thread shows through the clear beads strung on it, something shines through this young woman's beauty.

Elementary

If you thread clear glass beads on a string, you can see the string running through them. The poet says his beloved’s beauty is like those beads: you can see a hidden feeling running through it, if you look closely.

University

The image is of a string of maṇi (gems or crystal beads) through which the nūl (thread) can be seen; the maṭantai (young woman) has something that likewise tikaḻvatu, shines through her aṇi (beauty or adornment). Varadarasanar and Kesikan gloss that ‘something’ as a kuṟippu, a hidden intent visible within her beauty; Solomon Pappaiah takes the gems as clear crystal (paḷiṅku) through which the thread is seen, and Kalaignar as a sign that enchants the speaker. In the traditional arrangement the husband speaks to the confidante, saying the wife’s hidden feeling is legible through her looks. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the thread-and-gem simile is also famous from Bhagavad Gita 7.7, though there it describes the divine as the string on which the world is threaded.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O conqueror of wealth, there is no truth superior to Me. Everything rests upon Me, as pearls are strung on a thread.”
— Bhagavad Gita 7.7 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna says he is the hidden thread holding all things together, as a string holds a necklace of pearls.
The Kural uses the same thread-through-gems image for a hidden meaning running through visible beauty.
Christianity
“A good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is good; and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is evil: for of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh.”
— Luke 6:45 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that what is inside a person shows itself on the outside.
The Kural sees the same movement from inside to outside, an inner feeling showing through her beauty.
Islam
“Their mark is on their faces from the trace of prostration.”
— Qur’an 48:29 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says the inner devotion of the faithful leaves a visible sign on their faces.
As the thread shows through the beads, an inner state shows through the face in both texts.
Judaism
“A man’s wisdom maketh his face to shine, and the boldness of his face is changed.”
— Ecclesiastes 8:1 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Ecclesiastes says inner wisdom becomes visible as a light in the face.
The Kural also sees something inward shining through outward beauty.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares the meditator’s insight to a man of good eyesight holding a clear, well-polished beryl gem through which a coloured thread runs, and seeing both the gem and the thread inside it.
— Digha Nikaya 2, Samaññaphala Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The simile describes seeing clearly how consciousness is bound up with the body, as a thread is seen through a gem.
The Kural uses the identical picture of a thread visible within a gem, but for a feeling seen within beauty.
Jainism
“A young woman, putting on fine ornaments and clothes, will say to a Sramana: ‘I shall give up (my former way of life) and practise the rough (viz. control). Reverend sir, teach me the Law!’”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga warns monks that a woman’s ornaments and pious words may carry a hidden intention.
The Kural also sees an intention running beneath a woman’s adornment, but reads it tenderly where the Jain text reads it with suspicion.
Sikhism
“The universe is strung on His Thread.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1150, Raag Bhairao, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that all creation is held together by the Lord, like beads on a string that is hidden but runs through them all.
The Kural sees the thread showing through crystal beads as a picture of the hidden feeling shining through the young woman's beauty; Guru Arjan uses the same thread-and-beads image for the hidden Lord running through all things.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod tells how the gods adorned Pandora with a golden band, garlands and every grace, while Hermes placed within her a hidden nature of his own devising.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 60-82 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
In Hesiod the first woman’s adornment conceals something set inside her.
The Kural also sees something within a woman’s beauty, though for Valluvar it is a tender feeling, while for Hesiod it is guile.
Philosophy
Alcibiades compares Socrates to the statues of Silenus sold in workshops, which open up to reveal images of the gods inside.
— Plato, Symposium 215a-b (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato uses the image to say that something precious lies inside an outward form and can be seen by those who look within.
The Kural also speaks of something within a visible form that shines out to the attentive eye.

Real Life Example

Dorothy Hodgkin reveals the structures hidden inside crystals
1940s-1964 · England
Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin became a leading practitioner of X-ray crystallography, which uses the way X-rays pass through a crystal to work out the arrangement of the atoms within it. Using this method she determined the structures of penicillin in the 1940s, vitamin B12 in 1956, and later insulin. She received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1964.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her work showed that the inner pattern of a substance can be read by looking through the crystal that contains it.
The kural compares a hidden feeling showing through a woman's beauty to the thread visible through crystal beads; Hodgkin's science literally read the hidden structure through the crystal.
