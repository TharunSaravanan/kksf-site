Kural 1102

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

பிணிக்கு மருந்து பிறமன் அணியிழை தன்நோய்க்குத் தானே மருந்து.
Pinikku marundhu piraman aniyizhai thannoikkuth thaane marundhu.

Meaning

For other illnesses, the medicine is something different from the illness. But for the love-sickness this jeweled girl causes, she herself is the only medicine.

Elementary

When you miss your best friend who has moved away, no toy or sweet makes the sad feeling go. The only thing that fixes it is seeing that friend again. The person you miss is both the reason you feel sad and the cure.

University

The couplet sets ordinary medicine against love: for a piṇi (பிணி, disease) the marundu (மருந்து, remedy) is piṟa (‘something other’), while the nōy (நோய், sickness) caused by the aṇiyiḻai (அணியிழை, ‘she of fine jewels’) is cured only by her. Man in piṟaman is an expletive particle (asai) with no meaning of its own, as the commentators note. Solomon Pappaiah sharpens the logic: remedies usually have a nature opposite to the disease, yet here the cause and the cure are the same. The idea continues ch. 110’s opening kural (1091), where one glance wounds and another heals, and moves it from the look to union itself. Pope: ‘Disease and medicine antagonists we surely see; / This maid, to pain she gives, herself is remedy.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Dear Kṛṣṇa, please pour the nectar of Your lips upon the fire within our hearts — a fire You ignited with Your smiling glances and the sweet song of Your flute.”
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29.35 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The gopis tell Krishna that the burning love he lit in them by his glances and his flute can be soothed only by his own nectar.
As in the Kural, the one who causes the love-sickness is the only one who can cure it.
Christianity
“Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up.”
— Hosea 6:1 (KJV) [4]
The prophet tells Israel that the God who wounded them is the same God who will heal them.
The pattern of the Kural, where the one who wounds is the one who heals, appears here in divine rather than human love.
Islam
“There is nothing like marriage, for two who love one another.”
— Sunan Ibn Majah 1847 (graded hasan by Darussalam) [5]
The Prophet teaches that for two people who love each other, marriage is the best answer to that love.
The Kural’s cure for love-sickness is the beloved herself; this hadith names lawful union with the beloved as the cure.
Judaism
“I kill, and I make alive; I have wounded, and I heal; And there is none that can deliver out of My hand.”
— Deuteronomy 32:39 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
In the Song of Moses, God declares that wounding and healing both come from Him alone.
The Kural applies to the beloved what this verse says of God: the same hand gives the wound and the healing.
Buddhism
“There is no fire like lust and no crime like hatred. There is no ill like the aggregates (of existence) and no bliss higher than the peace (of Nibbana).”
— Dhammapada 202 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names lust as the worst fire and the peace of Nibbana as the true bliss that ends every ill.
Buddhism agrees that love is a sickness, but it places the cure in Nibbana, not in the beloved who caused it.
Jainism
“Misery ceases on the absence of delusion, delusion ceases on the absence of desire, desire ceases on the absence of greed, greed ceases on the absence of property.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira traces suffering back through delusion and desire, and teaches that it ends only when desire itself ends.
Jainism also asks what cures the pain of desire, but its answer is the end of desire, not the beloved who stirred it.
Sikhism
“The physician was called in; he touched my arm and felt my pulse. The foolish physician did not know that the pain was in the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1279, Raag Malar, Malar ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Malar ki Vaar, Guru Nanak pictures a doctor taking the pulse of someone whose sickness is really the pain of separation from the Beloved, which no ordinary medicine can reach.
The Kural says ordinary illnesses have ordinary cures but love-sickness can be cured only by the beloved; Guru Nanak's foolish physician fails for the same reason, because the pain is love-longing.
Greek Mythology
When Telephus was wounded by Achilles’ spear, an oracle told him that only the one who had wounded him could heal him, and he was cured by rust scraped from that same spear.
— Apollodorus, Epitome 3.17-20 (tr. J. G. Frazer) [10]
In this story the weapon that caused the wound is the only thing that can close it.
The Kural’s beloved is like Achilles’ spear: the source of the wound is also its only cure.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus Socrates says that the soul of the lover, stung and aching when apart, forgets mother, brothers and friends and holds that the beloved is the only physician of its greatest pains.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-252b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato the beloved is both what wounds the lover’s soul with longing and what alone can heal it.
This is the Kural’s claim almost word for word: she who causes the sickness is its only medicine.

Real Life Example

Berlioz writes the Symphonie fantastique for Harriet Smithson
1827–1833 · Paris, France
In 1827 the young composer Hector Berlioz saw the Irish actress Harriet Smithson play Ophelia in Hamlet in Paris and became infatuated with her, but she did not answer his letters. He poured his feelings into the Symphonie fantastique, first performed on 5 December 1830, in which a recurring melody called the idée fixe represents the beloved. Smithson attended a later performance in 1832, and the two married in October 1833, though they later separated.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One of the most famous symphonies of the Romantic era grew directly out of a lovesick obsession, and the woman who caused it went on to become the composer's wife.
The kural says that ordinary illnesses have cures different from their causes, but love-sickness is healed only by the woman who caused it; Berlioz's long fever of love was answered only when Smithson herself accepted him.
