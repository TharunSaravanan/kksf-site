Kural 688

The Envoy · Thoodhu · தூது

தூய்மை துணைமை துணிவுடைமை இம்மூன்றின் வாய்மை வழியுரைப்பான் பண்பு.
Thooymai thunaimai thunivudaimai immoondrin Vaaymai vazhiyuraippaan panbu.

Meaning

A person who carries the king's message exactly as given must be honest and clean, have good helpers, be brave, and above all tell the truth.

Elementary

When your teacher gives you a note for your parents, you shouldn’t swap the words for a treat, you should have a grown-up nearby who can help, you should be brave enough to deliver it even if it’s bad news, and you must never change what it says.

University

From here Parimelazhagar turns to the second kind of envoy, the vazhi uraippāṉ, who repeats his king’s words exactly as spoken. He gives each quality a job. Tūymai is purity from greed for wealth or women (poruḷ kāmaṅkaḷ), so bribes cannot bend the message. Tuṇaimai is having the foreign ministers’ support, so that if the envoy’s praise of his own king angers them, they can smooth it over by saying ‘that is how our people speak’. Tuṇivuṭaimai is boldness, so he does not hold back out of fear of harm. Vāymai is truthfulness, so everyone can trust him. He reads ‘immūṉṟiṉ’ with the -iṉ in the sense of ‘together with’: truthfulness is a fourth quality alongside the three. Manakkudavar differs: tuṇaimai is having kin, tuṇivu is resolving after inquiry. Pappaiah follows Parimelazhagar closely. On the text: all sources agree; the sandhi-joined text writes யிம்மூன்றின்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Before the war, Dhritarashtra sends his charioteer Sanjaya to the Pandavas. Sanjaya is trusted on both sides for his honesty. He delivers the king’s message and carries Yudhishthira’s reply faithfully back to the Kaurava court.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Sanjaya-yana sections (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [5]
Sanjaya is a messenger valued above all for integrity and accurate reporting.
He fits the Kural’s vazhi uraippāṉ, the envoy whose purity and truthfulness make him trusted by both courts.
Christianity
“But have renounced the hidden things of dishonesty, not walking in craftiness, nor handling the word of God deceitfully; but by manifestation of the truth commending ourselves to every man’s conscience in the sight of God.”
— 2 Corinthians 4:2 (KJV) [6]
Paul insists that he passes on the message entrusted to him without falsifying it, and wins trust through truthfulness.
This is the Kural’s tūymai and vāymai: a messenger who does not tamper with what he was given.
Islam
At al-Hudaybiyya, the Prophet chose ‘Uthman ibn ‘Affan as envoy to the Quraysh partly because his clan in Mecca could protect him. A kinsman there gave him protection while he delivered the message.
— Ibn Ishaq, Sirat Rasul Allah (trans. A. Guillaume), account of al-Hudaybiyya [7]
The envoy was picked in part because he had allies in the other camp.
This is Parimelazhagar’s tuṇaimai: supporters at the foreign court who keep the envoy safe and the talks smooth.
Judaism
“A wicked messenger falleth into mischief: but a faithful ambassador is health.”
— Proverbs 13:17 (KJV) [6]
The proverb contrasts the damage a corrupt messenger causes with the good a faithful envoy brings.
The Kural too makes faithfulness and purity the envoy’s defining traits.
Buddhism
King Ajātasattu sends his minister Vassakāra to the Buddha with instructions to repeat his question exactly and bring back exactly what the Buddha says, since Tathāgatas do not speak falsely. Vassakāra does both.
— Dīgha Nikāya 16, Mahāparinibbāna Sutta 1.1-1.5 [8]
The minister acts as a word-for-word envoy between the king and the Buddha.
He is a clear example of the Kural’s vazhi uraippāṉ, the one who carries a message exactly as given.
Jainism
“A monk should avoid untruth, nor should he speak positively (about future things, his plans, &c.); he should avoid sinful speech, and always keep free from deceit.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The sutra requires truthful, guileless speech free of deceit from the monk.
Of the Kural's four qualities for the envoy who repeats his king's words (purity, support, boldness, truthfulness), the Jain verse supports the truthfulness and freedom from deceit.
Sikhism
After the battle of Chamkaur (1704), Guru Gobind Singh would not trust Mughal couriers with his Zafarnama, a letter rebuking Aurangzeb for breaking his oath of safe passage, so he sent his own Sikhs, Bhai Daya Singh and Bhai Dharam Singh, to carry it to the emperor personally.
— Wikipedia, Zafarnama (letter), citing Isher Singh Nara and Louis E. Fenech [10]
The Guru chose envoys whose loyalty and courage he could rely on to deliver a bold, unwelcome message exactly as written, into the hands of a hostile emperor.
The Kural's envoy who carries his master's message unchanged must be incorruptible, bold and truthful; Daya Singh and Dharam Singh were chosen precisely because they could be trusted to deliver the Guru's words intact.
Greek Mythology
Answering the embassy, Achilles says he hates like the gates of Hades the man who hides one thing in his heart and says another, and that he will therefore speak plainly.
— Homer, Iliad 9.308-313 [11]
Achilles demands transparent truthfulness in the envoys’ exchange and practises it himself.
His ideal is the Kural’s vāymai, truthfulness joined to boldness.
Philosophy
Harold Nicolson named truthfulness the first quality of the ideal diplomat, followed by precision, calm, good temper, patience, modesty and loyalty. He argued that a negotiator’s lasting credit rests on never deceiving.
— Harold Nicolson, Diplomacy (1939), ch. 5 [12]
A twentieth-century diplomat’s classic study puts moral reliability at the centre of the envoy’s craft.
Nicolson’s truthfulness and loyalty match the Kural’s vāymai and tūymai.

Real Life Example

American envoys refuse the bribe demanded in the XYZ Affair
1797 to 1798 · Paris, France
President John Adams sent Charles Cotesworth Pinckney, John Marshall and Elbridge Gerry to France to restore relations and protect American shipping. Intermediaries for the French foreign minister Talleyrand told them that negotiations would require a large loan to France and a personal bribe for Talleyrand. The envoys refused, Pinckney and Marshall prepared to leave, and when their dispatches were published in 1798 with the French agents' names replaced by the letters X, Y and Z, the demand caused a public outcry.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
The envoys would not let money bend their mission and reported exactly what had happened, even though it meant failing to reach an agreement.
The kural asks of the envoy who carries his master's message a purity that bribes cannot touch, boldness and truthfulness, all of which the American commissioners showed in Paris.
