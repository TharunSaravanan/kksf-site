Kural 136

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

ஒழுக்கத்தின் ஒல்கார் உரவோர் இழுக்கத்தின் ஏதம் படுபாக் கறிந்து.
Ozhukkaththin olgaar uravoar izhukkaththin Edham padupaak karindhu.

Meaning

Strong-minded people never slip in their behaviour, because they know the trouble a slip brings.

Elementary

A smart bike rider always wears a helmet, not because they are scared, but because they know how badly a fall can hurt. Strong people keep behaving well for the same reason: they know what happens when you don't.

University

Uravor, ‘the strong’, refers to strength of mind rather than of body (uram means firmness or resolve). Their steadiness is not naive. It rests on knowing (arindhu) the edham (fault, harm, calamity) that falls on those who lapse. That makes the couplet an argument about moral knowledge: firmness comes from seeing consequences clearly. Olkaar (‘they do not bend or slacken’) suggests constant, everyday vigilance rather than heroic resistance to one temptation. Pappaiah adds that they hold on ‘even when it is hard’ and reads the harm as loss of standing. Many traditions picture true strength as self-mastery: Ben Zoma's ‘Who is mighty?’, the Buddhist rock unshaken by the storm, and Odysseus binding himself because he knows what the Sirens do.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Yama teaches Nachiketas that the good (śreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both come to a person. The wise one examines them and chooses the good, while the fool, out of greed, chooses the pleasant and falls short of the goal.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1–2 [3]
Wisdom lies in seeing where each path leads and holding firmly to the good one.
Like the Kural's uravor, the wise stay steady because they understand the cost of the other path.
Christianity
“But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:27 (KJV) [4]
Paul says he disciplines himself constantly because he knows he could fall and be disqualified.
This is the same logic as the Kural: steady discipline driven by awareness of the danger of lapsing.
Islam
The Prophet taught that the strong person is not the one who overpowers others in wrestling, but the one who controls himself when angry.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 [5]
Real strength is inner mastery, not physical force.
This fits the Kural's word uravor: strength means holding firm in one's conduct.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma defines the mighty person as the one who masters their own impulses.
This is the same redefinition of the ‘strong’ that the Kural's uravor implies.
Buddhism
“Just as a storm cannot prevail against a rocky mountain, so Mara can never overpower the man who lives meditating on the impurities, who is controlled in his senses, moderate in eating, and filled with faith and earnest effort.”
— Dhammapada 8 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The disciplined person is compared to a mountain that no storm can move.
Both picture the firm person who never gives way.
Jainism
“Though others sleep, be thou awake! Like a wise man, trust nobody, but be always on the alert; for dangerous is the time and weak the body. Be always watchful like a Bhâruṇḍa bird!”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Mahāvīra urges constant vigilance (apramāda), because the monk knows how dangerous time is and how easily one falls.
Valluvar's strong-minded never slacken in conduct because they know the harm a lapse brings; the Jain call to unsleeping watchfulness rests on the same clear sight of danger.
Sikhism
“Those who remain awake and aware are saved, while those who sleep are plundered.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 34, Sri Raag, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das teaches that only constant alertness against the vices keeps one's inner wealth from being stolen.
The Kural's strong-minded never slacken because they know the harm a lapse brings; Gurbani likewise prizes unbroken vigilance and warns that the careless are robbed.
Greek Mythology
Warned by Circe that the Sirens' song lures sailors to their death, Odysseus has his crew plug their ears and has himself bound to the mast so that he cannot give in.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.37–200 [10]
Knowing the danger in advance, Odysseus arranges things so that he cannot slip.
This mirrors the Kural: firmness grounded in knowing exactly what harm a lapse would bring.
Philosophy
In Plato's image of the soul as a charioteer with two horses, the charioteer must keep a firm grip on the unruly dark horse, which always pulls toward what is shameful, if the soul is not to be dragged down.
— Plato, Phaedrus 253c–254e [11]
Reason must hold desire steady at every moment, or the whole soul falls.
The Kural's uravor are like the firm charioteer who never loosens the reins.

Real Life Example

Jackie Robinson promises Branch Rickey he will not retaliate
28 August 1945 (meeting); 1947 (major league debut) · Brooklyn, New York, United States
At a meeting in 1945, Brooklyn Dodgers executive Branch Rickey spent hours acting out the racial hostility Jackie Robinson would face as the first Black player in modern major league baseball, and told him he wanted a player with the courage not to fight back. Robinson, who by his own later account had believed in retaliation since childhood, promised that there would be no incident. When he joined the Dodgers in 1947 he endured abuse without striking back, and he went on to become one of the game's stars.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Robinson's self-control was a deliberate strategy: both men understood that a single angry incident could be used to halt the integration of the sport.
The kural describes the strong-minded who never slacken because they know the harm a lapse would bring; Robinson held firm precisely because he understood what one lapse would cost.
