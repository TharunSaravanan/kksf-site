Kural 1031

Agriculture · Uzhavu · உழவு

சுழன்றும்ஏர்ப் பின்னது உலகம் அதனால் உழந்தும் உழவே தலை.
Suzhandrum-Erp pinnadhu ulagam adhanaal uzhandhum uzhavae thalai.

Meaning

The world turns to many kinds of work, but it still has to follow the plough. So even though it is hard, farming is the best of all jobs.

Elementary

People can be bakers, builders, drivers or doctors, but every one of them still needs lunch, and lunch starts in a farmer's field. Farming is hard, sweaty work, yet Valluvar says it is the most important job of all, because every other job leans on it.

University

The chapter opens with its thesis: suzhandrum (சுழன்றும், 'though it whirls about', i.e. though people turn to many other callings) the world is Erp pinnadhu (ஏர்ப் பின்னது, 'behind the plough'), so uzhandhum (உழந்தும், 'even though it means toil') uzhavu is thalai (தலை, 'head, chief'). Puliyur Kesikan, in the commentarial line of Parimelazhagar, reads suzhandrum as people roaming through other trades because they see the hardship of farming, and the second line as a concession: farming is best despite that hardship; Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar agree. Uzhavu (ch. 104) stands in the Kudiyiyal section of Porutpal, between Kudiseyalvagai (ch. 103) and Nalkuravu (ch. 105, poverty), so Valluvar presents productive labour as the household's answer to want. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“All living bodies subsist on food grains, which are produced from rains.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda, vedabase.io) [3]
Krishna places food grain at the base of the whole chain of life, ritual and duty.
If every body lives on grain, the world really does stand behind whoever grows it.
Christianity
“cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; … In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground”
— Genesis 3:17-19 (KJV) [4]
After the fall, Adam is told that human bread will come only from hard toil on the soil.
Genesis, like the Kural, makes painful tillage the condition every human life rests on.
Islam
“And have you seen that [seed] which you sow? Is it you who makes it grow, or are We the grower?”
— Qur'an 56:63-64 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an points to sowing as a sign that all human sustenance depends on growth people do not control.
Both texts treat sowing and harvest as what all human life hangs on, though the Qur'an credits God where the Kural praises the farmer.
Judaism
“All craftsmen are destined to stand upon and work the land”
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 63a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar reads Ezekiel 27:29 to mean that every craftsman will end up working the land.
This comes close to the Kural's picture of a world that wanders through trades and still ends up behind the plough.
Buddhism
“by whatsoever activity a householder earns his living, whether by farming, by trading, by rearing cattle, by archery, by service under the king, or by any other kind of craft — at that he becomes skillful and is not lazy.”
— Dighajanu (Vyagghapajja) Sutta, AN 8.54 (tr. Narada Thera, accesstoinsight.org) [7]
The Buddha lists farming first among the livelihoods in which a householder must be skilled and tireless.
Farming heads the Buddha's list, much as the Kural ranks it first, though he does not argue that it is supreme.
Jainism
Jain tradition holds that the first Tirthankara, Rishabhanatha, taught people the means of livelihood at the start of the present age, before he renounced the world; later Jain writers name these as asi (arms), masi (writing) and krishi (agriculture).
— Life of Rishabha, Kalpa Sūtra (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) and later Digambara purāṇa tradition (asi-masi-kṛṣi) [8]
In the Jain account, settled human life begins when Rishabha teaches cultivation, among other arts, to a humanity that can no longer live from wish-fulfilling trees.
In this account agriculture is one of the founding arts of civilised life, which fits the Kural's claim that all society trails behind the plough.
Sikhism
“The True Lord Himself knows all; He makes no mistakes. He is the Great Farmer of the Universe.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 19, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sri Raag, Guru Nanak reaches for the farmer as his image for God Himself: the Creator is the wise cultivator who never errs in sowing and reaping. The verse treats farming as the work most worthy to describe God's own care for the world.
The Kural ranks ploughing as the chief of all callings that the whole world finally depends on; Guru Nanak gives the farmer the highest honour possible by naming God the Great Farmer.
Greek Mythology
“For the gods keep hidden from men the means of life. Else you would easily do work enough in a day to supply you for a full year even without working”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 42-46 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, 1914, sacred-texts.com) [10]
Hesiod explains, through the Prometheus myth, why people must labour on the land for their bread.
Hesiod also makes hard farm work an unavoidable law of human life.
Philosophy
“It was an excellent saying of his who named husbandry ‘the mother and nurse of all the arts,’ … where the land is forced to remain desert, the spring that feeds the other arts is dried up”
— Xenophon, Oeconomicus 5.17 (tr. H.G. Dakyns, Project Gutenberg) [11]
Xenophon's Socrates calls agriculture the source that keeps every other art alive.
This is almost exactly the Kural's claim that all trades follow the plough.

Real Life Example

Thailand revives the Royal Ploughing Ceremony
1960 · Sanam Luang, Bangkok, Thailand
Thailand's Royal Ploughing Ceremony, a rite of royal origin that marks the start of the rice-growing season, was suspended after 1936. The Cabinet revived it in 1960, King Bhumibol Adulyadej presided over it every year of his reign, and since 1966 the ploughing day has also been observed as National Farmers' Day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A modern state chose to honour the plough each year with its highest ceremony, at the head of the nation's calendar.
The kural says that however the world turns to other callings, it still follows behind the plough, and Thailand's crown and government still walk ceremonially behind it.
