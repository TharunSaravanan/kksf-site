Kural 167

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அவ்வித்து அழுக்காறு உடையானைச் செய்யவள் தவ்வையைக் காட்டி விடும்.
Avvithu azhukkaaru udaiyaanaich cheyyaval Thavvaiyaik kaatti vidum.

Meaning

The goddess of good luck turns away from the jealous person and points them out to her older sister, the goddess of bad luck.

Elementary

Imagine Good Luck as a friendly person who visits your house. When she sees you're always jealous, she leaves, and on her way out she tells her grumpy sister, Bad Luck, “This house is yours now.”

University

Valluvar here uses a mythological image unusual for a text that mostly avoids sectarian deities. செய்யவள் (“the red/fair one”) is Lakshmi (Tiru), and தவ்வை (“elder sister”) is Mudevi/Jyeshtha, goddess of misfortune, who in the Padma and Linga Puranas rises from the churned ocean before Lakshmi and is told to dwell among the quarrelsome and wicked. அவ்வித்து is read by Parimelazhagar as “being herself envious [of him],” so the goddess answers envy with envy, a measure-for-measure punishment. The image turns an abstract claim, that envy drives away prosperity, into a vivid scene, a technique Valluvar uses sparingly and to strong effect.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Jyeshtha (Mudevi), elder sister and opposite of Lakshmi, emerges from the churning of the ocean before Lakshmi; in the Padma and Linga Puranas she is told to dwell in homes of quarrel, harsh speech, and wicked people.
— Padma Purana and Linga Purana, as summarized in the Wikipedia article on Jyestha (citing Leslie 1992 and Kinsley 1997) [3]
Hindu myth pairs prosperity (Lakshmi) with her elder sister misfortune (Jyeshtha), who takes over where vice lives.
The Kural uses this exact pair: Lakshmi leaves the envious man and hands him over to her elder sister.
Christianity
“He that hasteth to be rich hath an evil eye, and considereth not that poverty shall come upon him.”
— Proverbs 28:22 (KJV) [4]
The proverb warns that the grasping, “evil-eyed” person does not see that poverty is coming for him.
Like the Kural, it says the envious “evil eye” drives prosperity away and invites poverty.
Islam
“Iblis refuses to bow to Adam, saying “I am better than him,” and is told: “Descend from it [i.e., Paradise]... So get out; indeed, you are of the debased.”
— Qur'an 7:12-13 (Saheeh International) [5]
Iblis resents the honor given to Adam and is expelled from the place of favor.
As Lakshmi abandons the envious man, divine favor is withdrawn from Iblis because he resented another's elevation.
Judaism
“And Saul eyed David from that day and forward.” ... “And Saul was afraid of David, because the LORD was with him, and was departed from Saul.”
— 1 Samuel 18:9, 12 (KJV) [4]
After hearing David praised above him, Saul watches him with an envious eye, and the text notes that God's presence has left Saul and gone to David.
As in the Kural, the envious man is abandoned by divine favor, which passes to the one he envies.
Buddhism
“There is the case where a woman or man is envious. He/she envies, begrudges, & broods about others' gains, honor, respect, reverence, salutations, & veneration. ... If instead he/she comes to the human state, then he/she is not influential wherever reborn.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 135 (Cula-kammavibhanga Sutta), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [6]
The Buddha teaches that envy leads to rebirth in bad destinations or, if reborn human, to a life without influence or standing.
Envy drives good fortune away, which is the Kural's point in karmic rather than mythological terms.
Jainism
When the envious god Saṅgamaka returned to heaven after failing to shake Mahāvīra, Śakra at once turned his back on him and declared him unfit to be seen; Saṅgamaka, his beauty blurred like a mirror sprinkled with water, was expelled from the Saudharma heaven.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X, ch. IV, Part 4 “Attacks by Saṅgamaka” (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS) [7]
In Hemacandra's telling the envious god loses his splendour and his place among the gods, and the king of gods publicly turns away from him and casts him out.
Valluvar pictures Fortune turning from the envious man and pointing him out to Misfortune, and the nearest Jain image is Śakra turning his back on the envious Saṅgamaka and driving him from heaven's prosperity.
Sikhism
“All the nine treasures, and the eighteen supernatural powers are held by our Lord and Master in the Palm of His Hand.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 10, Rehras (So Dar section), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan teaches that all wealth and power rest in the hand of the one God, not with any separate deity of fortune.
The Kural pictures the goddess of fortune leaving the envious man for her sister Misfortune; Sikh teaching sets aside such goddesses and places all fortune in the One Lord's hand alone.
Greek Mythology
“In Hesiod's iron age, “Envy, foul-mouthed, delighting in evil, with scowling face, will go along with wretched men one and all. And then Aidos and Nemesis ... will go from the wide-pathed earth.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 195-200 (trans. Evelyn-White) [9]
When Envy becomes people's companion, the goddesses of shame and righteous retribution leave the earth.
As Lakshmi leaves the envious man, Hesiod's good goddesses abandon a humanity that keeps company with Envy.
Philosophy
“For jealousy has no place in the celestial choir.”
— Plato, Phaedrus 247a (trans. Jowett) [10]
In Socrates' myth of the soul's journey, the gods move freely through heaven and anyone able may follow, because envy has no place among the divine.
Plato puts envy outside the divine company; the Kural shows the goddess of fortune leaving the envious.

Real Life Example

Li Si brings down his gifted schoolmate Han Fei
233 BCE and 208 BCE · Qin state (Xianyang), China
Han Fei, a brilliant Legalist thinker whose writings the King of Qin admired, came to the Qin court on a diplomatic mission in 233 BCE. According to Britannica, Li Si, the chief minister and Han Fei's former schoolmate, apparently fearing that Han Fei might win the king's favour with his superior learning, had him imprisoned, and Han Fei died in prison. Li Si rose to great power under the First Emperor, but after the emperor's death he fell out with the eunuch Zhao Gao, who had him executed in 208 BCE.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Li Si removed a more gifted rival and reached the summit of power, yet his fortune did not last and he ended in ruin.
The kural pictures fortune deserting the envious and handing him over to misfortune, and Li Si's high fortune ended in disgrace and death.
