Kural 223

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

இலனென்னும் எவ்வம் உரையாமை ஈதல் குலனுடையான் கண்ணே யுள.
Ilan ennum evvam uraiyaamai eedhal Kulanudaiyaan kanne ula.

Meaning

Not saying the sad words “I have nothing,” and giving anyway, is found only in a person from a truly good family.

Elementary

When someone asks for help, it is easy to say, “Sorry, I don’t have anything.” A truly good person doesn’t hide behind that excuse. Even when they have only a little, they find something to share.

University

The couplet turns on the phrase இலன் என்னும் எவ்வம், ‘the misery of saying “I have not”’. Commentators divide on whose words these are: Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasan read it as giving before the needy person has to humiliate himself by declaring his poverty, while others (e.g. Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar) read it as the giver not pleading poverty himself even when he is in straitened circumstances. Both readings put the burden of dignity on the giver: generosity is shown most clearly when one’s means are small or the asker’s shame can be spared. குலன் (kulan), usually glossed ‘noble birth’, is best understood as nobility of character and upbringing, in line with the Kural’s repeated view that conduct, not birth, defines a person (cf. Kural 972).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata’s Ashvamedhika Parva, a starving Brahmin family who live by gleaning grain give their only meal of barley flour to a hungry guest, one after another, rather than turn him away. A mongoose that rolls in the spilled flour finds half its body turned to gold, and later declares that Yudhishthira’s grand horse-sacrifice was not equal to this poor family’s gift.
— Mahabharata, Ashvamedhika Parva (Anugita section), story of the mongoose and the Brahmin’s flour [3]
The story teaches that a small gift given from poverty outweighs lavish gifts given from abundance.
Exactly the Kural’s ideal: a household with almost nothing does not plead poverty, but gives.
Christianity
“Verily I say unto you, That this poor widow hath cast more in, than all they which have cast into the treasury: For all they did cast in of their abundance; but she of her want did cast in all that she had, even all her living.”
— Mark 12:43-44 (KJV) [4]
Jesus praises a poor widow whose two small coins, given out of her poverty, were worth more than the large gifts of the rich.
Like the Kural, it honours the person who gives despite having little, instead of pleading poverty.
Islam
“They love those who emigrated to them and find not any want in their breasts of what they [i.e., the emigrants] were given but give [them] preference over themselves, even though they are in privation. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul - it is those who will be the successful.”
— Qur'an 59:9 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises the Ansar of Medina, who shared with the refugees from Mecca and put them first even while poor themselves.
Same ideal of giving from one’s own want rather than pleading it.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother: But thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need.”
— Deuteronomy 15:7-8 (KJV) [4]
The Torah commands that one must not close one’s hand to a poor person but give generously for his need.
Shares the Kural’s refusal to turn away the asker; the ‘shut hand’ corresponds to the evasive ‘I have nothing’.
Buddhism
“Speak the truth; yield not to anger; when asked, give even if you only have a little. By these three means can one reach the presence of the gods.”
— Dhammapada 224 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada tells people to give when asked even if they have only a little.
Directly parallels the Kural: when someone asks, don’t say ‘I have nothing’; give from the little you have.
Jainism
“Not saying they have very little, not saying they have not anything, let them ever exercise fruitful charity to all. Like the pitcher of the mendicant who enters the house-door for alms, it will, in due course, gradually become full.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 9 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaṭiyār tells people never to excuse themselves with 'I have little' or 'I have nothing', and to keep giving. Small gifts add up, it says, the way a mendicant's pitcher slowly fills.
Valluvar finds nobility in giving without first uttering the miserable words 'I have nothing', and this stanza turns down exactly that excuse.
Sikhism
“His Blessings are so abundant that there can be no written account of them. The Great Giver does not hold back anything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib, Pauri 25, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Japji's twenty-fifth pauri praises God as the Great Giver whose gifts are beyond counting and who withholds nothing.
The Kural praises the noble person who never says 'I have nothing' and gives anyway; Guru Nanak presents the ideal giver as one who holds nothing back.
Greek Mythology
In Odyssey Book 14, the swineherd Eumaeus, a poor servant, welcomes the disguised Odysseus as a ragged stranger, kills two of his own pigs to feed him, and tells him that strangers and beggars come from Zeus and that even a small gift is welcome.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 14 [9]
A man of humble means makes no excuse of poverty and shares what he has with a beggar.
Eumaeus shows the Kural’s nobility of character: giving generously from little, without pleading want.
Philosophy
“I can find nothing to give you which is worthy of you; I feel my poverty in this respect alone. Therefore I present you with the only thing I possess, myself.”
— Seneca, On Benefits I.8 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [10]
Seneca tells how Aeschines, a poor pupil of Socrates, gave himself when he had no money, and how Socrates valued this above the rich pupils’ gifts.
Like the Kural, it praises the person who does not use poverty as an excuse but finds something to give.

Real Life Example

The Maasai of Enoosaen give fourteen cows to the United States
2 June 2002 · Enoosaen, Kenya
After the September 11 attacks, Kimeli Naiyomah, a Maasai student who had been in New York, asked the elders of his village in western Kenya to let him give a cow to the American people, and the community responded with fourteen cattle from the small herds of local herdsmen. At a ceremony on 2 June 2002 the cows were blessed and presented to the deputy chief of mission of the U.S. Embassy in Nairobi. The cattle remained in Kenya in the care of Maasai herdsmen.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A village with little material wealth made one of its most valued possessions into a gift of sympathy for the richest country in the world.
The kural praises those who do not plead that they have nothing but give anyway, as the herdsmen of Enoosaen did from their own small stocks.
