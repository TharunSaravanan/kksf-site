Kural 151

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

அகழ்வாரைத் தாங்கும் நிலம்போலத் தம்மை இகழ்வார்ப் பொறுத்தல் தலை.
Agazhvaaraith thaangum nilampoalath thammai, Igazhvaarp poruththal thalai.

Meaning

The ground holds up even the people who dig into it. In the same way, the best thing you can do is calmly put up with people who insult you and look down on you.

Elementary

Think about the ground under your feet. People dig holes in it, stomp on it and poke it with shovels, and it still holds everybody up without complaining. This kural says the best kind of person is like that ground: when someone is mean to you, you stay steady and kind instead of hitting back.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with one of the most famous images in Tamil ethics: the earth (நிலம்) that supports the very people who dig into it (அகழ்வார்). The two verbs are set side by side on purpose. அகழ் (to dig, to wound the ground) matches இகழ் (to scorn, to wound with words), so an insult is pictured as digging into a person, and the patient person takes it in the way soil does, without caving in. Commentators such as Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah point out that the earth doesn't just put up with the digger; it keeps holding him up so he doesn't fall, which makes endurance an active kind of support. The last word, தலை ('the foremost'), puts forbearance at the head of the virtues and sets up the thesis the rest of the chapter develops. Other traditions reach for the same metaphor: the Sanskrit kṣamā means both 'earth' and 'forgiveness,' and the Buddha compares abusive speech to a man trying to dig away the whole earth with a hoe.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And because it is seen that there are in the world men who are forgiving like the Earth, it is therefore that creatures derive their life and prosperity.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section XXIX (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
In exile, Draupadi urges Yudhishthira to take revenge on the Kauravas. He refuses, arguing that the world survives only because some people forgive the way the earth does.
This is the kural's own image: forbearance modeled on the ground, which endures injury and keeps supporting others.
Christianity
“But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.”
— Matthew 5:39 (KJV) [5]
In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus tells his followers not to strike back when they are hit, and to take the next blow instead of returning it.
Like the earth holding up the one who digs it, the disciple takes the injury without hitting back.
Islam
“And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily, and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace,”
— Qur'an 25:63 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an describes God's true servants as people who walk gently on the earth and answer rude or ignorant speech with peace instead of insults.
Both texts link a humble relationship with the earth to a calm, non-retaliating answer when someone insults you.
Judaism
“Now the man Moses was very meek, above all the men that were upon the face of the earth.”
— Numbers 12:3 (JPS 1917) [7]
Miriam and Aaron have just spoken against Moses (Numbers 12:1-2). He doesn't defend himself, and the Torah praises his meekness above everyone on earth.
Moses quietly bears the people who belittle him, which is the kural's ideal of putting up with those who scorn us.
Buddhism
“Suppose that a man were to come along carrying a hoe & a basket, saying, ‘I will make this great earth be without earth.’”
— Kakacūpama Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya 21 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha compares abusive speech aimed at a trained mind to a man trying to dig away the whole earth with a hoe. He only wears himself out. Monks are told to keep goodwill 'equal to the great earth' whatever others say to them.
The image is almost the same as the kural's: a digger with a tool against an earth that can't be used up, standing for a patient mind under insult.
Jainism
“…steady and firm like Mount Mandara, deep like the ocean, mild like the moon, refulgent like the sun, pure like excellent gold; like the earth he patiently bore everything; like a well-kindled fire he shone in his splendour.”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Mahāvīra § 118 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Kalpa Sūtra describes Mahāvīra during his years as a wandering ascetic with a string of similes, and patient endurance is given the image of the earth, which bears everything.
Valluvar opens his chapter on patience with the earth that holds up even those who dig into it, and Jain scripture uses the same image, 'like the earth he patiently bore everything', for Mahāvīra's forbearance.
Sikhism
“The God-conscious being has a steady patience, like the earth, which is dug up by one, and anointed with sandal paste by another.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 272, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 8, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan compares the patience of the God-conscious person to the earth, which stays the same whether it is dug into or honoured with sandalwood.
The Kural's image is the earth that keeps holding up those who dig into it; Guru Arjan uses the very same image of the dug-up earth for steady forbearance.
Greek Mythology
“Ulysses stood firm as a rock and the blow did not even stagger him”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book XVII (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Odysseus comes home disguised as a beggar, and the suitor Antinous throws a footstool at him. He doesn't flinch or strike back, although Homer adds that he silently thought about revenge.
Odysseus stands like rock or earth under the insult and blow, the way the kural's ideal person bears whoever wounds him.
Philosophy
“Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.49 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius tells himself to be like a headland that the waves keep hitting. It doesn't move, and in the end it calms the water around it.
The Stoic uses a landform as his model of patience, just as Valluvar uses the earth.

Real Life Example

Gandhi declines to prosecute the Durban mob that assaulted him
January 1897 · Durban, Natal (now South Africa)
When Mohandas Gandhi, then a lawyer campaigning for the rights of Indians in Natal, landed at Durban in January 1897, a white mob inflamed by distorted reports of his speeches in India assaulted him and nearly lynched him. The British Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, cabled the Natal government asking it to prosecute the attackers, and the colony's Attorney General offered to act if Gandhi would identify them. Gandhi declined, saying the attackers had been misled and that it was his principle not to seek redress of a personal wrong in a court of law.
— Sources: [13] [14]
By refusing to retaliate through the courts, Gandhi turned public opinion: by his own account the local press declared him innocent and condemned the mob.
The kural pictures the earth holding up even those who dig into it; Gandhi went on supporting the community that had attacked him rather than striking back at those who wounded him.
