Kural 933

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

உருளாயம் ஓவாது கூறின் பொருளாயம் போஒய்ப் புறமே படும்.
Urulaayam oovaadhu koorin porulaayam pooip purame padum.

Meaning

If a person keeps betting on the rolling dice without stopping, their money and everything it brings in will leave them and go to others.

Elementary

A king who spends all day shouting bets at a dice board has no time left to look after his kingdom. Little by little, his treasure and his lands end up in other people's hands.

University

The couplet plays two compounds against each other: urulaayam (உருளாயம், 'gain from what rolls', i.e. the die, கவறு) and porulaayam (பொருளாயம், 'wealth and its revenue'). Oovaadhu koorin (ஓவாது கூறின்) means 'if one ceaselessly calls', which Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar take as calling out the stake, while Solomon Pappaiah reads it as boasting of one's winnings. Pooip (போஒய், alapedai of போய்) purame padum says the wealth 'goes and lands outside', which Mu. Varadarasanar and Kesikan gloss as 'reaches the enemies' (பகைவர்). Pope's English ('If prince unceasing speak of nought but play') and his prose gloss follow the traditional Parimelazhagar reading, which takes the subject to be a king, in keeping with the chapter's place in Porutpaal. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Sabha Parva of the Mahabharata, Yudhishthira plays dice against Shakuni, staking and losing his treasury, his kingdom and finally his brothers to Duryodhana.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (dice-game episode), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
A righteous king who could not stop calling out stakes handed his whole realm to his rivals in one sitting.
This is exactly the Kural's picture of a king's wealth and revenue passing to his enemies.
Christianity
“And not many days after the younger son gathered all together, and took his journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living.”
— Luke 15:13 (KJV) [4]
In the parable, the prodigal son quickly squanders the property his father gave him.
Like the Kural's gambler, he lets his wealth drain away into others' hands.
Islam
“O you who have believed, indeed, intoxicants, gambling, [sacrificing on] stone alters [to other than Allah], and divining arrows are but defilement from the work of Satan, so avoid it that you may be successful.”
— Qur'an 5:90 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an commands believers to avoid gambling so that they can prosper.
The Kural shows the reverse case: a ruler who will not avoid it loses his wealth.
Judaism
“...those who play dice are disqualified because they are not involved in settling the world, i.e., in productive occupations that demand hard work.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 24b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
Rav Sheshet says the habitual dice-player is not trusted because he contributes nothing to the productive life of society.
The Kural's player who does nothing but call stakes earns nothing, so his income drains away.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who brings ruin be understood as a foe in the guise of a friend: ... (iv) he is a companion in indulging in gambling which causes heedlessness.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), trans. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha warns that a gambling companion is really an enemy dressed up as a friend.
The Kural's gambler likewise ends up handing his wealth to the very people across the board from him.
Jainism
“After a man has done acts which cause the death of living beings, his pleasure-seeking relations take possession of his wealth, whilst the doer of the acts must suffer for them.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.9.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga warns that the wealth a person gathers by sinful acts passes into other hands, while he alone bears the karmic consequences.
Valluvar says the man who keeps calling out stakes on the rolling dice will see his wealth and income go and fall to others; the Jain text likewise shows wealth slipping to others while the doer is left with the loss.
Sikhism
“Like the gambler losing the game of chance, you are held in the power of sensory desires.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 212, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan tells the soul that it is losing its life the way a gambler loses at play, because the senses have got hold of it. The image is of someone who keeps playing and keeps losing.
The Kural warns that the one who keeps calling stakes on the rolling dice will see his wealth and income pass to others, and Guru Arjan’s losing gambler is that same figure.
Greek Mythology
“The Athenians from the city had just finished their midday meal, after which they had betaken themselves, some to dice, others to sleep, when Pisistratus with his troops fell upon them and put them to the rout.”
— Herodotus, Histories 1.63 (trans. George Rawlinson) [10]
Herodotus tells how Athens fell to Pisistratus while its defenders were busy at dice and napping.
A city lost to a rival while its men played dice is the Kural's warning on a political scale.
Philosophy
“The king is surrounded by persons whose only thought is to divert the king, and to prevent his thinking of self.”
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées §139 (trans. W. F. Trotter) [11]
Pascal notes that kings are kept in constant play and amusement so they never stop to reflect.
The Kural's king, endlessly calling dice, is the diverted ruler who loses track of his realm.

Real Life Example

The Louisiana Lottery drains money from across the United States until Congress closes the mails to it
1868-1893 (federal anti-lottery law, September 1890) · New Orleans, Louisiana, United States
The Louisiana State Lottery Company, a private corporation chartered in 1868, sold tickets in almost every state, drawing about 90 percent of its sales from outside Louisiana and paying its stockholders dividends of well over 100 percent a year between 1887 and 1890. Other states, unhappy that their citizens' money was flowing to the company, pressed President Benjamin Harrison to act, and in September 1890 he signed a law banning lottery tickets and advertisements from the mail; the Postmaster General soon reported that business at the company's New Orleans office had fallen off by a third. Louisiana refused to renew the company's charter, which expired at the end of 1893.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Millions of small stakes from players across the country became extraordinary profits for a handful of shareholders, which is why so many states fought to shut the lottery down.
The kural says that the wealth of one who keeps calling stakes on the rolling dice leaves him and falls into the hands of others, and the Louisiana Lottery's ticket buyers steadily enriched its owners.
