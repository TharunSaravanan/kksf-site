Kural 129

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

தீயினாற் சுட்டபுண் உள்ளாறும் ஆறாதே நாவினாற் சுட்ட வடு.
Theeyinaar suttapun ullaarum aaraadhe Naavinaar sutta vadu.

Meaning

A burn from fire heals inside, but a wound made by the tongue never heals.

Elementary

If you touch a hot stove, it hurts a lot, but in a week or two it heals. If someone says something really hurtful to you, you might remember it for years. Words can burn deeper than fire.

University

Parimelazhagar notes Valluvar’s careful word choice: the fire-burn is called pun (a wound, which heals), while the tongue-burn is called vadu (a scar, which stays). He classifies the couplet as vetrumai alankaram, a figure of contrast: fire and harsh words share the act of burning, but because harsh words never heal they are shown to be worse than fire. The phrase ul aarum (‘heals within’) concedes that a fire-burn may leave an outward mark while the inner hurt fades; with verbal wounds the reverse holds. This is among the most widely quoted of all kurals, and its near-twin in the Mahabharata’s counsel of Vidura shows how old and widespread the insight is. Modern research on the lasting psychological effects of verbal abuse gives it contemporary resonance.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A forest pierced by arrows, or cut down by hatchets may again grow, but one’s heart wounded and censured by ill-spoken words never recovereth.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 34 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura tells Dhritarashtra that physical damage recovers but hearts wounded by harsh words never do.
The same structure as the Kural: physical harm heals, word-wounds do not.
Christianity
“And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity: so is the tongue among our members, that it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell.”
— James 3:6 (KJV) [5]
James calls the tongue a fire that sets a person’s whole life ablaze.
Both texts compare the tongue to fire, and both say its burning is worse than ordinary fire.
Islam
“Woe to every scorner and mocker … It is the fire of Allah, [eternally] fueled, Which mounts directed at the hearts.”
— Qur’an 104:1, 6–7 (Sahih International) [6]
Surah al-Humazah condemns the backbiter and mocker to a fire that reaches right into the heart.
The link is fire and hearts: in the Kural wounding words burn the victim’s heart, and in the Qur’an the mocker is repaid with a fire that reaches his own.
Judaism
“There is that speaketh like the piercings of a sword; but the tongue of the wise is health.”
— Proverbs 12:18 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs compares careless speech to sword-thrusts; the Talmud (Bava Metzia 58b) goes further, likening public humiliation to shedding blood.
Both texts treat words as weapons that can wound as deeply as physical violence, or more.
Buddhism
“Speak not harshly to anyone, for those thus spoken to might retort. Indeed, angry speech hurts, and retaliation may overtake you.”
— Dhammapada 133 [8]
The Buddha warns that harsh speech wounds and brings painful consequences back on the speaker.
Both texts insist that harsh words really wound, so they must be held back.
Jainism
“One can bear all kinds of unbearable pain caused by spikes in expectation of wealth etc. But he alone who tolerates, without any motive of worldly gain, harsh words spoken to him is venerable.”
— Daśavaikālika Sūtra 9.3.6, English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [9]
The Daśavaikālika sets harsh words beside iron spikes and treats bearing harsh words as the harder test; the next verse in the same lesson (9.3.7) goes on to contrast iron thorns, which are pulled out, with thorns of speech that stay and breed enmity.
Like Valluvar's fire-burn that heals and tongue-burn that never does, the Jain text ranks the wound of harsh words above physical injury.
Sikhism
“Sweetness of honey cannot match the sweetness of sweetly spoken words. No poison is as discomforting as bitter words.”
— Kabit Savaiye Bhai Gurdas, Kabit 256 (English translation as given in the BaniDB database used by searchgurbani.com) [10]
Bhai Gurdas compares bitter words to poison and sweet words to honey; the next line of the same Kabit sets the coolness of sweet words against the burning heat (tapat) of harsh ones.
The Kural says the scar of a burning tongue never heals; Bhai Gurdas likewise ranks the injury of harsh words above any physical poison.
Greek Mythology
Agamemnon publicly insults Achilles and takes his prize, Briseis. The dishonour sparks Achilles’ wrath, which lasts through most of the war and costs countless Achaean lives before he is finally reconciled.
— Homer, Iliad 1.101–303 and passim (paraphrase) [11]
The Iliad’s whole plot turns on a wound of words and dishonour that proves harder to heal than any battle wound.
Achilles’ lasting wrath shows the Kural’s point: a verbal and public wound can outlast and outweigh physical injury.
Philosophy
“Anger may be defined as an impulse, accompanied by pain, to a conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight directed without justification towards what concerns oneself or towards what concerns one’s friends.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.2, 1378a (trans. W. Rhys Roberts) [12]
Aristotle analyses how an unjust slight, often delivered in words, causes real pain and lasting resentment.
Aristotle explains the psychology behind the Kural: slights wound self-worth, which is why their pain lingers.

Real Life Example

Lincoln's unsent letter to General Meade
14 July 1863 · Executive Mansion (White House), Washington, D.C., United States
After the Union victory at Gettysburg, Confederate General Robert E. Lee's army escaped back across the Potomac River, and General George Meade, stung by word of the president's disappointment, asked to be relieved of his command. On 14 July 1863 Abraham Lincoln wrote Meade a letter thanking him for Gettysburg but sharply criticising him for letting Lee escape, calling the lost chance a misfortune that would prolong the war. Lincoln never sent it; he placed the letter in an envelope on which he wrote that it was to Gen. Meade, never sent, or signed.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Lincoln put his anger into words on paper but kept those words from wounding a general he still needed, a habit the American Battlefield Trust describes as one of his favourite coping mechanisms.
The kural says a burn from fire heals but a wound from the tongue never does; by holding back a cutting letter, Lincoln avoided inflicting a wound that could not be undone.
