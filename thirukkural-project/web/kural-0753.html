Kural 753

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

பொருளென்னும் பொய்யா விளக்கம் இருளறுக்கும் எண்ணிய தேயத்துச் சென்று.
Porulennum poyyaa vilakkam irularukkum enniya theyaththuch chendru.

Meaning

Money is a lamp that never fails. Take it to any land you choose, and it will drive away the darkness of trouble there.

Elementary

Think of a torch that never runs out of batteries. Wherever you walk at night, it lights the path. Valluvar says money can be like that: when you go to a new place, it can help you make friends and solve problems.

University

The metaphor pairs poyyaa vilakkam (the lamp that does not fail or lie) with iruL (darkness), and the second line sends the lamp to enniya theyam, ‘the country one has in mind’ (theyam from Sanskrit desha). Parimelazhagar glosses the darkness as enmity (பகையாகிய இருள்), and Pope and Drew’s English follows him with ‘the darkness (of enmity)’; Solomon Pappaiah likewise says wealth enters even foreign lands and dispels the darkness of hostility. Mu. Varadarasanar reads iruL more broadly as any obstacle (இடையூறு), and Kalaignar as suffering (துன்பம்). The verse sets up 759, where wealth becomes the sharpest weapon against a foe’s pride. The sandhi-joined text reads விளக்க மிருளறுக்கு மெண்ணிய, which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthashastra lists dana, giving gifts, among the four means (upayas) a ruler uses with other states, alongside conciliation, sowing dissension and force, so that gifts can turn a hostile neighbour into an ally.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra (four upayas: sama, dana, bheda, danda), trans. R. Shamasastry [3]
Indian statecraft treated well-placed gifts of wealth as a standard way to win over enemies without war.
This is the Kural’s lamp at work: wealth sent into another land to dispel the darkness of enmity.
Christianity
“I say unto you, Make unto yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness; that, when ye fail, they may receive you into the everlasting habitations.”
— Luke 16:9 (KJV), as quoted in John Wesley, Sermon 50 [4]
After the parable of the shrewd steward, Jesus tells his disciples to use worldly wealth to make friends who will welcome them later.
Jesus, like Valluvar, sees wealth as something that turns strangers into friends.
Islam
“Zakah expenditures are only for the poor and for the needy and for those employed to collect [zakah] and for bringing hearts together [for Islam] and for freeing captives [or slaves] and for those in debt and for the cause of Allah and for the [stranded] traveler.”
— Qur’an 9:60 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an sets aside a share of zakat for ‘bringing hearts together’, meaning people whose hostility could be softened and whose goodwill could be won by wealth.
This category of zakat recipients is a direct religious use of wealth to dispel enmity, as the Kural describes.
Judaism
“A gift in secret subdues anger, A present in private, fierce rage.”
— Proverbs 21:14 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that a well-timed private gift calms even fierce anger.
The proverb and the Kural agree that wealth can put out the darkness of hostility.
Buddhism
“Furthermore, with his legitimate wealth he defends himself against threats from such things as fire, flood, rulers, bandits, or unloved heirs. He keeps himself safe.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.61, Pattakamma Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha lists protecting oneself against dangers as the second fitting use of rightly earned wealth.
Like the Kural’s lamp, rightly earned wealth here drives off threats wherever they arise.
Jainism
“Wealth will not protect a careless man in this world and the next. Though he had seen the right way, he does not see it, even as one in the dark whose lamp has suddenly been put out.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's sermon pairs wealth with the image of a lamp in the dark, but warns that wealth gives no lasting protection and that the careless lose their way as when a lamp goes out.
The Kural calls wealth an unfailing lamp that dispels darkness wherever its owner goes; the Jain verse uses the same lamp-and-darkness image to deny that wealth can be relied on, a pointed counter-reading.
Sikhism
“The One Name is my lamp; I have put the oil of suffering into it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 358, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak names the divine Name as the lamp that lights his way, burning away suffering as its oil.
The Kural calls wealth an unfailing lamp that cuts through darkness wherever its owner goes; Guru Nanak's unfailing lamp is the Name, which Gurbani treats as the true wealth.
Greek Mythology
In the myth of Danaë, her father Acrisius locks her in a bronze chamber, but Zeus reaches her as a shower of gold that passes through the roof; Roman moralists such as Horace later read the story as showing that gold gets through every barrier.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.4.1; Horace, Odes 3.16 [10]
Gold enters the one place that was sealed against every visitor.
Like the Kural’s lamp, gold goes wherever it is aimed and gets past whatever stands in the way.
Philosophy
Cicero, writing to Atticus, recalls Philip of Macedon’s saying that any fortress could be taken if only a donkey laden with gold could climb up to it.
— Cicero, Letters to Atticus 1.16.12; also Plutarch, Sayings of Kings [11]
Philip’s point was that gold opens gates that armies cannot.
Philip’s donkey of gold is the Kural’s lamp carried into an enemy’s land to undo its resistance.

Real Life Example

President Truman signs the Marshall Plan into law
3 April 1948 · Washington, D.C., United States, and Western Europe
President Harry Truman signed the Economic Cooperation Act, which created the European Recovery Program known as the Marshall Plan. Over about four years the United States transferred some 13 billion dollars in aid to sixteen Western European countries, including former wartime enemies, to rebuild their economies. Aid was also offered to the Soviet Union and the countries of eastern Europe, which declined to take part.
— Sources: [12] [13]
American wealth, sent abroad, helped rebuild war-shattered economies and turned recent battlefield enemies into partners.
The kural calls wealth an unfailing lamp that dispels the darkness of hostility in any land its owner chooses, and Marshall aid carried that light into a Europe darkened by war.
