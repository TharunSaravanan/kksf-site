Kural 226

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

அற்றார் அழிபசி தீர்த்தல் அஃதொருவன் பெற்றான் பொருள்வைப் புழி.
Atraar azhipasi theerththal agdhoruvan Petraan porul vaippuzhi.

Meaning

Feeding the poor whose hunger is crushing them is the best place for a person with money to store it.

Elementary

People keep money in a piggy bank or a bank to keep it safe. This kural says the safest place to keep your money is in a hungry person’s full stomach. When you feed someone, your wealth becomes goodness that can never be stolen.

University

The couplet takes the image of a treasury (பொருள் வைப்புழி, ‘the place where wealth is deposited’) and relocates it: the proper strongroom for wealth is the relief of அழிபசி, ‘destroying hunger’, suffered by those who have nothing (அற்றார்). The metaphor inverts ordinary prudence, which stores wealth by withholding it, and claims that wealth is truly secured only by giving it away. Commentators read this both as moral merit that follows the giver into future lives and as social security in this life. The same ‘treasury’ inversion appears in Jesus’ ‘treasures in heaven’, in King Munbaz’s reply in the Talmud and in the Buddhist ‘well-stored fund’ of Khp 8, showing a widely shared intuition that generosity is the only wealth immune to loss.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let the rich satisfy the poor implorer, and bend his eye upon a longer pathway. Riches come now to one, now to another, and like the wheels of cars are ever rolling.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.5 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Rig Vedic hymn on Liberality urges the rich to feed the poor and look to the long run, since wealth never stays in one place.
Like the Kural, it treats feeding the poor as the far-sighted use of wealth that cannot be kept anyway.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal.”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts perishable earthly treasure with lasting heavenly treasure.
Same ‘true storehouse’ inversion as the Kural.
Islam
“The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allāh is like a seed [of grain] which grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains. And Allāh multiplies [His reward] for whom He wills.”
— Qur'an 2:261 (Sahih International) [5]
Wealth spent in God’s way is compared to a seed that multiplies many times over.
Like the Kural, giving is presented not as loss but as the best investment of wealth.
Judaism
“My ancestors stored up below, whereas I am storing above... My ancestors stored up treasures in a place where the human hand can reach, and so their treasures could have been robbed, whereas I am storing up treasures in a place where the human hand cannot reach.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 11a (William Davidson/Koren translation) [6]
King Munbaz, criticised for spending his family treasure on the poor during a drought, replies that he is storing it in a safer place.
An almost exact parallel: relieving hunger in famine is the true treasury of wealth.
Buddhism
“But when a man or woman has laid aside a well-stored fund of generosity, virtue, restraint, & self-control... That’s a well-stored fund. It can’t be wrested away. It follows you along.”
— Khuddakapatha 8, Nidhi Kanda (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Reserve Fund discourse compares buried treasure, which can be lost, with the ‘fund’ of generosity, which cannot.
Same idea as the Kural: generosity is where wealth is kept safe.
Jainism
“When by blameless means thou hast acquired great wealth, then eat with others rice imported by oxen, for wealth never remaineth in the centre with anyone, but changes its position like a cart-wheel.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 1 ('Unstable Wealth'), st. 2 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār's chapter on unstable wealth says that wealth, once honestly gained, should be shared at the table, because riches never stay with anyone for long.
Valluvar tells the person who has gained wealth to 'store' it in relieving others' hunger; the Nālaṭiyār gives the same advice for the same reason, that wealth kept to oneself does not last.
Sikhism
According to the Janamsakhi tradition recorded by Macauliffe, Guru Nanak visited Duni Chand, a millionaire of Lahore who flew a flag for every lakh of rupees he owned. The Guru gave him a needle to return in the next world; when Duni Chand realised that not even a needle could go with him, the Guru told him to give some of his wealth in God's name and feed the poor so that it would accompany him, and Duni Chand distributed his treasure.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter XI, pp. 129-130 (paraphrase) [9]
The story teaches that wealth hoarded stays behind at death, while wealth used to feed the poor goes with the giver.
The Kural names relief of the destitute's hunger as the proper place to deposit wealth, and the Guru's counsel to Duni Chand, feed the poor and your wealth shall accompany you, makes exactly that point.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s tale, the poor elderly couple Baucis and Philemon share their meagre meal with two disguised gods whom everyone else in the village had turned away. The gods flood the village but turn the couple’s cottage into a temple and grant their wish to serve there together and to die at the same moment.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 [10]
The couple’s generosity from poverty becomes their lasting reward.
Their small gift of food turns out to be the safest ‘deposit’ they ever made, as the Kural suggests.
Philosophy
“These fine things show their quality better in the spending than in the hoarding... money is only precious when it is given away.”
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy II, Prose 5 (tr. W. V. Cooper) [11]
Lady Philosophy tells Boethius that riches have value only when they are spent or given, not when they are piled up.
Like the Kural, it relocates the value of wealth from keeping it to giving it.

Real Life Example

Quaker soup kitchens in the Great Irish Famine
1846-1847 · Ireland
In late 1846, as famine spread in Ireland, Quakers led by Joseph Bewley formed the Central Relief Committee of the Society of Friends in Dublin, with a sister committee in London. Although Irish Quakers numbered only about 3,000, they set up soup kitchens in towns such as Waterford, Limerick, Clonmel and Cork, supplied large boilers, and distributed thousands of tons of food and seed. When the government adopted soup kitchens as its main relief in 1847, the Quakers provided boilers and cauldrons for them.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A small religious community poured its resources into feeding the starving and pioneered the method the state then adopted.
The kural says the proper place to store wealth is in relieving the killing hunger of the destitute, which is exactly where the Quakers put theirs.
