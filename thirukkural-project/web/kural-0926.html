Kural 926

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

துஞ்சினார் செத்தாரின் வேறல்லர் எஞ்ஞான்றும் நஞ்சுண்பார் கள்ளுண் பவர்.
Thunjinaar seththaarin verallar enjnjaandrum nanjunpaar kallun pavar.

Meaning

People who are asleep are no different from the dead. In the same way, people who drink alcohol are always no different from people who drink poison.

Elementary

When someone is fast asleep, they can't see, hear or help anyone, almost as if they weren't there at all. The Kural says drinking liquor is like taking a little poison: it switches off your mind the same way.

University

The couplet is an udhaaranam (உதாரணம், exemplum) built on two equations. Thunjinaar (துஞ்சினார், those asleep) are 'not different from' (verallar, வேறல்லர்) seththaar (செத்தார், the dead), because both lack awareness. Likewise kallunbavar (கள்ளுண்பவர், toddy-drinkers) are always nanjunbaar (நஞ்சுண்பார், poison-eaters). Mu. Varadarasanar gives the shared term as arivu mayangudhal (அறிவு மயங்குதல், clouding of understanding). Puliyur Kesikan applies enjnjaandrum (எஞ்ஞான்றும், 'always') to the second clause: the drinker is permanently poisoned, while the sleeper only appears dead for a while. Kalaignar reverses the order and reads it as a hyperbole. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“What Kshatriya is there who, armed with weapons, will slay men locked in the embraces of sleep and, therefore, already dead?”
— Mahabharata, Mausala Parva, Section 3 (K. M. Ganguli trans.) [3]
At the drunken revel before the Yadavas' self-destruction, Satyaki taunts Kritavarma for killing sleeping warriors, men he calls ‘already dead’.
The epic uses the Kural's own equation of the sleeper with the dead, spoken in a scene where drink is the poison.
Christianity
“Therefore let us not sleep, as do others; but let us watch and be sober. For they that sleep sleep in the night; and they that be drunken are drunken in the night.”
— 1 Thessalonians 5:6-7 (KJV) [4]
Paul puts sleep and drunkenness side by side as two kinds of unawareness that belong to the night.
This is the same pairing the Kural makes: the sleeper and the drinker are both absent from life.
Islam
“Satan only wants to cause between you animosity and hatred through intoxicants and gambling and to avert you from the remembrance of Allāh and from prayer. So will you not desist?”
— Qur'an 5:91 (Sahih International) [5]
Intoxicants cut a person off from remembering God and from prayer.
The drinker is spiritually ‘asleep’, as the Kural's drinker is as good as poisoned.
Judaism
“They taught that an intoxicated person is responsible for all his actions only in a case where he did not reach the state of intoxication of Lot; however, if he reached the state of intoxication of Lot, so that he is altogether unaware of his actions, he is exempt from all liability.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 65a (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud describes a level of drunkenness so deep that a person counts as no longer present as a moral agent.
Lot's state is the Kural's sleeper-as-dead: there, but not aware.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that heedfulness is the path to the Deathless and heedlessness the path to death; the heedful do not die, while the heedless are as if dead already.
— Dhammapada 21 (Appamada Vagga) [7]
Heedlessness (pamāda), which intoxicants produce, is treated as a living death.
Like the Kural, it says that losing awareness is already a kind of dying.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana compares sense pleasures to the kimpāka fruit, which tastes sweet but is a deadly poison once it is digested.
Valluvar says toddy-drinkers are always no different from those who swallow poison; the Jain sūtra makes the same equation between a pleasant indulgence and poison, though it speaks of pleasures in general.
Sikhism
“The Great Giver has given the intoxicating drug of falsehood. The people are intoxicated; they have forgotten death, and they have fun for a few days.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that worldly attachment works on people like a narcotic: under its influence they forget that they must die and enjoy themselves for a few short days. Intoxication here is the state of a life heading toward death without knowing it.
The Kural says drinkers are always like people swallowing poison, and Guru Nanak’s intoxicated crowd, revelling while they forget death, is the same picture.
Greek Mythology
Elpenor, youngest of Odysseus' crew, gets drunk at Circe's house, sleeps on the roof, and on waking falls and breaks his neck; his shade later meets Odysseus in the underworld.
— Homer, Odyssey, Books 10-11 [10]
A drunken sleep becomes real death.
The Kural's chain of sleep, death and drink-as-poison happens literally to Elpenor.
Philosophy
“For if you try to prove that the wise man can souse himself with much wine and yet keep his course straight, … you may go on to infer by syllogisms that he will not die if he swallows poison, that he will not sleep if he takes a sleeping-potion, …”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 83.27 (Gummere trans.) [11]
Seneca mocks the idea that the wise can drink heavily unharmed, placing wine beside poison and sleeping draughts.
Seneca puts wine, poison and sleep together just as the Kural does.

Real Life Example

Repin paints the dying Modest Mussorgsky
March 1881 · St Petersburg, Russia
After the triumphs of Boris Godunov and Pictures at an Exhibition in 1874, the composer Modest Mussorgsky fell into drinking bouts in St Petersburg taverns that could last for days, and his worsening alcoholism eventually made him unemployable in the government posts that supported him. In early 1881 he was hospitalised with chronic alcoholism, and the collector Pavel Tretyakov sent Ilya Repin to paint him; Repin's red-nosed portrait, made over a few sittings, now hangs in the Tretyakov Gallery. Mussorgsky died on 28 March 1881, aged only forty-two, before Repin could return for a final sitting.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One of Russia's most original composers was destroyed by drink at forty-two, and the portrait painted in his last days became one of the most famous images of a ruined genius.
The kural says that drinkers are no different from those who swallow poison, and Mussorgsky's drinking worked on him like a slow poison that killed him in his prime.
