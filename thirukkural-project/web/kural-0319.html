Kural 319

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

பிறர்க்கின்னா முற்பகல் செய்யின் தமக்குஇன்னா பிற்பகல் தாமே வரும்.
Pirarkku innaa murpagal seyyin thamakku innaa Pirpagal thaame varum.

Meaning

If you harm others in the morning, harm will come back to you on its own by the afternoon.

Elementary

It’s like throwing a ball hard against a wall: it bounces right back at you. This kural says being mean to someone in the morning means trouble will come bouncing back to you by the afternoon.

University

This is among the best-known couplets in Tamil, often quoted as a proverb. Its power lies in its compression of time: murpagal (forenoon) and pirpagal (afternoon) shrink the karmic interval, usually imagined across lifetimes, to a single day. The word thaame (of their own accord) makes the return automatic; no avenger or judge is named, and the consequence seems built into the act. The couplet thus combines a moral law with a psychological one, since harm tends to provoke retaliation and hostility quickly. Parallels range from the Mahabharata’s observation that the injured turns round and injures the injurer, to Proverbs’ image of the stone that rolls back on the one who pushes it, to Theseus giving each bandit on the road to Athens the death he dealt to others.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When One injures another, the injured turns round and injures the injurer.”
— Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Section 113 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Brihaspati warns Yudhishthira that harm done to another comes back to the one who did it.
The same law of return as the Kural: the harm you send out comes back to you.
Christianity
“Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap.”
— Galatians 6:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that everyone harvests exactly what they have planted.
Both teach that harm returns to its author; the Kural adds that the harvest comes fast.
Islam
“If you act rightly, it is for your own good, but if you do wrong, it is to your own loss.”
— Qur’an 17:7 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that the effects of one’s good and evil deeds fall back on oneself.
Both say that wrongdoing ultimately harms the wrongdoer.
Judaism
“Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein: and he that rolleth a stone, it will return upon him.”
— Proverbs 26:27 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs pictures harm as a pit that swallows the one who dug it, or a stone that rolls back on the one who pushed it.
Both use the image of harm rebounding naturally on its maker, without any outside avenger.
Buddhism
“Like fine dust thrown against the wind, evil falls back upon that fool who offends an inoffensive, pure and guiltless man.”
— Dhammapada 125 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha compares harming an innocent person to throwing dust into the wind: it blows straight back into your own face.
Both picture harm flying straight back at the one who sends it.
Jainism
“As the burglar caught in the breach of the wall perishes by the work the sinner himself had executed, thus people in this life and the next cannot escape the effect of their own actions.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Jacobi's commentator explains the image: a burglar caught halfway through the hole he dug in a wall is crushed when the wall he undermined collapses on him.
Valluvar says harm done in the morning returns by itself before evening. The burglar is destroyed by his own handiwork almost at once, which shows the same automatic and swift return of one's deeds.
Sikhism
“You shall harvest what you plant.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 4, Japji Sahib, Pauri 20, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Japji, Guru Nanak states the law of action plainly: what a person sows in deeds, that same person reaps.
The Kural warns that harm done in the morning returns to the doer by afternoon; Guru Nanak teaches the same return of one's deeds to oneself.
Greek Mythology
On his journey to Athens, the young Theseus meets a series of bandits who torment travellers. He kills each one by the same method they used on their victims, bending Sinis’ pine trees, hurling Sciron off his cliff, and fitting Procrustes to his own bed.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 8–11 [9]
Each bandit suffers exactly the harm he used to inflict on others.
The Kural’s law played out as myth: the harm each villain inflicted comes back to him in the same form.
Philosophy
In Book IV of the Consolation of Philosophy, Lady Philosophy argues that goodness is what makes us human, so whoever abandons it stops being fully human and sinks into the likeness of a beast. Wickedness therefore is its own punishment and falls on the wicked at once.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book IV, prose 3 [10]
Boethius argues that evildoers are punished by their very wickedness, immediately and unavoidably.
Both see the harm done to others turning back on the doer without delay; Boethius places the punishment inside the soul.

Real Life Example

Danton is tried and executed by the Revolutionary Tribunal he helped create
March 1793 - 5 April 1794 · Paris, France
In March 1793 Georges Danton played an instrumental role in establishing the Revolutionary Tribunal, and the following month he helped create the Committee of Public Safety, laying groundwork for the Reign of Terror. When he later opposed the Terror, he was arrested on the night of 29-30 March 1794 and tried before that same Revolutionary Tribunal from 2 to 4 April. He was executed by guillotine in Paris on 5 April 1794.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Within about a year, the instrument of harm that Danton had helped build was turned against him.
The kural warns that if you harm others in the morning, harm will come back to you by the afternoon, and Danton was condemned by the very tribunal he had helped set up barely a year earlier.
