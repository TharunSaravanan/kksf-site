Kural 382

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

அஞ்சாமை ஈகை அறிவூக்கம் இந்நான்கும் எஞ்சாமை வேந்தர்க் கியல்பு.
Anjaamai eegai arivookkam innaankum Enjaamai vendhark kiyalbu.

Meaning

Courage, generosity, wisdom and energy: never lacking these four is the true nature of a king.

Elementary

A good team captain is brave, shares with everyone, knows what to do, and never gets lazy. This kural says a real king must have all four of these — and never run out of any of them.

University

Having described the external assets of the state, Valluvar now names the four inner qualities of the ruler: anjaamai (fearlessness), eegai (liberality), arivu (wisdom), and ookkam (energy or zeal). The key word is enjaamai — ‘without deficiency’ — which makes the standard one of constancy: a king who is brave in one crisis but timid in the next, or generous only when convenient, does not meet it. Commentators such as Parimelazhagar pair the qualities functionally: courage and energy to act, wisdom to decide rightly, and generosity to bind people to the ruler. The list resembles the Bhagavad Gita’s catalogue of kshatriya traits and Plato’s requirements for the philosopher-ruler, suggesting a widely shared intuition that rulership needs both a warrior’s nerve and a sage’s judgment.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Heroism, power, determination, resourcefulness, courage in battle, generosity and leadership are the natural qualities of work for the kṣatriyas.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.43 [3]
Krishna lists the natural qualities of the warrior-ruler class: bravery, strength, firmness, skill, generosity, and the ability to lead.
Heroism and courage match anjaamai, generosity matches eegai, and determination/resourcefulness match ookkam and arivu.
Christianity
“And the spirit of the LORD shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the LORD;”
— Isaiah 11:2 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah describes the coming ideal king, whom Christians identify with Christ, as filled with wisdom, good counsel, and strength.
Wisdom and might together echo the Kural’s pairing of arivu with courage and energy.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah has chosen him over you and has increased him abundantly in knowledge and stature.”
— Qur’an 2:247 (Sahih International) [5]
When the Israelites doubted Talut (Saul) as king because he was not rich, their prophet replied that God had given him more knowledge and strength.
Kingship is justified by wisdom and physical/moral strength — two of the Kural’s four royal qualities.
Judaism
“Have not I commanded thee? Be strong and of a good courage; be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the LORD thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest.”
— Joshua 1:9 (KJV) [4]
God tells Joshua, the new leader of Israel, to be strong and fearless as he takes command.
Echoes anjaamai — fearlessness as the first requirement of leadership.
Buddhism
The ‘ten royal virtues’ (dasa raja dhamma) listed in the Jataka tradition begin with dana (generosity) and include sila (morality), pariccaga (self-sacrifice), and tapa (self-discipline/energy).
— Jataka tradition (e.g. Mahahamsa Jataka, no. 534) [6]
Buddhist stories give kings a list of ten virtues, starting with generosity.
Shares the Kural’s idea of a fixed set of royal virtues with generosity central, though Buddhist lists stress gentleness over fearlessness.
Jainism
“He (bears everything) like the earth; he annihilates (his Karman); he is free from greed; he, the Omniscient, does not keep store (of anything); he has crossed the ocean of life like the sea: he, the Hero, who grants protection to all, and whose perception is infinite.”
— Sutrakritanga I.6.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In the hymn to Mahavira, the ideal person is called the Hero (Vira), who gives protection, the gift of fearlessness (abhaya), to all beings, and whose knowledge has no limit. The same lecture ranks 'giving safety' as the best of gifts.
Valluvar's four royal qualities are fearlessness, liberality, wisdom and energy. The Jain praise of Mahavira joins heroism, the giving of protection and boundless wisdom in a single figure, though it describes a spiritual hero rather than a king.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else - says Nanak, listen, mind: call him spiritually wise.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahala 9, Salok 16, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In his saloks Guru Tegh Bahadur defines the truly wise person as one who neither intimidates others nor is intimidated by them.
The Kural names fearlessness and wisdom among the four qualities a ruler must never lack; Guru Tegh Bahadur joins the same two, making fearlessness itself a mark of wisdom.
Greek Mythology
Sarpedon tells Glaucus that kings are honoured with the best seats, meat, and lands only because they must fight in the front ranks; privilege is earned by courage.
— Homer, Iliad 12.310-328 (tr. A. T. Murray) [9]
A Trojan-allied prince explains that kings get special honours only because they are the bravest in battle.
Links royal standing to fearlessness, just as the Kural makes anjaamai a permanent kingly trait.
Philosophy
Plato has Socrates list the natural gifts required in the philosopher who would rule: good memory, quickness to learn, magnificence (greatness of spirit), graciousness, and kinship with truth, justice, courage, and temperance.
— Plato, Republic 6.487a (tr. Jowett) [10]
Plato says the ideal ruler must be quick-minded, big-hearted, and brave.
Wisdom, greatness of spirit, and courage correspond to arivu, eegai, and anjaamai.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth I addresses her troops at Tilbury
August 1588 · Tilbury, Essex, England
As the Spanish Armada threatened England in the summer of 1588, Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, commander of the land forces, arranged for Queen Elizabeth I to visit the army camp at Tilbury on the Thames. In early August she reviewed the troops and delivered a speech meant to encourage the men to stand firm against a possible invasion led by the Duke of Parma. The speech became one of the most celebrated in English history.
— Sources: [11] [12]
At a moment of national danger the queen went in person among her soldiers rather than remaining safe in London. Her visit became a lasting symbol of a ruler's courage and energy in a crisis.
Valluvar names fearlessness and energetic drive among the qualities a ruler must never lack, and Elizabeth showed both by going to the front to rally her army.
