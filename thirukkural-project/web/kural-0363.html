Kural 363

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

வேண்டாமை அன்ன விழுச்செல்வம் ஈண்டில்லை ஆண்டும் அஃதொப்பது இல்.
Vendaamai anna vizhuchchelvam eendillai Aandum aqdhoppadhu il.

Meaning

There is no treasure in this world as precious as being free from desire. Even in the next world, nothing is equal to it.

Elementary

A kid with a giant pile of toys who always wants more still feels poor. A kid who is happy with what they have feels rich. Valluvar says not wanting is the biggest treasure there is, bigger than anything here or even in heaven.

University

Vizhuchchelvam (“choicest wealth”) turns the idea of wealth inside out: a chapter on renunciation borrows the language of ownership to describe the absence of wanting. The pair eendu (“here”) and aandu (“there”) covers both this world and the heavenly worlds that ritual religion promised as rewards. Valluvar therefore ranks desirelessness above celestial pleasures, in line with the Upanishadic and Buddhist view that heaven is only a finer object of desire. The paradox that wanting less makes one richer shows up almost word for word in Seneca, in the Hadith on ‘richness of the soul’ and in Ben Zoma’s “Who is rich?”, which makes this one of the most cross-culturally shared couplets in the chapter.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Pārtha, when a man gives up all varieties of desire for sense gratification, which arise from mental concoction, and when his mind, thus purified, finds satisfaction in the self alone, then he is said to be in pure transcendental consciousness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.55 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes the person of steady wisdom as one who has dropped all desires and is fully content within himself.
Both say that being content without desire is the real fullness. It needs nothing outside itself, which is the very definition of wealth.
Christianity
“But godliness with contentment is great gain.”
— 1 Timothy 6:6 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Timothy that the real profit in life is godliness joined with contentment, not riches.
Both reverse the usual idea of gain. Not wanting more is itself the greatest wealth.
Islam
“Wealth is not in having many possessions, but rather (true) wealth is feeling sufficiency in the soul.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6446 [5]
The Prophet teaches that real richness is inner sufficiency (ghina al-nafs), not a large amount of property.
This is almost the Kural’s exact claim. The greatest wealth is a soul that no longer craves.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit) [6]
Ben Zoma teaches that the truly rich person is the one who is content with what he has.
Both redefine riches as freedom from wanting more.
Buddhism
“Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth. A trustworthy person is the best kinsman, Nibbana the highest bliss.”
— Dhammapada 204 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names contentment as the greatest wealth a person can have.
This is a direct parallel: the absence of craving is the supreme treasure.
Jainism
“There is much happiness for the sage, for the houseless monk, who is free from all ties, and knows himself to be single and unconnected (with the rest of the world).” … “Here (on earth) you are the highest man, Reverend sir, and hereafter you will be the highest; exempt from all blemishes you will reach Perfection, a higher state than which there is none in this world.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.16 and 9.58 (context 9.46–49) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
King Nami renounces Mithilā. When Indra, disguised as a Brāhmaṇa, urges him to fill his treasury with gold, silver and jewels, he refuses, saying that freedom from ties is true happiness. Indra then reveals himself and declares Nami the highest of men both here and hereafter.
Valluvar calls freedom from desire a treasure without equal 'here' and 'there'. Nami turns down an actual treasury for that freedom and is praised as supreme in this world and the next.
Sikhism
“Without contentment, no one is satisfied. Like the objects in a dream, all his efforts are in vain. Through the love of the Naam, all peace is obtained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Guru Arjan says that without contentment nothing satisfies, and that every kind of peace is found in love of the Name.
The Kural calls freedom from desire the choicest wealth in this world and the next; Guru Arjan says that without contentment no possession satisfies and that all peace comes from the Name.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor old couple, live contentedly in a humble cottage and gladly share what little they have with disguised gods. Their hut is turned into a temple, and they ask only to serve there and to die together.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 (paraphrase) [10]
The couple’s happiness comes from contentment, not from possessions. Even when the gods reward them, they ask for almost nothing.
They live out the Kural’s idea that not wanting is the richest state. They are poor in goods but lack nothing.
Philosophy
“It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 2.6 (trans. R. M. Gummere) [11]
Seneca argues that poverty is measured by craving, not by possessions.
This is the mirror image of the Kural: if craving is poverty, then being free of craving is the greatest wealth.

Real Life Example

Diocletian gives up the throne and retires to Split
1 May 305 · Nicomedia (now Izmit, Turkey) and Split, Croatia (Roman Empire)
On 1 May 305 the Roman emperor Diocletian, who had ruled for more than twenty years, voluntarily abdicated, an almost unheard-of step for a Roman emperor. He retired to the great palace he had built on the Adriatic coast at what is now Split, in Croatia. Apart from a brief appearance at an imperial conference at Carnuntum in 308, he stayed in his palace for the rest of his life while rivals fought over the empire.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Diocletian walked away from supreme power at a time when most emperors kept it until violent death, and did not take it back while the empire's rulers fought for it.
The kural ranks freedom from wanting above any treasure of this world, and Diocletian's choice of a quiet palace over the throne of the Roman world shows the highest worldly prize being let go.
