Kural 845

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

கல்லாத மேற்கொண் டொழுகல் கசடற வல்லதூஉம் ஐயம் தரும்.
Kallaadha merkon dozhugal kasadara valladhooum aiyam tharum.

Meaning

Pretending to know things you never learned makes people doubt even the things you really do know well.

Elementary

If a boy who is great at football starts bragging that he is also a chess champion, and everyone sees he can’t play chess, they start wondering whether he is really good at football either. Faking one thing makes people doubt the real things too.

University

Kallaadha (கல்லாத, what one has not learned) set against kasadara valladhu (கசடற வல்லது, what one has mastered without flaw) gives the kural its hinge, and the emphatic -ooum (‘even that’) shows the reach of the damage: aiyam (ஐயம், doubt) spreads from the pretended to the genuine. Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read kallaadha as unread books (nool), so the fool pretends to scholarship; Kalaignar widens it to any field one pretends to know. Puliyur Kesigan reads merkondu ozhugal more actively as rashly undertaking a task one has not learned, which casts doubt even on work one could do perfectly. The kural is a pointed observation about credibility: a single exposed pretense devalues an entire reputation. Both sources give identical text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Panchatantra a jackal who falls into a vat of indigo dye passes himself off as a new king of the beasts; when he hears other jackals howl and howls back, his true nature is exposed and he loses everything.
— Panchatantra, Book I, ‘The Blue Jackal’ [3]
The fable mocks the impostor whose pretense is found out by a single slip.
Once the pretense is exposed, nothing he claimed is believed any more.
Christianity
“Desiring to be teachers of the law; understanding neither what they say, nor whereof they affirm.”
— 1 Timothy 1:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes would-be teachers who claim expertise they do not have.
This is the posture Valluvar warns against: acting learned in what one has not learned.
Islam
“And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, the hearing, the sight and the heart - about all those [one] will be questioned.”
— Qur’an 17:36 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an forbids claiming or acting on what one does not know.
Valluvar gives the worldly cost of the same fault: lost trust.
Judaism
“And concerning that which he has not heard, he says: I have not heard; And he acknowledges the truth.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:7 (Kulp) [6]
Among the seven marks of the wise person, the Mishnah lists honestly admitting what one has not learned.
The wise admit gaps; the fool pretends and so loses credit for what he does know.
Buddhism
In the Sihacamma Jataka a merchant’s donkey, dressed in a lion’s skin, is left to graze in fields; villagers fear it until it brays, revealing itself, and it is beaten.
— Jataka 189, Sihacamma Jataka (Cowell ed.) [7]
The borrowed image of strength collapses the moment the true voice is heard.
Pretended mastery, once exposed, brings disgrace instead of respect.
Jainism
In its chapter on purity of speech, the Daśavaikālika Sūtra tells the monk that where there is doubt about a matter, he must not declare 'it is just so'. He may speak with certainty only about what he truly knows.
— Daśavaikālika Sūtra ch. 7 (Vākyaśuddhi, 'purity of speech'), Prakrit 'jattha saṃkā bhave taṃ tu, evameyaṃ ti no vae' [8]
Jain monastic rules on speech forbid asserting as certain anything one does not know, because false certainty breaks the vow of truthfulness (satya).
The Kural warns that pretending to know what one never learned makes people doubt even one's real knowledge, and the Jain rule removes the cause by forbidding any confident claim beyond what one knows.
Sikhism
“O Nanak! The blind man may go to appraise the jewels, but he will not know their value; he will return home after exposing his ignorance.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 954, Raag Raamkalee, Ramkali Ki Vaar (salok 1 before pauri 15), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Ramkali Ki Vaar Guru Angad describes the blind man who sets himself up to value jewels and only shows how little he knows.
The Kural says pretending to know what you never learned destroys your credibility; Guru Angad's blind appraiser exposes his own ignorance the same way.
Greek Mythology
Phaethon insists on driving his father Helios’ sun chariot though he has never learned how; he loses control, scorches the earth and is struck down.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2 [10]
He takes up a task he has not mastered, with disastrous results.
Undertaking what one has not learned, as Puliyur Kesigan reads the kural, brings ruin.
Philosophy
“Because they were good workmen they thought that they also knew all sorts of high matters, and this defect in them overshadowed their wisdom.”
— Plato, Apology 22d (Jowett) [11]
Socrates found that skilled craftsmen spoiled their real expertise by claiming knowledge beyond it.
Exactly the kural: pretending to unlearned knowledge clouds even real mastery.

Real Life Example

Henry Ford's ignorance of history is exposed at his libel trial
July 1919 · Mount Clemens, Michigan, United States
Henry Ford sued the Chicago Tribune for libel after it called him an ignorant idealist and an anarchist. At the 1919 trial in Mount Clemens, the newspaper's lawyers questioned him on subjects outside his expertise, and he mangled basic facts of American history, placing the American Revolution in 1812 and describing Benedict Arnold as a writer. The jury found in Ford's favour but awarded him only six cents in damages.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The trial made the world's most famous industrialist an object of ridicule on subjects he had pronounced on publicly, even as his genius in manufacturing remained undisputed.
The kural warns that pretending to know what one never learned makes people doubt even what one has mastered, and Ford's public pronouncements beyond his field brought scorn that touched his whole reputation.
