Kural 648

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

விரைந்து தொழில்கேட்கும் ஞாலம் நிரந்தினிது சொல்லுதல் வல்லார்ப் பெறின்.
Viraindhu thozhilkekkum gnaalam nirandhinidhu Solludhal vallaarp perin.

Meaning

If the world finds people who can speak in an orderly and pleasant way, it will quickly do what they ask.

Elementary

If a coach explains the plan step by step and in a friendly way, the whole team gets moving straight away. This kural says people are happy to follow someone whose words are clear, well arranged and kind.

University

Nirantu, “in proper sequence, arranged in order”, is the key technical term: it describes dispositio, the arrangement of points in the right order, joined with initu, pleasantly. Toḻil kēṭkum ñālam, “the world that hears or obeys the command”, uses kēḷ in the sense of obedience, and viraintu (swiftly) stresses how quickly the result follows. Parimelazhagar reads ñālam as the people of the world and the couplet as describing the minister whose orderly, sweet speech gets the king’s business done at once. Pope’s rendering, the world gathering round, softens the sense of obedience. The older edition prints கேட்கு ஞால நிரந்தினிது.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Rigvedic hymn to Knowledge says that when the wise fashioned speech with their minds, sifting it as grain is cleaned with a sieve, friends recognised friendship and a blessed mark was set upon their speech.
— Rigveda 10.71.2, trans. R. T. H. Griffith [3]
The hymn pictures good speech as carefully sifted and arranged, producing fellowship among those who hear it.
Speech sifted and ordered by the mind is the Kural’s nirantu, orderly speech that wins people over.
Christianity
At Pentecost Peter gives a carefully built sermon, moving from prophecy to the resurrection to a call to repentance; the hearers are cut to the heart and about three thousand accept his word that same day.
— Acts 2:14-41 (KJV) [4]
Peter’s orderly argument produces an immediate and large response.
Ordered speech followed by swift response is the pattern the Kural describes.
Islam
“Those are the ones of whom Allah knows what is in their hearts, so turn away from them but admonish them and speak to them a far-reaching word.”
— Qur’an 4:63 (Sahih International) [5]
The Prophet is told to speak qawlan balīghan, an effective word that reaches deep into the hearers’ souls.
Speech that reaches and moves its hearers is the kind the Kural says people act on at once.
Judaism
Abigail meets David on his way to take revenge on her husband Nabal and makes a gracious, well-ordered plea; David at once blesses her good sense and turns back from bloodshed.
— 1 Samuel 25:23-35 (JPS 1917) [6]
Her tactful, well-arranged speech changes an armed leader’s course on the spot.
David’s immediate change of course shows the Kural’s point: orderly, pleasant speech gets swift results.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches the householder Upāli by a step-by-step talk (anupubbikathā) on generosity, virtue, heaven, the drawbacks of sensual pleasure and renunciation, then the Four Noble Truths, and Upāli gains the vision of the Dhamma where he sits.
— Majjhima Nikaya 56 (Upāli Sutta) [7]
The Pali texts often show this graded, ordered discourse leading to immediate understanding in the listener.
A talk ordered step by step that brings instant acceptance matches the Kural’s swift response to orderly, sweet speech.
Jainism
“But in that case they should say: 'N. N.! O long-lived one! O long-lived ones! O layman! O pupil! O faithful one! O lover of faith!' Considering well, they should use such sinless, blameless, &c., speech.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.4.1.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Instead of abusive words, the monk is told to address people with courteous, kindly forms of speech that cause no offence.
The kural says the world quickly does the bidding of those who speak in good order and pleasantly; the Jain rule supplies the pleasant, courteous manner, though not the promise of ready obedience.
Sikhism
“Humility is the word, forgiveness is the virtue, and sweet speech is the magic mantra. Wear these three robes, O sister, and you will captivate your Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid, Salok 127 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, calls sweet speech a magic mantra that, with humility and forgiveness, wins over even the divine Beloved.
The Kural says that if the world finds those who speak in good order and pleasantly, it will quickly do their bidding; Farid likewise makes sweet speech the spell that brings the one addressed under the speaker's sway.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that when the Muses honour a king, they pour sweet dew on his tongue, so gentle words flow from his mouth; the people look to him as he settles disputes with straight judgements and quickly ends even a great quarrel.
— Hesiod, Theogony 81-93 [10]
The Muses’ gift of sweet, well-formed speech lets a ruler bring the people along at once.
Sweet speech that quickly settles affairs and wins the people’s trust is the Kural’s image.
Philosophy
Socrates says every speech should be put together like a living creature, with its own body, head and feet, and a middle and ends that fit each other and the whole.
— Plato, Phaedrus 264c [11]
Plato makes well-ordered structure the mark of a speech worth hearing.
An organic, well-arranged speech is the Kural’s nirantu, orderly speech.

Real Life Example

Roosevelt's first fireside chat ends the bank panic
12 March 1933 · Washington, D.C., United States
After declaring a nationwide bank holiday, President Franklin D. Roosevelt spoke to Americans by radio on 12 March 1933, explaining in plain, orderly terms what had happened to the banks, how the new Emergency Banking Act would work, and when sound banks would reopen. When banks reopened the next day, long lines of customers came to return their hoarded cash, and by the end of March the public had redeposited about two-thirds of the currency withdrawn in the panic. On 15 March the New York Stock Exchange recorded its largest one-day percentage gain.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The broadcast worked because Roosevelt laid out the situation step by step in a warm, direct manner, and millions of listeners acted on what he asked of them within days.
The kural says that if the world finds someone who can speak in good order and pleasantly, it will quickly do what that person asks, and the public's swift return to the banks after Roosevelt's talk is a clear case.
