Kural 523

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

அளவளா வில்லாதான் வாழ்க்கை குளவளாக் கோடின்றி நீர்நிறைந் தற்று.
Alavalaa villaadhaan vaazhkkai kulavalaak Kotindri neernirain thatru.

Meaning

A person who does not share their heart with their relatives is like a big pond with no banks: whatever water comes in just flows away.

Elementary

Imagine pouring water into a pond with no edges. The water spreads out and disappears, and the pond never stays full. A person who keeps away from family is like that pond: good things come, but with no one around to help hold on to them, they slip away.

University

The key word அளவளாவு means intimate mingling, literally ‘measuring together’. Parimelazhagar explains that heart-union grows out of exchanging one’s own measure with another’s, so the word stands by metonymy for close intimacy with kin, and ‘with kin’ is supplied from the chapter heading. குளவளா is the broad expanse of a tank and கோடு is its bank. For Parimelazhagar வாழ்க்கை here stands for the wealth that sustains life, and his point is that without kin to ‘hold’ it, a man’s wealth drains outward just as water spills from a tank that has no banks. He groups kurals 522-524 as showing kin to be the cause (ஏது), the fortress (அரண்) and the fruit (பயன்) of wealth, and this couplet shows kin as the fortress. Some printed texts divide the first word as அளவளாவு இல்லாதான், and thirukkural.net prints it with a space as அளவளா வில்லாதான்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And the tree that stands singly, though gigantic and strong and deep-rooted, hath its trunk soon smashed and twisted by a mighty wind. Those trees, however, that grow in close compact are competent owing to mutual dependence to resist winds more violent still.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 36 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura tells the king that a man cut off from his relatives is as easy to destroy as a lone tree in a storm.
The isolated man in the Kural is the bankless tank, and in Vidura he is the lone tree; both are defenceless without kin.
Christianity
“And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is;”
— Hebrews 10:24-25 (KJV) [4]
The letter to the Hebrews warns believers not to drift away from the fellowship of the community.
Drawing away from one’s own people is the very failure the Kural describes.
Islam
“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided. And remember the favor of Allah upon you - when you were enemies and He brought your hearts together and you became, by His favor, brothers.”
— Qur’an 3:103 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an calls on believers to hold together and warns against splitting apart, since joined hearts are a divine favour.
Hearts joined together, like the Kural’s அளவளாவு, are what hold a community’s strength in place.
Judaism
“Hillel said: do not separate yourself from the community,”
— Pirkei Avot 2:4 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit) [6]
Hillel’s maxim warns against cutting oneself off from the collective life of one’s people.
The Kural pictures the man who stays apart from his kin as a tank with no banks, and Hillel names the same danger.
Buddhism
In the Rukkhadhamma Jataka, the Buddha tells his quarrelling Sakya and Koliya kinsmen about a forest where the trees stood close together and survived a great storm, while a tree growing alone in the open was uprooted. The lesson he draws is that kinsfolk should stay united.
— Rukkhadhamma Jataka (Jataka No. 74), trans. E. B. Cowell et al. [7]
The story shows kin standing together as protection against disaster.
The Kural’s tank without banks and the Jataka’s lone tree both picture a life without kin as unprotected.
Jainism
“if he is angry even with friends; if he speaks evil even of a good friend behind his back”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the list of the ill-behaved monk, Mahavira includes the man who turns on his own friends and maligns them; the same list ends with the one who 'does not share with others'.
The Kural's man who never mingles heart to heart with his kin lives like a tank without banks; the Jain list likewise marks as failed the man who cuts himself off from those closest to him.
Sikhism
“No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes the state reached in the company of the holy, in which the heart mixes freely with all and no one remains an outsider.
The Kural warns that a life without heart-to-heart mingling with one's people is like a tank without banks; Guru Arjan's open fellowship with everyone is the very opposite of that isolation.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the father and his quarrelling sons, the father asks each son to break a bundle of sticks, and none can. He then unties the bundle and shows how easily each stick snaps alone, teaching them that they are strong while united and easy to beat when divided.
— Aesop’s Fables, The Father and His Sons (Perry Index 53) [10]
The fable makes family unity the source of strength and separation the source of weakness.
The single stick and the bankless tank both picture life without kin.
Philosophy
“for no one would choose the whole world on condition of being alone, since man is a political creature and one whose nature is to live with others.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.9 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that even the happiest man needs people to share his life with, because humans are social by nature.
A life without close ties is empty however much it holds, just as the Kural’s tank stays empty however much water pours in.

Real Life Example

The Inca civil war between Huascar and Atahualpa
late 1520s-1532 · Inca Empire (present-day Peru and Ecuador)
After the Inca ruler Huayna Capac died in an epidemic without a clearly settled successor, his sons Huascar, based at Cuzco, and Atahualpa, based in the north at Quito, fought a long civil war for the throne. The war divided the Inca nobility and armies and was costly to both sides, and Atahualpa's generals finally captured Huascar in 1532. Only months later, in November 1532, a small Spanish force under Francisco Pizarro captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca, and the empire soon fell under Spanish rule.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Historians count the brothers' war, together with epidemic disease and Spanish weapons, among the reasons the largest empire in the Americas collapsed so quickly.
The kural compares a man who does not live heart to heart with his kin to a wide tank without banks, and the quarrel between royal brothers left the Inca realm unable to hold on to what it had built.
