Kural 602

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

மடியை மடியா ஒழுகல் குடியைக் குடியாக வேண்டு பவர்.
Madiyai madiyaa ozhugal kudiyaik Kudiyaaga vendu bavar.

Meaning

If you want your family to rise and be truly respected, treat laziness as something deadly and get rid of it in the way you live.

Elementary

If you want your team to be a real team, you can’t treat ‘I’ll do it later’ as harmless. You have to see laziness as the thing that breaks teams, and push it out every day. Families work the same way.

University

The couplet is built on a double pun. மடியை மடியா can mean ‘treating sloth as sloth’, recognising it for the vice it is (Varadarasanar: சோம்பலைச் சோம்பலாகக் கொண்டு), or, because மடி also means ‘to die, perish’, ‘treating sloth as something to be killed’ or ‘as death itself’. Kalaignar’s gloss (சோம்பலை ஒழித்து) takes the second sense. The echo குடியைக் குடியாக, ‘to make the family a family’, implies that a lineage only truly exists as a குடி while it keeps its standing; Parimelazhagar takes குடி here as the illustrious family, not the bare kin-group. ஒழுகல், ‘conduct, practice’, makes this a matter of daily habit, not of a single resolution. Both source texts agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org prints the sandhi-joined மடியா வொழுகல்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa’s ‘charaiveti’ (keep moving) verses, Indra tells Rohita that fortune sits still with the one who sits and sleeps with the one who sleeps, and each stanza ends with the refrain: keep moving on.
— Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 7.15 (Śunaḥśepa episode) [3]
The god Indra urges the prince Rohita to keep wandering and striving, teaching that stillness and sleep bring decline while effort brings prosperity.
Like the Kural, it tells a prince who wants his line to prosper to reject sloth completely and keep acting.
Christianity
“Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord;”
— Romans 12:11 (KJV) [4]
Paul lists freedom from sloth, together with fervour of spirit, among the marks of a life given over to God.
Paul, like Valluvar, makes rejecting sloth a daily rule of conduct (ஒழுகல்), not an occasional effort.
Islam
In a hadith in Sahih Muslim, the Prophet says the strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak one, and tells believers to strive for what benefits them, seek God’s help, and not give way to helplessness.
— Sahih Muslim 2664 [5]
The hadith tells believers to strive energetically for what is beneficial and forbids giving in to incapacity or listlessness (ʿajz).
Refusing to ‘give up’ matches the Kural’s demand that anyone who wants to raise their family must put sloth to death.
Judaism
“Rabbi Tarfon said: the day is short, and the work is plentiful, and the laborers are indolent, and the reward is great, and the master of the house is insistent.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:15 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon describes the human condition as a short working day with much to do, and names laziness as the workers’ typical failing.
Tarfon’s ‘indolent labourers’ are the people Valluvar warns: a house (bayit / குடி) is only upheld when that laziness is overcome.
Buddhism
In the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, the Buddha’s last words to his monks are that all conditioned things are subject to decay, and they should strive on with diligence (appamādena sampādetha).
— Dīgha Nikāya 16 (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta), 6.7 [7]
The Buddha’s final teaching makes diligence, the opposite of heedless sloth, the one thing his followers must hold on to.
Just as the Kural makes killing sloth the condition for a family’s survival, the Buddha makes diligence the condition for the community’s survival after him.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira's famous refrain to Gautama (samayaṃ Goyama mā pamāyae, 'do not be careless even for a moment') makes constant vigilance against negligence the core discipline.
Like the Kural's call to treat sloth as sloth and kill it in one's conduct, Mahavira tells his foremost disciple never to let carelessness in for a moment.
Sikhism
“O mind, why are you so lazy? Become Gurmukh, and meditate on the Naam.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 28, Siree Raag, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das challenges the mind's laziness and sets against it the active, Guru-facing life of the gurmukh.
The Kural tells those who want their family to stand to see laziness for what it is and kill it in their conduct; Guru Amar Das likewise names laziness and answers it with a daily discipline.
Greek Mythology
“Do not put your work off till to-morrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 410-412 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod warns his brother Perses that a household’s stores are filled only by those who refuse to procrastinate.
Hesiod’s full barn, like the Kural’s thriving குடி, belongs only to the one who treats sloth as the enemy.
Philosophy
“If, therefore, you will be negligent and slothful, and always add procrastination to procrastination, purpose to purpose, and fix day after day in which you will attend to yourself, you will insensibly continue without proficiency”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 50 in Carter’s numbering (51 in some editions) [11]
Epictetus warns that someone who keeps putting off self-reform because of sloth will slide, without noticing, into a life of no progress.
Epictetus, like Valluvar, says that sloth has to be named and stopped now, not managed later.

Real Life Example

Fourteen-year-old Alfred Krupp takes on his father's failing works
1826-1848 · Essen, Germany
When Friedrich Krupp died on 8 October 1826, he left his family a small, heavily indebted steel works in Essen where production had almost come to a standstill. His fourteen-year-old son Alfred, working alongside his widowed mother, took on the management of the workshop and its handful of employees. He became sole proprietor in 1848 and built the firm into one of the largest industrial enterprises in Europe.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The family's standing was saved not by inheritance, which was nearly worthless, but by years of relentless daily work by a teenage heir.
The kural says those who want their family to be a family in the full sense must put laziness to death in their daily conduct, and young Alfred Krupp restored his family's fortunes through exactly that kind of unremitting work.
