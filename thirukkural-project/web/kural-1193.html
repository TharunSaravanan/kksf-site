Kural 1193

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

வீழுநர் வீழப் படுவார்க்கு அமையுமே வாழுநம் என்னும் செருக்கு.
Veezhunar veezhap paduvaarkku amaiyume vaazhunam ennum serukku.

Meaning

Only a woman truly loved by the one she loves can proudly say, 'We will live happily again,' even while he is away.

Elementary

If your mom goes on a trip and you know she loves you and will come back, you can wait bravely and even smile. Knowing you are loved makes waiting easier.

University

Vīḻunar vīḻappaṭuvār is ‘those who are desired by the ones who desire them’; for such women alone amaiyumē (it is fitting) to hold the ceruukku (pride, proud confidence) of saying vāḻunam, ‘we shall live (in joy).’ In the separation context Parimelazhagar and Varadarasanar read the pride as forward-looking: despite present parting, the loved wife can be sure that reunion and happiness will come; Pappaiah adds ‘by the certainty that he will soon return.’ The heroine’s unspoken complaint is that she cannot claim this pride. Pope: ‘Who love and are beloved to them alone / Belongs the boast, “We’ve made life’s very joys our own.”’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Yama comes for her husband Satyavan, the devoted Savitri follows the god of death, and her steadfast love and wisdom win back Satyavan’s life so that the two can return home together.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Pativrata-mahatmya Parva (Savitri episode) (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Savitri’s certainty in a love that is mutual gives her the courage to argue with Death himself.
Savitri embodies the Kural’s ‘we shall live’ confidence that belongs to the wife who knows her love is shared.
Christianity
“Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it”
— Song of Solomon 8:7 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Solomon declares love able to survive every flood that might overwhelm it.
This is the ground of the loved wife’s confidence in the Kural: real, returned love will outlast the present separation.
Islam
In the hadith of Umm Zar‘, Aisha relates eleven women describing their husbands; Umm Zar‘ proudly recalls a husband who treated her with lavish kindness, and the Prophet tells Aisha that he is to her as Abu Zar‘ was to Umm Zar‘.
— Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Marriage, hadith of Umm Zar‘ (paraphrase) [5]
A wife’s happy boast about a loving husband is affirmed by the Prophet as true of his own marriage.
Umm Zar‘’s pride is the ‘boast’ the Kural says belongs only to a wife whose love is returned.
Judaism
“for love is strong as death, jealousy is cruel as the grave; the flashes thereof are flashes of fire, a very flame of the LORD.”
— Song of Songs 8:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The Song’s climax claims for love a strength equal to death itself.
The loved wife of the Kural can say ‘we shall live’ because she trusts a love as strong as this.
Buddhism
Describing seven kinds of wives, the Buddha praises the wife who is like a friend: she rejoices on seeing her husband as one rejoices at seeing a long-absent companion.
— Bhariya Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya, Book of Sevens (paraphrase) [7]
The ‘friend-wife’ of this sutta is marked by glad, confident affection toward her husband.
Her joy at reunion is the Kural’s ‘we shall live’, available only where affection runs both ways.
Jainism
In the Jain story of Shripal and Mayanasundari, the princess is married off in anger to a man suffering from leprosy; trusting in her husband and in her faith, she remains devoted and confident, and through their shared worship of the Siddhachakra he is healed and the couple prosper.
— Shripal Ras (Vinayavijaya and Yashovijaya, 17th c.), story of Shripal and Mayanasundari (paraphrase) [8]
Mayanasundari’s calm certainty that she and her husband will flourish together is a celebrated Jain model of faithful love.
Her confidence echoes the Kural’s ‘we shall live’, though the Jain story credits faith and merit rather than love alone.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, the soul-bride is forever the Lord's favorite wife; her Husband Lord does not die, and He shall never leave.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 245, Raag Gauree, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das says the soul-bride who is truly loved by the Lord is sure of her happiness, because her Husband never dies and never leaves her.
The Kural says only a woman loved by the one she loves can say with proud confidence, 'We shall live happily yet'; Gurbani gives the loved soul-bride the same sure confidence, since her Beloved will never leave.
Greek Mythology
After twenty years apart, Penelope tests Odysseus with the secret of their marriage bed, carved from a living olive tree; when he describes it, she knows him, and the two are reunited in the home he built.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.173-240 (paraphrase) [10]
The bed rooted in a living tree is the sign that their love was fixed on both sides throughout the long separation.
Penelope’s long patience could hold because, as the Kural says, the one she loved also loved her.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that most people, out of ambition, wish to be loved rather than to love, since being loved is close to being honoured, and that people enjoy being loved for its own sake.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.8, 1159a (paraphrase) [11]
Aristotle recognises the special satisfaction and standing that comes from being loved.
The Kural’s ‘pride’ of the loved wife is the same satisfaction Aristotle describes in being loved.

Real Life Example

Sullivan Ballou's last letter to his wife Sarah
14 July 1861 · Camp Clark, Washington, D.C., United States
A week before the First Battle of Bull Run (First Manassas), Union Major Sullivan Ballou wrote a long letter to his wife Sarah, telling her of his love and of the possibility that he might not return. He was killed in the battle. The letter was never mailed; it was found among his belongings when Rhode Island's governor went to Virginia to recover the remains of the state's soldiers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter became one of the most famous love letters in American history because it speaks of a love so secure that even separation and death could not shake it.
The kural says only a woman truly loved can say with proud confidence that they will yet live happily even while he is away, and Ballou's letter is the kind of assurance that gives a wife that confidence.
