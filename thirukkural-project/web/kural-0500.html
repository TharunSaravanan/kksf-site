Kural 500

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

காலாழ் களரில் நரியடும் கண்ணஞ்சா வேலாள் முகத்த களிறு.
Kaalaazh kalaril nariyadum kannanjaa Velaal mukaththa kaliru.

Meaning

A brave war elephant that can pierce spear-carrying soldiers with its tusks can be killed even by jackals when it gets stuck in deep mud.

Elementary

A huge elephant is the strongest animal in the forest, but if it gets stuck in deep mud, even small animals can bother it and it can’t fight back. Being big and strong doesn’t help if you’re stuck in the wrong place.

University

The chapter closes with its strongest contrast. ‘Kaal aazh kalar’ is mire or saline swamp where legs sink; ‘kann anjaa’ is ‘unflinching of eye’, never afraid; ‘velaal mukaththa kaliru’ is the tusker that carries spear-bearing warriors (velaal) impaled on its tusks (mukam, the front), which Parimelazhagar explains as an elephant that has gored armed soldiers. The ‘nari’ is the jackal, the most contemptible of predators, so the verse sets the mightiest animal against the meanest. The old continuous text (thirukkural-thamizh.org) shows the sandhi ‘காலாழ் களரி னரியடுங் கண்ணஞ்சா வேலாண் முகத்த களிறு’, where ‘வேலாண்’ is ‘வேலாள்’ joined to ‘முகத்த’. The kural returns to 491’s warning: the greatest strength, put on the wrong ground, falls to the weakest enemy.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Karna Parva, the left wheel of Karna’s chariot sinks into the earth in the middle of his duel with Arjuna. While Karna struggles to lift it free, the peerless warrior is struck down.
— Mahābhārata, Karṇa Parva, Sections 90–91 (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The Mahabharata’s great tragic hero is not beaten by lack of skill or courage but by ground that gives way under him.
This is the fearless war elephant stuck in the mire, told of a human champion.
Christianity
“He brought me up also out of an horrible pit, out of the miry clay, and set my feet upon a rock, and established my goings.”
— Psalm 40:2 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist thanks God for lifting him out of sinking mud and putting his feet on firm ground.
The contrast between miry clay and a rock underfoot is the Kural’s contrast between the swamp and firm footing.
Islam
“[Remember] when He… sent down upon you from the sky, rain by which to purify you… and to make steadfast your hearts and plant firmly thereby your feet.”
— Quran 8:11 (Sahih International) [5]
Before Badr, rain firmed up the sandy ground under the Muslim position. Commentators add that the same rain made the Quraysh side heavy and slippery.
Firm footing for one side and bad ground for the other is exactly the kural’s lesson.
Judaism
Sisera, commander of nine hundred iron chariots, the elite weapon of his day, lost the battle by the river Kishon when the ground turned against his chariots. He fled on foot and was killed by Jael, a tent-dwelling woman.
— Judges 4:13–21; 5:21 [4]
The most feared force in Canaan was destroyed by bad ground and finished off by an unlikely hand.
This is the mighty tusker in the mire, killed by the weakest of foes.
Buddhism
“Delight in heedfulness! Guard well your thoughts! Draw yourself out of this bog of evil, even as an elephant draws himself out of the mud.”
— Dhammapada 327 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada uses the familiar image of an elephant stuck in a bog to urge vigilance.
Same image as the kural, the mighty elephant helpless in mud, turned into advice to get out of it.
Jainism
“As an elephant, sinking down in a quagmire, sees the raised ground but does not get to the shore, so do we who long for sensual pleasures, not follow the path of monks.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
King Brahmadatta confesses that his attachment to pleasure leaves him like an elephant stuck in a bog, able to see firm ground but unable to reach it.
The kural's mighty elephant becomes helpless once its feet sink in mud; the Jain text uses the same image of a powerful elephant stuck in a quagmire, here as a picture of the soul trapped by its desires.
Sikhism
“Attracted by the lure of sexual desire, the elephant is captured, O crazy mind, and now the halter is placed around its neck.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 335, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir recalls how the mighty elephant is drawn onto ground where it can be trapped, and once caught it must bear the goad and halter.
The Kural's fearless war elephant is brought down even by jackals once it is stuck in the mire; Kabir's elephant likewise loses all its power once it is lured onto the wrong ground.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad 21, Achilles, unmatched on the plain, fights the river-god Scamander in his own element. The flood breaks down on him, the ground washes away beneath his feet, and the greatest warrior of Troy nearly drowns until the gods step in.
— Homer, Iliad 21.233–271 (tr. Samuel Butler) [9]
Even Achilles is almost undone once he fights in the river’s domain rather than on firm ground.
Like the tusker in the mire, the mightiest fighter becomes helpless where his footing gives way.
Philosophy
“In crossing salt-marshes, your sole concern should be to get over them quickly, without any delay.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War IX.7 (tr. Lionel Giles) [10]
Sun Tzu treats marshland as dangerous ground where an army must not linger or give battle if it can avoid it.
His warning rests on the Kural’s point that boggy ground can destroy even a mighty force.

Real Life Example

Flemish townsmen defeat the French knights at the Battle of the Golden Spurs
11 July 1302 · Courtrai (Kortrijk), Flanders, Belgium
When the towns of Flanders rose against French rule, a French army of professional mounted knights was sent against them. The Flemish army, made up largely of townspeople and craft guildsmen fighting on foot, took a stand on ground bounded by streams and ditches, where marshy soil hampered the horsemen. The French cavalry charge broke down on this ground and the knights were defeated by the foot soldiers, who afterwards collected the fallen knights' spurs as trophies.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The battle showed that the most prestigious troops of the age could be beaten by ordinary infantry once they were caught on ground that suited the defender.
The kural says the fearless war elephant can be killed even by jackals once it is stuck in mud, and the proud French knights were brought down by humble townsmen in the marshes of Courtrai.
