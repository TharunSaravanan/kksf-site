Kural 899

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

ஏந்திய கொள்கையார் சீறின் இடைமுரிந்து வேந்தனும் வேந்து கெடும்.
Endhiya kolkaiyaar seerin itaimurindhu vendhanum vendhu ketum.

Meaning

When people with high ideals rise up in anger, even a king will be broken in the middle of his rule and lose his kingdom.

Elementary

Imagine a team captain who bullies the fairest, most honest player. When the whole team sees it and stands with that honest player, the captain loses the job. Even powerful people can fall if they anger those who live by high principles.

University

Eendhiya koLgaiyaar (ஏந்திய கொள்கையார்) are those who bear aloft high principles or vows; if they seeRin (சீறின், flare up in anger), even the vendhan (வேந்தன், king) will iDai murindhu (இடை முரிந்து, be broken midway) and vendhu keDum (வேந்து கெடும், his kingship will perish). Pope’s explanation glosses the king as Indra and the great as those of exalted vows, reflecting the traditional commentary’s allusion to Nahusha, who was cast down from Indra’s throne by the curse of the sage Agastya. Modern commentators generalize: Solomon Pappaiah speaks of a ruler losing office midway, and Kalaignar reads it politically as tyranny collapsing when principled people rise up. The text is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Nahusha, raised to the throne of Indra, grows arrogant, makes the great sages carry his palanquin, and kicks Agastya; the sage’s curse hurls him from heaven to earth as a serpent.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Sections XI-XVII (Nahusha), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
A king at the summit of power fell in the middle of his reign because he offended a sage of great vows.
This is the story traditionally linked to this very couplet: even the king of the gods is broken midway when the great flare up.
Christianity
“He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree.”
— Luke 1:52 (KJV) [4]
Mary’s song praises God who topples the powerful from their thrones.
The Kural likewise says even kings can be brought down from their seats.
Islam
“Say, ‘O Allah, Owner of Sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will and You take sovereignty away from whom You will. You honor whom You will and You humble whom You will.’”
— Qur’an 3:26 (Saheeh International) [5]
The verse affirms that kingship is given and withdrawn by God.
As in the Kural, no king’s rule is so secure that it cannot be taken away.
Judaism
King Uzziah, grown proud, enters the Temple to burn incense, defying the priests who withstand him; he is struck with leprosy while still raging, and lives apart while his son governs.
— 2 Chronicles 26:16-21 [4]
A powerful and successful king was cut off from rule after defying those consecrated to holy service.
This mirrors the Kural’s king who is broken midway when he opposes those of high vows.
Buddhism
In the Sarabhanga Jataka, King Dandaki insults the ascetic Kisavaccha, and his kingdom is destroyed, becoming the wilderness later known as the Dandaka forest.
— Jataka 522, Sarabhanga Jataka (trans. ed. E. B. Cowell) [6]
A ruling king lost his realm because he offended an ascetic.
This matches the Kural’s warning that a king falls when those of lofty vows are provoked.
Jainism
“Enough of this conciliatory talk of yours, Kṛṣṇa. Beaten by your sons, I have made a nidāna—to burn Dvārakā with its people. There is no escape from that, except of you.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 8 (Neminātha-caritra), ch. 11, part 2 (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [7]
When Kṛṣṇa, king of Dvārakā, begs the injured ascetic for pardon, Dvaipāyana refuses and vows to burn the royal city, and Kṛṣṇa loses his capital and kingdom.
The Kural says that when those who hold high vows flare up in anger even a king is broken midway and loses his kingdom, and Kṛṣṇa, unable to appease an angered ascetic, sees his city destroyed.
Sikhism
“The slanderer of the Saint breaks down mid-way.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 280, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan says the slanderer is broken in mid-course, and elsewhere in the same passage that he has no kingdom.
The Kural says that when those of lofty principle flare up, even a king is broken midway and loses his kingdom, and Gurbani uses the same image of breaking midway.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Diomedes recalls how Lycurgus, king of the Edonians, attacked the nurses of the young Dionysus; Zeus blinded him and he did not live long, hated by all the gods.
— Homer, Iliad 6.130-140 [9]
A king who assaulted the sacred was cut down in the midst of his life and rule.
As in the Kural, even a king is broken midway when he provokes those under divine protection.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes in Politics V that many monarchies have been overthrown because of insult and contempt shown toward subjects, which provoke attacks on the ruler.
— Aristotle, Politics V.10 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Rulers who treat people with outrage invite their own overthrow.
The Kural similarly says that even kings fall when the high-minded are provoked to anger.

Real Life Example

The Seven Bishops are acquitted
30 June 1688 · Westminster Hall, London, England
In 1688 King James II ordered his Declaration of Indulgence to be read in churches, and the Archbishop of Canterbury and six other bishops petitioned him to withdraw the order, arguing that the royal power to suspend laws on which it rested was illegal. The king had them imprisoned in the Tower and tried for seditious libel, but on 30 June 1688 a jury acquitted them amid public celebration. That same day seven leading Englishmen invited William of Orange to intervene, and by the end of the year William had landed and James had fled the country.
— Sources: [11] [12]
By prosecuting the bishops, the king turned respected men of principle into public heroes and lost the confidence of the nation within months.
The kural says that when those of lofty principle are roused, even a king is broken midway and loses his kingdom, which describes James II's fall in 1688.
