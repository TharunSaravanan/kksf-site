Kural 1042

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

இன்மை எனவொரு பாவி மறுமையும் இம்மையும் இன்றி வரும்.
Inmai enavoru paavi maRumaiyum immaiyum indri varum.

Meaning

The evil thing called poverty, when it comes, takes away a person's happiness in this life and in the next life too.

Elementary

Think of a villain in a story who sneaks in and takes away both your lunch today and your treat for tomorrow. Valluvar calls poverty a villain like that: it takes away the good things you could enjoy now, and also the good you could do for others, which helps you later.

University

Valluvar personifies inmai as oru paavi (ஒரு பாவி), ‘a sinner, a wicked one’, a vivid figure for an abstract condition. The second line says it comes so that maRumai (மறுமை, the next world) and immai (இம்மை, this world) are both lost. Solomon Pappaiah spells out the logic the commentators share: the poor cannot give to others, so they lose the merit that secures the next world, and they have nothing to enjoy themselves, so they lose this world too; Puliyur Kesikan likewise speaks of losing worldly pleasure here and the bliss of heaven hereafter. Sivayogi Sivakumar instead reads the pair temporally (present and future), and Kalaignar secularises it as loss of peace now and later. The Kural thus ties wealth to aram (virtue) through the duty of giving, a link developed in the Arathuppaal chapters on Eekai (giving) and Oppuravu (helpfulness). The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He that is without wealth hath neither this world, nor the next, O best of men!”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna argues that without wealth a person can neither enjoy life nor perform the religious acts that secure the hereafter.
This is almost word for word the Kural’s claim that poverty takes away both this world and the next.
Christianity
“So shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth, and thy want as an armed man.”
— Proverbs 6:11 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs pictures poverty arriving like a traveller or an armed robber who overpowers the idle.
Like the Kural’s ‘wicked one’, poverty is personified as an intruder that comes and takes away.
Islam
“And He found you poor and made [you] self-sufficient.”
— Qur’an 93:8 (Saheeh International) [5]
God reminds the Prophet that he too was once poor, and that his poverty did not cut him off from divine favour.
The verse quietly counters the Kural: in Islam poverty need not cost a person the next world.
Judaism
“Three classes of people do not see the face of Gehenna... Those suffering the depths of extreme poverty, those afflicted with intestinal disease, and those oppressed by creditors.”
— Talmud Bavli, Eruvin 41b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
The Talmud teaches that the suffering of extreme poverty atones for sin, so the very poor are spared Gehenna.
Where the Kural says poverty loses the next world, the rabbis hold that it can secure it, a pointed contrast on the same question.
Buddhism
“But one with confidence, living at home, making gifts of his belongings, righteously-gained, wins both goals: advantage in the here-&-now, & happiness in the world beyond.”
— Ina Sutta, AN 6.45 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes the householder who gives from rightly earned wealth as winning benefit both now and hereafter.
This is the mirror image of the Kural: wealth used for giving wins both worlds, so its absence forfeits both.
Jainism
“He will sink through anger; he will go down through pride; delusion will block up his path; through greed he will incur dangers in both worlds.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.54 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Nami tells Indra that it is greed, not lack of wealth, that endangers a person in this world and the next.
The Jain sage relocates the Kural’s threat to both worlds from poverty to the craving for possessions.
Sikhism
“I am so hungry, I cannot perform devotional worship service. Here, Lord, take back Your mala.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 656, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir tells God bluntly that a starving man cannot pray, and goes on to ask only for simple flour, ghee, lentils and bedding so that he can worship with love. Gurbani here recognises that grinding want blocks both worldly life and spiritual life.
The Kural says poverty robs a person of the joys of this life and the good of the next; Kabir confesses that hunger has made even his devotion, his path to the next world, impossible.
Greek Mythology
In Aristophanes’ comedy Wealth (Plutus), Poverty (Penia) appears in person as a fearsome goddess, and Chremylus resolves to drive her out of Greece as the source of men’s miseries, while she argues that she is what keeps men industrious.
— Aristophanes, Plutus (Wealth), the Penia scene [10]
Greek imagination, like Valluvar’s, turned poverty into a personified being who stalks human households.
Penia as a menacing figure parallels the Kural’s ‘poverty, a wicked one’ who comes and takes everything.
Philosophy
“For it is better to die with hunger, exempt from grief and fear, than to live in affluence with perturbation.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 12 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus insists that peace of mind matters more than material security, even at the cost of hunger.
The Stoic denies the Kural’s premise that losing wealth means losing life’s good.

Real Life Example

Germany's hyperinflation wipes out a lifetime of savings
1923 · Germany
During 1923 the German paper mark collapsed as the government printed ever more banknotes, and by November it took about 4.2 trillion marks to buy a single US dollar. Middle-class Germans who lived on savings, pensions, investments or rents saw the provision they had built up over a lifetime become almost worthless, and the crisis was ended only by the introduction of the Rentenmark late in 1923.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The hyperinflation reduced respectable, provident families to want almost overnight, and historians link the experience to a lasting loss of trust in money and in the Weimar Republic.
The kural calls poverty a wicked thing that robs a person of both this life and the next, and the hyperinflation took from savers at a stroke both their comfort in the present and the security they had stored up for the years ahead.
