Kural 921

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

உட்கப் படாஅர் ஒளியிழப்பர் எஞ்ஞான்றும் கட்காதல் கொண்டொழுகு வார்.
Utkap pataaar oliyizhappar enjnjaandrum katkaadhal kondozhugu vaar.

Meaning

People who always crave alcohol will never be feared by their enemies, and they will lose the good name they once had.

Elementary

Imagine a strong, respected team captain who starts doing something silly every day. Soon nobody on the other team worries about him any more, and even his own friends stop looking up to him. The Kural says a love of strong drink does exactly that: it makes a person look small.

University

The chapter opens with two losses: the drinker is not utkappadaar (உட்கப்படாஅர், 'not feared', from உட்கு, awe or dread, with an alapedai lengthening) and he oliyizhappar (ஒளியிழப்பர், 'loses his lustre'), where oli (ஒளி) means both brightness and reputation. The habitual sense comes from katkaadhal kondozhuguvaar (கட்காதல் கொண்டொழுகுவார்), 'those who conduct themselves holding a love of toddy', so the target is addiction rather than a single lapse. Because the chapter sits in Porutpaal beside Varaivin Magalir (92) and Soothu (94), the traditional reading inherited from Parimelazhagar treats it as one of the vices a ruler must shun; Puliyur Kesikan's gloss on thirukkural.net explicitly makes the subject 'kings' whose enemies no longer fear them and who lose the fame won by their ancestors. Mu. Varadarasanar keeps the subject general. The couplet is identical on ytamizh and thirukkural.net, though the two sites attach the Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar glosses in opposite order.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Son killed sire, and sire killed son, O Bharata. Inebriated with wine, they rushed and fell upon one another.”
— Mahabharata, Mausala Parva, Section 3 (K. M. Ganguli trans.) [3]
At Prabhasa the invincible Vrishni and Andhaka heroes begin a drinking revel, quarrel, and destroy their own clan before Krishna's eyes.
A warrior people who were once feared lose all their glory through drink, as the Kural warns.
Christianity
“Woe unto them that rise up early in the morning, that they may follow strong drink; that continue until night, till wine inflame them! … but they regard not the work of the LORD, neither consider the operation of his hands.”
— Isaiah 5:11-12 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah condemns the leaders of Judah who spend their days drinking and lose sight of what truly matters.
The prophet's ‘woe’ on habitual drinkers matches the Kural's picture of a person whose constant craving ruins his standing.
Islam
“O you who have believed, indeed, intoxicants, gambling, [sacrificing on] stone alters [to other than Allāh], and divining arrows are but defilement from the work of Satan, so avoid it that you may be successful.”
— Qur'an 5:90 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an ties avoiding intoxicants directly to falah, true success and flourishing.
The Kural's drinker loses his lustre; the Qur'an promises success to those who abstain.
Judaism
“Wine is not for kings, O Lemuel; Not for kings to drink, Nor any strong drink for princes, Lest they drink and forget what has been ordained, And infringe on the rights of the poor.”
— Proverbs 31:4-5 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The queen mother of King Lemuel warns that rulers who drink forget the law and fail in justice.
Like the traditional royal reading of this Kural, Proverbs says drink is unworthy of a ruler and destroys his authority.
Buddhism
“Who plays with dice and drinks intoxicants, goes to women who are dear unto others as their own lives, associates with the mean and not with elders — he declines just as the moon during the waning half.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (Narada Thera trans.) [7]
The Buddha tells the young householder Sigala that drink is among the habits that make a man's fortunes wane.
The waning moon is a direct image of the Kural's oliyizhappar, the drinker who loses his light.
Jainism
“He is desirous of women and pleasures, he enters on undertakings and business, drinks liquor, eats meat, becomes strong, a subduer of foes. He eats crisp goats’ meat, his belly grows, and his veins swell with blood--but he gains nothing but life in hell, just as the ram is only fed to be killed for the sake of a guest.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.6–7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the Parable of the Ram, Mahāvīra describes the ignorant sinner who drinks and feasts and seems strong and victorious over his enemies, but is only being fattened for ruin, like a ram raised for slaughter.
Valluvar says the habitual drinker loses the awe of his enemies and the lustre of his name; the Jain text grants that the drinker may look strong and fearsome for a while, but says the real outcome is the same downfall.
Sikhism
The Sikh Rehat Maryada, the code of conduct issued by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, forbids Sikhs to take any intoxicant, naming hemp, opium, liquor and tobacco among them.
— Sikh Rehat Maryada (Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, Amritsar), English edition on sgpc.net (paraphrase) [9]
In Sikh practice abstaining from intoxicants is part of the discipline of a Sikh life, which is meant to keep the Sikh alert, self-governed and fit to serve. Drinking is treated as a breach of that discipline, not a private matter.
The Kural says the habitual drinker loses the awe of enemies and the lustre of his name, and the Rehat Maryada treats sobriety as part of the dignity a Sikh must keep.
Greek Mythology
The man-eating Cyclops Polyphemus, terror of Odysseus' crew, accepts strong wine, drinks himself into a stupor and is blinded by the very men he had trapped.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 9 (Butler trans.) [10]
A monster everyone feared becomes helpless as soon as wine takes hold of him.
Wine strips Polyphemus of the dread he inspired, as the Kural says the drinker is no longer feared.
Philosophy
“This evil has betrayed to their enemies the most spirited and warlike races; … this evil has conquered by the wine-cup those who in the field were invincible.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 83.22 (Gummere trans.) [11]
Seneca lists the military disasters drunkenness has brought on peoples and leaders, Alexander among them.
Seneca's point is the Kural's: the drinker stops being a threat to his enemies.

Real Life Example

General Mahabat Khan seizes the heavy-drinking Emperor Jahangir on the Jhelum
March 1626 · Imperial camp on the Jhelum River, Mughal Empire (now Pakistan)
The Mughal emperor Jahangir was a heavy drinker and opium user who, even in the settled middle years of his reign, kept a court official whose charge was the care of the imperial intoxicants. In March 1626 the general Mahabat Khan, summoned to court to answer charges, arrived at the imperial camp on the Jhelum River with about 5,000 Rajput soldiers and, finding the imperial household isolated on one bank, took the emperor captive. Nur Jahan's attempt to rescue her husband failed, and the royal couple remained in Mahabat Khan's hands for months before they managed to slip free later that year.
— Sources: [12] [13]
An emperor of the richest state in South Asia was seized in his own camp by one of his own commanders, a public humiliation that showed how little awe the throne then commanded.
The kural warns that those who live in love with drink are not feared by their enemies and lose their lustre, and the habitually intoxicated Jahangir was captured by his own general and had to be rescued by his wife.
