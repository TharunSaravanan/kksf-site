Kural 1100

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

கண்ணொடு கண்இணை நோக்கொக்கின் வாய்ச்சொற்கள் என்ன பயனும் இல.
Kannodu kaninai nokkokkin vaaichchorkal enna payanum ila.

Meaning

When eyes meet eyes and the looks agree, the words of the mouth are no use at all.

Elementary

Sometimes you and your best friend just look at each other and both burst out laughing, because you are thinking the same thing. You didn’t need to say a single word. Your eyes did all the talking.

University

Kaṇṇoṭu kaṇ iṇai nōkku okkin (கண்ணொடு கண்இணை நோக்கொக்கின்): ‘if the pair of eyes agree in look with the other pair’. Okkin (‘if they match’) is the key word: mutual, matching love, not one-sided longing. Vāyccoṟkaḷ (வாய்ச்சொற்கள்) are words of the mouth, which then have enna payanum ila, ‘no use whatever’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss: when eyes join in equal love, spoken words become unnecessary; Puliyur Kesikan specifies the two who are united in kāmam. The couplet closes the chapter by stating its principle: in love, the eyes are the true language, which is why the whole chapter has been about reading looks. Pope: ‘When eye to answering eye reveals the tale of love, / All words that lips can say must useless prove.’ The ytamizh transliteration writes kaN-inai, matching the Tamil கண்இணை; the text is identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Why, the good king shows his love for her in his tender glances, and he has been wasting away, as if he were losing sleep.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
Shakuntala’s friend says the king’s love is already plain from his glances, before he has said anything.
Love declared through the eyes, making words unnecessary, is the Kural’s point.
Christianity
“My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth.”
— 1 John 3:18 (KJV) [4]
John teaches that real love is shown in what is done rather than in what is said.
Both the epistle and the Kural place true love beyond mere words of the mouth.
Islam
“Souls are like recruited troops: Those who are like qualities are inclined to each other, but those who have dissimilar qualities, differ.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3336 [5]
The Prophet teaches that kindred souls recognise and are drawn to each other.
Two hearts that ‘agree’ at a glance, needing no words, is the instant recognition the hadith describes.
Judaism
“The soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.”
— I Samuel 18:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
At their first meeting Jonathan’s soul becomes bound to David’s.
An instant union of souls that needs no long speeches matches the Kural’s agreement of the eyes.
Buddhism
“If both husband & wife want to see one another not only in the present life but also in the life to come, they should be in tune [with each other] in conviction, in tune in virtue, in tune in generosity, and in tune in discernment.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells a devoted couple that being in tune with each other in deeper qualities is what keeps them together.
The Kural’s matching looks are a sign of the same deep agreement between two people.
Jainism
Rajimati was betrothed to Arishtanemi, who renounced the world on the way to their wedding; without any further exchange of words between them, she cut her hair and followed him into the ascetic life, and both reached perfection.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.27-30, 48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45); cf. the Nemidutakavya noted by Jacobi [8]
Rajimati’s devotion to Arishtanemi is shown by following his path, not by speeches.
Jain narrative gives a love so in tune that words are unnecessary, though it ends in shared renunciation rather than union.
Sikhism
“Says Nanak, the Saints savor this sublime essence, like the mute, who tastes the sweet candy, but only smiles.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1302, Raag Kaanra, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Kaanra, Guru Arjan compares the joy of union with God to a mute person tasting sweets: the delight is complete, but it can only be shown by a smile, never put into words.
The Kural ends by saying that when two pairs of eyes agree, words are of no use; Guru Arjan likewise says the deepest joy of love is beyond speech.
Greek Mythology
“So they two stood face to face without a word, without a sound, like oaks or lofty pines, which stand quietly side by side on the mountains when the wind is still.”
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.967-970 (tr. R. C. Seaton, 1912) [10]
When Medea and Jason meet in secret, they stand facing each other in silence, already joined in love.
Face to face with no words needed is the Kural’s final image.
Philosophy
“There is no less eloquence in the voice, in the eyes and in the air of a speaker than in his choice of words.”
— La Rochefoucauld, Maxims 249 (tr. J. W. Willis Bund and J. Hain Friswell) [11]
La Rochefoucauld notes that the eyes and manner speak as eloquently as words.
The Kural goes further, saying that when the eyes agree, words are not needed at all.

Real Life Example

The golden throne of Tutankhamun
about 1330s BCE (found 1920s) · Thebes (Valley of the Kings), Egypt; now in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo
Among the treasures found in the antechamber of Tutankhamun's tomb was a throne covered in gold and inlaid with silver, semi-precious stones and glass paste. Its back panel shows the young king seated and his queen, Ankhesenamun, facing him and reaching out to touch his shoulder, beneath the spreading rays of the sun. Howard Carter called it the most beautiful thing yet found in Egypt.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The scene is one of the most famous images of tenderness between a husband and wife to survive from the ancient world.
The kural says that when two lovers' looks meet, words are useless; the throne shows king and queen facing each other in a wordless moment of affection.
