Kural 716

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

ஆற்றின் நிலைதளர்ந் தற்றே வியன்புலம் ஏற்றுணர்வார் முன்னர் இழுக்கு.
Aatrin nilaithalarndh thatre viyanpulam Etrunarvaar munnar izhukku.

Meaning

Making a mistake in speech in front of people who understand many subjects is like slipping and falling off the right path.

Elementary

A spelling mistake in a note to your friend is no big deal. The same mistake in the final round of a spelling bee, in front of the judges, feels like tripping on stage. Mistakes count most in front of experts.

University

Viyanpulam (wide fields, meaning vast areas of learning) etrunarvaar (those who have received and understood them) are the most demanding audience there is, and an izhukku (slip or lapse) before them is compared to aatrin nilaithalarndhatru, losing one’s footing on the way. Commentators disagree about aaru. Parimelazhagar and most modern commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar) take it as the path of right conduct, so a verbal slip before the learned is as disgraceful as a good man’s moral lapse. Puliyur Kesikan takes aaru as a river and reads the image as a swimmer losing his footing mid-stream, so the lapse is dangerous and hard to recover from. The kural thus explains why 715’s restraint matters: before the learned the cost of error is very high. The text agrees in all sources; the sandhi text reads “ஆற்றி னிலைதளர்ந் தற்றே வியன்புல மேற்றுணர்வார் முன்ன ரிழுக்கு”.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At King Janaka’s sacrifice, the boy-sage Ashtavakra challenges Bandin, the court debater who had defeated and drowned many scholars. In the debate before the learned assembly Ashtavakra wins, and Bandin, now silenced, meets the same fate he had imposed on others.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Sections 132-134 (story of Ashtavakra), trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
The Mahabharata tells how a famous debater’s failure before an assembly of the learned brought his complete downfall.
Bandin’s fall is the Kural’s image made literal: a slip before the learned is a fatal loss of footing.
Christianity
“For in many things we offend all. If any man offend not in word, the same is a perfect man, and able also to bridle the whole body.”
— James 3:2 (KJV) [5]
James treats stumbling in speech as the hardest failing to avoid, and calls the person who never slips in word perfect.
The Kural’s izhukku, a slip of speech, is the stumbling James speaks of.
Islam
“A slave of Allah may utter a word without thinking whether it is right or wrong, he may slip down in the Fire as far away a distance equal to that between the east.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6477 (narrated by Abu Huraira) [6]
The Prophet warns that one careless word can make a person slip and fall a great distance.
The hadith uses the same picture of a verbal slip as a fall, as the Kural does.
Judaism
“To slip upon a pavement is better than to slip with the tongue: so the fall of the wicked shall come speedily.”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 20:18 (KJV Apocrypha) [7]
Ben Sira compares a slip of the tongue with a physical fall, and judges the verbal one worse.
This is the Kural’s own comparison, a verbal slip likened to losing one’s footing.
Buddhism
Saccaka, a famous debater, boasts that he can make the Buddha tremble. In front of a large assembly of Licchavis he is refuted, and then sits silent, dismayed, with shoulders drooping and head bowed, unable to reply.
— Culasaccaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 35 [8]
The sutta shows how a proud speaker’s reputation collapses at once when he stumbles before a discerning audience.
Saccaka’s humiliation is the Kural’s izhukku before the learned, a public loss of footing.
Jainism
“He should not pervert nor render obscure (the truth); he should fabricate neither text nor meaning, being a saviour; being devoted to the Teacher and considering well his words, he delivers faithfully what he has learned.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Jain expounder must speak with great care, because a slip in handing on the doctrine distorts the truth that others rely on.
The Kural compares a slip of speech before the deeply learned to falling from the path, and the Jain verse likewise treats error in learned speech as a serious lapse to guard against.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, speaking insipid words, the body and mind become insipid. He is called the most insipid of the insipid; the most insipid of the insipid is his reputation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak warns that careless, graceless speech spoils the speaker inwardly and ruins his name.
The Kural compares a slip of speech before the deeply learned to losing one's footing on the path; Guru Nanak says bad speech brings a fall in one's whole standing.
Greek Mythology
The Thracian singer Thamyris boasted that he could out-sing the Muses themselves. In the contest he failed before them, and they maimed him and took away his gift of song.
— Homer, Iliad 2.594-600 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Homer’s brief story shows how overreaching before the greatest masters of an art ends in disgrace.
Failing before the Muses, the ultimate learned audience, is the Kural’s slip before those of vast learning.
Philosophy
Socrates describes questioning men with a reputation for wisdom, first politicians, then poets and craftsmen, and exposing, often in front of listeners, that they did not know what they claimed to know. This earned him their lasting resentment.
— Plato, Apology 21b-22e [12]
Plato shows how damaging it is to have one’s claim to knowledge fail under the questioning of someone more discerning.
Socrates’ targets suffered what the Kural warns of: a lapse exposed before a keen examiner.

Real Life Example

Benjamin Disraeli's maiden speech is shouted down in the House of Commons
1837 · House of Commons, London, England, United Kingdom
Benjamin Disraeli, elected for Maidstone in 1837, was already ridiculed for his dandified appearance and affected manner when he rose to make his first speech in the House of Commons. Members heckled and mocked him, and he defiantly declared as he sat down that the time would come when they would hear him. He went on to become a celebrated parliamentary debater and, in 1868 and again from 1874, prime minister.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Before the most demanding audience in the country, a gifted speaker's misjudged first appearance turned into a famous humiliation that took him years to overcome.
The kural compares a slip in speech before learned and experienced people to losing one's footing on the path, and Disraeli's maiden speech was just such a fall before the Commons.
