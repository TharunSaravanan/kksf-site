Kural 593

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

ஆக்கம் இழந்தேமென்று அல்லாவார் ஊக்கம் ஒருவந்தம் கைத்துடை யார்.
Aakkam izhandhemendru allaavaar ookkam Oruvandham kaiththudai yaar.

Meaning

People who keep a firm hold on their inner drive do not cry, “We have lost our money.”

Elementary

If a strong wind knocks down your block tower, you can cry about it, or you can say ‘I still have my hands and my ideas’ and build again. People with real energy don’t sit around moaning about what they lost; they know they can make more.

University

The key image is ‘kaiththu udaiyaar’: those who hold ookkam as kaipporul, ready money in the hand, which Parimelazhagar reads as the secure capital that makes lost aakkam (prosperity) recoverable. ‘Oruvandham’ means certainly or firmly (nichchayamaaga), qualifying the grip on energy; ‘allaavaar’ (will not be distressed) derives from allal, affliction. Mu. Varadarasanar supplies the implied condition ‘even when prosperity has actually been lost’, while Kalaignar stresses that such people do not lose their ookkam along with their wealth. The couplet thus answers 592: if wealth departs, energy remains the seed of its return, which is why the chapter treats ookkam as a king’s fundamental resource. The Tamil text is identical in both base sources, differing only in word-splitting (izhandhemendru vs. izhandhaem endru).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One whose mind remains undisturbed amidst misery, who does not crave for pleasure, and who is free from attachment, fear, and anger, is called a sage of steady wisdom.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna describes the steady-minded person who does not fall apart when troubles come.
The Kural’s energetic person, like the Gita’s sthita-prajña, is not shaken into lament by loss.
Christianity
“I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need.”
— Philippians 4:12 (KJV) [4]
Paul says he has learned to stay steady whether he has plenty or nothing.
Like the Kural’s energetic person, Paul does not collapse into complaint when his resources vanish.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you.”
— Qur’an 57:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches believers not to be crushed by what they have lost nor puffed up by what they have gained.
The Kural likewise says the strong-spirited do not lament lost prosperity.
Judaism
“Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, And naked shall I return thither; The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; Blessed be the name of the LORD.”
— Job 1:21 (JPS 1917) [6]
Having lost his flocks, servants and children in one day, Job refuses to curse or despair.
Job embodies the Kural’s refusal to cry ‘we have lost our wealth’, though from faith rather than energy.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada pictures the wise as a rock that stays put whatever storms of fortune blow against it.
The Kural’s energetic person is similarly unshaken by the storm of lost prosperity.
Jainism
“Happy are we, happy live we who call nothing our own; when Mithilâ is on fire, nothing is burned that belongs to me.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
When Indra tells the renouncing King Nami that his city and palace are burning, Nami answers that he grieves at nothing because he no longer claims any of it as his own.
The Kural says those who hold energy firmly do not grieve over lost wealth; Nami likewise does not lament the loss of his kingdom, though his calm rests on non-possession rather than on confidence that energy will win wealth back.
Sikhism
“That man, who in the midst of pain, does not feel pain, who is not affected by pleasure, affection or fear, and who looks alike upon gold and dust”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 633, Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the person in whose heart God dwells as one who is not shaken by suffering and values gold no more than dust.
The Kural says those who firmly hold energy do not grieve over lost wealth; Guru Tegh Bahadur's steady person likewise does not lament loss, though his steadiness comes from detachment rather than confidence in winning wealth back.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus loses every ship, every companion and all his Trojan treasure, yet he washes up naked on Scheria and still presses on until he regains his home and kingdom.
— Homer, Odyssey, Books 5-13 [10]
Stripped of everything, Odysseus keeps his resourceful, tireless spirit and uses it to win back all he had.
He shows the Kural’s idea that energy held ‘in hand’ outlasts lost wealth and can restore it.
Philosophy
Seneca tells how Stilpo, whose city had been captured and his family and property lost, was asked by Demetrius whether he had lost anything, and replied that he had lost nothing, for all his goods were with him.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 9.18-19 [11]
The philosopher Stilpo insists that real goods live inside a person and cannot be plundered.
Like the Kural’s ookkam-holder, Stilpo does not say ‘I have lost my wealth’ because his real capital is within.

Real Life Example

Thomas Edison rebuilds after the West Orange factory fire
December 1914 · West Orange, New Jersey, United States
In December 1914 a fire destroyed much of the factory complex surrounding Thomas Edison's laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey, although the laboratory buildings themselves survived. The loss was very large, but Edison led the rebuilding of the works. He continued his business and research there through the First World War, during which he also chaired the Naval Consulting Board.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Edison treated the destruction of a great part of his property as a setback to be repaired rather than a disaster to be mourned.
The kural says those who firmly hold energy do not grieve over lost wealth, and Edison's response to the fire was to rebuild rather than lament.
