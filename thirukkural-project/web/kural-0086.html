Kural 86

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

செல்விருந்து ஓம்பி வருவிருந்து பார்த்திருப்பான் நல்வருந்து வானத் தவர்க்கு.
Selvirundhu ombi varuvirundhu paarththiruppaan, Nalvarundhu vaanath thavarkku.

Meaning

A person who cares for the guest who is leaving and watches the road for the next guest will be a welcome guest of the gods in heaven.

Elementary

Some people are glad when the visitors finally leave. This kural describes the opposite kind of person: he waves one guest off and then sits by the door looking down the road, hoping someone else will come. That person, it says, is exactly the kind of visitor heaven would love to have.

University

The verse is built on a triple play with விருந்து: செல்விருந்து (the departing guest), வருவிருந்து (the arriving guest), and நல்விருந்து (the good guest) — the host, by attending to the first two, becomes the third. Parimelalagar glosses the final phrase as meaning that the man is reborn a deva and becomes a welcome guest among those in heaven, while Manakkudavar adds the sharper practical note that 'watching for the arrival' means not eating at all until a guest is present. The verse thus introduces the chapter's only explicitly eschatological claim, and it does so by an elegant reversal: the perfect host is rewarded not with wealth or honour but by being transformed into the perfect guest. Note that the Tamil here reads நல்வருந்து rather than the expected நல்விருந்து; this is the received reading, attested across the manuscript tradition and reproduced by Parimelalagar, who nonetheless glosses it as நல்விருந்து.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Rantideva, after forty-eight days of famine and fasting, is about to break his fast when a brahmin arrives; he gives him his food. Then a śūdra comes, then an outcaste with dogs, then finally a thirsty man — and Rantideva surrenders even his last cup of water, saying he wants nothing but to relieve the suffering of beings. The gods then reveal themselves before him.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.21.2–18 (the story of Rantideva) [4]
The Bhagavata Purana's most celebrated hospitality narrative has guest after guest arrive in sequence, each one received more eagerly than the last.
Rantideva is the Kural's host who, having served one guest, is already waiting for the next — and is met by the heavenly ones as a result.
Christianity
“For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in:”
— Matthew 25:35 (King James Version) [5]
In the judgement scene, the King welcomes the righteous into the kingdom on the sole ground of how they treated hungry and strange arrivals, whom he identifies with himself.
Both passages make the earthly host into the honoured guest of heaven on the strength of how he received those who came to his door.
Islam
“Gardens of perpetual residence; they will enter them with whoever were righteous among their fathers, their spouses and their descendants. And the angels will enter upon them from every gate,”
— Qur'an 13:23 (Sahih International); the angels' greeting follows at 13:24 [6]
The passage pictures the reward of the faithful as a dwelling into which angels enter from every side to greet them with peace — the believer received as an honoured resident of heaven.
Supplies the same final image as the Kural: the righteous householder ends as the one being welcomed by heaven's inhabitants.
Judaism
On each night of Sukkot, Jewish households invite the ushpizin — seven exalted guests, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Aaron and David — into the temporary booth, and tradition holds that these heavenly visitors will not enter a sukkah unless the poor have also been welcomed there.
— Ushpizin custom of Sukkot (Zohar, Emor 103b; widespread liturgical practice) [7]
The custom, rooted in the Zohar, makes an ordinary family's booth simultaneously a place where earthly guests are fed and where celestial guests are received.
Enacts the Kural's double movement — the host who keeps receiving guests becomes a host to, and a guest of, heaven.
Buddhism
“As kinsmen welcome a dear one on arrival, even so his own good deeds will welcome the doer of good who has gone from this world to the next.”
— Dhammapada 220 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha uses the image of a traveller returning home to relatives who run out to greet him, and applies it to the merit that awaits a person after death.
Both verses picture the good householder arriving in the next world and being received there as a welcome guest.
Jainism
“To those men the gates of heaven shall never be closed, who with tender hearts and with a mind in accordance with their alms, greatly rejoicing, give even in poverty according to their ability, even as they did in the day of prosperity.”
— Nālaṭiyār ch. 10 'Liberality', st. 1 (= st. 91), English translation in The Indian Antiquary vol. II (1873) [9]
The Nālaṭiyār promises a heavenly birth to the steady, glad giver who keeps giving even when poor. In Jain terms this reward comes from merit (puṇya), not as a god's gift.
Valluvar's host, who tends the departing guest and watches for the next, becomes a welcome guest in heaven, and the Nālaṭiyār likewise opens heaven's gates to the habitual, glad-hearted giver.
Sikhism
“In the midst of this world, do seva, and you shall be given a place of honor in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak teaches that selfless service (seva) done in ordinary worldly life earns a seat of honour in God's presence.
As the Kural's perfect host becomes a welcome guest of the gods, the one who serves here is given a seat in the divine court.
Greek Mythology
“Aye, and the gods in the guise of strangers from afar put on all manner of shapes, and visit the cities, beholding the violence and the righteousness of men.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17.485–487 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb) [11]
A suitor rebukes Antinous for striking a beggar, warning that any unknown arrival might be a god in disguise testing how a household behaves.
Grounds the Kural's logic: because the next arrival at the door may be a heavenly being, the host who keeps watching the road is watching for heaven.
Philosophy
Derrida distinguishes conditional hospitality — extended to a guest whose name, status and arrival time are known — from unconditional or absolute hospitality, which means holding the house open to whoever may come, without demanding in advance that the arrival announce or identify himself.
— Jacques Derrida, Of Hospitality (1997), and 'Hostipitality' [12]
For Derrida the ethical charge of hospitality lies entirely in this readiness for the unexpected arrival, since a guest who has been fully vetted is no longer truly a stranger.
This is exactly the posture of the Kural's host — having finished with the guest he knows, he sits watching for the one who has not yet come.

Real Life Example

The hospice of the Great St Bernard Pass is founded
c. 1050 · Great St Bernard Pass, Switzerland
Around 1050 Bernard of Montjoux, archdeacon of Aosta, founded a hospice at the summit of the Great St Bernard Pass to shelter and protect travellers crossing the Alps. Canons have lived there ever since, welcoming every traveller, and from the seventeenth century the monks kept dogs that helped them find travellers lost or buried in the snow. Bernard was later venerated as a saint and the pass and the dog breed bear his name.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
The hospice community did not merely receive those who reached its door; it went out watching and searching for the travellers still on the road.
The kural honours the host who cares for the departing guest while watching for the one still to come, and the St Bernard monks spent centuries tending travellers and looking out for the next.
