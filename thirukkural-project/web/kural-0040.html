Kural 40

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

செயற்பால தோரும் அறனே ஒருவற்கு உயற்பால தோரும் பழி.
Seyarpaala thorum arane oruvarku, Uyarpaala thorum pazhi.

Meaning

What a person should do is always what is right. What a person should avoid is always what deserves blame.

Elementary

There are only two piles in life: things to do and things to keep away from. This kural puts everything kind and fair in the first pile, and everything you would be ashamed of in the second.

University

The chapter closes by compressing its whole argument into a definition. செயற்பால ('the doable', what belongs to the category of what should be done) is equated with அறன், and உயற்பால ('the avoidable') with பழி — blame, or what is blameworthy. The particles ஓரும் are treated by Parimelazhagar as அசைநிலை, metrical fillers carrying no independent sense, while the emphatic ஏ in அறனே is carried over to the second line by implication, making both halves exclusive. Manakkudavar adds a pointed reason for placing this verse last: Kural 38 had promised that virtue blocks rebirth, and a reader might suppose that good deeds could simply be added on top of bad ones — so Valluvar closes by insisting that the avoidance of blame is as constitutive of the definition as the doing of good. Notably, Valluvar chooses பழி (blame) rather than a word for sin, keeping his ethics answerable to public judgement rather than to divine law.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Pṛthā, that understanding by which one knows what ought to be done and what ought not to be done, what is to be feared and what is not to be feared, what is binding and what is liberating, is in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.30 (Bhaktivedanta, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna defines sāttvic intelligence by its capacity to sort actions into kārya and akārya — what must be done and what must not.
It uses the identical binary that closes the Kural's chapter: the whole of practical wisdom is knowing what to do and what to shun.
Christianity
“Let love be without dissimulation. Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good.”
— Romans 12:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul reduces ethical life to a double movement of recoil and attachment, addressed to the disposition rather than to particular rules.
It is the Kural's two-column summary in the same compressed imperative form.
Islam
“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful.”
— Qur'an 3:104, Ali 'Imran (Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
This is the locus classicus for al-amr bi'l-ma'ruf wa'l-nahy 'an al-munkar, the twin obligation to enjoin the recognised good and forbid the recognised wrong.
The Islamic formula and the Kural's couplet share not just content but structure — a paired positive and negative that together exhaust moral duty.
Judaism
“Shun evil and do good, seek amity and pursue it.”
— Psalms 34:15 (JPS/Sefaria Tanakh; numbered 34:14 in Christian Bibles) [6]
The psalm's instruction to the one who wants a good life pairs departure from evil with the doing of good as a single inseparable movement.
It is the Kural's two categories, stated in the same order and with the same finality.
Buddhism
“To avoid all evil, to cultivate good, and to cleanse one's mind — this is the teaching of the Buddhas.”
— Dhammapada 183, Buddhavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
Known as the Ovāda-pāṭimokkha, this verse is presented as the common teaching of all Buddhas, reducing the whole path to three clauses.
The first two clauses are Valluvar's two categories exactly, and the third recalls Kural 34's insistence on purity of mind.
Jainism
“One should desist from one thing, and practise another: desist from neglect of self-control, and practise self-control.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 31.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The 'Mode of Life' lecture opens by reducing the whole Jain discipline to one twofold rule: turn away from lack of restraint (asamyama) and take up restraint (samyama).
Like the Kural's closing definition, the Jain rule splits all conduct into what is to be done and what is to be shunned. The Jain criterion is restraint for the soul's sake, where the Kural speaks of public blame (pazhi).
Sikhism
“Virtue and vice do not come by mere words; actions repeated, over and over again, are engraved on the soul. You shall harvest what you plant.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 4, Japji Sahib, Pauri 20, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Japji, Guru Nanak teaches that virtue and sin (punn and paap) are made by repeated deeds, which leave their mark on the self and bear their own fruit.
Both close on the plain binary of good to be done and wrong to be avoided, judged by deeds rather than talk.
Greek Mythology
“To you, foolish Perses, I will speak good sense. Badness can be got easily and in shoals: the road to her is smooth, and she lives very near us. But between us and Goodness the gods have placed the sweat of our brows: long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the first; but when a man has reached the top, then is she easy to reach, though before that she was hard.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 286-292, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White [10]
Hesiod's two roads divide all conduct into a smooth descent toward kakotēs and a steep climb toward aretē, with the gods themselves having fixed the difference.
It renders the Kural's two categories as two roads, one to be taken and one to be shunned.
Philosophy
In the Crito, Socrates gets Crito to agree that one must never do wrong — not even in return for wrong, and not even when wronged first — and treats this as the single fixed premise from which every practical decision, including whether to escape from prison, must follow.
— Plato, Crito 49a-e [11]
The argument establishes wrongdoing as an absolute category to be shunned without exception, rather than something to be weighed against consequences.
Like the Kural, it reduces the whole of practical deliberation to one thing to be done and one thing to be avoided under all circumstances.

Real Life Example

Frances Kelsey refuses to approve thalidomide
1960-1962 · Washington, D.C., United States
In 1960, during her first month as a medical officer at the US Food and Drug Administration, Frances Oldham Kelsey was assigned the application to market thalidomide, a sleeping pill already sold in many countries. Despite constant pressure from the company, she refused to approve it until she had scientifically reliable evidence of its safety. About a year later researchers linked the drug to severe birth defects in thousands of babies in other countries; thalidomide was never marketed in the United States, and in 1962 President Kennedy gave Kelsey the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By doing exactly what her duty required and refusing what would have been blameworthy, Kelsey prevented a tragedy in her country and helped bring about stronger drug laws.
The kural says what ought to be done is virtue and what ought to be avoided is what deserves blame, and Kelsey held firmly to the one and refused the other under heavy pressure.
