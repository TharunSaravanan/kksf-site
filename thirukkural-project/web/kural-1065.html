Kural 1065

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

தெண்ணீர் அடுபுற்கை ஆயினும் தாள்தந்தது உண்ணலின் ஊங்கினிய தில்.
Thenneer adupurkai aayinum thaaldhandhadhu unnalin oonginiya thil.

Meaning

Even if it is only porridge as thin as clear water, nothing is sweeter than food you have earned with your own hard work.

Elementary

A sandwich you made yourself, even a plain one, can taste better than a fancy one someone handed you. The Kural says food you earned with your own work is the sweetest food of all.

University

Thenneer (தெண்ணீர், 'clear water') describes the gruel: adupurkai (அடுபுற்கை, 'cooked porridge', taken by Puliyur Kesikan as gruel of coarse grass-grain, pullarisi) so watery it looks like clear water. Thaal thandhadhu (தாள்தந்தது, literally 'what the foot/effort gave') is glossed by commentators following Parimelazhagar as what is brought by lawful effort (muyarchi); Puliyur Kesikan adds 'by effort joined with right conduct'. Oonginiyadhu il means 'there is nothing sweeter beyond it'. Pope renders it 'Nothing is sweeter than to taste the toil-won cheer, / Though mess of pottage as tasteless as the water clear.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man who cooketh in his own house, on the fifth or the sixth part of the day, with scanty vegetables, but who is not in debt and who stirreth not from home, is truly happy.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section CCCXI (Yaksha's questions) (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Asked by the Yaksha who is truly happy, Yudhishthira names the man who eats poor food at home but owes nothing to anyone.
Like the Kural, it finds the greatest happiness in a meagre meal that is one's own.
Christianity
“For thou shalt eat the labour of thine hands: happy shalt thou be, and it shall be well with thee.”
— Psalm 128:2 (KJV) [4]
The psalm promises happiness to the one who eats the fruit of his own labour.
The Kural likewise finds sweetness in food earned by one's own effort.
Islam
“Nobody has ever eaten a better meal than that which one has earned by working with one's own hands. The Prophet (ﷺ) of Allah, David used to eat from the earnings of his manual labor.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2072 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet says no food is better than food earned by one's own hands.
This is the Kural's claim nearly word for word: nothing is sweeter than eating what one's effort has earned.
Judaism
“One who benefits from his hard labor is greater than a God-fearing person … while with regard to one who benefits from his hard work, it is written: ‘By the labor of your hands you will live; you are happy and it is good for you’ (Psalms 128:2).”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 8a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud ranks a person who lives from his own labour above one who is merely God-fearing.
The Talmud, like the Kural, puts the joy of living from one's own effort at the top.
Buddhism
“There is the case where the son of a good family has wealth earned through his efforts & enterprise, amassed through the strength of his arm, and piled up through the sweat of his brow, righteous wealth righteously gained. When he thinks, ‘I have wealth earned through my efforts & enterprise ...’ he experiences bliss, he experiences joy.”
— Anana Sutta, AN 4.62 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha calls the joy of having honestly earned wealth one of the householder's kinds of bliss.
Like the Kural, the sutta finds a special sweetness in what one's own effort has gained.
Jainism
The Acaranga's account of Mahavira's ascetic years describes him living on rough food such as rice, pounded jujube and beans, accepting whatever plain fare came to him without craving for better.
— Acaranga Sutra I.8.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain scripture holds up Mahavira's contentment with coarse food as a model of freedom from desire.
The Kural also finds even the thinnest gruel sweet; the Jain stress is on non-attachment rather than on earning it.
Sikhism
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, Guru Nanak stayed at Saidpur (Eminabad) with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter, and ate his plain bread rather than attend the feast of the rich official Malik Bhago. He explained that Lalo's food came from honest labour, while Bhago's wealth was squeezed from the poor.
— Janamsakhi tradition, as summarised in the Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entries 'Bhago, Malik' and 'Lalo, Bhai' [9]
The story is the best-known Sikh lesson on honestly earned food. In the popular retelling, Guru Nanak squeezed Lalo's bread and milk came out, and squeezed Bhago's and blood came out.
The Kural says even watery gruel earned by one's own effort is the sweetest food; in the Lalo story, the carpenter's coarse bread, earned by his own work, is preferred to a rich man's feast.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, the poor old couple Baucis and Philemon serve disguised gods a humble meal of garden herbs, a little bacon, olives, cheese and eggs in plain earthenware, offered gladly from their own small store.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Baucis and Philemon) (paraphrase) [10]
Their meagre but honest meal, shared freely, is the most praised feast in the Metamorphoses.
Like the Kural's thin gruel, their humble food is made precious by being their own.
Philosophy
In his Letter to Menoeceus, Epicurus says that plain fare gives as much pleasure as a costly diet once the pain of want is removed, and that bread and water give the highest pleasure when brought to hungry lips.
— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus (in Diogenes Laertius X, tr. R. D. Hicks) (paraphrase) [11]
Epicurus argues that simple food fully satisfies and that self-sufficiency is a great good.
The Kural shares the claim that the plainest food can be the sweetest, though it ties the sweetness to effort.

Real Life Example

Millet exhibits The Gleaners
1857 · Paris, France
Jean-Francois Millet showed The Gleaners at the Paris Salon of 1857. The painting depicts three peasant women bent over a harvested field gathering the stray ears of wheat left by the harvesters, which the rural poor were customarily allowed to collect, while the abundant harvest is gathered in the distance. The Musee d'Orsay, which holds the painting, notes that Millet gave the women an emblematic dignity, and some critics of the time read the picture as a political statement.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Millet presented the poor winning their meagre bread by their own labour as worthy of the grandeur usually reserved for history painting.
The kural says that even gruel thin as water is sweetest when it is earned by one's own effort, and The Gleaners honours poor women gathering a scanty harvest with their own hands rather than asking for it.
