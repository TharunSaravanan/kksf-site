Kural 1016

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

நாண்வேலி கொள்ளாது மன்னோ வியன்ஞாலம் பேணலர் மேலா யவர்.
Naanveli kollaadhu manno viyangnaalam paenalar melaa yavar.

Meaning

Great people would not want even the whole wide world if it meant losing shame, the fence that guards them.

Elementary

A garden has a fence so goats can't come in and eat the flowers. Valluvar says good people keep a fence of shame around their hearts. Even if someone offered them the whole world, they wouldn't trade away that fence.

University

Naan veli (நாண்வேலி, 'the hedge or fence that is shame') is the central image. The melaayavar (மேலாயவர், 'the superior ones') will not paenal (பேணல், 'cherish, desire') the viyan gnaalam (வியன்ஞாலம், 'vast world') without taking (kollaadhu) that hedge; manno (மன்னோ) is an emphatic particle. Kesikan and Pappaiah, in the line of Parimelazhagar, take it to mean that the great choose shame rather than the wide world as their protection. Varadarasanar reads it as saying the great would not want to live in the wide world without the protecting hedge of shame. Pope gives a more poetic turn: ‘For men of lofty soul the earth's vast realms no charms retain’ unless the hedge stays intact. Sivayogi Sivakumar's modern reading, that one cannot rise to greatness without the hedge, is a minority interpretation. The text is identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Yama offers the boy Nachiketas long life, cattle, horses, gold, wide lands and every pleasure if only he will stop asking about what lies beyond death. Nachiketas refuses: let Yama keep the horses, the dance and the song; he will hold to the one boon he asked for.
— Katha Upanishad 1.1.23-29 [3]
The Upanishad praises the one who turns down the whole pleasant world for the sake of what is truly good (śreyas).
Nachiketas, like the Kural's great ones, would not take the vast world at the price of what guards his soul.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that winning the whole world is worthless if it costs a person his soul.
The same trade the Kural rejects: the vast world is not worth losing the inner safeguard.
Islam
“Once Allah's Messenger (ﷺ) passed by an Ansari (man) who was admonishing his brother regarding Haya'. On that Allah's Messenger (ﷺ) said, ‘Leave him as Haya' is a part of faith.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 24 (sunnah.com) [5]
A man scolds his brother for being too bashful, presumably because it cost him worldly advantage, and the Prophet defends the bashfulness as part of faith.
Like the Kural's great ones, the Prophet would rather keep haya' than gain worldly advantage by giving it up.
Judaism
“Tradition is a fence to the Torah; Tithes a fence to wealth, Vows a fence to abstinence; A fence to wisdom is silence.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:13 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Akiva describes protective practices as fences (seyag) that guard a deeper good.
This is the Kural's image of shame as a fence guarding the good person.
Buddhism
“Who in the world is a man constrained by conscience, who awakens to censure like a fine stallion to the whip? Those restrained by conscience are rare”
— Samyutta Nikaya 1.18, Hiri Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha pictures hiri (conscience, shame) as the restraint that keeps a person on the path, like a thoroughbred that answers to a mere touch of the whip.
Shame here is the guard of the noble person, as in the Kural's hedge.
Jainism
“…by hearing and understanding which the monks will reach a high degree of self-discipline, of Samvara, and of contemplation, will be well protected (by the three Guptis), will guard their senses, guard their chastity, and will thus never be remiss”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 16, prose introduction (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana sets out ten protective conditions around celibacy. Later Jain tradition calls these the nava vāḍa, literally ‘nine fences’, of brahmacarya.
Like the Kural's naan veli, these are deliberate fences that guard a monk's purity.
Sikhism
“If I were to become an emperor and raise a huge army, and sit on a throne, issuing commands and collecting taxes-O Nanak, all of this could pass away like a puff of wind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 14, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak imagines himself given an emperor's throne and power, and refuses to value it if it would make him forget the Name.
The Kural says the great would not want the whole wide world at the price of their hedge of shame; Guru Nanak would not want a whole empire at the price of forgetting God.
Greek Mythology
Through twenty years and the pressure of many powerful suitors who could give her a new household and status, Penelope keeps her faith with absent Odysseus. She puts them off with the trick of weaving and unweaving Laertes' shroud.
— Homer, Odyssey 2.93-110; 24.191-198 [10]
Penelope's guarded fidelity, which Homer praises as her renown, protects her and her house better than any worldly alliance could.
She is the Kural's noble one, who prefers the guard of honour to the gains the world offers.
Philosophy
Epictetus tells someone urging him to get money for his friends that if it can be had while keeping his self-respect, fidelity and magnanimity, he will get it. But if he is asked to lose his own proper good so that others can gain what is not truly good, the request is foolish and unjust.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 24 [11]
For the Stoic, a person's aidos (self-respect, modesty) and good faith are worth more than any external gain.
Like the Kural's great ones, Epictetus will not trade the guard of his own integrity for the goods of the world.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela rejects a conditional release from prison
10 February 1985 · Jabulani Stadium, Soweto, South Africa
On 31 January 1985 State President P. W. Botha offered Nelson Mandela, in prison since the Rivonia Trial, his release on condition that he renounce violence as a means of political change. Mandela refused, and on 10 February 1985 his daughter Zindzi read his written reply to a rally at Jabulani Stadium in Soweto, where he said he could not accept freedom while his organisation remained banned and his people were not free.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mandela chose to remain in prison, where he stayed for five more years, rather than accept personal freedom on terms he considered dishonourable to his cause.
The kural says the truly great would not take even the wide world without the hedge of shame, and Mandela declined freedom itself rather than accept it on dishonouring terms.
