Kural 534

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

அச்ச முடையார்க்கு அரணில்லை ஆங்கில்லை பொச்சாப் புடையார்க்கு நன்கு.
Achcha mutaiyaarkku aranillai aangillai Pochchaap putaiyaarkku nanku.

Meaning

A fort cannot protect someone who is full of fear. In the same way, nothing good can help someone who is careless.

Elementary

A castle with tall walls won't help a guard who is too scared to stand at his post. And a house full of good locks won't help if you forget to lock the door. What really protects you is what's going on inside your own head.

University

The couplet is built on a simile (உவமை) marked by ஆங்கு (‘in the same way’). Fear inside makes outer defences (அரண்) useless, and forgetfulness inside makes outer goods (நன்கு) useless. Parimelazhagar spells out the அரண் as forest, mountain and similar strongholds, and glosses நன்கு as wealth of every kind, called ‘the good’ because it is a means to good. His conclusion is that just as the stronghold of the fearful falls, the riches of the heedless perish. Pavanar adds armies to the list of advantages and derives நன்கு from நல் > நன். Manakkudavar sums the couplet up as saying that the heedless have no protection. By pairing அச்சம் (fear) with பொச்சாப்பு (heedlessness), Valluvar treats both as inner failures of a ruler's mind that no outward resource can make up for.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One must deliver himself with the help of his mind, and not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is, A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
Krishna teaches that the decisive ally or enemy is one's own mind, not any outer circumstance.
The Kural too says an inner defect, fear or forgetfulness, cancels every outward protection.
Christianity
“But know this, that if the goodman of the house had known in what watch the thief would come, he would have watched, and would not have suffered his house to be broken up.”
— Matthew 24:43 (KJV) [5]
Jesus points out that a house is lost not for lack of walls but for lack of watching.
This is the Kural's point: defences and possessions are useless to someone who is not vigilant.
Islam
“Allah has already given you victory in many regions and [even] on the day of Hunayn, when your great number pleased you, but it did not avail you at all, and the earth was confining for you with its vastness; then you turned back, fleeing.”
— Qur'an 9:25 (Saheeh International) [6]
At Hunayn the Muslim army trusted in its unprecedented size and was ambushed and scattered at first.
As in the Kural, a great outward advantage is worth nothing when the mind is complacent.
Judaism
“Then the five men departed, and came to Laish, and saw the people that were therein, how they dwelt in security, after the manner of the Zidonians, quiet and secure; for there was none in the land, possessing authority, that might put them to shame in any thing, and they were far from the Zidonians, and had no dealings with any man.”
— Judges 18:7 (JPS 1917) [7]
The Danite spies find Laish rich, remote and carelessly at ease. The tribe soon comes against the unsuspecting city and burns it (Judges 18:27-28).
Laish had every natural advantage but no vigilance, and its good fortune did it no good, as the Kural says.
Buddhism
“By effort and heedfulness, discipline and self-mastery, let the wise one make for himself an island which no flood can overwhelm.”
— Dhammapada 25, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [8]
The only true refuge, the Buddha says, is an island built within oneself out of effort and heedfulness.
The Kural too places real security in the inner state, not in outer walls.
Jainism
“Wealth will not protect a careless man in this world and the next. Though he had seen the right way, he does not see it, even as one in the dark whose lamp has suddenly been put out.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira teaches that outward advantages are useless to the careless, who lose even the insight they once had.
Valluvar says no advantage helps the heedless, just as no fortress helps the fearful; the Jain verse likewise says wealth cannot protect the careless.
Sikhism
“Raawan made castles and fortresses of gold, but he had to abandon them when he left.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1104, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir points to the mythical king Ravana, whose golden fortresses could not hold him or save him.
The Kural says no fortress helps the fearful and no advantage helps the heedless; Kabir likewise shows outer strongholds failing a ruler whose inner life had gone astray.
Greek Mythology
Aeolus gives Odysseus all the adverse winds tied up in an ox-hide bag, and the west wind carries his fleet home. With Ithaca in sight, Odysseus, worn out from steering for nine days, falls asleep. His crew open the bag hoping to find treasure, and the storm blows them all the way back.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.1-55 [11]
Odysseus had every advantage a sailor could want, and lost it all in one lapse of watchfulness.
This is the Kural's heedless man, for whom even the greatest good fortune does no good.
Philosophy
Machiavelli asks whether fortresses are useful to princes. He concludes that they help or harm according to circumstances, that the best fortress is not to be hated by one's people, and that the Countess of Forlì's citadel did not save her in the end.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 20 (trans. W. K. Marriott) [12]
For Machiavelli the stone walls matter less than the political condition inside them.
The Kural's thought is similar: a stronghold cannot make up for a defect within.

Real Life Example

The Dutch raid on the Medway
June 1667 · River Medway, Chatham, Kent, England
During a war between England and the Dutch Republic, while peace talks were under way at Breda, much of the English fleet lay laid up in the River Medway near the royal dockyard at Chatham, above a defensive chain across the river. In June 1667 a Dutch fleet under Michiel de Ruyter took Sheerness, sailed up the Medway, broke through the chain, burned a number of English ships and towed away the English flagship Royal Charles, while the English sank some of their own large ships to block the channel. The raid put pressure on Charles II, and the Dutch obtained better terms in the Treaty of Breda.
— Sources: [13] [14]
England possessed one of the strongest navies of its day, yet ships left idle behind a weak boom could not protect even their own home dockyard.
The kural says that just as no fortress helps the fearful, no advantage helps the heedless, and England's great warships were of no use while they lay unready in the Medway.
