Kural 988

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

இன்மை ஒருவற்கு இளிவன்று சால்பென்னும் திண்மைஉண் டாகப் பெறின்.
Inmai oruvarku ilivandru saalbennum thinmaiun daakap perin.

Meaning

Being poor is no shame for a person who has the inner strength called perfect character.

Elementary

Some people have lots of toys and money, and some have very little. Valluvar says having little is nothing to be ashamed of, as long as you are honest, kind and strong inside. That inner strength is worth more than any treasure.

University

Inmai (இன்மை, 'having nothing, poverty') is not ilivu (இளிவு, 'disgrace') for one who has gained the thinmai (திண்மை, 'firmness, solidity') called saalbu. The image of perfection as a kind of solidity anticipates the stability of Kural 989. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Karunanidhi all read inmai as material poverty, and Karunanidhi adds that such a person treats saalbu itself as wealth; Sivayogi Sivakumar widens inmai to 'hardships'. Textual note: ytamizh.com reads இளிவன்று (ilivandru, 'is not disgrace'), the standard reading supported by the commentaries' gloss இழிவு; thirukkural.net's main text prints இனிவன்று, which appears to be a typographical error (ன for ள), since its own commentaries gloss the word as இழிவு. This edition follows இளிவன்று. Placed just before the chapters on Nalkuravu (Poverty, ch. 105) and Iravu (Begging, ch. 106), the couplet establishes that character, not wealth, sets the limits of dignity.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, a poor brahmin friend of Krishna's boyhood (known in later tradition as Sudama or Kuchela), content and self-controlled despite his poverty, visits Krishna at Dvaraka carrying only a handful of flattened rice; Krishna receives him with great honour.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.80-81 [3]
The story shows that the poor man's devotion and contentment made him worthy of the highest honour despite his lack of wealth.
Like the Kural, it presents poverty as no disgrace to a person of firm character.
Christianity
“Better is the poor that walketh in his uprightness, than he that is perverse in his ways, though he be rich.”
— Proverbs 28:6 (KJV) [4]
The proverb ranks an upright poor person above a rich person of crooked ways.
This is the Kural's point: integrity removes any shame from poverty.
Islam
“Wealth is not in having many possessions, but rather (true) wealth is feeling sufficiency in the soul.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6446 (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that real wealth lies in inner contentment, not in property.
Like the Kural, it relocates worth from possessions to inner strength.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot, as it is said: “You shall enjoy the fruit of your labors, you shall be happy and you shall prosper” (Psalms 128:2)”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines wealth as being content with what one has.
This supports the Kural's view that lack of possessions does not diminish a person of firm character.
Buddhism
“Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth. A trustworthy person is the best kinsman, Nibbana the highest bliss.”
— Dhammapada 204 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names contentment as the greatest wealth.
Like the Kural, it implies that a person rich in inner qualities is not truly poor.
Jainism
“Happy are we, happy live we who call nothing our own; when Mithilâ is on fire, nothing is burned that belongs to me.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Nami, having renounced his kingdom, tells the disguised god Indra that he lives happily owning nothing, so even the burning of his former city costs him nothing.
Like the Kural, it shows that having nothing is no disgrace, and even a joy, to one of firm spiritual character.
Sikhism
“No one respects the poor man. ... Says Kabir, he alone is poor, who does not have the Naam, the Name of the Lord, in his heart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1159, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir begins by describing how the world despises the poor and fawns on the rich. He ends by turning the word around: the only real poverty is to lack the Name of God within.
The Kural says poverty is no disgrace to one who has the inner strength of perfect character, and Kabir likewise denies that poverty of goods is real poverty for someone inwardly rich.
Greek Mythology
“... I have no time to give either to any public matter of interest or to any concern of my own, but I am in utter poverty by reason of my devotion to the god.”
— Plato, Apology 23b-c (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Socrates tells the Athenian jury that his philosophical mission has left him in complete poverty.
Socrates' poverty, worn without shame, illustrates the Kural's claim that want is no disgrace to the person of real worth.
Philosophy
Seneca tells Lucilius that honest poverty is not a bad thing, and that it is not the man who has too little but the man who craves more who is poor.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 2.6 (cf. tr. Richard M. Gummere) [11]
The Stoic argues that poverty is a matter of craving, not of possessions.
This supports the Kural's view that lack of wealth does not lower a person of firm character.

Real Life Example

Spinoza declines the Heidelberg chair and lives by grinding lenses
1673 · The Hague, Dutch Republic (now the Netherlands)
The philosopher Baruch (Benedict de) Spinoza supported himself modestly by grinding optical lenses for spectacles and microscopes. In 1673 he was offered the chair of philosophy at the University of Heidelberg, a prestigious post, and declined it because he feared he would not have full freedom to teach and think as he wished. He continued to live simply in The Hague until his death in 1677.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Spinoza chose a plain, modest life of independent work over position and comfort, and his modest means did nothing to lessen his standing.
The kural says that poverty is no disgrace to someone with the firmness called perfect character, and Spinoza's modest circumstances never diminished the respect in which he was held.
