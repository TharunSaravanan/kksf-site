Kural 528

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

பொதுநோக்கான் வேந்தன் வரிசையா நோக்கின் அதுநோக்கி வாழ்வார் பலர்.
Podhunokkaan vendhan varisaiyaa nokkin Adhunokki vaazhvaar palar.

Meaning

If a king does not treat everyone the same but gives each person the respect their worth deserves, many people will stay close to him because of it.

Elementary

A good coach doesn’t give every player the same job. The fastest runner plays forward, and the one with the best hands becomes goalkeeper. When each person feels truly seen for what they’re good at, they all want to stay on the team.

University

The couplet contrasts பொது நோக்கு (‘common or uniform regard’) with வரிசை நோக்கு (‘regard according to rank or order’). Parimelazhagar reads வரிசை as each person’s own worth (தத்தம் தகுதி). He explains that uniform treatment is rejected because it drives away the superior, who feel slighted, while graded regard is prescribed because it keeps everyone loyal. This makes it a principle of proportional, not arithmetic, equality, very like Aristotle’s distributive justice. With this kural Parimelazhagar closes the four-verse unit (525-528) on the ‘means of embracing kin’. Karunanidhi’s modern gloss softens the hierarchy (‘all people are equal, but use each according to ability’), and Pappaiah places the kural explicitly within the ruler’s treatment of his kin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Laws of Manu list wealth, kindred, age, the performance of sacred rites and sacred learning as grounds for respect, each later one weighing more, so that honour is to be given by graded merit rather than uniformly.
— Manusmriti 2.136 (trans. G. Bühler, SBE vol. 25) [3]
Manu sets out a scale for how much respect each person should receive.
This is graded regard (வரிசை நோக்கு) rather than uniform treatment (பொது நோக்கு), as the Kural prescribes.
Christianity
“And unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability;”
— Matthew 25:15 (KJV) [4]
In the parable, the master entrusts each servant with a share matched to his ability.
Treating each person according to capacity rather than uniformly is the Kural’s principle.
Islam
“And it is He who has made you successors upon the earth and has raised some of you above others in degrees [of rank] that He may try you through what He has given you.”
— Qur’an 6:165 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an treats differences of rank among people as part of the divine order and a test.
People are not all placed alike, so treating them according to rank reflects that order, as in the Kural.
Judaism
At the end of his life Jacob gathers his twelve sons and blesses each one differently, according to his character and deeds; the text notes that he blessed every one according to his own blessing.
— Genesis 49:1-28 [6]
The patriarch does not bless his sons all alike but fits each blessing to the son.
Individual rather than uniform regard among kin is exactly the Kural’s வரிசை நோக்கு.
Buddhism
“Generosity, sweet speech, Helpfulness to others, Impartiality to all, As the case demands.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The closing verse of the Sigalovada lists the four ‘winning ways’ and qualifies them as applied ‘as the case demands’.
‘As the case demands’ calls for the fitting treatment of each person that the Kural recommends.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra teaches four right attitudes rather than one uniform one: friendliness toward all living beings, joy at the sight of the more virtuous, compassion for the afflicted, and equanimity or tolerance toward the insolent and ill-behaved.
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.11 (Digambara numbering; English rendering at Jain Square) [8]
Jain ethics asks a person to adjust his inner regard to the condition and merit of each being he meets.
The Kural advises regarding each person according to his worth instead of treating all alike; the Tattvartha's four attitudes likewise grade one's regard by the other's virtue and situation.
Sikhism
Guru Nanak passed over his own two sons and appointed his devoted disciple Lahina, renamed Angad, as his successor, because Lahina's conduct persuaded him that he was the more suitable choice.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Sikhism: Guru Angad” by W. H. McLeod (paraphrase) [9]
Nanak judged by proven worth rather than by family claim when choosing who would lead the community.
The Kural tells the ruler to give each person the regard their merit deserves rather than the same to all; Guru Nanak's choice of a successor by merit rather than birth is a Sikh instance of weighing people by worth.
Greek Mythology
After defeating the Titans, the gods urge Zeus to rule, and he divides the privileges (timai) among them, giving each god a fitting share of honour, which secures their loyalty to his reign.
— Hesiod, Theogony 881-885 [10]
Zeus keeps his divine kin loyal by giving each a distinct and appropriate honour.
This is graded regard keeping the circle together, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“To kinsmen, too, and fellow-tribesmen and fellow-citizens and to every other class one should always try to assign what is appropriate, and to compare the claims of each class with respect to nearness of relation and to virtue or usefulness.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.2 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that we owe different things to different people and must weigh each person’s closeness and merit.
This is the Kural’s வரிசை நோக்கு, regard given to kin according to their worth.

Real Life Example

Peter the Great issues the Table of Ranks
24 January 1722 · St Petersburg, Russia
Peter the Great's Table of Ranks arranged Russia's military, naval and civil services into a hierarchy of fourteen grades and based promotion on personal ability and performance rather than on birth and family descent. Anyone who reached the eighth rank gained the status of a hereditary noble, which caused resentment among parts of the old aristocracy that lost its exclusive claim to high office. With minor changes, the system remained in use until 1917.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By ranking servants of the state according to service and merit, Peter tied capable people of many backgrounds to the throne while keeping a clear order of precedence.
The kural says a king who gives each person the regard their merit deserves, rather than treating everyone the same, will have many staying by him, and the Table of Ranks wrote merit-based, graded regard into law.
