Kural 69

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

ஈன்ற பொழுதின் பெரிதுவக்கும் தன்மகனைச் சான்றோன் எனக்கேட்ட தாய்.
Eendra Pozhudhin Peridhuvakkum Thanmakanaich, Chaandron Enakketta Thaai.

Meaning

A mother who hears people call her son a good and wise man feels more joy than she did on the day he was born.

Elementary

The day a baby is born is one of the happiest days a mother ever has. But this kural says there is a day that beats it: the day she overhears other people saying her child has grown up to be a genuinely good person.

University

The couplet's force comes from the word சான்றோன் (cāṉṟōṉ), from the root சால் — "filled up, complete, sufficient." A cāṉṟōṉ is not merely a clever or successful man but one who is full in character, and the term carries enormous weight in Tamil ethical vocabulary; the Kural devotes a whole later chapter, சான்றாண்மை, to the quality. The second load-bearing word is கேட்ட (kēṭṭa, "having heard"): the mother's supreme joy arrives not from seeing but from hearing others say it. Parimēlaḻakar reads this carefully, arguing that the ordinary joy of birth is the joy of what is seen with the eyes on one's lap, while this is the distinct and greater joy of hearing one's child pronounced complete by those qualified to judge. He also notes that the couplet names the mother specifically because her joy admits of no measure, which is why Valluvar separates it from the father's case in 67. Read alongside 70, the chapter closes with a matched pair — the mother's joy, then the father's requital — with public reputation as the currency of both.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kausalyā's happiness in the Rāmāyaṇa is repeatedly portrayed not as pride in having borne a prince but as joy at the universal testimony of Ayodhyā's people to Rāma's righteousness — and her deepest grief, at his exile, is grief at being separated from a son whom everyone calls good.
— Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Ayodhyā Kāṇḍa (Kausalyā and Rāma) [5]
The epic consistently locates a mother's happiness in what the community says about her son's character rather than in his rank or her own act of bearing him.
Both texts make the mother's great joy depend on hearing her son publicly reckoned a man of worth.
Christianity
“I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth.”
— 3 John 1:4 (King James Version) [6]
The elder writing to Gaius reports that travelling brethren have brought back news of Gaius's conduct, and says that receiving such a report is the height of his happiness.
This is the Kural's exact structure — the joy is in hearing the report of a child's moral worth, and it is named as the greatest joy there is.
Islam
“And when she delivered her, she said, 'My Lord, I have delivered a female.' And Allah was most knowing of what she delivered... Every time Zechariah entered upon her in the prayer chamber, he found with her provision. He said, 'O Mary, from where is this [coming] to you?'”
— Qur'an 3:36–37, Sūrat Āl ʿImrān (Sahih International) [7]
The wife of ʿImrān dedicates her child before birth and is initially disappointed at the outcome; the narrative then vindicates the daughter through Zechariah's repeated astonishment at Mary's standing with God.
It touches the Kural's theme — a mother's estimate of her child transformed by what is later witnessed of that child's worth — but only obliquely.
Judaism
“The father of the righteous shall greatly rejoice: and he that begetteth a wise child shall have joy of him. Thy father and thy mother shall be glad, and she that bare thee shall rejoice.”
— Proverbs 23:24–25 (King James Version) [6]
The proverb moves from the general case to a direct address, and singles out the woman who gave birth as the one who will rejoice.
The phrase "she that bare thee shall rejoice" is almost a translation of the Kural's ஈன்ற... தாய் — the one who bore you, rejoicing at your wisdom.
Buddhism
Rūpasārī, the brahmin mother of Sāriputta, had long resented her son's renunciation; on the night of his death in the room where he was born, she saw the great deities of the heavens come in succession to attend him, realised what her son actually was, and only then rejoiced in him — after which he taught her and she attained stream-entry.
— Pali commentarial tradition on Sāriputta's death (Dhammapada Commentary; see also SN 47.13) [8]
The commentarial tradition preserves this as the story of a mother who came late and overwhelmingly to the knowledge of her son's true stature.
Like the Kural, it makes the mother's supreme joy arrive not at birth but at the moment her son is recognised for what he has become.
Jainism
“Then the eye-disease was washed away like mud by Lady Marudevī’s copious tears of joy, when she heard this. She saw her son’s Tirthakṛt-splendor accompanied by the supernatural powers, and from joy at the sight of that, her absorption-in-that took place.”
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book I (Âdîshvaracharitra), ch. III, part 12, 'Marudevî's omniscience and death' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS, 1931) [9]
Marudevî had wept herself nearly blind with grief for her son Rishabha, the first Tîrthankara, who had become a wandering ascetic; when Bharata told her of her son's glory, her tears of joy healed her eyes and she attained omniscience and liberation then and there.
A mother whose joy on hearing her son praised exceeds anything she felt before is exactly this kural's picture, and the Jain account makes that joy powerful enough to liberate her.
Sikhism
“O son, this is your mother’s hope and prayer, that you may never forget the Lord, Har, Har, even for an instant. May you ever vibrate upon the Lord of the Universe.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 496, Raag Gujri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In this hymn, known among Sikhs as 'Poota Mata Ki Asees' (a mother's blessing to her son), Guru Arjan puts into a mother's mouth her deepest wish: not wealth or rank for her son, but a life of constant remembrance of God.
Like Valluvar's mother, whose greatest joy is to hear her son called a person of true worth, this mother's highest hope is fixed on her son's character, not on his birth or fortune.
Greek Mythology
“She then, seized with wonder, went back to her chamber, for she laid to heart the wise saying of her son.”
— Homer, Odyssey 1.360 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb Classical Library, 1919) [11]
Penelope has come down to ask the bard to stop his song; Telemachus, for the first time in the poem, answers her as a grown man and sends her back upstairs — and Homer records that what she takes away is not offence but amazement at his wisdom.
It is the Kural's moment precisely: a mother's wonder at hearing her son reveal himself as a man of sense.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that mothers who have given their children away to be raised elsewhere go on loving them and rejoice when they recognise them prospering, without ever seeking to be loved in return — because loving, rather than being loved, is what the affection of a parent consists in.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.8, 1159a (Bekker) [12]
For Aristotle a mother's characteristic pleasure is in the child's good as such, not in any benefit she receives from it.
It explains philosophically why the Kural's mother rejoices at a report she gains nothing from — it is the good of the child, heard about, that constitutes the joy.

Real Life Example

Einstein sends his ailing mother news that his theory has been confirmed
27 September 1919 · Berlin, Germany
In September 1919 Albert Einstein learned by telegram from the physicist H. A. Lorentz that British eclipse expeditions had confirmed the bending of starlight by the Sun predicted by his general theory of relativity. He at once sent a postcard with the good news to his mother, Pauline, who was seriously ill, and in the same card wished he could keep her company. Einstein became world-famous after the results were announced in November 1919, and his mother, whom he brought to his Berlin home at the end of that year, died there in February 1920.
— Sources: [13] [14]
In the last months of her life, a mother heard that her son had been proved right before the whole scientific world.
The kural says a mother's joy on hearing her son called a person of true worth exceeds her joy at his birth, and Pauline Einstein lived just long enough to hear her son acclaimed.
