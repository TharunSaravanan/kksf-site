Kural 962

Honour · Maanam · மானம்

சீரினும் சீரல்ல செய்யாரே சீரொடு பேராண்மை வேண்டு பவர்.
Seerinum seeralla seyyaare seerodu Peraanmai vendu pavar.

Meaning

People who want both a good name and true greatness will not do anything shameful, even if it would make them famous.

Elementary

Some people will do anything to become famous, even mean or silly things. This kural says that people who want to be both well-known and truly good will never do something shameful just to get noticed.

University

The couplet plays on seer (சீர், renown, excellence): seerinum (சீரினும், 'even for renown') and seeralla (சீரல்ல, 'what is not excellent', unworthy deeds). Those who desire seerodu peraanmai (சீரொடு பேராண்மை), renown together with great 'manliness' or nobility of spirit, do not do such things. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses peraanmai as பெரிய ஆண்மை and the unworthy acts as those unfit for kudi-p-perumai (family honour); Kalaignar reads it as a heroic life that will not chase fame by acts contrary to self-respect. The point sharpens the previous kural: not only necessity but even fame itself cannot justify a lowering act. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Katha Upanishad, Yama teaches Nachiketas that the good (shreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both come to a person; the wise examine them and choose the good, while the foolish choose the pleasant and miss the true goal.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2 [3]
The Upanishad separates what is truly good from what merely looks attractive, and praises choosing the good.
The Kural's noble person likewise refuses an attractive gain (fame) when it requires an unworthy act.
Christianity
“Again, the devil taketh him up into an exceeding high mountain, and sheweth him all the kingdoms of the world, and the glory of them; And saith unto him, All these things will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me. Then saith Jesus unto him, Get thee hence, Satan”
— Matthew 4:8-10 (KJV) [4]
Jesus is offered all the glory of the world on condition of one unworthy act, and refuses.
A vivid case of the Kural's rule: glory is not worth an act that lowers one.
Islam
“Whoever desires honor [through power] - then to Allah belongs all honor. To Him ascends good speech, and righteous work raises it. But they who plot evil deeds will have a severe punishment, and the plotting of those - it will perish.”
— Quran 35:10 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran says true honour comes only through good speech and righteous work, while evil schemes come to nothing.
Like the Kural, it teaches that real honour cannot be reached by unworthy means.
Judaism
“A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches, And loving favour rather than silver and gold.”
— Proverbs 22:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs ranks a good name above wealth.
The Kural adds that a good name joined to nobility is not to be bought even with fame won unworthily.
Buddhism
“Gains, Offerings, Fame, & Status wrongly gained, and whoever would praise self & disparage others. That, Namuci, is your army, the Dark One's commando force.”
— Sutta Nipata 3.2, Padhana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu; accesstoinsight.org) [7]
The Bodhisatta names fame and status gained wrongly as part of Mara's army that he must defeat.
The Kural too treats fame won by unworthy deeds as something the noble refuse.
Jainism
“The excellent will not do the things which will bring upon them the effects of sin in another birth, or disgrace in this birth, though it should cost them their lives.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 30 (மானம், 'Honour'), stanza 5 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain quatrain says the noble avoid any act that brings sin in the next life or disgrace in this one, whatever the cost.
This matches the Kural's claim that the honourable will not do unworthy deeds even for renown.
Sikhism
“with a good name and reputation, with praise and fame throughout the world- still, if the Lord does not bless you with His Glance of Grace, then who cares? What is the use?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Japji Sahib (pauri 7), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the seventh pauri of Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak says that even worldwide fame is worth nothing without God's grace.
The Kural says those who want true glory will not do unworthy deeds even for fame; Guru Nanak likewise puts fame below something higher.
Greek Mythology
In Prodicus' fable, the young Heracles meets two women at a crossroads: Vice offers him an easy, pleasant road, while Virtue offers a hard road of toil that leads to true and lasting glory; he chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus' Choice of Heracles, in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 [10]
Heracles chooses the road where glory is earned honestly over an easier road of pleasure.
Like the Kural's seekers of renown with nobility, he wants glory only on worthy terms.
Philosophy
Epictetus says that if he can gain wealth while keeping himself modest, faithful and high-minded, he will; but if he must give up his own proper good so that others can gain what is not truly good, it is unjust and foolish to ask it.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 24 [11]
Epictetus will accept outward gains only if they leave his character intact.
The Kural applies the same test to fame: it is welcome only if no unworthy act is needed to get it.

Real Life Example

Lawrence Lemieux stops racing to rescue rival sailors at the Seoul Olympics
September 1988 · Off Busan, South Korea (1988 Seoul Olympic Games)
During a Finn-class race at the 1988 Olympic sailing regatta, held in strong winds off Busan, Canadian sailor Lawrence Lemieux was running in second place overall when he saw that two sailors from Singapore had been thrown from their capsized boat. He left the course, pulled the injured sailors from the water and stayed with them until a patrol boat arrived, then rejoined the race and finished far back, losing his chance of a medal. The International Olympic Committee awarded him the Pierre de Coubertin Medal for sportsmanship.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Lemieux showed that an Olympic medal was worth less to him than doing what was right by other competitors in danger.
The kural says those who want true nobility will not do anything unworthy even if it would win them fame, and Lemieux would not sail on toward a medal while others were in danger.
