Kural 980

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

அற்றம் மறைக்கும் பெருமை சிறுமைதான் குற்றமே கூறி விடும்.
Atram maraikkum perumai sirumaidhaan kutrame koori vidum.

Meaning

Greatness hides other people's weak points. Smallness talks about nothing but their faults.

Elementary

If a friend trips and falls in the playground, a kind person helps them up and doesn't laugh. A mean person runs around telling everyone, 'Did you see him fall?' Valluvar says greatness is the helping hand, and smallness is the tattletale.

University

Atram (அற்றம்) means a weak, exposed or shameful point, literally a gap or defenceless state; maraikkum (மறைக்கும்) is 'conceals'. Against this, sirumai kutrame kooRi vidum (குற்றமே கூறி விடும்): 'littleness will speak out only faults', the emphatic -e (ஏ) on kutram marking that it says nothing else. Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah expand the contrast: the great speak of others' merits and hide their failings, the small hide their merits and broadcast their failings. Pope's 'Greatness will hide a neighbour's shame; / Meanness his faults to all the world proclaim' follows this reading. The chapter thus ends on how the great treat others, after nine couplets on how they hold themselves. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…truthfulness; freedom from anger; renunciation; tranquillity; aversion to faultfinding; compassion for all living entities…”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Among the divine qualities Krishna lists apaiśunam, aversion to faultfinding.
It names as a godly trait the very restraint the Kural calls greatness.
Christianity
“And above all things have fervent charity among yourselves: for charity shall cover the multitude of sins.”
— 1 Peter 4:8 (KJV) [4]
Peter teaches that love covers over others' failings.
Like the Kural, it makes covering others' faults the sign of a large heart.
Islam
“…he who conceals (the faults) of a Muslim, Allah would conceal his faults in the world and in the Hereafter.”
— Sahih Muslim 2699a (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet promises that whoever covers a fellow believer's faults will have his own covered by God.
This is the Kural's greatness that hides another's weakness.
Judaism
“Ham, the father of Canaan, saw his father’s nakedness and told his two brothers outside. But Shem and Japheth took a cloth, placed it against both their backs and, walking backward, they covered their father’s nakedness…”
— Genesis 9:22-23 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
When Noah lies exposed, Ham broadcasts it while Shem and Japheth cover him without looking.
The story enacts the Kural almost literally: greatness covers the exposed weakness, littleness tells it.
Buddhism
“Let none find fault with others; let none see the omissions and commissions of others. But let one see one's own acts, done and undone.”
— Dhammapada 50 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha tells his followers to watch their own conduct rather than catalogue others' failings.
Like the Kural, it rejects the small habit of dwelling on others' faults.
Jainism
Among the eight limbs of right faith (samyag-darshana), Jain teaching lists upaguhana: covering up the faults that weak or ignorant followers bring upon the path, rather than exposing them to public reproach.
— Ratnakaranda Shravakachara of Samantabhadra, v. 15 (tr. Champat Rai Jain) [8]
Concealing a fellow believer's lapse is treated as a component of right faith itself.
Upaguhana is very close to the Kural's greatness that hides others' weakness.
Sikhism
“Farid, if you have a keen understanding, then do not write black marks against anyone else. Look underneath your own collar instead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, salok 6 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, the Sufi saint whose saloks the Gurus placed in the scripture, tells the wise not to tally other people's faults but to look at their own. Elsewhere Gurbani condemns nindaa (slander) as a sign of the self-willed.
The Kural says greatness covers others' weaknesses while littleness speaks only of faults, and Farid gives the same instruction: refuse to blacken others and look inward instead.
Greek Mythology
In the assembly before Troy, Thersites, described as the ugliest and most foul-mouthed man in the army, rails at Agamemnon and the leaders, listing their failings to the troops, until Odysseus silences him with his staff.
— Homer, Iliad 2.211-277 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer portrays Thersites as the mean man whose speech consists of finding fault.
Thersites is the Kural's littleness that does nothing but proclaim others' faults.
Philosophy
“The marks of a proficient are, that he censures no one, praises no one, blames no one, accuses no one, says nothing concerning himself as being anybody, or knowing anything…”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 48 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus lists refusing to censure, blame or accuse others among the signs of moral progress.
Like the Kural, it counts restraint from exposing others' faults as a mark of the great.

Real Life Example

Eisenhower writes a note taking all the blame if D-Day fails
5 June 1944 · England, United Kingdom (Allied command for the Normandy invasion)
On 5 June 1944, after giving the final order for the Allied invasion of Normandy, General Dwight D. Eisenhower wrote a short statement to be issued if the landings failed, saying that the troops had been withdrawn and that his decision had been based on the best information available. He praised the soldiers, airmen and sailors for their bravery and devotion to duty and ended with the words, "If any blame or fault attaches to the attempt it is mine alone." The invasion succeeded, the note was never issued, and an aide retrieved it after Eisenhower discarded it; in his haste Eisenhower had misdated it July 5.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Supreme Allied Commander prepared, in advance, to shield everyone under him and to carry any failure himself.
The kural says greatness covers up the weaknesses of others while littleness speaks only of their faults, and Eisenhower was ready to hide any failings of his forces behind his own acceptance of the blame.
