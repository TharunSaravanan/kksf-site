Kural 239

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

வசையிலா வண்பயன் குன்றும் இசையிலா யாக்கை பொறுத்த நிலம்.
Vasaiyilaa vanpayan kundrum isaiyilaa Yaakkai poruththa nilam.

Meaning

When the land carries the body of a person with no good name, its rich, healthy harvest will grow smaller.

Elementary

Think of a garden where one plant takes all the water and sunshine but never gives flowers or fruit. The whole garden does a little worse because of it. A person who takes from the world but gives nothing good back is a bit like that plant.

University

Here Valluvar moves from social reproach to cosmic consequence: the earth (நிலம்) that ‘bears’ (பொறுத்த) a renownless body (இசையிலா யாக்கை) suffers a decline (குன்றும்) in its bountiful, faultless yield (வசையிலா வண்பயன்). The choice of யாக்கை, the bare physical frame, pointedly denies such a person any fuller identity: they are mere body, a burden on the ground. The underlying belief, common to many ancient cultures, links moral quality to fertility: righteous people and rulers bring rain and harvest, the unrighteous bring dearth, an idea found in the Gita’s cycle of rain and sacrifice, the Buddha’s discourse on unprincipled kings, and Homer’s blameless king under whom the earth yields abundantly. Modern readers may take it as a vivid image of free-riding: a life that consumes without contributing diminishes the common stock, close to Cicero’s charge that the self-absorbed are ‘traitors to social life’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“All living bodies subsist on food grains, which are produced from rain. Rains are produced by performance of yajña [sacrifice], and yajña is born of prescribed duties.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.14 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes a cycle in which rightful action sustains the rain that feeds the crops that feed all beings.
In both, the fertility of the land depends on how human beings conduct themselves.
Christianity
“Behold, these three years I come seeking fruit on this fig tree, and find none: cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?”
— Luke 13:7 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus’ parable, an owner finds a fig tree barren for three years and asks why it should keep taking up the soil.
The fruitless tree ‘cumbers the ground’ just as the renownless body burdens the land in the Kural.
Islam
“Corruption has appeared throughout the land and sea by [reason of] what the hands of people have earned.”
— Qur’an 30:41 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an attributes disorder and ruin on land and sea to human wrongdoing.
Both texts see the earth’s well-being as damaged by the moral failures of those who live on it.
Judaism
“When thou tillest the ground, it shall not henceforth yield unto thee her strength; a fugitive and a vagabond shalt thou be in the earth.”
— Genesis 4:12 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
After Cain murders Abel, God tells him the ground will no longer give him its strength.
The land’s yield diminishes because of the moral state of the one who lives on it, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
“When the people of town and country are unprincipled, the courses of the moon and sun become erratic.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.70, Adhammika Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [6]
The Buddha describes a chain in which unprincipled rulers and people lead to erratic seasons, insufficient rain, badly ripening crops and sickly people, and the reverse when people are principled.
Human moral quality directly affects the land’s produce, exactly as the Kural claims.
Jainism
“From the moment that this our boy has been begotten, our silver increased, our gold increased, &c. … Now our wishes have been fulfilled, therefore shall the name of our boy be Vardhamâna.”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Mahāvīra §§106–107 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Mahāvīra's parents named him Vardhamāna, 'the Increaser', because their household prospered from the moment he was conceived.
Valluvar says the land that carries a renownless person sees its harvest fail. The Kalpa Sūtra tells the reverse story, in which a virtuous soul's presence makes the family's wealth and grain increase.
Sikhism
“Those who forget the Naam, the Name of the Lord, are a burden on the earth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sheikh Farid, in a hymn in the Guru Granth Sahib, says that people who forget God are only a weight on the earth that carries them.
The Kural says the land that bears a body without renown loses its yield; Farid uses the same image of the earth burdened by an empty life.
Greek Mythology
“The earth yields its wheat and barley, the trees are loaded with fruit, the ewes bring forth lambs, and the sea abounds with fish by reason of his virtues.”
— Homer, Odyssey 19 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
The disguised Odysseus tells Penelope that her fame reaches heaven like that of a blameless king whose virtue makes land and sea abundant.
The positive mirror image of the Kural: virtue and renown make the land fruitful, so their absence would make it fail.
Philosophy
“They are traitors to social life, for they contribute to it none of their interest, none of their effort, none of their means.”
— Cicero, De Officiis I.29 (trans. Walter Miller) [10]
Cicero condemns those who, absorbed in their own affairs, give nothing to the common life of society.
The rationalist version of the Kural: a life that takes but contributes nothing diminishes the shared stock.

Real Life Example

Norman Borlaug's wheat transforms harvests in Mexico and South Asia
1944-1970 · Mexico, India and Pakistan
From 1944 Norman Borlaug worked on a Rockefeller Foundation programme in Mexico, where he developed high-yielding, disease-resistant wheat varieties, and Mexico became self-sufficient in wheat in 1956. In the 1960s these varieties were introduced to India and Pakistan, where wheat yields nearly doubled between 1965 and 1970. Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The work of one renowned scientist and his colleagues changed the yield of whole countries' land and averted famines that had been widely predicted.
The kural says the land bearing a person without renown sees its harvest decline; Borlaug shows the converse, a person of lasting renown under whose work the land's yield multiplied.
