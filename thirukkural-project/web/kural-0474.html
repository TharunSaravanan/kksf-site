Kural 474

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

அமைந்தாங் கொழுகான் அளவறியான் தன்னை வியந்தான் விரைந்து கெடும்.
Amaindhaangu ozhugaan alavariyaan thannai Viyandhaan viraindhu kedum.

Meaning

A person who does not get along with others, does not know their own limits, and thinks too highly of themselves will soon be ruined.

Elementary

Someone who won’t get along with others, doesn’t know how strong they really are, and keeps bragging about how great they are will fall very fast.

University

Three faults are linked here. The first is failing to fit one’s conduct to others (amaindhaangu ozhugaan, which commentators read as refusing to accommodate those around one, especially more powerful neighbours). The second is ignorance of one’s own measure (alavu ariyaan). The third is self-admiration (thannai viyandhaan). The first two are errors of judgement, and the third is the emotional state that keeps them going, because the self-admirer stops checking. Viraindhu (‘swiftly’) matters: pride does not merely lead to ruin eventually, it speeds it up.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Confident in his army and his own prowess, Duryodhana rejects Krishna’s peace embassy and every counsel of Bhishma, Drona, Vidura and his own parents. He refuses to give the Pandavas even five villages, and the war that follows destroys him and his house.
— Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva (Bhagavad-yana sections; tr. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Duryodhana will not accommodate others, misjudges his real strength, and is swollen with self-regard, the three faults the Kural lists.
His swift ruin at Kurukshetra illustrates the Kural’s viraindhu kedum.
Christianity
“For I say, through the grace given unto me, to every man that is among you, not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think; but to think soberly, according as God hath dealt to every man the measure of faith.”
— Romans 12:3 (KJV) [5]
Paul warns against inflated self-estimation and calls for a sober judgement of oneself according to the measure actually given.
It addresses the Kural’s alavu ariyaan and thannai viyandhaan: not knowing one’s measure, and thinking too highly of oneself.
Islam
“And do not turn your cheek [in contempt] toward people and do not walk through the earth exultantly. Indeed, Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful.”
— Qur’an 31:18 (Sahih International) [6]
Luqman’s counsel to his son forbids arrogance toward people and conceited self-display.
It matches the Kural’s pairing of failing to get along with others and self-admiration. The Kural adds the practical consequence of swift ruin.
Judaism
“Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall.”
— Proverbs 16:18 (KJV) [5]
The proverb holds that pride is the prelude to collapse.
It gives the same causal chain as the Kural’s thannai viyandhaan … kedum: self-exaltation leading to ruin.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is called a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada treats inflated self-assessment as the deepest form of folly.
It matches the Kural’s alavu ariyaan and thannai viyandhaan: not knowing one’s real measure and admiring oneself.
Jainism
In the Jain story of Bahubali, son of Rishabhanatha, Bahubali renounces the world after his contest with his brother Bharata, but at first will not go to bow before his younger brothers, who had become monks before him. He stands motionless in meditation for a year, yet omniscience does not come to him until his sisters Brahmi and Sundari tell him to get down from the elephant of pride. When he lets go of his pride, he attains kevala-jnana.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Parva 1 (life of Ṛṣabha; tr. Helen M. Johnson, Gaekwad's Oriental Series) [8]
The story teaches that even heroic austerity is blocked by pride (mana), one of the four passions that bind karma to the soul.
The Kural says that self-admiration and refusing to fit in with others bring swift ruin; in the Bahubali story it is exactly such pride that holds back a great soul until he gives it up.
Sikhism
“The humble beings abide in peace; subduing egotism, they are meek. The very proud and arrogant persons, O Nanak, are consumed by their own pride.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib (ashtapadi 12, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan contrasts the humble, who live in peace, with the proud, who are destroyed by their own pride (haumai).
The Kural says the self-admiring man who ignores his own measure soon comes to ruin; Guru Arjan says the arrogant are consumed by their pride.
Greek Mythology
Arachne, a gifted weaver, boasts that her skill surpasses Athena’s and refuses to admit any debt to the goddess. She rejects Athena’s warning, delivered in the guise of an old woman, and challenges her to a contest. Enraged, Athena destroys her work, and Arachne is turned into a spider.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.1–145 [10]
Arachne’s self-admiration and refusal to accept advice bring swift ruin, even though her skill is real.
It illustrates the Kural’s three faults: refusing to accommodate, misjudging one’s standing, and admiring oneself.
Philosophy
After questioning a man reputed to be wise, Socrates concludes that he himself is wiser in one small way: neither of them knows anything fine and good, but the other man thinks he knows when he does not, while Socrates does not think he knows.
— Plato, Apology 21d [11]
Socrates makes accurate knowledge of one’s own limits the beginning of wisdom and exposes self-admiration as ignorance.
It parallels the Kural’s alavu ariyaan and thannai viyandhaan: self-flattery comes from not knowing one’s own measure.

Real Life Example

Charles XII of Sweden is defeated at Poltava
8 July 1709 · Poltava, Ukraine
After years of victories in the Great Northern War, Charles XII of Sweden invaded Russia and turned south into Ukraine, far from his supplies. Though wounded in the foot, he chose to attack Peter the Great's fortified camp at Poltava, the assault failed, and three days later most of the Swedish army surrendered at Perevolochna. Charles escaped to Ottoman territory, where he spent about five years trying in vain to persuade the Ottomans to attack Russia.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A king who had won many battles staked his army on one more deep campaign and lost Sweden's standing as a great power.
The kural warns that one who misjudges his own measure and acts without regard to others is quickly ruined, and Poltava destroyed Charles's army in a single day.
