Kural 750

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

எனைமாட்சித் தாகியக் கண்ணும் வினைமாட்சி இல்லார்கண் இல்லது அரண்.
Enaimaatchith thaakiyak kannum vinaimaatchi illaarkan illathu aran.

Meaning

No matter how great a fort is, it is no fort at all if the people in it are not skilled and strong in action.

Elementary

You could have the best football in the world, but if nobody on your team can kick, you will still lose. A fort is the same. The tallest walls are useless if the people inside don’t know how to defend them. Valluvar ends the chapter by saying that people matter more than walls.

University

The closing couplet undercuts everything before it. Enai maatchiththu aakiya kannum means ‘even when it has every kind of excellence’, summing up the natural defenses, walls, provisions and garrison of 742-749. Yet it is illathu, ‘non-existent’, among those who lack vinai maatchi, excellence in action. Varadarasanar glosses vinai maatchi as skill in the ways of action, Pappaiah as the ability to understand and act against the enemy, and Kalaignar simply says that if those inside are incompetent there is no use at all. The play on maatchi (‘excellence’) in both lines sets the fort’s excellence against its holders’ excellence, and the holders win. Pope’s verse rendering (‘To craven souls no fortress strength supplies’) makes the lack a matter of courage, while the Tamil commentators stress practical competence more broadly. The ytamizh.com Varadarasanar gloss ends with the words ‘பொருள் செயல்வகை’, the title of the next chapter, which appears to be a page artifact rather than part of the commentary. The sandhi-joined text reads ‘எனைமாட்சித் தாகியக் கண்ணும் வினைமாட்சி / யில்லார்க ணில்ல தரண்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, Hanuman reports to Rama that Lanka is guarded by moats, high walls, four great gates and huge numbers of rakshasa troops, making it seem impregnable. Yet the city falls once Ravana, blinded by pride and deaf to good counsel, loses his brother Vibhishana and fights an unrighteous war.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda, Sarga 3 (Hanuman’s description of Lanka) and following [3]
Lanka had every material advantage but was lost because its master lacked wisdom and right action.
Lanka proves the Kural’s last word: a fort with every excellence is nothing without excellence in those who hold it.
Christianity
“Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it: except the LORD keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain.”
— Psalm 127:1 (KJV) [4]
The psalm says walls and watchmen are useless unless God guards the city.
Both texts say the walls themselves are not what protects a city. The psalm points to God, the Kural to capable defenders.
Islam
“They will not fight you all except within fortified cities or from behind walls. Their violence among themselves is severe. You think they are together, but their hearts are diverse.”
— Qur’an 59:14 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes enemies who hide behind walls but are divided among themselves, and so are weak despite their fortifications.
Walls cannot save a people divided at heart, which is the Kural’s point that a fort is nothing without worthy defenders.
Judaism
The Talmud relates that when Rabbi Yehuda Nesia levied a tax for repairing the city wall even on the Sages, Reish Lakish objected that Torah scholars do not need protection. He cited verses from the Psalms to argue that the merit of the people, not the stones, guards a city.
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 7b-8a [6]
The rabbis ask what really protects a city and put the quality of its people above the wall.
Like the Kural, the Talmud holds that a wall’s protective power depends on the worth of those within.
Buddhism
“With persistence as his army, the disciple of the noble ones abandons what is unskillful, develops what is skillful, abandons what is blameworthy, develops what is blameless, and looks after himself with purity.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Nagara Sutta’s application, the fortress’s army stands for the disciple’s own persistent effort.
The sutta, like the Kural, makes the active effort of those inside what finally decides whether the fortress stands.
Jainism
“You cannot prolong your life, therefore be not careless; you are past help when old age approaches. Consider this: what (protection) will careless people get, who kill living beings and do not exert themselves?”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The fourth lecture of the Uttaradhyayana warns that nothing can protect those who are careless and do not exert themselves; protection depends on one's own vigilant effort.
The Kural ends by saying even the finest fortress is no fortress for those who lack skill and vigour in action, and the Jain verse likewise denies any protection to people who will not exert themselves.
Sikhism
“Millions of religious leaders failed to halt the invader, when they heard of the Emperor’s invasion. He burned the rest-houses and the ancient temples; he cut the princes limb from limb, and cast them into the dust. None of the Moguls went blind, and no one performed any miracle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417-418, Raag Aasaa (Ashtapadi, Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes Babur's sack of the Punjab and mocks the defenders who trusted in the spells of holy men rather than in competent action; their strongholds and palaces fell all the same.
The Kural says no fortress is a fortress in the hands of people who lack skill in action; Guru Nanak shows a whole realm's defences failing because its holders relied on charms instead of deeds.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, the Greeks build a great wall and ditch around their ships. While Achilles withdraws from battle and the other leading Greek heroes are wounded, Hector breaks the gate with a stone and the Trojans pour over the wall.
— Homer, Iliad 7.433-441 and Book 12 [10]
The Greeks’ fortification failed as soon as its best fighters were missing.
It is the Kural’s conclusion in story form: a strong wall is nothing without worthy men to hold it.
Philosophy
“For men, and not walls or ships in which are no men, constitute a state.”
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 7.77 (trans. Benjamin Jowett, Perseus Digital Library) [11]
The Athenian general Nicias tells his defeated army in Sicily that the city is its people, not its walls.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s closing verdict: without capable people, walls count for nothing.

Real Life Example

Fort Douaumont falls almost without a shot
25 February 1916 · Douaumont, near Verdun, France
Fort Douaumont, the largest and one of the most modern of the forts guarding Verdun, had been stripped of most of its guns and much of its garrison in 1915 on the orders of the French high command. When German troops approached it on 25 February 1916, only a small body of reserve gunners remained inside. A German sergeant found his way in through one of the gun embrasures, other small parties followed, and the fort was taken without a real fight.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Douaumont showed that even the most modern fortress was worthless once too few capable defenders were left inside to use it, and its easy fall shocked France early in the Battle of Verdun.
The kural ends the chapter by saying that however excellent a fortress may be, it is no fortress at all without people of skill and vigour to hold it, and Douaumont is almost a literal case.
