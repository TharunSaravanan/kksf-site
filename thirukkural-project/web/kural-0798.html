Kural 798

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

உள்ளற்க உள்ளம் சிறுகுவ கொள்ளற்க அல்லற்கண் ஆற்றறுப்பார் நட்பு.
Ullarka ullam sirukuva kollarka allarkan aatraruppaar natpu.

Meaning

Do not think about things that make your spirit weak, and do not make friends with people who will leave you when trouble comes.

Elementary

Some friends are like a bicycle that only works on sunny days: the moment it rains, the chain falls off. Do not count on a friend like that. Pick friends who will still be pedalling with you when the road gets muddy.

University

The couplet sets two prohibitions side by side. Ullarka ullam siruguva (உள்ளற்க உள்ளம் சிறுகுவ) is ‘do not think the things that make the mind small’; Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah take this as avoiding whatever saps one’s energy and resolve (ookkam), and Parimelazhagar treats the first line as an analogy for the second: just as one should not entertain spirit-shrinking thoughts, one should not take up such friends. Allarkan aatraruppaar (அல்லற்கண் ஆற்றறுப்பார்) are ‘those who cut off the way in time of distress’, people who withdraw their support exactly when it is needed. Puliyur Kesigan reads the first line differently, as a warning not to think meanly of a friend, which shows the line is open to more than one interpretation. The sandhi-joined text reads உள்ளற்க வுள்ளஞ் … வல்லற்க ணாற்றறுப்பார், the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He is a true friend who does not forsake us in time of need, misfortune, famine, or war, in a king’s court, or at the crematorium (smasana).”
— Chanakya Niti 1.12 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya lists the hard places where a true friend still stands beside you.
Chanakya defines the true friend by the same test the Kural uses: he does not leave in times of trouble.
Christianity
“At my first answer no man stood with me, but all men forsook me: I pray God that it may not be laid to their charge.”
— 2 Timothy 4:16 (KJV) [5]
Writing from prison, Paul recalls that at his first hearing his supporters deserted him.
Paul describes the very friends the Kural warns against: those who vanish in the hour of distress.
Islam
“[The hypocrites are] like the example of Satan when he says to man, ‘Disbelieve.’ But when he disbelieves, he says, ‘Indeed, I am disassociated from you. Indeed, I fear Allah, Lord of the worlds.’”
— Qur’an 59:16 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse compares false allies to Satan, who urges a person into wrongdoing and then disowns him when the consequences arrive.
It describes the companion who abandons you at the moment of trouble.
Judaism
“Be careful [in your dealings] with the ruling authorities for they do not befriend a person except for their own needs; they seem like friends when it is to their own interest, but they do not stand by a man in the hour of his distress.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:3 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Rabban Gamaliel warns that powerful patrons seem friendly while it serves them but are gone when trouble comes.
This is the Kural’s deserting friend, described almost in its own words.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who renders lip-service be understood as a foe in the guise of a friend: (i) he makes friendly profession as regards the past, (ii) he makes friendly profession as regards the future, (iii) he tries to gain one’s favor by empty words, (iv) when opportunity for service has arisen, he expresses his inability.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
The Buddha describes the false friend who talks warmly but is never available when help is actually needed.
The lip-service friend is the one who, in the Kural’s words, cuts off the way in time of trouble.
Jainism
“Avoiding the friendship of those who resemble elephants, seek the friendship of those who resemble dogs; for an elephant will kill his driver whom he has known for a long time, but a dog will wag his tail while the spear thrown at him is still in his body.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 3 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar warns against friends who, like an elephant turning on its own driver, betray a long companion, and praises those who stay loyal even when wounded.
The Kural forbids taking up the friendship of people who abandon you in trouble, and the Naladiyar likewise tells us to avoid such friends and seek those who stay faithful through suffering.
Sikhism
“The world is entangled in its own pleasures; no one is for anyone else. In good times, many come and sit together, surrounding you on all four sides. But when hard times come, they all leave, and no one comes near you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 634, Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In this Sorath shabad Guru Tegh Bahadur describes companions who crowd around in comfort and vanish in distress, because their attachment was only to their own pleasure.
The Kural forbids taking up the friendship of those who cut off their support in time of trouble; Guru Tegh Bahadur describes exactly those people, who leave as soon as hard times come.
Greek Mythology
“He gave me this advice,” his companion replied. “Never travel with a friend who deserts you at the approach of danger.”
— Aesop, The Bear and the Two Travelers (trans. G. F. Townsend) [11]
When a bear appears, one traveller scrambles up a tree and leaves his companion to save himself by playing dead.
The fable gives the Kural’s own advice: do not keep a friend who runs at the first sign of danger.
Philosophy
“Those who are friends for the sake of utility part when the advantage is at an end; for they were lovers not of each other but of profit.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.4 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle explains that friendships based on usefulness dissolve as soon as one party is no longer useful.
Aristotle gives the reason fair-weather friends desert: they never loved the person, only the benefit.

Real Life Example

Charles I abandons the Earl of Strafford
May 1641 · London, England, United Kingdom
Thomas Wentworth, Earl of Strafford, had become King Charles I's chief adviser and in 1641 was impeached by the Long Parliament. When his trial failed to prove treason, his opponents turned to a bill of attainder, and after anguished hesitation Charles, fearing for his own security, gave his assent to it. Strafford was executed on Tower Hill on 12 May 1641.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Strafford had served the king with total devotion, yet in the crisis Charles let him die to protect himself, a decision the king later came to regard as a grave sin.
The kural warns against taking up the friendship of those who will cut you off when trouble comes, and Strafford gave everything to a master who deserted him at the decisive moment.
