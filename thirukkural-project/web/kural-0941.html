Kural 941

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

மிகினும் குறையினும் நோய்செய்யும் நூலோர் வளிமுதலா எண்ணிய மூன்று.
Miginum kuraiyinum noiseyyum noolor valimudhalaa enniya moondru.

Meaning

The three forces in the body that medical books name, starting with wind, cause sickness when there is too much or too little of them.

Elementary

Think of a bicycle tyre: pump in too much air and it bursts, let out too much and it goes flat. Valluvar says the body has three such forces inside it, and it stays healthy only when each one is just right.

University

The chapter opens with theory before practice: noolor (நூலோர், 'the authors of the treatises') are the medical writers, and vali mudhalaa enniya moondru (வளிமுதலா எண்ணிய மூன்று, 'the three reckoned with wind first') are the three humours that Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss as vaatham, piththam and silethumam (வாதம், பித்தம், சிலேத்துமம்), i.e. the vata-pitta-kapha triad of Ayurveda and Siddha medicine. The decisive words are miginum kuraiyinum (மிகினும் குறையினும், 'whether in excess or in deficiency'): illness is defined not as an invading thing but as loss of proportion. Following Parimelazhagar, Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan add that the humours are disturbed through a person's food and activity (உணவும் செயல்களும்), which is why the following couplets turn at once to eating. The chapter closes the Natpiyal section of Porutpaal in the traditional arrangement, a placement commentators explain by treating bodily health as the last of the things a ruler and householder must secure. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Charaka Samhita defines disease as disequilibrium of the body's constituents and health as their equilibrium, and treats vata, pitta and kapha as the doshas whose increase or decrease produces illness.
— Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 9.4 (paraphrase) [3]
Classical Ayurveda makes balance the very definition of health and imbalance, in either direction, the definition of disease.
This is the medical theory the Kural's noolor are summarising: the three humours harm by being too much or too little.
Christianity
Ecclesiastes counsels against being righteous overmuch or wicked overmuch, urging the one who fears God to avoid both extremes.
— Ecclesiastes 7:16-18 (KJV), paraphrase [4]
The Preacher warns that going too far in either direction destroys a person.
Like the Kural, it treats harm as coming from both excess and its opposite, though the subject is conduct rather than the body.
Islam
“And the heaven He raised and imposed the balance That you not transgress within the balance. And establish weight in justice and do not make deficient the balance.”
— Qur'an 55:7-9 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an presents creation as resting on a divinely set balance that must be neither exceeded nor made deficient.
The Kural's rule for the body's three humours mirrors this cosmic warning against both transgressing and short-weighting the balance.
Judaism
“Since maintaining a healthy and sound body is among the ways of God - for one cannot understand or have any knowledge of the Creator, if he is ill - therefore, he must avoid that which harms the body and accustom himself to that which is healthful and helps the body become stronger.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:1 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides, himself a physician, opens his health code by making bodily soundness a religious duty and framing medicine as avoiding what harms and cultivating what strengthens.
Both texts begin a treatment of health with the principle that the body must be kept from what disturbs it.
Buddhism
The Buddha reminds the over-strenuous monk Sona that a lute sounds well only when its strings are neither too tight nor too slack, and tells him to tune his effort the same way.
— Sona Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 6.55 (paraphrase) [7]
The simile teaches that both overstrain and slackness spoil the result; balance is what makes the instrument play.
The Kural applies the same neither-too-much-nor-too-little rule to the body's three humours.
Jainism
“Vêdanîya is twofold, pleasure and pain; there are many subdivisions of pleasure and so there are of pain also.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 33.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In Jain doctrine the experience of bodily pain and pleasure is the fruition of vedaniya karma, one of the eight kinds of karmic matter binding the soul.
Where the Kural traces disease to an imbalance of three humours, Jainism traces the felt pain of disease further back, to karma, a different causal account of the same suffering.
Sikhism
“The union of air, water and fire / the body is the play-thing of the fickle and unsteady intellect.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 152, Raag Gauri, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Raag Gauri, Guru Nanak describes the human body as a compound of the elements, air named first, held together for a while and driven about by a restless mind.
The Kural's physicians count three bodily forces led by wind; Guru Nanak likewise sees the body as a mixture of elements led by air, whose condition depends on how they are held together.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus warns Icarus to fly a middle course, neither so low that the sea's damp weighs down his wings nor so high that the sun's heat melts their wax; Icarus soars too high and falls.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Daedalus and Icarus), paraphrase [10]
The myth makes both extremes deadly and only the middle path safe.
It dramatises the Kural's claim that harm comes from too much and from too little alike.
Philosophy
In the Timaeus, Plato explains that the body is compacted of four elements, and that the unnatural excess or defect of these, or their shifting out of their proper place, is the origin of disorders and diseases.
— Plato, Timaeus 82a (tr. Benjamin Jowett), paraphrase [11]
Plato gives a Greek version of humoral pathology in which disease is the excess or deficiency of the body's constituents.
This is almost exactly the Kural's thesis, with four elements in place of three humours.

Real Life Example

Christiaan Eijkman traces beriberi to polished rice
1890s · Batavia, Java, Dutch East Indies (now Jakarta, Indonesia)
Working in the Dutch government's medical laboratory in Batavia, Christiaan Eijkman found that chickens fed on polished white rice developed a nerve disease resembling beriberi and recovered when they were given unpolished rice. He at first explained this as a harmful substance in the rice grain that the outer layer counteracted, but the finding was later understood as the lack of a vital substance in the rice's outer layer. The work led to the concept of vitamins and earned Eijkman the 1929 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Eijkman's experiment helped show that illness can come not only from something harmful being present but from something necessary being missing, a turning point in medical thinking.
The kural says the body's forces cause disease whether they grow too strong or fall too low, and Eijkman's beriberi work moved from a theory of harmful excess to the discovery that a deficiency was to blame.
