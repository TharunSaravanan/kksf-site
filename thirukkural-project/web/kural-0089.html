Kural 89

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

உடைமையுள் இன்மை விருந்தோம்பல் ஓம்பா மடமை மடவார்கண் உண்டு.
Udaimaiyul inmai virundhombal ombaa, Madamai madavaarkan undu.

Meaning

Being poor while having plenty is what happens when you refuse to welcome guests. Such foolishness is found only in fools.

Elementary

Someone can have a full fridge, a big house and lots of money, and still be poor — if they never share any of it. This kural calls that a kind of silliness: having everything and enjoying none of it, because you're too closed-up to let anyone in.

University

The verse is built on two paradoxes stacked on each other. The first, உடைமையுள் இன்மை ('destitution inside possession'), is a genuine oxymoron and the phrase for which the couplet is best known. The second is the near-tautology of the closing line: மடமை மடவார்கண் உண்டு, 'that folly exists in fools' — a construction that looks empty but functions as a sharp dismissal, refusing to dignify such conduct with any explanation beyond stupidity. மடமை denotes not mere ignorance but a wilful dullness of perception, the inability to see what is obvious. Valluvar thus classifies the inhospitable rich not as wicked but as stupid, a rhetorically devastating move that resurfaces in the Hebrew Bible's treatment of Nabal (whose name is literally 'fool') and in Jesus's address to the rich man as ἄφρων. The couplet completes the chapter's argument: having established hospitality as purpose (81), habit (83), sacrifice (87) and lost opportunity (88), it now names its absence as a form of self-inflicted poverty.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The man with food in store who, when the needy comes in miserable case begging for bread to eat, hardens his heart against him — even when of old he did him service — finds not one to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.2–6 (trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [4]
The Rig Veda's hymn on liberality moves from praising the generous to a direct portrait of the man who has plenty and withholds it, and pronounces him friendless.
Same figure as the Kural's: the man whose stores are full and whose life is nonetheless empty.
Christianity
“Because thou sayest, I am rich, and increased with goods, and have need of nothing; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and poor, and blind, and naked:”
— Revelation 3:17 (King James Version) [5]
The letter to Laodicea diagnoses a community whose self-assessment as prosperous is precisely what conceals its actual destitution from it.
This is the Kural's paradox stated in identical terms — poverty existing inside, and hidden by, possession.
Islam
“Every day two angels come down from Heaven and one of them says, ‘O Allah! Compensate every person who spends in Your Cause,’ and the other (angel) says, ‘O Allah! Destroy every miser.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1442, Book of Zakat [6]
The hadith is placed by Bukhari under the Qur'anic verses contrasting the one who gives with the one who is stingy and thinks himself self-sufficient, and pictures the two fates being petitioned for daily.
States the Kural's claim as a standing cosmic arrangement: withholding turns possession into ruin.
Judaism
“for as his name is, so is he; Nabal is his name, and folly is with him”
— 1 Samuel 25:25 (King James Version); narrative at 1 Samuel 25:2–38 [5]
Nabal, a man of vast flocks in Carmel, refuses food and drink to David's men who had protected his shepherds; his wife Abigail intercepts the reprisal with this assessment of her husband, and he dies within ten days.
The Bible's paradigm case of exactly what the Kural describes — enormous wealth, refused hospitality, and folly named as the cause.
Buddhism
King Pasenadi tells the Buddha of a Sāvatthī financier who died heirless leaving many millions, and who in life had eaten only broken rice and sour gruel, worn coarse hemp, and travelled in a dilapidated cart. The Buddha replies that when an unworthy person acquires great wealth and neither makes himself happy with it nor shares it with ascetics and brahmins, it goes to waste, unused — like a pool of cool clear water in a demon-haunted place that no one drinks from.
— Saṃyutta Nikāya 3.19, Aputtaka Sutta (Heirless) [7]
The simile of the undrunk pool is the Buddha's image for wealth that exists but nourishes nobody, not even its owner.
An almost exact narrative rendering of உடைமையுள் இன்மை — a millionaire living in destitution because nothing he owns is ever shared.
Jainism
“He who employs not his time in enjoying his property, or gives not of it to ascetics, but lays it up (like a miser)--at him, the foolish one, about to perish, his hoarded wealth shall mock, and the favour of the world shall mock.”
— Nālaṭiyār ch. 28 'Illiberality', st. 3 (= st. 273), English translation in The Indian Antiquary vol. II (1873) [8]
In its chapter on illiberality, the Nālaṭiyār calls the rich man who neither uses nor gives his wealth a fool. His hoard ends up mocking him.
Valluvar calls poverty in the midst of plenty a folly found only in fools, and the Nālaṭiyār likewise names the wealthy man who will not give 'the foolish one'.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, those houses in which neither the Holy nor the Lord are served - those houses are like cremation grounds; demons dwell within them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1374, Salok Kabir (salok 192) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In one of his saloks in the Guru Granth Sahib, Kabir says a household that does not serve holy visitors is spiritually dead, whatever it possesses.
Like Valluvar's poverty in the midst of plenty, a house that fails to serve those who come to it is counted as empty and lifeless.
Greek Mythology
Erysichthon, who scorned the gods and felled the sacred grove of Ceres, is punished with a hunger no food can satisfy; he consumes his whole fortune, then sells his own daughter repeatedly to buy more, and finally, with nothing left, begins to devour his own limbs.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738–878 (Erysichthon) [10]
Ovid's most grotesque cautionary tale makes appetite without reverence into a self-consuming engine that converts abundance into absolute want.
The extreme literalisation of the Kural's paradox — a rich man made destitute by an inability to direct any of it outward.
Philosophy
“It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 2.6 (trans. Richard M. Gummere), quoting Epicurus [11]
Seneca, quoting Epicurus and saying he crosses into the enemy's camp as a scout rather than a deserter, argues that contented poverty is not poverty at all, and by the same logic that discontented wealth is.
This is the Kural's உடைமையுள் இன்மை, poverty inside possession, as a philosophical proposition.

Real Life Example

The voyage of the St. Louis
May-June 1939 · Havana, Cuba, and the Atlantic coast of the Americas
In May 1939 the liner St. Louis sailed from Hamburg carrying 937 passengers, almost all Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany. Cuba admitted only 28 of them and ordered the ship to leave, and the passengers' appeals to the United States and Canada were turned down. The ship returned to Europe, where Britain, the Netherlands, Belgium and France agreed to take the passengers; 254 of them were later killed in the Holocaust.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Wealthy nations with room to spare turned away desperate guests at their very shores, a refusal now widely remembered as a moral failure.
The kural calls the failure to keep up hospitality poverty in the midst of plenty and a folly found only in fools, and the St. Louis was refused by countries that had ample means to receive it.
