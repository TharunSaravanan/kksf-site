Kural 1318

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

தும்முச் செறுப்ப அழுதாள் நுமர்உள்ளல் எம்மை மறைத்திரோ என்று.
Thummuch cheruppa azhudhaal numar-ullal emmai maraiththiroa endru

Meaning

So I held back my sneeze. Then she cried and said, "Are you hiding from me that your people are thinking of you?"

Elementary

Imagine you are told off for sneezing, so next time you hold it in, and then you get told off for hiding it! The poor husband cannot win: sneeze or no sneeze, his wife finds something to pretend-cry about.

University

This couplet is the sequel to 1317: afraid of another quarrel, he suppresses (ceṟuppa) his sneeze (tummu), and she weeps, asking whether he is hiding from us (emmai maṟaittirō) the thought of him held by ‘your people’ (numar uḷḷal). Numar (‘your own ones’) is read by Mu. Varadarasanar as ‘those dear to you’ and by Solomon Pappaiah as ‘someone you care for’, a veiled hint at another woman. The two couplets together show the ‘subtlety’ of pulavi: every possible response becomes an occasion for sulking. Pope: ‘And so next time I checked my sneeze; she forthwith wept and cried, (that woman difficult to please), “Your thoughts from me you hide.”’ The wording is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, during the rasa dance, Krishna vanishes from the cowherd girls, and they search the forest in anguish, suspecting he has gone off with one favoured companion.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.30 (paraphrase) [3]
The gopis’ grief springs from a beloved who has hidden himself and from their guess about whom he is with.
The Kural’s wife weeps because she thinks her husband is hiding something, and imagines who might be behind it.
Christianity
“For there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; neither hid, that shall not be known.”
— Luke 12:2 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that what is hidden will come to light.
The wife assumes the same: whatever her husband hides, even a sneeze, must conceal something waiting to come out.
Islam
Aisha said that she was never as jealous of any of the Prophet’s wives as she was of Khadija, because the Prophet used to remember and praise her so often.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5229 (sunnah.com; paraphrase) [5]
Aisha’s jealousy was stirred not by any rival present, but by someone held in her husband’s memory.
The Kural’s wife is jealous in the same way, of whoever might be holding her husband in their thoughts.
Judaism
“Better is open rebuke than love that is hidden.”
— Proverbs 27:5 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs prizes openness, even when it stings, over feelings that are kept concealed.
The wife’s complaint is exactly that her husband is hiding something; she would rather have it out in the open.
Buddhism
“Easily seen is the fault of others, but one’s own fault is difficult to see. Like chaff one winnows another’s faults, but hides one’s own, even as a crafty fowler hides behind sham branches.”
— Dhammapada 252 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes how quickly we suspect hidden faults in others while overlooking our own.
The wife imagines her husband hiding behind his held-back sneeze like the fowler behind his branches.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra teaches that anger destroys love, pride destroys humility, deceit destroys friendship, and greed destroys everything.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.38 (paraphrase) [8]
Among the four passions (kashayas), deceit (maya) is singled out as what ruins friendship and trust.
The wife treats her husband’s held-back sneeze as a small deceit, the very thing Jain teaching says erodes intimacy.
Sikhism
“Even without our speaking, He knows everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 661, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says the Lord knows our inner state without our telling Him.
The Kural's wife accuses her husband of hiding who is thinking of him; Gurbani reminds that nothing can be hidden from the One who knows every heart.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, after seeing Thetis plead with Zeus, Hera reproaches him: it is always his pleasure, she says, to plan and decide things in secret, apart from her, and never to tell her his mind.
— Homer, Iliad 1.540-543 (paraphrase) [10]
Hera’s complaint is not about what Zeus did but about what he kept from her.
The Kural’s wife makes the same charge over a stifled sneeze: you are hiding your thoughts from me.
Philosophy
La Rochefoucauld writes that jealousy feeds on doubt, turning into fury or ending as soon as doubt gives way to certainty.
— La Rochefoucauld, Maxims 32 (paraphrase) [11]
Jealousy, he notes, lives on what is uncertain or hidden.
The husband’s hidden sneeze gives his wife exactly the doubt on which her playful jealousy feeds.

Real Life Example

The Affair of the Diamond Necklace
1785-1786 · Versailles and Paris, France
In 1784 and 1785 the confidence trickster Jeanne de La Motte persuaded Cardinal de Rohan that Queen Marie Antoinette secretly wanted a famous diamond necklace, arranging a night meeting with a woman who impersonated the queen. Rohan obtained the necklace on 1 February 1785 and handed it to La Motte, who disappeared with it. When the jewellers asked for payment the plot came to light, Rohan was arrested at Versailles on 15 August 1785 and acquitted by the Parlement de Paris on 31 May 1786, yet the innocent queen's reputation was badly damaged.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The affair shows how a secret kept or a story hidden can make an innocent person look guilty in the eyes of others.
In this kural the husband holds back even a sneeze to avoid suspicion, only to be accused of hiding something, and Marie Antoinette, who had done nothing, was blamed all the more because the affair seemed to involve secrets.
