Kural 376

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

பரியினும் ஆகாவாம் பாலல்ல உய்த்துச் சொரியினும் போகா தம.
Pariyinum aagaavaam paalalla uyththuch Choriyinum pogaa thama.

Meaning

What fate has not made yours will not stay, however carefully you guard it. What fate has made yours will not leave, even if you throw it away.

Elementary

Imagine holding sand tightly in your fist: it still slips out. Now imagine a boomerang: you throw it away and it comes back. This kural says things that aren’t meant for you slip away however hard you hold on, and things that are meant for you come back even if you throw them away.

University

The couplet turns on பால் (paal), ‘share’ or ‘portion’, the same word used for the three books of the Kural. Things outside one’s allotted share (பாலல்ல) cannot be kept even with painful guarding (பரியினும்), and things within it (தம) will not leave even if deliberately poured out (உய்த்துச் சொரியினும்). It is an extreme statement of possession as destiny. Greek tradition gives a near-perfect illustration in Polycrates’ ring, which was thrown into the sea and came back inside a fish. The ethical point, drawn out by Stoics like Epictetus, is that anxious clinging is irrational: one should hold possessions loosely, as things on loan.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of action.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita teaches that results are not within a person’s control or ownership.
Both teach that what comes to you is not secured by grasping; results belong to a higher order.
Christianity
“Riches certainly make themselves wings; they fly away as an eagle toward heaven.”
— Proverbs 23:5 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that wealth can simply vanish however much one fixes one’s eyes on it.
It matches the first half of the Kural: riches not truly yours fly off despite careful watching.
Islam
“O Allāh, Owner of Sovereignty, You give sovereignty to whom You will and You take sovereignty away from whom You will.”
— Qur'an 3:26 (Saheeh International) [5]
Power and possession are given and taken by God, whatever the holder does.
Both say that keeping or losing possessions depends on a higher decree, not on the owner’s grip.
Judaism
“There is a sore evil which I have seen under the sun, namely, riches kept for the owners thereof to their hurt. But those riches perish by evil travail: and he begetteth a son, and there is nothing in his hand.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:13-14 (Hebrew Bible; KJV rendering) [4]
Qoheleth describes carefully hoarded wealth that is lost anyway, leaving the heir with nothing.
Close to the first half of the Kural: guarding wealth cannot keep what is not truly one’s portion.
Buddhism
“The fool worries, thinking, ‘I have sons, I have wealth.’ Indeed, when he himself is not his own, whence are sons, whence is wealth?”
— Dhammapada 62 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says the fool clings to possessions, not seeing that even the self is not truly owned.
Both expose the futility of anxious clinging to possessions.
Jainism
“Time elapses and quickly pass the days; the pleasures of men are not permanent; they come to a man and leave him just as a bird leaves a tree void of fruit.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.31 (cf. 13.10) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
King Brahmadatta, enjoying the rewards of past merit, admits to the monk Citra that worldly pleasures cannot be held: they settle on a person and then leave, like birds deserting a tree once its fruit is gone. Citra had already told him that every deed bears its fruit (13.10).
Valluvar says what is not one's allotted share will not stay however carefully guarded. The Jain image of pleasures leaving a man as birds leave a fruitless tree expresses the same idea: when the karmic fruit is spent, possessions go.
Sikhism
“He obtains ten things, and puts them behind him; for the sake of one thing withheld, he forfeits his faith. But what if that one thing were not given, and the ten were taken away? Then, what could the fool say or do?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 268, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib (Ashtpadi 5), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Sukhmani Guru Arjan reminds the ungrateful person that everything they hold was given by God and can be taken back at will, and that nothing they do can then keep it.
The Kural says what is not one's allotted share cannot be kept however carefully it is guarded; Guru Arjan likewise teaches that what the Giver withholds or takes back cannot be held on to.
Greek Mythology
The tyrant Polycrates, warned that his luck was too perfect, throws his most treasured ring into the sea to ward off misfortune. Days later a fisherman brings him a large fish, and the ring is found inside it. His ally Amasis breaks off their friendship, sensing that Polycrates is doomed.
— Herodotus, Histories 3.40-43 [9]
What fate has assigned cannot be thrown away, and a doom that is fated cannot be bought off.
An almost literal picture of the second half of the Kural: what is yours comes back even when you throw it away.
Philosophy
“Never say of anything, ‘I have lost it,’ but, ‘I have restored it.’”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 11 (tr. T. W. Higginson) [10]
Epictetus teaches that possessions are only on loan and return to the one who gave them.
Both remove the anxiety of loss: what is not truly yours goes back, whatever you do.

Real Life Example

Steve Jobs leaves Apple and returns
1985-1997 · Cupertino, California, United States
In 1985 Steve Jobs, who had co-founded Apple Computer in 1976, lost a boardroom power struggle with chief executive John Sculley, was removed from running the Macintosh division and left the company to found NeXT. In early 1997 Apple, which needed a new operating system, bought NeXT, and Jobs returned, becoming interim chief executive later that year. Under his leadership the struggling company recovered and went on to become one of the most valuable companies in the world.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Jobs lost the company he had founded, yet a decade later Apple came back to him through the very company he had started after being pushed out.
The kural says that what is truly one's own will not leave even if it is carried off and thrown away, and Apple, lost by Jobs in 1985, returned to him in 1997.
