Kural 888

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

அரம்பொருத பொன்போலத் தேயும் உரம்பொருது உட்பகை உற்ற குடி.
Aramporudha ponpolath theyum uramporudhu utpakai utra kuti.

Meaning

A family with hostility inside wears itself down and loses its strength, like metal worn away by a file.

Elementary

When you rub a file back and forth on a piece of metal, a little dust falls off each time until the metal gets thinner and thinner. A family that keeps fighting inside loses a little strength every day in the same way, until there is not much left.

University

The image is aram porudha pon (அரம் பொருத பொன், metal struck by a file), which theyum (தேயும், wears away). The second line, uram porudhu (உரம் பொருது), means that the family's own strength (uram) is ground away by the friction of utpakai. Pon usually means gold, as Pope and thirukkural.net render it, but Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss it as iron (இரும்பு), reflecting the wider sense of pon as metal. The echo of porudha / porudhu (struck / striking) emphasises that the family is both the file and the metal, wearing itself down. The text agrees in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Samba, cursed by sages his kinsmen had mocked, produces an iron bolt, King Ugrasena has it ground into fine powder and cast into the sea; the Vrishnis and Andhakas later destroy one another, and the Mahabharata says they were consumed by that iron bolt.
— Mahabharata, Mausala Parva, Section 1 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The Yadava clan, invincible to outsiders, perishes in a drunken quarrel among its own members.
As in the Kural, the clan's strength is ground away from within, and the epic even uses the image of iron filed to powder.
Christianity
“But if ye bite and devour one another, take heed that ye be not consumed one of another.”
— Galatians 5:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns the Galatian church that infighting will wear the community away.
Both texts picture internal friction slowly consuming a group's strength.
Islam
“And obey Allah and His Messenger, and do not dispute and [thus] lose courage and [then] your strength would depart; and be patient.”
— Qur'an 8:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an teaches that internal disputes sap a community's courage and strength.
Like the Kural, it says quarrelling within makes a group's strength wear away.
Judaism
After Solomon's death, the people quarrel with his son Rehoboam, and the ten northern tribes break away, leaving Israel split into two rival kingdoms.
— 1 Kings 12:1-20 [4]
The Hebrew Bible presents the division as a lasting weakening of the nation.
The Kural's image of strength worn down by internal friction fits the divided kingdom.
Buddhism
After the death of the Jain leader Nigantha Nataputta, his followers split into factions and attacked one another with sharp words; the Buddha then taught Ananda ways of settling disputes so that his own Sangha would not suffer the same fate.
— Majjhima Nikaya 104, Samagama Sutta [6]
The Buddha uses a neighbouring community's decay through infighting as a warning.
The Kural's picture of a group wearing itself away through internal enmity is exactly what the sutta describes.
Jainism
“Even one who has subsided or repressed all his passions, once more experiences a terrible spiritual degeneration, hence one ought not to become complacent when some remnants of passions still continue.”
— Saman Suttam 133 (English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [7]
Jain teaching warns that passions which have only been suppressed, not destroyed, keep working on the soul and can bring about a steep fall from spiritual heights already reached.
The Kural pictures a family worn down by internal enmity as metal ground away by a file, and the Jain verse likewise describes a hidden remnant that keeps wearing away what has been won.
Sikhism
“The five thieves plunder their homes, and egotism breaks in.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 854, Raag Bilaaval, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das pictures the home of the heart being robbed from the inside by the vices, with ego breaking through its walls.
The Kural pictures a family worn away by enmity within, like metal under a file, and Gurbani likewise shows a house emptied from within rather than by an outside attacker.
Greek Mythology
The quarrel between Agamemnon and Achilles over a captive woman leads Achilles to withdraw from battle, and the Greek army suffers heavy losses at Trojan hands.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
The Iliad opens with internal discord that weakens the Greeks more than any enemy has.
Like the Kural's filed metal, the army loses its strength through friction within its own ranks.
Philosophy
“Nam concordia parvae res crescunt, discordia maximae dilabuntur.”
— Sallust, The Jugurthine War 10.6 [10]
The dying King Micipsa tells his sons that through harmony small states grow, while through discord the greatest fall apart.
This is the Kural's principle in Latin: internal discord wears down even the greatest strength.

Real Life Example

The Byzantine civil war of 1341-1347
1341-1347 · Constantinople and Thrace, Byzantine Empire
After Emperor Andronicus III died in 1341, John Cantacuzenus, who claimed the regency, and the court of the young John V, led by the empress Anna of Savoy, fell into civil war, and Cantacuzenus proclaimed himself emperor. During the war he allied with the Ottoman Turks and in 1347 retook Constantinople with Turkish help, becoming co-emperor. The fighting ruined much of what remained of the Byzantine economy, allowed the Serbian ruler Stefan Dushan to seize large territories, and helped the Ottomans gain their first foothold in Europe at Gallipoli in 1354.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Six years of fighting between rival factions within the court wore away the strength of an empire already in decline and invited in the power that would later conquer it.
The kural pictures a house afflicted by internal enmity wearing itself away like metal under a file, which is what the civil war did to Byzantium.
