Kural 598

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

உள்ளம் இலாதவர் எய்தார் உலகத்து வள்ளியம் என்னுஞ் செருக்கு.
Ullam ilaadhavar eydhaar ulagaththu Valliyam ennum serukku.

Meaning

People without inner drive will never know the proud joy of saying, “We are the generous givers of the world.”

Elementary

To share lots of cookies with your friends, you first have to bake them. The Kural says people without energy never get to feel the happy pride of being the one who gives to everyone, because they never make enough to give.

University

‘Ullam ilaadhavar’ again uses ullam for ookkam; ‘vallium ennum serukku’ is the elation or proud self-regard (serukku) of being able to say ‘we are liberal’ (valliyam, from vallal, a munificent patron). The logic, drawn out by Parimelazhagar, is that liberality requires wealth, wealth requires effort, and effort requires ookkam, so the unenergetic are cut off from one of the highest satisfactions of kingship and householdership, the role of the vallal celebrated in Sangam poetry (the seven great patrons, kadaiyezhu vallalgal). Solomon Pappaiah calls it ‘mana uyarvu’, uplift of mind, while Kalaignar reads ‘ullam’ as the eager will to give freely. Sivayogi Sivakumar’s reading, ‘pride of having conquered the world’, diverges from the mainstream. Thirukkural.net’s ‘the poor in spirit can never attain the pride of being the generous patrons of the world’ matches the majority view; the Tamil is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The teacher’s parting instruction to graduating students tells them to give with faith, not without faith, to give with liberality (śriyā), with modesty, with awe and with understanding.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.3 [3]
The Upanishad treats generous giving as a mark of a noble, full-hearted life.
The Kural adds that such magnificent giving is only possible for the energetic.
Christianity
“Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul says true giving flows from a willing, glad heart rather than reluctance.
Both texts see generous giving as springing from largeness of heart.
Islam
“The upper hand is better than the lower hand. The upper hand is that of the giver and the lower (hand) is that of the beggar.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1429 [5]
The Prophet praises the dignity of being the one who gives rather than the one who must ask.
The Kural likewise prizes the honour of being a giver, which the unenergetic never attain.
Judaism
Proverbs observes that one person scatters freely and yet grows richer, while another withholds and comes to want, and that the generous soul will be enriched and the one who waters others will himself be watered.
— Proverbs 11:24-25 [6]
The sages praise the open-handed, large-spirited person who gives freely.
Like the Kural, Proverbs treats generosity as the mark of a big spirit.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that misers do not reach the heavenly realms and fools do not praise generosity, but the wise rejoice in giving and are happy because of it.
— Dhammapada 177 [7]
The Buddha contrasts small-minded misers with the wise, who find joy in giving.
The Kural similarly says only the large-spirited taste the joy of generosity.
Jainism
“He gave up his gold and silver, his troops and chariots, and distributed, portioned out, and gave away his valuable treasures (consisting of) riches, corn, gold, pearls, &c., and distributed among those who wanted to make presents to others. Thus he gave away during a whole year.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra 2.15.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Before renouncing the world Mahavira spent a whole year giving away his wealth, a great gift that Jain tradition says every Tirthankara makes before renunciation.
The Kural says only those with energy of spirit can know the joy of being the world's generous givers; in Jainism the year of giving comes from the same resolute spirit that leads to renunciation.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak joins honest labour (kirat karni) to sharing (vand chhakna): one who earns by his own effort can give from his own hand.
The Kural says those without energy of spirit will never know the joy of being generous givers; Guru Nanak likewise makes one's own labour the basis of true giving.
Greek Mythology
The poor elderly couple Baucis and Philemon, who had almost nothing, eagerly served disguised Zeus and Hermes their best food and wine when every rich household had turned the gods away.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 [10]
The poorest couple in the district proved the most generous because their hearts were large.
The story shows that the pride of generosity belongs to the large-hearted, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
Aristotle’s great-souled (megalopsychos) person is the sort who confers benefits but is ashamed to receive them, and who likes to repay more than he received.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3 (1124b) [11]
Aristotle’s ideal of greatness of soul includes taking pride in being a benefactor.
This matches the Kural’s ‘pride of being generous’, which belongs only to those with a large spirit.

Real Life Example

Milton Hershey rises from bankruptcy to found a school for orphaned boys
1876-1918 · Philadelphia, New York and Hershey, Pennsylvania, United States
Milton Hershey's first candy business in Philadelphia went bankrupt after six years, and a second attempt in New York also failed. Returning to Lancaster with little money, he built the Lancaster Caramel Company, sold it in 1900 for one million dollars, and went on to build the Hershey Chocolate Company. On 15 November 1909 he and his wife Catherine signed the deed of trust founding the Hershey Industrial School for orphaned boys, and in 1918 he placed his controlling interest in the chocolate company in trust for the school.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The same drive that carried Hershey through repeated business failures later made him one of the most generous benefactors of his time.
The kural says those without energy of spirit will never know the proud joy of being generous givers, and Hershey's school shows the giving that energy made possible.
