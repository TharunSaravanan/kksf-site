Kural 410

Ignorance · Kallaamai · கல்லாமை

விலங்கொடு மக்கள் அனையர் இலங்குநூல் கற்றாரோடு ஏனை யவர்.
Vilangodu makkal anaiyar ilangunool Katraarodu enai yavar.

Meaning

Next to people who have studied great books, uneducated people are like animals next to human beings.

Elementary

Think of how different a person is from an animal. Valluvar says the difference between someone who has studied great books and someone who hasn’t is just as big. Learning is what makes us fully human.

University

The closing kural states the chapter’s thesis as a proportion: learned : unlearned :: humans : animals. Ilangu nool (‘shining, illustrious texts’) specifies that it is serious learning, not mere literacy, that marks the difference. The analogy draws on a long tradition, shared across cultures, that locates the distinctively human in reason and cultivated understanding. Bhartrhari’s Nitishataka 12 says one devoid of literature, music and art is a beast without tail and horns; Aristotle, asked how the educated differ from the uneducated, replied ‘as much as the living from the dead’; Psalm 49:20 compares the man in honour without understanding to the beasts that perish; the Qur’an (7:179) likens those who do not use their hearts to understand to livestock; and the Dhammapada’s man of little learning grows old ‘like a bull’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari says a man devoid of literature, music and the arts is truly a beast, only without a tail and horns — and it is the animals’ great good fortune that he does not also eat grass.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka, verse 12 [4]
Without cultivated learning and art, a human being is really an animal in human shape.
Almost exactly the Kural’s proportion between the learned and the unlearned.
Christianity
“But these speak evil of those things which they know not: but what they know naturally, as brute beasts, in those things they corrupt themselves.”
— Jude 1:10 (KJV) [5]
Jude describes people who talk about what they don’t understand and know only what brute beasts know by instinct.
Both compare those without understanding to animals.
Islam
“They have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. Those are like livestock; rather, they are more astray.”
— Qur’an 7:179 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an says people who do not use their faculties to understand are like cattle — or even worse off.
The same comparison: failing to cultivate understanding reduces a human to the level of animals.
Judaism
“Man that is in honour, and understandeth not, is like the beasts that perish.”
— Psalm 49:20 (Hebrew Bible; KJV wording; Ps 49:21 in Hebrew numbering) [5]
The psalmist says that a person, even one of high standing, who lacks understanding is no better than an animal.
Very close to the Kural: without understanding, humans are like beasts.
Buddhism
“The man of little learning grows old like a bull. He grows only in bulk, but, his wisdom does not grow.”
— Dhammapada 152 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
Someone who does not learn only gets older and bigger, like an ox — never wiser.
Both compare the unlearned to an animal.
Jainism
“As a pig leaves a trough filled with grain to feed on faeces, so a brute (of a man) turns away from virtue, and takes to evil ways. Hearing a man thus compared to a dog and a pig, he who desires his own welfare, should adhere to good conduct.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.5–6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's discipline lecture compares the person who turns from good teaching to animals that choose filth over grain.
Valluvar says the unlearned compare with the learned as beasts compare with men. The Jain verse uses the same animal comparison for those who turn away from instruction.
Sikhism
“They belong to the human species, but they act like animals.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 267, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan describes people who are human in form but beast-like in conduct.
The Kural says the unlearned compare with the learned as beasts with humans; Guru Arjan uses the same human-and-beast contrast.
Greek Mythology
Circe turns Odysseus’s companions into swine, though their minds remain as before; Odysseus alone is protected by the herb moly, which Hermes gives him along with knowledge of how to resist her.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.233–306 [10]
Those without the protecting knowledge are reduced to animals; the one who has it remains fully human.
An allegorical parallel: lack of knowledge leaves a person reduced to the level of beasts.
Philosophy
“Asked how much the educated are superior to the uneducated, Aristotle replied: “As much as the living are to the dead.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 5.19 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
Aristotle says the gap between the educated and the uneducated is as great as the gap between life and death.
The same proportional form as the Kural, with living/dead in place of humans/animals.

Real Life Example

King Sejong creates the Korean alphabet for ordinary people
1443-1446 · Hanseong (Seoul), Joseon Korea
In fifteenth-century Korea people wrote in Chinese characters, which were very hard for ordinary people to master, so literacy was largely confined to the elite. King Sejong, the fourth ruler of the Joseon dynasty, had a new phonetic alphabet devised in 1443 and promulgated it in 1446 in the book Hunminjeongeum, The Proper Sounds for Instructing the People, whose preface by the king explains his purpose of enabling common people to express themselves in writing. The 1446 book was inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 1997.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Sejong treated access to letters as something all his subjects should share, opening learning to people who had been shut out of it.
The kural sets the learned above the unlearned as humans above beasts, and Sejong's alphabet was created so that common people, excluded from letters, could share fully in the human life of reading and writing.
