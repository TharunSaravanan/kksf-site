Kural 1257

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

நாணென ஒன்றோ அறியலம் காமத்தால் பேணியார் பெட்ப செயின்.
Naanena ondroa ariyalam kaamaththaal paeniyaar petpa seyin.

Meaning

When the one I love, out of love, does the things I long for, I no longer know anything called modesty.

Elementary

When someone you love is kind to you and does just what you hoped, you forget to be shy around them. You just feel happy and close. The heroine says that is how she feels when her husband shows his love.

University

Here nāṇ (shyness, modesty) is not broken by force but forgotten: when pēṇiyār (the one I cherish) does peṭpa (what I desire) out of kāmam, ‘we do not know anything called shame’ (nāṇ eṉa oṉṟō aṟiyalam). The plural aṟiyalam can be inclusive (‘we women’) or the heroine’s own, and commentators differ on tense: Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it conditionally (‘if he does…, we shall not know’), Solomon Pappaiah as past experience (‘when he did… I did not know shame’). Pope’s rendering on ytamizh.com (‘No sense of shame my gladdened mind shall prove, / When he returns my longing heart to bless with love’) takes it as a hope for his return. Read within the chapter, the couplet explains why reserve fails: in the presence of a loving husband, nāṇ has no place. The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the last canto of the Gita Govinda, after Radha and Krishna are reunited, Radha playfully asks Krishna to paint her forehead mark, set her ornaments and arrange her hair, and he gladly does as she asks.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 12 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva ends his poem with a beloved who, with a loving partner, sets shyness aside entirely.
The Kural’s heroine says the same: when her beloved does what she desires, she knows no shyness.
Christianity
“And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.”
— Genesis 2:25 (KJV) [4]
In the garden before the fall, the first husband and wife stand before each other with no shame at all.
The Kural’s heroine describes the same freedom: in love returned, shame has no place.
Islam
“They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them.”
— Qur’an 2:187 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes husband and wife as each other’s garment: closeness, comfort and covering.
The Kural’s couple share the same closeness: in mutual love, the wife no longer needs the cover of shyness.
Judaism
“Oh that thou wert as my brother, that sucked the breasts of my mother! When I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; yea, and none would despise me.”
— Song of Songs 8:1 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman of the Song wishes she could show her love openly, without anyone thinking it shameful.
The Kural’s heroine says that when her love is returned, she does not even feel shyness.
Buddhism
Nakula’s father and Nakula’s mother tell the Buddha that since they were married young they have never been unfaithful to each other even in thought, and that they wish to see each other in this life and in lives to come.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Anguttara Nikaya praises a lay couple whose love is open, mutual and wholehearted.
Their unguarded devotion parallels the Kural’s picture of love so mutual that shyness falls away.
Jainism
“Come, let us enjoy pleasures, for it is a rare chance to be born a human being; after we have enjoyed pleasures, we shall enter on the path of the Ginas.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Overcome by passion, the monk Rathanemi forgets his vows and the shame they should bring, and urges Rajimati to enjoy pleasures with him.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Rathanemi forgets shame in desire; but the Jain text treats this forgetting as a fall, whereas the Kural sees it within a loving marriage.
Sikhism
“She surrenders her body and mind to Him, and does not remain separate from Him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 793, Raag Soohee, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas describes the beloved bride who holds nothing back from her Husband and keeps no distance from Him.
The Kural's wife says that when her beloved lovingly does what she desires she no longer knows anything called shyness; Ravidas's bride likewise keeps no reserve at all before the Husband she loves.
Greek Mythology
Sappho says the most beautiful thing is whatever one loves, and gives Helen as proof: she left behind her husband, her child and her parents and sailed to Troy, led astray by love.
— Sappho, fragment 16 (paraphrase) [10]
Sappho uses Helen to show that love can make a person forget every claim of propriety and family.
The Kural’s heroine also forgets shyness in love, though within her own marriage.
Philosophy
Pascal writes that the heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées, no. 277 (Brunschvicg numbering; paraphrase; cf. tr. W.F. Trotter) [11]
Pascal argues that the heart follows a logic of its own that calm reason cannot fully grasp.
The Kural’s heroine finds this in her own heart: when love is returned, the reasoned reserve of shyness simply disappears.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria's journal on her wedding day
10 February 1840 · London, England
Queen Victoria married Prince Albert on 10 February 1840. That night she wrote in her journal about Albert's great love and affection, calling him her precious Angel and saying his love gave her a happiness she had never hoped to feel. She ended the entry by calling it the happiest day of her life.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A reigning monarch, trained to be reserved in public, wrote with complete openness once her husband showed her love.
The kural says that when the beloved, out of love, does what one longs for, shyness is forgotten; Victoria's unguarded wedding-night journal shows reserve melting in the warmth of returned love.
