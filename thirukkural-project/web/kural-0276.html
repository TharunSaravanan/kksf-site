Kural 276

Inconsistent Conduct · Koodaavozhukkam · கூடாவொழுக்கம்

நெஞ்சின் துறவார் துறந்தார்போல் வஞ்சித்து வாழ்வாரின் வன்கணார் இல்.
Nenjin thuravaar thurandhaarpol vanjiththu Vaazhvaarin vankanaar il.

Meaning

No one is more hard-hearted than people who have not truly given up the world in their hearts, yet trick others by acting as if they have.

Elementary

Some people pretend to be kind helpers who need nothing, so others trust them and give them things — but secretly they are greedy. Valluvar says that tricking people who trust you like this is about the meanest thing a person can do.

University

Valluvar’s strongest verdict in the chapter uses வன்கணார் — literally “the hard-eyed,” those without compassion — and makes it superlative: none are worse (இல்). The contrast நெஞ்சின் துறவார் / துறந்தார்போல் (“not renounced in heart” / “like those who renounced”) sharpens the gap between inner reality and outward role. The special cruelty lies in exploiting the reverence and generosity that society extends to genuine renunciants, and so corrupting the very institution of trust. Plato made the same ranking when Adeimantus and Glaucon called seeming-just-while-being-unjust the ‘highest reach’ of injustice, and Manu classes such pretenders as ‘cats’ bent on doing injury.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“(A man) who, ever covetous, displays the flag of virtue, (who is) a hypocrite, a deceiver of the people, intent on doing injury, (and) a detractor (from the merits) of all men, one must know to be one who acts like a cat.”
— Manusmriti 4.195 (Bühler translation) [3]
Manu describes the ‘cat-like’ pretender: greedy inside, flying the banner of virtue outside, deceiving people and bent on harm.
A point-by-point match: inner craving, outward show of virtue, deceit, and cruelty toward others.
Christianity
“Ye serpents, ye generation of vipers, how can ye escape the damnation of hell?”
— Matthew 23:33 (KJV) [4]
Jesus closes his denunciation of the hypocritical scribes and Pharisees with his harshest words anywhere in the Gospels.
Like the Kural, it treats religious hypocrisy as the worst category of wrongdoing.
Islam
“Indeed, the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of the Fire – and never will you find for them a helper.”
— Qur’an 4:145 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an places hypocrites in the very lowest level of Hell, below open disbelievers.
Shares the Kural’s superlative judgement: those who feign faith are worse than any other sinners.
Judaism
“The words of his mouth were smoother than butter, but war was in his heart: his words were softer than oil, yet were they drawn swords.”
— Psalm 55:21 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes a betrayer whose gentle words hide a heart set on violence.
Captures the cruelty the Kural sees in soft outward piety covering a hostile, grasping heart.
Buddhism
“Not by shaven head does a man who is indisciplined and untruthful become a monk. How can he who is full of desire and greed be a monk?”
— Dhammapada 264 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha asks how anyone still full of craving could truly be a monk, whatever his shaved head suggests.
Matches the Kural’s ‘not renounced in heart yet appearing renounced’.
Jainism
“A cut-throat enemy will not do him such harm as his own perversity will do him; the man without pity will feel repentance in the hour of death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Speaking of monks who break their vows, the text calls such a man pitiless and says his own perversity harms him more than any murderer could.
Valluvar calls those who pose as renunciants while deceiving others the most hard-hearted of all; the Jain verse likewise labels the false monk 'without pity' and says his perversity will turn on him.
Sikhism
“The man-eaters say their prayers. Those who wield the knife wear the sacred thread around their necks.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 471, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak attacks officials of his day who devoured the people and preyed on the poor while making a show of prayer and wearing the sacred thread.
Valluvar calls none more hard-hearted than those who live by deceiving others while posing as holy; Guru Nanak's man-eaters at prayer show the same cruelty under a religious surface.
Greek Mythology
King Lycaon receives Zeus (Jupiter) as a guest with an outward show of hospitality, but secretly plans to murder him and serves him human flesh; Zeus destroys his house and turns him into a wolf.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.209–243 [9]
A sacred duty — hospitality to a guest — is used as a cover for murderous cruelty.
Like the false renunciant, Lycaon exploits a sacred role to harm the trusting, and is shown to be a beast within.
Philosophy
“…for the highest reach of injustice is: to be deemed just when you are not.”
— Plato, Republic 2.361a (tr. Jowett) [10]
In the Republic, Glaucon argues that the perfectly unjust man is the one who does wrong while enjoying a reputation for justice.
The same ranking as the Kural: the person who does wrong behind a reputation for goodness is the worst of all.

Real Life Example

Moliere's Tartuffe is banned
12 May 1664 · Versailles and Paris, France
Moliere's comedy Tartuffe, about a man who feigns intense piety in order to gain control of a trusting family's household and fortune, was first performed before Louis XIV at Versailles on 12 May 1664. After attacks from devout critics, the king banned public performances, and a revised version was banned again in Paris in 1667. Public performance was finally permitted on 5 February 1669, and the play became one of the most successful in the French repertoire.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Tartuffe's name became a byword in French and English for the religious hypocrite who preys on the trust of the devout.
The kural says none are more hard-hearted than those who live by deceiving others while posing as renunciants, which is precisely the character Moliere put on the stage.
