Kural 1195

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

நாம்காதல் கொண்டார் நமக்கெவன் செய்பவோ தாம்காதல் கொள்ளாக் கடை.
Naamkaadhal kondaar namakkevan seypavo thaamkaadhal kollaak kadai.

Meaning

If the one I love does not love me back, what good can he possibly do for me?

Elementary

If you keep waving at someone across the playground and they never wave back, the waving can’t make you happy. Love needs someone answering from the other side.

University

The question form nam(a)kku evaṉ ceypavō, ‘what will they do for us?’, is rhetorical: the heroine admits that her own love (nām kātal koṇṭār, ‘the one we have taken love for’) can bring her nothing if tām kātal koḷḷā kaṭai, ‘when he does not take love (for us).’ Parimelazhagar’s reading, followed by Varadarasanar, makes the implied answer ‘no good at all’; Pappaiah and Kalaignar render ‘what joy will he give?’ The polite plural (koṇṭār, ceypa) keeps the husband at a distance even as she complains. Pope: ‘From him I love to me what gain can be, / Unless, as I love him, he loveth me?’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhramara Gita, a gopi who has been left behind by Krishna sees a bee and addresses it as his messenger, bitterly asking what use his sweet words are when he has forgotten them, and comparing him to a bee that leaves each flower once it has drunk its nectar.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47, Bhramara Gita (paraphrase) [3]
The gopis voice the bitterness of a love that is not answered with presence or care.
The gopi’s question ‘what good is he to us now?’ is the heroine’s question in the Kural.
Christianity
“For if ye love them which love you, what thank have ye? for sinners also love those that love them.”
— Luke 6:32 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that love which only answers love is nothing special, and calls for love that expects no return.
Here Christianity pushes against the Kural’s complaint: the Gospel asks for love that does not depend on being loved back.
Islam
“O `Abbas ! are you not astonished at the love of Mughith for Barira and the hatred of Barira for Mughith?” … She said, “I am not in need of him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5283 [5]
Ibn Abbas recalls Mughith following his freed wife Barira through Medina, weeping, while she refused even the Prophet’s intercession for him.
Mughith’s tears show the Kural’s truth: love given to one who does not return it brings nothing but grief.
Judaism
“Because the LORD hath looked upon my affliction; for now my husband will love me.”
— Genesis 29:32 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Leah, married to Jacob who loves her sister Rachel, names her firstborn in the hope that this at last will win her husband’s love.
Leah’s repeated longing for a husband who does not love her is the heroine’s question in the Kural lived out across a family history.
Buddhism
In the Sakkapanha Sutta the heavenly musician Pancasikha sings to his lute a love song for Bhadda, who does not return his love, mixing his longing with praise of the Buddha and the arahants; the Buddha remarks on how well the song and the strings agree.
— Digha Nikaya 21, Sakkapanha Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The sutta preserves a song of one-sided longing, set within a discourse that leads toward freedom from desire.
Pancasikha’s unanswered song is the Kural’s situation: love offered to one who gives nothing back.
Jainism
Princess Rajimati is betrothed to Arishtanemi, but on the way to the wedding he hears the cries of animals penned for the feast, turns back and renounces the world; Rajimati, grieving, resolves that a love he will not return as a husband can only be followed into renunciation, and becomes a nun.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (Rathanemi) (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The twenty-second Tirthankara’s story makes the bride’s unreturned love the turning point of her own spiritual life.
Rajimati faces the Kural’s question, what can he do for me if he does not love me, and answers it the Jain way, by renunciation.
Sikhism
“But one who feels good only when good is done for him, and feels bad when things go badly - do not call him a lover. He trades only for his own account.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar, Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar, Guru Angad says that love which asks what it will get, and sours when it gets nothing, is not love but trading.
The Kural's wife asks what good her beloved can do her if he does not love her back; Guru Angad answers that real love does not ask what it gains, so the verse is a gentle Sikh counterpoint to her complaint.
Greek Mythology
In her hymn to Aphrodite, Sappho begs the goddess to come again, and recalls Aphrodite smilingly asking whom she must now persuade to love her, promising that one who now flees will soon pursue.
— Sappho, fragment 1 (Hymn to Aphrodite) (paraphrase) [10]
Sappho’s prayer arises because the one she loves does not love her back.
The Kural’s heroine and Sappho share the same ache: love that is not returned brings only pain.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that when we love something like ourselves, we strive as far as we can to bring it about that it loves us in return.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Proposition 33 (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, the desire to be loved back is built into the very nature of love.
The Kural’s question rests on this same law: love naturally seeks its return, and suffers when it fails.

Real Life Example

Ivan Turgenev's lifelong devotion to Pauline Viardot
1843–1883 · St Petersburg, Russia; Paris, France; Baden-Baden, Germany
The Russian writer Ivan Turgenev fell in love with the singer Pauline Viardot when she first performed in Russia in 1843. In 1847 he left Russia for Paris to be near her, and after the Crimean War he moved to Baden-Baden, again to be with her; he wrote the libretto for her operetta Le dernier sorcier (1869). He later settled at Bougival, near Paris, where he died in 1883.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Turgenev organised his whole adult life around a woman who gave him friendship and artistic partnership but never married him.
The kural asks what good the beloved can do if he does not love in return, and Turgenev's decades of following Viardot show both how much such love asks and how little it can be sure of receiving.
