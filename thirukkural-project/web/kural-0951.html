Kural 951

Lineage · Kudimai · குடிமை

இற்பிறந்தார் கண்அல்லது இல்லை இயல்பாகச் செப்பமும் நாணும் ஒருங்கு.
Irpirandhaar kan alladhu illai iyalpaagach cheppamum naanum orungu.

Meaning

Only people born into a good family naturally have these two together: being fair and honest, and feeling shame at doing wrong.

Elementary

Some children say sorry and play fair without anyone telling them, because they have seen it at home every day. Valluvar says that fairness and feeling bad about doing wrong come most naturally to people who grew up in a good home.

University

The chapter opens the Kudiyiyal (குடியியல், the section on the citizen and his family) of Porutpal, and its first couplet sets the theme: kudimai (குடிமை) is the character that a good lineage (இல், 'house', hence family) is expected to carry. Cheppam (செப்பம்) is glossed by Mu. Varadarasanar as நடுவு நிலைமை, 'impartiality, rectitude', and by Solomon Pappaiah as purity of mind, word and deed; naanum (நாணும்) is shame or modesty, the recoil from what is unworthy. The force lies in iyalpaaga (இயல்பாக, 'by nature') and orungu (ஒருங்கு, 'together'): others may show one quality by effort, but the high-born show both effortlessly. Kalaignar's modern gloss reverses the direction, saying that only those who possess these qualities may be counted high-born, an egalitarian reading of a couplet that the medieval commentarial tradition read more literally. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna lists the qualities of one born to the divine nature (daivi sampad), among them truthfulness, freedom from anger, modesty (hri), steadiness, and freedom from pride, and says these belong to those so born.
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 [3]
The Gita treats a cluster of virtues, including modesty, as the natural endowment of a certain kind of birth.
Like the Kural, it speaks of shame and rectitude as an inborn inheritance rather than a learned skill.
Christianity
“A good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is good; and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is evil: for of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh.”
— Luke 6:45 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that conduct flows naturally from the inner store a person carries.
The Kural's iyalpaaga, 'by nature', matches the idea that goodness comes out of what is already within.
Islam
“Leave him as Haya' is a part of faith.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 24 (USC-MSA translation, Sunnah.com) [5]
When a man scolded his brother for being too modest, the Prophet told him to stop, because modesty (haya') belongs to faith itself.
Both texts treat a sense of shame as a core part of a good character rather than a weakness.
Judaism
“There are three distinguishing marks of this nation, the Jewish people. They are merciful, they are shamefaced, and they perform acts of kindness.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 79a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
The Talmud has David name shame, mercy and kindness as the inherited marks of the descendants of Abraham, and says those who lack them are unfit to join the people.
Like the Kural, it treats shamefacedness as a sign that belongs naturally to a good lineage.
Buddhism
“Monks, these two bright qualities guard the world. Which two? Conscience & concern.”
— Lokapala Sutta, AN 2.9 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha names hiri and ottappa, shame at wrongdoing and fear of its results, as the pair that keeps the social order intact.
The Kural also pairs two qualities, rectitude and shame, and treats them as working together.
Jainism
Before Mahavira's birth, the god Sakra reflects that Arhats are never born into low, poor or mean families but only into noble lines such as the Ugras, Bhogas, Ikshvakus and Harivamsa, and so has the embryo moved to the womb of the Kshatriya queen Trisala.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira (Sakra's reflection and the embryo transfer), tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22 [8]
The Kalpa Sutra's account of the embryo transfer shows that Jain narrative, like the Kural, could treat noble birth as a fitting vessel for exceptional virtue.
The Kural's claim that certain virtues appear naturally only in the well-born finds an echo in this Jain story about where great souls are born.
Sikhism
“What good is social class and status? Truthfulness is measured within.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 142, Raag Majh, Majh ki Vaar, salok of Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Majh ki Vaar, Guru Nanak denies that birth or social class counts for anything before God; what is tested is the truth a person carries inside.
The Kural says rectitude and shame come naturally only to the well-born; Guru Nanak agrees that such qualities are what matter but places them in the person, not the family, much as Kalaignar's modern gloss does.
Greek Mythology
“You must be descended from a line of sceptre-bearing kings, for poor people do not have such sons as you are.”
— Homer, Odyssey 4 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Menelaus, seeing Telemachus and Pisistratus at his table, guesses their royal lineage from their bearing before they say a word.
Like the Kural, Homer assumes that noble birth shows itself naturally in a person's manner.
Philosophy
“… they seem to have power to encourage and stimulate the generous-minded among our youth, and to make a character which is gently born, and a true lover of what is noble, ready to be possessed by virtue, they are not able to encourage the many to nobility and goodness. For these do not by nature obey the sense of shame, but only fear …”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.9, 1179b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says that moral arguments work only on a character that is already well-born and loves what is noble. The many, by nature, respond to fear of punishment rather than to a sense of shame.
Like the Kural, Aristotle treats a natural responsiveness to shame as the mark of the well-born, something the many lack.

Real Life Example

Princess Alice of Battenberg hides the Cohen family in occupied Athens
1943-1944 · Athens, Greece
Princess Alice of Battenberg, mother of Prince Philip and a member of the Greek royal family by marriage, had known the family of Haimaki Cohen, a former Greek member of parliament, before the war. After German forces took control of Athens in September 1943, she took his widow Rachel Cohen and some of her children into her own residence and hid them there until the city was liberated, answering questions from the Gestapo without giving them away. She never spoke of it; her children learned of it only later, and in 1993 Yad Vashem recognised her as Righteous Among the Nations.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Alice's rescue combined uprightness in a time of danger with a modesty so complete that her own family did not know what she had done.
The kural says rectitude and a sense of shame, found together as second nature, mark those of good family, and Alice did right quietly and never sought credit for it.
