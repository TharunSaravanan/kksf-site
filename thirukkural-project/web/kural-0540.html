Kural 540

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

உள்ளியது எய்தல் எளிதுமன் மற்றுந்தான் உள்ளியது உள்ளப் பெறின்.
Ulliyadhu eydhal elidhuman matrundhaan Ulliyadhu ullap perin.

Meaning

It is easy to get what you set your mind on, as long as you keep thinking about it and never let it slip from your mind.

Elementary

If you want to learn to ride a bike, you have to keep that goal in your mind every day, even after you fall. Kids who keep thinking about their goal and don't let it slip away usually get there.

University

The couplet turns on repeating உள்ளியது (‘what was thought of’) together with the verb உள்ளு (‘to think of, keep in mind’): attaining your aim is easy if you can ‘think again what you thought’. Parimelazhagar reads it as advice to a king: he will easily obtain what he intended, exactly as he intended it, if he can keep thinking of that same thing, which he explains as striving towards it without forgetting. He takes மன் as an ஒழியிசை particle, one that implies an unspoken ‘but that is hard to manage’, so the couplet quietly admits that sustained attention is the difficult part. Pavanar rejects this analysis of மன் as unsuitable here and takes மற்று to mean ‘afterwards, further’. Manakkudavar reads the couplet as the requirement to remember, above all, what one first resolved. Parimelazhagar groups 539-540 as the means (உபாயம்) for achieving unforgetfulness.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Drona tests his pupils by setting a wooden bird on a treetop and asking each what he sees. The others describe the tree, the branches, their brothers and their teacher, and are sent away. Arjuna says he sees only the bird's head, is told to shoot, and brings it down.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva (Sambhava Parva), Drona's test of the Kuru princes, trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
The episode is the classic Indian image of a mind that holds unwaveringly to what it has aimed at.
Arjuna embodies the Kural's formula: to keep thinking of what one has set one's mind on is to attain it easily.
Christianity
“Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus.”
— Philippians 3:13-14 (KJV) [5]
Paul describes a life gathered into ‘this one thing’: fixing on the goal ahead and pressing towards it.
Like the Kural, it presents steady, single-minded attention to the goal as the way to reach it.
Islam
“The reward of deeds depends upon the intentions and every person will get the reward according to what he has intended.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1 [6]
The opening hadith of Bukhari makes the intention held in the heart the determining factor in what a person obtains from his deeds.
The Kural too ties attainment to what one keeps firmly in mind.
Judaism
“I have set the LORD always before me; Surely He is at my right hand, I shall not be moved.”
— Psalms 16:8 (JPS 1917) [7]
The Psalmist describes keeping his focus constantly before his eyes, and finds in that steadiness the source of his firmness.
Like the Kural, it links continual remembrance of one's aim with unshakeable success.
Buddhism
“All compounded things are subject to vanish. Strive with earnestness.”
— Digha Nikaya 16 (Mahaparinibbana Sutta) §3.63, trans. Sister Vajira and Francis Story [8]
The Buddha repeats this exhortation near the end of his life, and it is echoed in his final words (appamādena sampādetha, ‘accomplish the goal through heedfulness’). He makes unremitting heedfulness the one thing to hold to until the goal is reached.
The Kural's key to attainment is keeping one's aim before the mind without lapse, which is the Buddha's own last counsel.
Jainism
“You have crossed the great ocean; why do you halt so near the shore? make haste to get on the other side; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira urges Gautama not to slacken near the goal but to keep his mind steadily on reaching the far shore.
The Kural says the goal is easily reached if one keeps thinking of it steadily; Mahavira likewise tells Gautama that sustained, unslackening attention completes the crossing.
Sikhism
“Meditate, meditate, meditate in remembrance of Him, and find peace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 262, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sukhmani opens by urging repeated, unbroken remembrance as the way to peace.
The Kural says what one has set the mind on is easily reached if one keeps thinking of it; Guru Arjan likewise makes sustained, repeated remembrance the means of reaching what is sought.
Greek Mythology
Atalanta, the swiftest runner, can beat any suitor. Hippomenes drops three golden apples from Aphrodite during their race, and each time Atalanta turns aside to pick one up. She loses the race she would certainly have won.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.560-680 (trans. A. S. Kline) [11]
Atalanta loses not through weakness but because her attention is pulled away from the finish line.
She is the opposite of the Kural's ideal: had she kept her mind on what she aimed at, winning would have been easy.
Philosophy
“My experience is what I agree to attend to.”
— William James, The Principles of Psychology (1890), ch. 11 ‘Attention’ [12]
William James argues that selective, sustained attention shapes what a person's mind and life actually become.
The Kural likewise makes steady attention to one's aim the decisive condition for achieving it.

Real Life Example

Howard Carter's last season in the Valley of the Kings
4 November 1922 · Valley of the Kings, Luxor, Egypt
The archaeologist Howard Carter, funded by Lord Carnarvon, had searched the Valley of the Kings for several seasons from 1917 with little to show for it, and in 1922 Carnarvon agreed to pay for one more season. On 4 November 1922, only days after work resumed, Carter's team uncovered a step cut into the rock, and further digging revealed a stairway leading down to a sealed doorway. It was the almost intact tomb of the pharaoh Tutankhamun, into which Carter and Carnarvon first looked on 26 November 1922.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Years of fruitless searching ended in one of the most famous archaeological discoveries ever made, because the searcher kept his goal in view when others were ready to stop.
The kural says reaching what you have set your mind on is easy if you keep thinking of it steadily, and Carter's refusal to let go of his search in the Valley of the Kings was rewarded.
