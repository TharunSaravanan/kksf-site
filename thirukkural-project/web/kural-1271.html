Kural 1271

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

கரப்பினுங் கையிகந் தொல்லாநின் உண்கண் உரைக்கல் உறுவதொன் றுண்டு.
Karappinung kaiyikan thollaanin unkan uraikkal uruvadhon rundu.

Meaning

Even though you try to hide it, your eyes, darkened with eye makeup, will not stay quiet. They have something to tell me.

Elementary

Imagine a friend who says ‘I’m fine’ but whose eyes look sad. You can tell something is wrong, because eyes often say what words keep hidden. The poet says love works the same way: the eyes give away the secret.

University

The chapter title kuṟippaṟivuṟuttal means ‘making known by signs’ (kuṟippu, a hint or covert sign; aṟivuṟuttal, causing to be known), and it belongs to the kaṟpu (wedded love) section, where partners communicate feelings about separation without open speech. Here the husband addresses his wife: karappiṉum (‘though you hide it’), her uṇkaṇ (eyes darkened with collyrium) kai ikantu ollā (‘will not stay within bounds’) and are about to utter (uraikkal uṟuvatu) one thing. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it as a message the eyes force past her concealment; Solomon Pappaiah stresses the painted eyes; Kalaignar names the hidden message as a love that does not want separation, which fits the chapter’s setting of an impending departure. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (ytamizh prints the sandhi as கையிகந் தொல்லாநின்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s play, King Dushyanta notes that Shakuntala does not join her words to his, yet she listens when he speaks, and though she does not turn her face to him, her eyes rest on nothing else for long.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 1 (paraphrase) [3]
Shakuntala is too modest to speak her love, but her eyes betray it, and the king reads them.
Both texts describe a woman whose speech hides her heart while her eyes give it away to the one who loves her.
Christianity
“For there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; neither hid, that shall not be known.”
— Luke 12:2 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that what people keep hidden will in the end come to light.
The Kural applies the same principle to a lover’s heart: what she hides, her eyes reveal.
Islam
“He knows that which deceives the eyes and what the breasts conceal.”
— Qur’an 40:19 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says God knows both the stolen glance and the secret held in the heart.
The Kural links the same two things, hidden feeling in the heart and its leak through the eyes, though here it is the husband, not God, who reads them.
Judaism
“Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my bride; thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes, with one bead of thy necklace.”
— Song of Songs 4:9 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The lover in the Song says a single glance from his bride is enough to capture him.
In both poems the beloved’s eyes speak with a force that needs no words.
Buddhism
The Buddha lists three things that are carried on in secret and three that shine openly, the sun, the moon and the Dhamma, which need no concealment.
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.129, Paticchanna Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The sutta contrasts what must hide from view with what is naturally open and shining.
The Kural shows a secret that cannot stay hidden; the sutta’s contrast between the concealed and the openly shining is the nearest Buddhist parallel, though its concern is truth, not love.
Jainism
“In the assembly he pronounces holy (words), yet secretly he commits sins; but the wise know him to be a deceiver and great rogue.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga observes that a monk who hides his lapses behind pious words is still seen through by the discerning.
As in the Kural, concealment fails before someone who knows how to read the signs, though the Jain text is exposing hypocrisy, not tender feeling.
Sikhism
“O my Lord and Master, You alone know the state of my inner being.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 735, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that the Lord sees what is hidden inside the devotee, whatever is shown outwardly.
In the Kural the husband sees through his wife's attempt to hide her feelings, because her eyes speak; Guru Ram Das likewise says the inner state cannot be hidden from the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
When Medea first sees Jason in the palace of Aeetes, she keeps stealing glances at him, and her eyes and blushing cheeks betray the love she is struggling to hide from her family.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.C. Seaton) [10]
Apollonius shows a young woman’s secret passion leaking out through her gaze despite her efforts to keep it silent.
Medea’s eyes, like the wife’s in the Kural, refuse to keep the heart’s secret.
Philosophy
Emerson writes that the eyes of men converse at least as much as their tongues, and that this ocular dialect needs no dictionary but is understood all the world over.
— Ralph Waldo Emerson, The Conduct of Life, ‘Behavior’ (paraphrase) [11]
Emerson treats the eye as a language of its own that communicates even when speech is guarded.
The Kural’s whole point is that the eyes speak a message the tongue holds back.

Real Life Example

Jeremiah Denton blinks a hidden message in Morse code
1966 · Hanoi, North Vietnam
U.S. Navy pilot Jeremiah Denton was shot down and captured on 18 July 1965 during the Vietnam War. In 1966 he was made to give an interview to a Japanese television crew for a North Vietnamese propaganda film, and while answering the questions he blinked the letters T-O-R-T-U-R-E in Morse code. The film reached the United States, where the hidden message was read, and Denton was released in February 1973 after more than seven years as a prisoner.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Though he was forced to speak words chosen by his captors, Denton's eyes carried the true message past them.
The kural says that however much one hides a feeling, the eyes will not stay within bounds and are trying to say something; Denton's eyes said what his spoken words were not allowed to.
