Kural 970

Honour · Maanam · மானம்

இளிவரின் வாழாத மானம் உடையார் ஒளிதொழுது ஏத்தும் உலகு.
Ilivarin vaazhaadha maanam udaiyaar Olidhozhudhu eththum ulagu.

Meaning

The world always bows to and praises the glory of honourable people who would rather die than live in shame.

Elementary

People remember and admire heroes who never gave up doing what was right, even when it was very hard. This kural says the whole world keeps honouring such people long after they are gone.

University

Ilivu (இளிவு) is disgrace, humiliation; ilivarin vaazhaadha (இளிவரின் வாழாத) are those who 'do not live on when disgrace comes'. Oli (ஒளி), literally 'light', here means radiant fame, the glory that survives a person; Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss it as pugazh (புகழ்) or pugazh vadivu (the form of one's fame). Thozhudhu eththum (தொழுது ஏத்தும்) is the language of worship: folding hands and praising. The chapter closes where Tamil heroic ethics often closes, with lasting fame as the reward of honour. Read today, its lasting point is that the world reveres those who keep their integrity at great cost. Pope: 'Who, when dishonour comes, refuse to live, their honoured memory / Will live in worship and applause of all the world for aye!' Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra keeps his word to the sage Vishvamitra though it costs him his kingdom, his wife and son, and his freedom, working at last as a servant at a cremation ground; the gods finally restore him, and his name became the Indian byword for truthfulness.
— Markandeya Purana, chapters 7-8 (tr. F. E. Pargiter) [3]
Harishchandra suffers every loss rather than break his word, and is remembered and revered for it.
He is the Kural's honourable person whose glory the world still praises.
Christianity
“By faith Abel offered unto God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain, by which he obtained witness that he was righteous, God testifying of his gifts: and by it he being dead yet speaketh.”
— Hebrews 11:4 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews says Abel's faithfulness still speaks long after his death.
The Kural too says the glory of the honourable outlives them.
Islam
“And We gave them of Our mercy, and We made for them a mention [i.e., reputation] of high honor.”
— Quran 19:50 (Sahih International) [5]
Speaking of Abraham and his descendants, the Quran says God gave them an honoured name among later generations.
Like the Kural, it promises enduring renown to those who held to what was right.
Judaism
The aged scribe Eleazar, ordered to eat pork, refuses even to pretend, saying it does not befit his years to deceive the young; he goes to his death, and the book says he left his death as an example of noble courage and a memorial of virtue for the young and for the whole nation.
— 2 Maccabees 6:18-31 (Apocrypha; KJV Apocrypha wording paraphrased) [6]
Eleazar chooses death over a disgraceful pretence and is remembered as a model.
Almost the Kural's scene: one who would not live in disgrace becomes a lasting memorial honoured by his people.
Buddhism
“Not the sweet smell of flowers, not even the fragrance of sandal, tagara, or jasmine blows against the wind. But the fragrance of the virtuous blows against the wind. Truly the virtuous man pervades all directions with the fragrance of his virtue.”
— Dhammapada 54 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The good name of the virtuous spreads everywhere, even against the wind.
The Kural similarly says the glory of the honourable spreads through the world.
Jainism
The opening verse of the Dasavaikalika Sutra calls dharma the highest good, names non-violence, self-control and austerity as its essence, and says that even the gods bow down to one whose mind is always set on dharma.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 1.1 (as Saman Suttam v. 82, tr. T. K. Tukol and K. K. Dixit) [8]
In Jainism it is the person firm in dharma who receives the homage of gods and humans.
The Kural's world bows to the honourable; the Jain verse says even the gods bow to the steadfast in dharma.
Sikhism
Guru Tegh Bahadur, the ninth Guru, was executed by the Mughal authorities in Delhi in 1675 and is remembered above all for his stand for religious freedom; Gurdwara Sis Ganj Sahib now stands at the place of his martyrdom.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Tegh Bahadur' (paraphrase) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur chose death over submission to religious coercion, and Sikhs and many others still honour his sacrifice centuries later.
The Kural says the world forever praises those who would not go on living in disgrace; Guru Tegh Bahadur's memory, kept at Sis Ganj and in Sikh prayer, is exactly such lasting honour.
Greek Mythology
Achilles tells the embassy that his mother foretold two fates: if he stays and fights at Troy he will not return home but his glory (kleos) will never perish; if he goes home he will live long without fame.
— Homer, Iliad 9.410-416 [10]
Achilles weighs a long life against undying glory.
The Kural promises the same reward, lasting worldly praise, to those who put honour before life.
Philosophy
In his funeral oration, Pericles says that the fallen gave their lives and in return won praise that never grows old, and that the whole earth is the tomb of famous men, whose memory lives on far from home.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 2.43 (tr. Richard Crawley) [11]
Pericles says those who died rather than accept defeat are remembered everywhere.
Close to the Kural's world that worships the glory of the honourable.

Real Life Example

Rani Lakshmi Bai of Jhansi is honoured as a national heroine
1857-1858 · Jhansi and Gwalior, India
After the death of her husband, the ruler of Jhansi, the British East India Company refused to recognise their adopted son as heir and annexed the state in 1854, rejecting Lakshmi Bai's petition to rule as regent. During the rebellion of 1857-58 she led the defence of Jhansi, escaped when the city fell in April 1858, joined other leaders in taking Gwalior, and was killed in combat near Gwalior in June 1858. Her British opponent, General Hugh Rose, called her the bravest and best of the rebel leaders, and she became a symbol of courage in India, honoured in poetry, statues and the names of institutions.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Lakshmi Bai's refusal to accept the loss of her state and her son's rights made her one of the most revered figures in Indian history.
The kural says the world forever honours those who would not go on living in disgrace, and India's lasting reverence for the Rani of Jhansi shows exactly that.
