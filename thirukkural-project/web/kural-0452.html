Kural 452

Avoiding Mean Associations · Sitrinanjeraamai · சிற்றினஞ்சேராமை

நிலத்தியல்பால் நீர்திரிந் தற்றாகும் மாந்தர்க்கு இனத்தியல்ப தாகும் அறிவு.
Nilaththiyalbaal neerthirindhu atraakum maandharkku Inaththiyalba thaagum arivu.

Meaning

Water changes its color and taste depending on the ground it flows over. In the same way, a person’s understanding takes on the nature of the people they spend time with.

Elementary

Rain is pure when it falls, but it turns muddy, salty or sweet depending on the ground it runs over. Our minds work the same way: they pick up the colour and taste of the people we spend time with.

University

The simile works because rain is pure when it falls and only takes on colour, salt or sweetness from the ground (நிலத்தியல்பு) it touches. அறிவு here is not raw intelligence but practical understanding, the judgement a person brings to life. Valluvar’s claim is environmental: the mind is not a sealed container but something that absorbs from its surroundings. The next two couplets (453–454) sharpen this into a paradox: intelligence seems to belong to the mind but is actually supplied by one’s company.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Queen Kaikeyi loves Rama as her own son, until her hunchbacked maid Manthara keeps working on her mind and turns her into the one who demands Rama’s exile.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (sargas 7–9) [4]
A noble queen’s judgement is changed not by her own nature but by the constant presence of a petty, scheming companion.
Kaikeyi is the water and Manthara is the soil. Her ‘arivu’ took on the nature of the company she kept.
Christianity
“Be not deceived: evil communications corrupt good manners.”
— 1 Corinthians 15:33 (KJV) [5]
Paul quotes a proverb (from the Greek poet Menander) warning that bad company corrupts good character.
Both claim that one’s inner character takes on the quality of one’s company, however good it started out.
Islam
“Oh, woe to me! I wish I had not taken that one as a friend. He led me away from the remembrance after it had come to me.”
— Qur’an 25:28–29 (Sahih International) [6]
On the Day of Judgement the wrongdoer regrets a friendship that pulled him away from guidance he had already received.
This is the Kural’s point seen from the end: understanding the person once had was reshaped by a bad friend.
Judaism
Maimonides writes that it is human nature to be drawn after the views and deeds of one’s friends and neighbours, so one should keep company with the righteous and the wise and stay away from the wicked.
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De’ot 6:1 [7]
Maimonides treats social influence as a law of human nature and builds a rule of conduct on it.
This is almost the Kural’s thesis in legal prose: character follows company the way water follows soil.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares following a fool to wrapping a rotten fish in a blade of kusa grass: the grass itself comes to smell rotten. Leaves wrapped around incense, by contrast, come to smell sweet.
— Itivuttaka 76 [8]
What we are wrapped up with passes its quality to us, whether that is foul or fragrant.
This is the same image-logic as the Kural. The grass, like the water, takes on the character of what it is in contact with.
Jainism
“Water when mixed with milk will become milk, not remaining water. Will it exhibit the appearance of water? In like manner, if you consider it, the meanness of the mean when united with the dignity of the excellent will utterly vanish.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 18 'Good Society', stanza 7 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar uses the image of water taking on the nature of the milk it is mixed with to show that a person takes on the character of the company they join.
This is the same liquid image as the kural, where water changes with the soil it runs through and the mind changes with its company. Here it is shown from the hopeful side, as the mean are transformed by the excellent.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, the mind has become a bird; it soars and flies in the ten directions. According to the company it keeps, so are the fruits it eats.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir compares the restless mind to a bird whose nourishment depends on where it settles, that is, on the company it keeps.
The Kural says understanding takes on the nature of one's company as water takes on the nature of the soil; Kabir says the mind feeds on whatever its company offers.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus’s men enter Circe’s hall, share her food and drink, and are turned into swine, keeping only their human minds.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 10 [11]
Often read allegorically (for example by the Stoics and later moralists) as the way a degrading environment turns people into beasts.
In both, the surroundings a person enters change what they become.
Philosophy
“For from the good you will learn what is good, but if you mix with the bad you will lose the intelligence which you already have.”
— Theognis, as quoted in Plato, Meno 95d (trans. Jowett) [12]
Socrates quotes the poet Theognis, who says that good company teaches goodness while bad company destroys the intelligence one already has.
Theognis’s ‘intelligence’ corresponds exactly to the Kural’s அறிவு, which company can raise or ruin.

Real Life Example

Solomon Asch's conformity experiments
1951 · Swarthmore College, Pennsylvania, United States
At Swarthmore College, psychologist Solomon Asch asked students to say which of three lines matched a reference line, a task with an obvious answer. Unknown to each real participant, the others in the room were working with Asch and on certain trials all gave the same wrong answer. A large share of participants, about three-quarters, went along with the group's clearly false answer at least once.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The experiments became a classic demonstration that even a plain judgement of the senses bends toward the view of the surrounding group.
As water takes on the nature of the soil it runs through, Asch's students showed their judgement taking on the colour of the company around them.
