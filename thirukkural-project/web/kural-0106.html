Kural 106

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

மறவற்க மாசற்றார் கேண்மை துறவற்க துன்பத்துள் துப்பாயார் நட்பு.
Maravarka maasatraar kenmai thuravarka Thunbaththul thuppaayaar natpu.

Meaning

Never forget the friendship of good, pure-hearted people. Never leave the friendship of those who stood by you in hard times.

Elementary

Some friends are simply good people, so don't forget them. And some friends helped you when you were sad or in trouble, so never walk away from them, even after things get better for you.

University

The couplet is built as two parallel prohibitions, மறவற்க ('do not forget') and துறவற்க ('do not forsake'), each governing a class of relationship: the கேண்மை (bond) of the மாசற்றார் (the stainless) and the நட்பு (friendship) of those who became துப்பு (strength, support) in துன்பம் (distress). Commentators take the first half to concern those free of fault in knowledge and conduct and the second those who helped in adversity; the chapter thereby widens gratitude from discrete favours to durable relationships. The verb துறத்தல், the same root used for renunciation, suggests that abandoning a former helper is a kind of moral apostasy. The theme anticipates the Kural's later friendship chapters (79-83), where adversity is repeatedly named as the true test of a friend.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, when Krishna reveals to Karna that he is Kunti's eldest son and offers him the throne if he joins the Pandavas, Karna refuses: Duryodhana had honoured and befriended him when others scorned his low birth, and he will not abandon that friend on the eve of war.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Karna's reply to Krishna; K. M. Ganguli trans.) [3]
Karna turns down a kingdom because he will not desert the friend who stood by him when everyone else looked down on him.
Karna lives out the Kural's command: never forsake the friendship of one who was your support in your time of humiliation.
Christianity
“The Lord give mercy unto the house of Onesiphorus; for he oft refreshed me, and was not ashamed of my chain:”
— 2 Timothy 1:16 (KJV) [4]
From prison, Paul blesses the family of Onesiphorus, who kept visiting and comforting him and was not ashamed to be seen with a prisoner.
Paul refuses to forget the friend who stood by him 'in his chains', the exact friend the Kural says never to forsake.
Islam
Aisha reported that, years after Khadijah's death, the Prophet mentioned her very often and, whenever he slaughtered a sheep, sent portions to Khadijah's women friends; when Aisha objected, he would reply by recalling what Khadijah had been to him.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3818 [5]
The Prophet kept honouring his first wife Khadijah, who had supported him from the very beginning, long after she died, even sending gifts to her friends.
He never forgot or let go of the bond with the one who had been his strength in the hardest early years, as the Kural commands.
Judaism
“Thine own friend, and thy father’s friend, forsake not; neither go into thy brother’s house in the day of thy calamity: for better is a neighbour that is near than a brother far off.”
— Proverbs 27:10 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs tells us never to abandon an old friend, or a friend of the family, and says a friend close by in trouble is worth more than a distant relative.
'Forsake not' is the Kural's துறவற்க, applied to the friend who is near in calamity.
Buddhism
“in misfortune he does not forsake one,”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (Narada Thera trans.) [6]
In advice to the layman Sigala, the Buddha lists the marks of a true friend, including that he does not abandon you when misfortune strikes.
The Buddha defines a real friend by the same test the Kural uses: staying with you in hard times.
Jainism
“Men cleave not to those to whom their hearts cleave not, within a short space of time; but will the friendship which cherishes the memory of those who are intertwined with one's heart be abandoned, though they are absent from us for a long time?”
— Nālaḍiyār ch. 22 'The choice of friends', st. 4 (= st. 214), English translation in The Indian Antiquary (wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaḍiyār holds that a bond woven into the heart is not given up, however long the separation.
Valluvar forbids both forgetting the friendship of the stainless and forsaking those who stood by us in distress, and this quatrain likewise insists that a true bond is neither forgotten nor abandoned.
Sikhism
“They alone are my friends, who travel along with me; and in that place, where the accounts are called for, they appear standing with me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak defines the true friend as the one who stays beside you at the moment of reckoning, when everyone else falls away.
The Kural's friend who was your strength in distress and must never be forsaken is the same kind of friend Guru Nanak names: one who stands by you when it counts.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides' Heracles, after the hero has killed his own wife and children in a god-sent madness, Theseus arrives, recalls that Heracles once rescued him from the underworld, refuses to shun him as polluted, and offers him a home and honours in Athens.
— Euripides, Heracles (c. 416 BCE), the Theseus episode [9]
When everyone else would run from Heracles after his terrible disaster, Theseus stands by him because Heracles had once saved him.
Theseus refuses to abandon the friend who had been his rescuer, embodying both halves of the Kural.
Philosophy
In On Friendship, Cicero quotes Ennius's line that a sure friend is discerned in an unsure situation, and argues that people who desert friends in adversity, or who grow proud in prosperity, show themselves unfit for true friendship.
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia 64 [10]
Cicero says you only really find out who your friends are when things go wrong, and that leaving a friend in trouble shows weak character.
He makes adversity the test of friendship, the same test behind the Kural's 'do not forsake'.

Real Life Example

The Netherlands' yearly gift of tulips to Canada
1945 onwards · Ottawa, Canada, and the Netherlands
After Germany invaded the Netherlands in 1940, Princess Juliana and her daughters took refuge in Ottawa, where her third daughter Margriet was born in 1943; Canadian troops also played a significant role in liberating the Netherlands in 1945. After returning home, Princess Juliana presented 100,000 tulip bulbs to Canada in gratitude and kept sending bulbs after she became queen. The gift became a lasting tradition, and every year the Dutch royal family and the people of the Netherlands each send 10,000 bulbs to Ottawa.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The gift began as thanks for shelter and liberation in wartime and has been renewed every year for decades, long after the danger passed.
The kural says never to forsake the friendship of those who stood by you in hard times; the Dutch have kept that friendship alive with tulips every spring since 1945.
