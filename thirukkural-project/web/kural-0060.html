Kural 60

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

மங்கலம் என்ப மனைமாட்சி மற்று அதன் நன்கலம் நன்மக்கட் பேறு.
Mangalam enba manaimaatchi matru adhan, nankalam nanmakkat peRu.

Meaning

The wise say a good wife is the blessing of a home, and good children are the fine jewels of that blessing.

Elementary

A birthday cake is the good part. The candles on top make it look even better — but they're not the cake. This kural says the wife's goodness is the cake and good children are the candles: lovely, but only because there's something underneath to put them on.

University

The couplet closes the chapter on a two-term metaphor of extraordinary economy: manaimaatchi is mangalam (the auspicious thing itself, the blessing), and children are its nan-kalam (its good ornament) — and the near-pun between mangalam and nankalam binds the two halves phonetically while separating them logically. The hierarchy matters: an ornament presupposes something to adorn, so Valluvar is explicitly subordinating progeny to character, a striking priority in a culture where sons carried ritual obligations for the ancestors. Parimelazhagar supplies the unstated subject — enba, 'so the knowing say' — and observes that the couplet functions as a thoRRuvaay, a doorway opening onto Chapter 7 (Makkat-peRu), so that the chapter's last word is also the next chapter's first subject. The comparative field here is unusually convergent, since almost every tradition pairs a virtuous wife with flourishing children as a single image of blessing: Psalm 128 does it with a vine and olive shoots, the Qur'an with 'comfort of the eyes', and the Pali Maṅgala Sutta — which shares the very word maṅgala with the Tamil mangalam through their common Indic root — lists care for partner and children among the highest blessings.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Rig Veda's marriage hymn blesses the bride as she enters her husband's house, asking that she rule there with authority, that the household prosper, and that she be the mother of living heroes — placing the wife's standing first and children as the blessing that flows from it.
— Rig Veda 10.85 (the Sūryā-vivāha / marriage hymn), esp. vv. 42–47 (trans. R. T. H. Griffith, 1896) [4]
The oldest Indic wedding liturgy already frames the bride's establishment in the home and the children who follow as one continuous benediction.
The same ordering as the Kural: the wife is the blessing, the children are its adornment.
Christianity
“Thy wife shall be as a fruitful vine by the sides of thine house: thy children like olive plants round about thy table.”
— Psalm 128:3 (King James Version) [5]
The psalm pictures the blessed household as one image with two elements — the wife at its centre and the children ranged around her.
Identical pairing and identical priority: the wife is the vine, the children are what grows around her.
Islam
“And those who say, 'Our Lord, grant us from among our wives and offspring comfort to our eyes and make us a leader [i.e., example] for the righteous.'”
— Qur'an 25:74 (Saheeh International translation) [6]
The Qur'an's portrait of the servants of the Most Merciful culminates in a prayer that names wives and offspring together as the qurrat a'yun — the coolness of the eyes.
The same closing gesture: wife and good children named as a single blessing on the household.
Judaism
Rav Huna teaches that one who is habitually careful with the Sabbath lamp will have sons who are Torah scholars — the household's light, kindled by the wife as a domestic religious act, being treated as the direct cause of the children's distinction.
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 23b (Rav Huna on the Shabbat lamp) [7]
The Talmud makes the wife's faithful keeping of the home's central ritual the thing that produces children worth having.
Same causal order: the wife's household excellence is the blessing, and good children are what that blessing yields.
Buddhism
“Caring for mother and father, kindness to children and partners, and unstressful work: this is the highest blessing.”
— Sutta Nipāta 2.4, Maṅgala Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato, SuttaCentral) [8]
The Maṅgala Sutta is the Buddha's canonical answer to the question of what the highest blessings are, and household relationships are among them.
The Tamil mangalam and the Pali maṅgala are the same word, and both couplets answer the same question: what actually blesses a home?
Jainism
“From the moment that this our boy has been begotten, our silver increased, our gold increased, &c. … Therefore when this our boy will be born, we shall give him the fit name, attributive and conformable to his quality--Vardhamāna.”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Mahāvīra §91 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [9]
Siddhārtha and Triśalā name their son Vardhamāna, 'the increasing one', because the household's wealth, glory and liberality grew from the moment he was conceived.
Valluvar calls the wife's excellence the home's blessing and good children its jewel, and the Kalpa Sūtra presents Triśalā's household as one whose good fortune is crowned by the birth of a child.
Sikhism
“In the first round of the marriage ceremony, the Lord sets out His Instructions for performing the daily duties of married life. Instead of the hymns of the Vedas to Brahma, embrace the righteous conduct of Dharma, and renounce sinful actions.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 773, Raag Soohee, Chhant (Laavan), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
This is the first of the four Laavan, the hymns sung while the couple walk around the Guru Granth Sahib at a Sikh wedding (Anand Karaj); marriage itself is presented as a blessed, God-given path of righteous conduct.
Like the Kural, which calls the wife's excellence the true blessing (mangalam) of a home, the Sikh marriage hymn makes righteous married life itself the auspicious thing, in place of ritual.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, poor and long married, unknowingly host Jupiter and Mercury and give them everything they have; the gods spare their cottage from the flood, turn it into a temple, and grant the couple's only wish — to serve there together and to die in the same hour, whereupon they become two intertwined trees.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses VIII.611–724 (Baucis and Philemon) [11]
Ovid's tale is the classical world's fullest picture of a marriage that is itself the blessing on a house, transforming the house into a sacred place.
The household made auspicious by the quality of the marriage within it — the Kural's mangalam in Latin verse.
Philosophy
“For since the reproductive instinct is by Nature's gift the common possession of all living creatures, the first bond of union is that between husband and wife; the next, that between parents and children; then we find one home, with everything in common.”
— Cicero, De Officiis I.17.54 (trans. Walter Miller, Loeb Classical Library, 1913) [12]
Cicero builds society outward from the marriage bond, with the parent–child bond as the second term and the shared household as their joint product.
Exactly the Kural's ordering — the marriage first, the children next, and the blessed home as what the two together constitute.

Real Life Example

The Curies: a shared Nobel Prize and a daughter who won her own
1903; 1935 · Paris, France
Marie and Pierre Curie, married in 1895, worked side by side on radioactivity, and in 1903 Marie became the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize, sharing the physics prize with Pierre and Henri Becquerel after Pierre insisted that his wife share the honour. Their elder daughter, Irène, became a physical chemist, and in 1935 she and her husband Frédéric Joliot-Curie were jointly awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their discovery of artificially produced radioactive isotopes.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
The Curie household produced two generations of Nobel laureates, a record of family achievement unmatched in science.
The kural calls the wife's excellence the blessing of the home and good children its jewel, and Marie Curie's excellence was crowned by a daughter who followed her to a Nobel Prize.
