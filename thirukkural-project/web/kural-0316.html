Kural 316

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

இன்னா எனத்தான் உணர்ந்தவை துன்னாமை வேண்டும் பிறன்கண் செயல்.
Innaa enaththaan unarndhavai thunnaamai Vendum piran kan seyal.

Meaning

Whatever you have learned is painful from your own experience, never do that to anyone else.

Elementary

Remember how much it hurt when someone left you out of a game? Then you already know not to leave anyone else out. This kural says: if something hurt you, don’t do it to others.

University

This is Valluvar’s clearest statement of the negative Golden Rule, and it is regularly cited alongside Hillel and the Mahabharata in comparative discussions of that principle. The phrase thaan unarndhavai, ‘what one has oneself felt or known,’ grounds the rule in lived experience rather than abstract principle: each person’s own suffering is a textbook of what not to do. Thunnaamai (not approaching, not engaging in) makes the prohibition wide enough to cover even drawing near to such acts. Unlike the positive form (‘do unto others’), the negative form sets a minimum that anyone can meet, and so needs no guesswork about what others want. It closely matches Brihaspati’s summary of dharma in the Mahabharata, Hillel’s one-sentence Torah, and Isocrates’ advice to the Cyprians.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One should never do that to another which one regards as injurious to one's own self. This, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness.”
— Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Section 113 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Brihaspati sums up all of dharma for Yudhishthira in one rule: never do to others what you would find harmful to yourself.
An almost word-for-word counterpart of the Kural: what you know hurts you, do not do to others.
Christianity
“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets.”
— Matthew 7:12 (KJV) [4]
Jesus sums up the Law and the Prophets as treating others the way you want to be treated.
The positive form of the Kural’s rule; both use one’s own experience as the measure of how to treat others.
Islam
“None of you will have faith till he wishes for his (Muslim) brother what he likes for himself.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 13 (trans. Muhsin Khan, sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet makes wishing for others what you wish for yourself a condition of true faith.
Both use one’s own desires and aversions as the standard for how to treat others.
Judaism
“That which is hateful to you do not do to another; that is the entire Torah, and the rest is its interpretation. Go study.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (William Davidson Edition, Sefaria) [6]
Asked to teach the whole Torah while standing on one foot, Hillel gives the negative Golden Rule.
Hillel and Valluvar give the same rule in the same negative form: do not do to others what you yourself find painful.
Buddhism
“Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful.”
— Udanavarga 5.18 (as quoted in comparative Golden Rule literature) [7]
The Udanavarga states the negative Golden Rule directly.
Nearly identical to the Kural: let your own experience of hurt tell you what never to do to others.
Jainism
“As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to tyrannise over. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to torment. … The righteous man who lives up to these sentiments, does therefore neither kill nor cause others to kill (living beings).”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.5.5.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Mahāvīra grounds non-violence in direct identification with the victim: whatever you would feel is what the other feels.
Valluvar's negative Golden Rule says never to do to another what you have learned to be painful. The Ācārāṅga's 'as it would be unto thee' rests on the same experiential ground.
Sikhism
“As you see yourself, see others as well; only then will you become a partner in heaven.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 480, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir, addressing the religious authorities of his day, makes seeing others as oneself the condition of true religion.
The Kural's rule is not to do to others what one has felt as painful oneself; Kabir grounds the same rule in seeing others exactly as one sees oneself.
Greek Mythology
In Homer’s Odyssey, when the nymph Calypso finally agrees to let Odysseus leave, he suspects a trap. She swears she is planning for him only what she would plan for herself in the same need, since her mind is just and her heart is not made of iron.
— Homer, Odyssey 5.173–191 [10]
Calypso uses her own needs as the measure of how she treats Odysseus.
An early Greek example of the Golden Rule: treat another as you would want to be treated in their place, the principle behind the Kural.
Philosophy
“Do not do to others that which angers you when they do it to you.”
— Isocrates, Nicocles (or The Cyprians) 61 (trans. George Norlin) [11]
Isocrates, writing as King Nicocles, advises his subjects to use their own resentment as a guide to how they should treat others.
A classical Greek statement of the same negative rule as the Kural.

Real Life Example

Hiroshima unveils the Cenotaph and its vow never to repeat the evil
6 August 1952 · Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, Hiroshima, Japan
On 6 August 1952 the Cenotaph for the A-bomb Victims, designed by Kenzo Tange, was unveiled at Hiroshima's Peace Memorial Ceremony; its stone chest holds the register of those who died from the bombing, regardless of nationality. Its inscription was written by Tadayoshi Saika, a Hiroshima University professor and himself an A-bomb survivor, after Mayor Shinzo Hamai asked for words expressing both a prayer and a vow. The English reads: "Let all the souls here rest in peace; For we shall not repeat the evil." Saika intended the vow to be made by all human beings.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A city that had suffered the first atomic bombing chose to answer its own experience of pain with a vow that no one should inflict such suffering again.
The kural says that whatever you have learned through your own experience to be painful, you must never do to another, and Hiroshima's survivors turned what they had suffered into a public vow never to repeat it.
