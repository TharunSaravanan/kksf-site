Kural 1196

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

ஒருதலையான் இன்னாது காமம்காப் போல இருதலை யானும் இனிது.
Orudhalaiyaan innaadhu kaamamkaap pola irudhalai yaanum inidhu.

Meaning

Love on one side only is painful. Like the load on a carrying pole, it is sweet only when it is balanced at both ends.

Elementary

Have you tried carrying two buckets on a stick across your shoulders? With one bucket it tips and hurts, but with one on each side it’s easy. Love is the same: it feels good only when both people carry it.

University

This is the Kural’s most famous image for mutual love. Kā is the kāvaṭi or shoulder-pole carried with a load at each end; orutalai (‘one end’) and irutalai (‘two ends’) give Tamil the still-current phrase orutalaik kātal for one-sided love. Kāmam orutalaiyāṉ iṉṉātu, ‘love on one end is painful’, irutalaiyāṉum iṉitu, ‘on both ends it is sweet.’ Parimelazhagar takes the kā simile as governing both halves; Kalaignar spells out that the pole’s two sides must be equally weighted, as love must bloom in both man and woman. Pope: ‘Love on one side is bad; like balanced load / By porter borne, love on both sides is good.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The closing hymn of the Rigveda prays for concord: that those gathered may have a common purpose, hearts in accord and minds alike, so that all may live together in harmony.
— Rigveda 10.191.4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Ralph T.H. Griffith) [3]
The Samjnana hymn makes agreement of hearts the basis of a good shared life.
The Kural’s balanced carrying pole is the domestic form of this Vedic ideal of hearts in accord.
Christianity
“Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers”
— 2 Corinthians 6:14 (KJV) [4]
Paul uses the image of an unevenly matched yoke to warn against a partnership in which the two sides are not one.
Like the Kural’s carrying pole, Paul’s yoke works only when both sides bear equally.
Islam
Rumi teaches that no lover ever seeks union unless the beloved is also seeking him; the thirst in the lover is matched by a drawing from the other side, as the thirsty seek water and water seeks the thirsty.
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book III (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.A. Nicholson) [5]
For Rumi true love is never one-sided: longing on one side always implies a pull from the other.
Rumi’s mutual seeking is the Kural’s two-ended pole, love sweet because it is carried from both sides.
Judaism
“Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow; but woe to him that is alone when he falleth”
— Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Koheleth praises the shared load of two who support each other, and pities the one left to carry life alone.
This is the Kural’s carrying pole in words: two sides make the burden light, one side makes it painful.
Buddhism
In the Kusa Jataka, the wise but ugly King Kusa loves Princess Pabhavati, who flees from him; he follows her to her father’s city and toils as a potter, basket-maker and cook to be near her, suffering greatly until she finally returns his love.
— Kusa Jataka (Jataka 531) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Jataka dramatises the long misery of a love carried by one side only, and the happiness when it is shared.
Kusa’s story is the Kural’s carrying pole: unbearable at one end, sweet once both ends bear the weight.
Jainism
“But all kinds of objects of the senses, sounds, &c., will cause to the indifferent neither a pleasant nor an unpleasant feeling.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.106 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that pain and pleasure come from one’s own attachment, and that the dispassionate are untouched by either.
Where the Kural seeks balance by making love mutual, Jainism seeks it by laying the burden of attachment down altogether.
Sikhism
“O Lord, if You will not break with me, then I will not break with You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 658, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas describes his love for God as a two-way bond, kept whole because neither side breaks it.
The Kural says love is painful when it rests on one end only and sweet, like a balanced carrying-pole, when it rests on both; Ravidas likewise sees true love as a bond held up from both sides.
Greek Mythology
Themistius tells how Aphrodite’s child Eros would not grow until Themis advised her to give him a brother, Anteros (‘love returned’); with Anteros beside him Eros flourished, but whenever they were parted Eros dwindled again.
— Themistius, Oration 24 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth makes requited love the condition for love to grow at all.
Eros and Anteros are the two ends of the Kural’s pole: love thrives only when it is returned.
Philosophy
Aristotle says goodwill is not yet friendship unless it is returned and each party knows of the other’s goodwill; friendship is goodwill that is reciprocated.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.2, 1155b-1156a (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle love on one side only is not yet the real thing; reciprocity is part of its definition.
This is the philosophical form of the Kural’s claim that love is sweet only when it runs both ways.

Real Life Example

Goethe falls in love with the engaged Charlotte Buff
1772 · Wetzlar, Germany
In May 1772 the young Goethe went to Wetzlar as a trainee at the Imperial Chamber Court. At a dance on 9 June he met Charlotte Buff, the fiancée of his friend Johann Christian Kestner, and fell in love with her; on 11 September he left the town. Within two years he turned the experience into his first novel, The Sorrows of Young Werther, in which the hero loves a Lotte who is engaged to another man, and the book became an immense success.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Goethe's one-sided love was painful enough to drive him away, while Charlotte and her fiancé, whose love ran both ways, stayed together and became the models for Lotte and Albert.
The kural compares love to a carrying pole that is painful when loaded at one end and sweet when balanced at both, and Wetzlar showed both ends: Goethe's lopsided love and the engaged couple's balanced one.
