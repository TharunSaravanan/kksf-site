Kural 772

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

கான முயலெய்த அம்பினில் யானை பிழைத்தவேல் ஏந்தல் இனிது.
Kaana muyaleydha ambinil yaanai Pizhaiththavel endhal inidhu.

Meaning

It is better to hold a spear that missed an elephant than an arrow that killed a rabbit in the forest.

Elementary

Would you rather say you beat a little kid at chess, or that you lost a close game to the school champion? Trying something truly hard, even if you miss, is more honourable than an easy win over someone weak.

University

The couplet contrasts kaana muyal (the hare of the forest, a small and timid quarry) with yaanai (the elephant, the most formidable target of ancient Tamil warfare), and the arrow (ambu) that succeeded against the hare with the spear (vel) that failed against the elephant (pizhaiththa vel). The key verb endhal, “to hold, to bear,” makes the line about what a warrior is proud to carry: honour belongs to the scale of the attempt, not merely its outcome. Parimelazhagar reads the hare and elephant as figures for weak and mighty enemies, so the teaching concerns the choice of a worthy adversary. Kalaignar’s modern gloss states the point directly: aiming at the strong and missing outranks aiming at the weak and hitting. Both base texts agree on the wording; the sandhi form முயலெய்த = muyal eydha.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Considering your specific duty as a kṣatriya, you should know that there is no better engagement for you than fighting on religious principles; and so there is no need for hesitation.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.31 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna reminds Arjuna that for a warrior the most worthy engagement is a righteous battle, not a lesser or easier task.
The Kural likewise ranks the worthy, difficult engagement above the easy one.
Christianity
“Then said David to the Philistine, Thou comest to me with a sword, and with a spear, and with a shield: but I come to thee in the name of the LORD of hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom thou hast defied.”
— 1 Samuel 17:45 (KJV) [4]
The young David chooses to face the giant Goliath, the most fearsome opponent on the field, when no one else will.
David goes after the “elephant,” not the hare, which is the Kural’s idea of where honour lies.
Islam
“How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the patient.”
— Qur’an 2:249 (Saheeh International) [5]
Facing Goliath’s army, the few faithful soldiers with Saul refuse to be daunted by the enemy’s size.
Like the Kural’s warrior who aims at the elephant, they take on the greater foe rather than shrink from it.
Judaism
“Come, let us cross over to the outpost of those uncircumcised fellows. Perhaps GOD will act in our behalf, for nothing prevents GOD from winning a victory by many or by few.”
— 1 Samuel 14:6 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Jonathan, with only his armour-bearer, decides to attack a whole Philistine garrison.
Jonathan picks the bold, outsized target, just as the Kural honours the spear raised against an elephant.
Buddhism
“Though one may conquer a thousand times a thousand men in battle, yet he indeed is the noblest victor who conquers himself.”
— Dhammapada 103 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names the hardest of all opponents: one’s own mind.
Buddhism agrees that the worthiest contest is the hardest one, but relocates the “elephant” inside the self.
Jainism
“Fight with your Self; why fight with external foes? He who conquers himself through himself, will obtain happiness.” … “difficult to conquer is one's self; but when that is conquered, everything is conquered.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.35–36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Nami names the self, with the senses, anger, pride, delusion and greed, as the hardest enemy of all and the only one worth fighting.
The Kural honours the warrior who aims at the mighty elephant rather than the easy hare, and Jainism likewise chooses the hardest adversary, the self, over lesser foes.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib, Pauri 28, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib Guru Nanak names the mind as the real adversary worth defeating; whoever masters it has won everything.
The Kural says it is nobler to aim at an elephant and miss than to kill a hare; Guru Nanak points the seeker toward the hardest of all enemies, one's own mind, rather than easy victories.
Greek Mythology
Sarpedon tells Glaucus that because death awaits all men in any case, they should go forward to the front of the fight and either win glory or give it to another.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 12 (paraphrase) [10]
In the Iliad the Lycian prince argues that nobility obliges the noble to take on the greatest danger, not to linger safely behind.
Sarpedon’s reasoning matches the Kural’s: the honourable warrior seeks the great encounter.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that courage is shown chiefly in the noblest dangers, above all death in battle, rather than in trivial risks.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.6 (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle the virtue is measured by the worth of what is faced; facing small dangers is not yet courage in the full sense.
The Kural similarly values the attempt on the elephant over success against the hare.

Real Life Example

Porus fights Alexander at the Hydaspes and keeps his kingdom
326 BCE · Hydaspes (Jhelum) River, Punjab, present-day Pakistan
When Alexander the Great invaded the Punjab, Ambhi, the king of Taxila, submitted, but Porus, who ruled the land between the Hydaspes and Acesines rivers, chose to resist. At the Battle of the Hydaspes his elephants and infantry were outmatched by Alexander's mobile cavalry and mounted archers, and he was defeated. Impressed by his skill and spirit, Alexander allowed Porus to keep his kingdom and may have given him further territory.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Porus took on the most formidable army of his age and lost, yet his choice to fight earned him a respect that his neighbour's easy submission did not.
The kural says it is better to hold a spear that missed an elephant than an arrow that killed a hare, and Porus, who aimed at the mightiest foe and failed, ended with more honour than one who bowed without a fight.
