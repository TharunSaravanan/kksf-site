Kural 328

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

நன்றாகும் ஆக்கம் பெரிதெனினும் சான்றோர்க்குக் கொன்றாகும் ஆக்கங் கடை.
Nandraakum aakkam peridheninum saandrorkkuk Kondraakum aakkam kadai.

Meaning

Even if killing brings a gain that is great and seems good, people of good character see any gain that comes from killing as the lowest of things.

Elementary

Imagine someone says, “If you trap and kill this bird, you’ll win a huge prize.” A good person says “No, thanks.” Valluvar says a prize that costs a life isn’t a prize at all — it’s the worst kind of win.

University

Valluvar concedes his opponent’s premise — the prosperity (ākkam) gained may indeed be “great” and even called “good” — and still pronounces it kaṭai, the very lowest, in the eyes of the cāṉṟōr (the noble, perfected ones). Parimelazhagar and Solomon Pappaiah read the verse as targeting Vedic animal sacrifice (vēḷvi), which promised wealth and heaven through ritual killing; the Kural thus sides with the Jain and Buddhist critique of sacrificial violence. More broadly the couplet rejects consequentialist justifications for killing: no outcome, however beneficial, can redeem the means. The same refusal to let ends justify the taking of life underlies Kant’s formula that persons may never be used merely as means.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It would be better to live in this world by begging than to live at the cost of the lives of great souls who are my teachers. … If they are killed, everything we enjoy will be tainted with blood.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.5 (As It Is) [4]
Arjuna, facing battle, refuses a kingdom won by killing his elders, calling such enjoyment blood-stained.
Arjuna voices the Kural’s judgement that gain through killing is contemptible — though Krishna goes on to overrule him on grounds of duty.
Christianity
“But go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice”
— Matthew 9:13 (KJV), quoting Hosea 6:6 [5]
Jesus quotes Hosea to teach that God desires mercy rather than ritual offerings.
Echoes the commentators’ reading of the Kural as rejecting the promised benefits of sacrificial killing in favour of mercy.
Islam
“…do not say to one who gives you [a greeting of] peace, “You are not a believer,” aspiring for the goods of worldly life; for with Allāh are many acquisitions.”
— Qur’an 4:94 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an forbids killing someone who offers peace for the sake of worldly spoils, reminding believers that God’s rewards are greater.
Like the Kural, it condemns gain obtained through killing, however tempting.
Judaism
“So are the ways of every one that is greedy of gain; which taketh away the life of the owners thereof.”
— Proverbs 1:19 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs warns against joining those who ambush and kill for plunder, noting that greed for gain destroys life.
Directly condemns, as the Kural does, prosperity sought through killing.
Buddhism
Advising the brahmin Kutadanta, the Buddha describes a great sacrifice of an ancient king in which no oxen, goats, fowl or other creatures were slain, and all was offered with ghee, oil and honey; Kutadanta then releases the animals he had gathered for slaughter.
— Digha Nikaya 5, Kutadanta Sutta [7]
The Buddha redefines sacrifice so that its benefits come without killing.
Matches the commentarial reading of the Kural as rejecting the promised gains of animal sacrifice.
Jainism
“The binding of animals (to the sacrificial pole), all the Vêdas, and sacrifices, being causes of sin, cannot save the sinner; for his works (or Karman) are very powerful.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 25.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Jayaghosha rejects animal sacrifice: whatever rewards it promises, ritual killing is a cause of sin and binds the sacrificer with karma.
Parimelazhagar reads the Kural as condemning the 'great gain' promised by sacrificial killing; this verse makes the same anti-sacrificial judgement from the Jain canon.
Sikhism
Macauliffe tells of Shaikh Sajjan, a robber who built a temple and a mosque for travellers, then threw his guests into a well at night and kept their property. After hearing Guru Nanak's hymn he confessed his murders; the Guru had him bring out all the goods of his victims and give them to the poor, and he became the Guru's follower.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter IV, pp. 45-47 (paraphrase) [9]
The story shows wealth gained through killing as wholly worthless: it cannot be kept, only given away in repentance.
The Kural says that however great the gain, profit won by killing is the lowest of things to the noble; Guru Nanak made Sajjan give up every coin he had gained that way.
Greek Mythology
Agamemnon sacrifices his daughter Iphigenia to gain favourable winds for the fleet to Troy; the ‘gain’ is won, but the killing brings his own murder on his return.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 184–247 [10]
In Aeschylus the great advantage bought with a life is shown to be ruinous and shameful.
Illustrates the Kural: even a great gain won through killing is the lowest of things.
Philosophy
Kant’s formula of humanity: always treat humanity, in yourself or in another, as an end, and never merely as a means.
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 4:429 [11]
Kant holds that persons have dignity, not a price, so no advantage can justify sacrificing one for an outcome.
Shares the Kural’s refusal to weigh a life against any gain, however great.

Real Life Example

The International Whaling Commission adopts a moratorium on commercial whaling
1982 (in force from 1986) · International Whaling Commission (member states worldwide)
In 1982 the International Whaling Commission decided that catch limits for all commercial whaling would be set to zero, a moratorium that came fully into force for the 1986 and 1985-86 seasons. The decision followed disagreement among members over the acceptability of whaling and uncertainty about whale stocks. The moratorium remains in place, though a few countries have objected to it or continue whaling under exceptions.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nations gave up a long-established and profitable industry built on killing, choosing to protect whale populations instead.
The kural says that gain through killing, however great, is the lowest of things to the noble, and the moratorium turned away from profits made by killing whales.
