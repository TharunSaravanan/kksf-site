Kural 1061

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

கரவாது உவந்தீயும் கண்ணன்னார் கண்ணும் இரவாமை கோடி உறும்.
Karavaadhu uvandheeyum kannannaar kannum iravaamai koadi urum.

Meaning

Even when there are people as dear as your own eyes who would gladly give without hiding anything, never asking them at all is worth ten million times more.

Elementary

Imagine your kindest aunt would happily give you anything you asked for. The Kural says it is still far better, and makes you stronger, to manage on your own and not ask, even from someone that kind.

University

The chapter Iravaccham (இரவச்சம், 'dread of begging') deliberately answers the preceding chapter Iravu (ch. 106), which permitted asking only of fitting, generous givers; here Valluvar raises the ideal higher, so that even such givers should not be asked. Karavaadhu (கரவாது, 'without concealing') and uvandhu eeyum (உவந்தீயும், 'who give with delight') describe the best possible donor, and kannannaar (கண்ணன்னார், 'those like one's eyes') marks them as most precious; the hyperbole koadi urum ('attains a crore', i.e. ten-million-fold good) is the commentators' measure of the superiority of not asking. In the line of interpretation that follows Parimelazhagar, as reflected in Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan, the point is that dignity (maanam) lies in enduring want rather than in depending even on a willing friend. Pope renders it 'Ten million-fold 'tis greater gain, asking no alms to live, / Even from those, like eyes in worth, who nought concealing gladly give.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Laws of Manu teach that everything that depends on others brings pain, while everything that depends on oneself brings happiness, and call this the short definition of pain and pleasure.
— Manusmriti 4.160 (tr. Georg Bühler, SBE 25) (paraphrase) [3]
Manu's householder code makes self-reliance the very definition of happiness and dependence the definition of pain.
The Kural applies the same principle so strictly that even a loving, willing giver should not be leaned on.
Christianity
“Yea, ye yourselves know, that these hands have ministered unto my necessities, and to them that were with me.”
— Acts 20:34 (KJV) [4]
Paul reminds the Ephesian elders that, though the churches would gladly have supported him, he provided for himself with his own hands.
Like the Kural, Paul declines to depend even on friends who were willing to give.
Islam
“Whoever abstains from asking others, Allah will make him contented, and whoever tries to make himself self-sufficient, Allah will make him self-sufficient.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1469 (sunnah.com) [5]
After giving to the Ansar until nothing was left, the Prophet told them that refraining from asking brings contentment and self-sufficiency.
The hadith matches the Kural closely: the Prophet was himself a generous giver, yet he urged people not to ask even of him.
Judaism
In the Grace after Meals, the traditional text asks God not to make the worshipper dependent on the gifts or loans of flesh and blood, but only on God's own full and open hand, so that one may never be put to shame.
— Birkat HaMazon, third blessing (traditional siddur text) (paraphrase) [6]
Jewish liturgy turns the wish never to depend on human gifts into a daily prayer.
The prayer, like the Kural, treats dependence on even well-meaning human givers as something to be avoided.
Buddhism
“And what is the bliss of debtlessness? There is the case where the son of a good family owes no debt, great or small, to anyone at all.”
— Anana Sutta, AN 4.62 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells Anathapindika that owing nothing to anyone is one of a householder's four kinds of bliss.
The Kural's ideal of asking no one, however kind, matches the Buddha's bliss of being beholden to no one.
Jainism
“Living without begging from friends, strong in affection and who are like the apple of our eyes, who withhold not their assistance from us, is life indeed.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 5 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil quatrains on the dread of mendicity call life without begging, even from loving and generous friends, the only true life.
This is almost the Kural itself: friends 'like the apple of our eyes' who 'withhold not' are exactly the kannannaar who give without concealing.
Sikhism
“Reliance on mortals is in vain - know this well. The Great Giver is the One Lord God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 281, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan tells the seeker not to lean on any human being for support, because God alone is the real Giver.
The Kural says it is ten million times better not to ask even generous friends; Guru Arjan likewise turns the needy person away from human patrons and toward the one Giver.
Greek Mythology
“There is nothing worse than being always ways on the tramp; still, when men have once got low down in the world they will go through a great deal on behalf of their miserable bellies.”
— Homer, Odyssey 15 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, thanks Eumaeus for sparing him from going about the town to beg.
Even the hero who must play the beggar calls a life of going about to ask the worst there is, which is the Kural's dread.
Philosophy
“And he is the sort of man to confer benefits, but he is ashamed of receiving them; for the one is the mark of a superior, the other of an inferior.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.3 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle's great-souled man is happy to give but ashamed to receive, because receiving puts him in a lower position.
The Kural shares the instinct that receiving, even from a willing benefactor, lowers one's standing.

Real Life Example

The Rochdale Pioneers open their own co-operative store
21 December 1844 · Rochdale, Lancashire, England, United Kingdom
Twenty-eight working people of Rochdale, many of them weavers, pooled their own small savings to open a co-operative store at 31 Toad Lane on 21 December 1844, stocked with modest supplies of butter, sugar, flour, oatmeal and candles. At first it opened only two evenings a week, but within months it was open five days a week. The Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers set out principles such as open membership and democratic control that became the model for the modern co-operative movement.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rather than depend on others' help, a group of poor workers met their own needs by joining their resources, and their example spread around the world.
The kural says that not asking at all, even of the kindest givers, is worth ten million times more, and the Rochdale Pioneers chose to provide for themselves through their own pooled earnings.
