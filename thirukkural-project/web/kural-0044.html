Kural 44

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

பழியஞ்சிப் பாத்தூண் உடைத்தாயின் வாழ்க்கை வழியெஞ்சல் எஞ்ஞான்றும் இல்.
Pazhiyanjip paaththoon utaiththaayin vaazhkkai, Vazhiyenjal egngnaandrum il.

Meaning

If a family fears doing anything shameful and shares its food with others, its family line will never die out.

Elementary

Suppose a family grows apples honestly and always gives some away to neighbours. Everyone keeps trading with them, everyone helps them in a bad year, and the orchard keeps going for generations. A family that steals its apples and eats them alone soon has neither friends nor orchard.

University

Two conditions are compressed into one line: பழியஞ்சி (pazhi-anji, “fearing blame/reproach”) governs *acquisition*, and பாத்தூண் (paath-ūṇ, from பகுத்து உண்ணல், “eating after apportioning”) governs *consumption*. The reward is stated negatively and absolutely: வழி எஞ்சல் … இல் — “there is never a failing of the line.” வழி (vazhi) means both “way” and “lineage,” so the promise is simultaneously that the household's path and its descendants will not run out. Note that Valluvar's ethical lever here is பழி (pazhi, public blame) rather than sin — an honour-and-reproach vocabulary rooted in Tamil Sangam social ethics rather than in a theology of divine punishment, which is part of why the Kural reads as secular to modern audiences.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The devotees of the Lord are released from all kinds of sins because they eat food which is offered first for sacrifice. Others, who prepare food for personal sense enjoyment, verily eat only sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.13 [4]
Krishna contrasts food that has first been offered and shared with food cooked purely for one's own gratification, calling the latter itself a form of wrongdoing.
This is precisely the Kural's பாத்தூண் principle: eating only what has first been apportioned to others is what keeps the household clean.
Christianity
“He hath dispersed abroad; he hath given to the poor: his righteousness remaineth for ever. Now he that ministereth seed to the sower both minister bread for your food, and multiply your seed sown, and increase the fruits of your righteousness.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:9-10 (KJV) [5]
Paul uses an agricultural image: giving away is sowing, and the giver's store is replenished and multiplied rather than depleted.
Both texts promise that the household which shares its food does not diminish but endures and multiplies.
Islam
“And they give food in spite of love for it to the needy, the orphan, and the captive,”
— Qur'an 76:8, Al-Insan (Sahih International) [6]
Surah al-Insan praises the righteous specifically for giving away food they themselves want, and promises them lasting reward.
It matches the Kural's second condition exactly — the household's virtue lies in apportioning its own meal outward.
Judaism
“I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread. He is ever merciful, and lendeth; and his seed is blessed.”
— Psalm 37:25-26 (KJV, Tanakh) [5]
The psalmist's lifelong observation is that generosity and descendants' security go together — the open-handed man's children do not end up beggars.
This is the Kural's exact promise: the household that shares its bread finds its line never fails.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177, Lokavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
The Dhammapada makes generosity the decisive factor in a layperson's future welfare, and hoarding the decisive obstacle.
Both texts tie the household's long-term fortune directly to whether it shares what it has.
Jainism
“Nyayasampannavibhavah : Possessed of honestly earned wealth.” … “Papabhiru : Apprehensive of sin.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra 1.47–56 (the thirty-five householder qualities, nos. 1 and 3), English as given in V. A. Sangave, Ethics of Jainism [8]
Hemacandra's list of the ideal layman's qualities opens with wealth earned by just means and soon adds fear of wrongdoing, putting ethical acquisition first among the householder's virtues.
Valluvar's 'fearing blame' in earning is matched almost word for word by Hemacandra's first and third qualities, honest wealth and fear of sin, and the Jain duty of giving supplies the Kural's 'sharing what one eats'.
Sikhism
At Saiyidpur Guru Nanak stayed with Lalo, a poor carpenter, and declined the feast of Malik Bhago, a steward grown rich by bribery and oppression. Holding Lalo's coarse bread in one hand and Bhago's rich bread in the other, he squeezed them; milk came from Lalo's and blood from Bhago's.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter IV, pp. 43-44 [9]
Macauliffe tells this well-known Janamsakhi story to show the Sikh principle that food earned by honest labour is pure, while food gained by exploiting others is tainted, however lavishly it is shared.
It is the Sikh image of Valluvar's two conditions: earning without blame and sharing what one eats; Bhago shares but fails the first test, and so his bread is refused.
Greek Mythology
“Wealth should not be seized: god-given wealth is much better; for it a man take great wealth violently and perforce, or if he steal it through his tongue … the gods soon blot him out and make that man's house low, and wealth attends him only for a little time.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 320-341 (Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod warns his brother Perses that dishonestly gotten wealth is punished by Zeus not just personally but at the level of the household, which is brought low.
This is the Kural's first condition stated as its negative: the household that does not fear blame in acquiring will see its line fail.
Philosophy
Aristotle analyses liberality (eleutheriotes) as a mean concerning wealth: the liberal person gives the right amounts to the right people at the right time, gives from his own resources rather than by taking wrongly, and cares more about giving well than about getting.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 [11]
For Aristotle the virtue is not generosity alone but the pairing of clean acquisition with well-judged distribution — the two are aspects of a single disposition.
The Kural makes exactly that pairing: blameless earning and apportioned eating are two halves of one household virtue.

Real Life Example

Mitsui Takatoshi opens the Echigoya shop
1673 · Edo (Tokyo), Japan
Mitsui Takatoshi, a merchant from Matsusaka who had earlier returned home to care for his mother, opened a textile shop called Echigoya in Edo in 1673. The shop became known for selling for cash at fixed prices with no mark-ups, as its signboards announced, and it grew into the largest textile store of the Edo period. Echigoya became the forerunner of the Mitsukoshi department store, and the family's money-exchange business led eventually to the modern Mitsui group.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mitsui's plain, fair pricing helped build one of the longest-lasting family enterprises in the world.
The kural promises that a household that earns without blame will never see its line fail, and the Mitsui name, founded on openly fair dealing, has continued for more than three centuries.
