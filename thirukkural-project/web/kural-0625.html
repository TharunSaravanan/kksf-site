Kural 625

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

அடுக்கி வரினும் அழிவிலான் உற்ற இடுக்கண் இடுக்கட் படும்.
Atukki varinum azhivilaan utra Itukkan itukkat patum.

Meaning

Even if troubles come one on top of another, a person who does not break down inside will make those troubles themselves end up in trouble.

Elementary

Sometimes bad things come in a row, like missing the bus, then spilling your lunch, then losing your pencil. If you keep your spirit strong and don’t give up, all those troubles run out of steam, one after another.

University

Aṭukki varinum, ‘even if they come stacked’, answers the natural objection to 623 and 624: what if troubles do not come singly but in a relentless series? The subject aḻivilāṉ is literally ‘one without destruction’, which Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both gloss as one whose mind does not collapse (kalaṅkāmal, maṉam taḷarātavaṉ). The closing iṭukkaṇ iṭukkaṭ paṭum repeats the reversal of 623 with iṭukkaṇ instead of iṭumpai, the sandhi iṭukkaṭ reflecting the following p-. Both sources agree on the text, and the commentarial tradition treats 623-625 as a graded sequence: undaunted by one trouble, dragging through many ruts, and unbroken even by a pile of calamities.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, tested by the sage Vishvamitra, loses his kingdom, sells his wife and son, becomes a servant at a cremation ground, and at last must face his own son’s corpse, yet at every step he refuses to abandon truth; in the end the gods restore everything to him.
— Markandeya Purana 7-8 (Harishchandra legend) [3]
The legend heaps calamity upon calamity on a single person to show a spirit that no accumulation of misfortune can break.
Harishchandra is the Kural’s aḻivilāṉ, the one whom troubles piled one on another cannot destroy, and who outlasts them all.
Christianity
“We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair; Persecuted, but not forsaken; cast down, but not destroyed;”
— 2 Corinthians 4:8-9 (KJV) [4]
Paul lists affliction after affliction and after each one insists that it has not crushed him.
Paul’s ‘cast down, but not destroyed’ is almost a translation of the Kural’s aḻivilāṉ, the one troubles cannot destroy.
Islam
“And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient, Who, when disaster strikes them, say, ‘Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.’”
— Qur’an 2:155-156 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns of a whole series of trials and promises good news to those who meet them with patient steadfastness (ṣabr).
Both texts expect troubles to come in numbers and give the final word to the one who remains unshaken.
Judaism
In the prologue of Job, messenger after messenger arrives, each still speaking when the next comes in: his oxen and asses are taken, his sheep burned, his camels seized, and finally his children killed; yet Job does not curse God or charge him with wrong.
— Job 1:13-22 [6]
Job is the classic figure of a man hit by calamities stacked one upon another who nevertheless is not broken.
The Kural’s ‘even if they come piled up’ is exactly the Job prologue, where disasters arrive faster than they can be absorbed.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The wise person is compared to a rock that the storm beats against without moving it.
The Kural’s aḻivilāṉ is the Dhammapada’s rock, battered by repeated blows and unshaken by them.
Jainism
“All these troubles have been declared by the Kâśyapa. A monk should not be vanquished by them, when attacked by any anywhere.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2, closing verse (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Having listed twenty-two troubles that may strike a monk, often several at once, the lecture closes by saying he must not be overcome by any of them.
The Kural says that even when troubles come piled on one another, the one who does not break inside defeats them; the Jain conclusion likewise requires the monk to stay undefeated by every trouble, wherever it attacks.
Sikhism
Guru Gobind Singh had to give up Anandpur after a long siege in December 1705, saw his two elder sons die in the battle of Chamkaur, and then learned that his two younger sons had been executed at Sirhind. With only a few companions left, he wrote the Zafarnamah to Emperor Aurangzeb in a mood of unbroken spirit, declaring that the loss of his four children mattered little while the Khalsa still stood.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (Punjabi University, Patiala, ed. Harbans Singh), 'Zafarnamah', via The Sikh Encyclopedia (paraphrase) [9]
This run of disasters, coming one after another within a few weeks, is remembered in Sikh history as the supreme test of Guru Gobind Singh's spirit, and his answer was the defiant Zafarnamah rather than despair.
The Kural says that when troubles come piled one on another, the person who does not break inside makes the troubles themselves suffer; Guru Gobind Singh met a whole cascade of losses without breaking.
Greek Mythology
Heracles is set labour after labour by Eurystheus, from the Nemean lion and the Hydra to the Augean stables and the descent for Cerberus, each harder than the last and some disallowed so that more had to be added, yet he completes them all.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5 [10]
The labours are a mythic catalogue of troubles stacked one on another, deliberately designed to break him.
Heracles is the Greek image of the Kural’s unbreakable one, whom an unending pile of trials only makes greater.
Philosophy
“Ignis aurum probat, miseria fortes viros.” (Fire tests gold, misfortune brave men.)”
— Seneca, De Providentia 5.10 [11]
Seneca argues that the gods expose good people to repeated hardships the way fire assays gold, to reveal and strengthen their worth.
For Seneca as for the Kural, a flood of misfortunes does not destroy the steadfast but proves them.

Real Life Example

Humayun loses his empire and regains Delhi years later
1540-1555 · North India, Persia (Iran) and Afghanistan
The Mughal emperor Humayun was defeated by Sher Shah Sur at Chausa in 1539 and at Kannauj in 1540 and was driven out of India. He took refuge with Shah Tahmasp of Persia, who gave him military aid, and he then took Kandahar and had to seize Kabul three times from his own disloyal brother Kamran. In 1555 he defeated Sikandar Sur at Sirhind and reoccupied Delhi and Agra, regaining his throne after more than a decade, though he died in an accident the following January.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Humayun suffered defeat, exile and betrayal by his own family one after another, yet he kept going and restored the Mughal dynasty that his son Akbar would make great.
The kural says that even when troubles come piled one on another, the person who does not break inside makes those troubles suffer, and Humayun's long string of calamities ended in the recovery of his kingdom.
