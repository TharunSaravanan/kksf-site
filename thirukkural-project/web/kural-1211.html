Kural 1211

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

காதலர் தூதொடு வந்த கனவினுக்கு யாதுசெய் வேன்கொல் விருந்து.
Kaadhalar thoodhodu vandha kanavinukku yaadhusey vaenkol virundhu.

Meaning

A dream came to me carrying a message from my faraway love. What special meal can I give that dream, like a guest, to thank it?

Elementary

Imagine you are missing a grandparent who lives far away, and one night you dream they send you a happy note saying they will visit soon. You wake up so glad that you want to thank the dream itself, as if it were a postman who knocked at your door with the best letter ever.

University

Chapter 122, kaṉavunilai uraittal (‘telling of the state of dreams’), continues the karpiyal sequence on separation: in the traditional reading associated with Parimelazhagar, the wife, long parted from her husband, tells her confidante (tōḻi) what she has seen in dreams, and the dreams become her way of enduring absence. The opening couplet personifies the dream as a guest who arrives with a tūtu (messenger, embassy), so that the hospitality word viruntu (feast offered to a guest, the virtue praised in Aṟam ch. 9) is turned on the dream itself; yātu ceyvēṉ-kol is a helpless rhetorical question, ‘what can I possibly do?’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss the line as asking what return (kaimmāṟu) she can make to the dream that came with her lover’s envoy. Pope renders: ‘It came and brought to me, that nightly vision rare, / A message from my love,- what feast shall I prepare?’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (which spells the chapter title as two words, kaṉavunilai uraittal).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the story of Nala and Damayanti, a golden swan that Nala has caught and released flies to Damayanti and praises Nala to her, carrying love between two people who have never met.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
The Mahabharata’s tale makes a winged messenger the first bearer of love’s news between separated lovers.
The Kural’s dream plays the same part as Nala’s swan: a go-between who brings the beloved’s message across distance, and earns the receiver’s gratitude.
Christianity
“The angel of the Lord appeared unto him in a dream, saying, Joseph, thou son of David, fear not to take unto thee Mary thy wife.”
— Matthew 1:20 (KJV) [4]
Matthew’s Gospel has a heavenly messenger arrive through a dream to settle Joseph’s anguish about the woman he is to marry.
As in the Kural, a dream comes bearing a message about the beloved, and it changes the dreamer’s heart.
Islam
“If anyone of you sees a dream that he likes, then it is from Allah, and he should thank Allah for it and narrate it to others.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6985 [5]
The Prophet teaches that a pleasing dream is a gift to be met with gratitude and shared.
The Kural’s heroine likewise wants to repay a welcome dream; Islam directs that gratitude to God and asks the dreamer to tell it, much as she tells her confidante.
Judaism
“A dream not interpreted is like a letter not read.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 55b (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
Rav Hisda, quoted in the Talmud’s long discussion of dreams, treats a dream as a sealed message addressed to the dreamer.
The Kural’s dream is exactly such a letter, arriving with the beloved’s envoy and waiting to be received with honour.
Buddhism
Queen Maya dreams that a white elephant enters her side; on waking she tells King Suddhodana, who summons learned brahmins, feasts them generously and asks them to explain the dream, which they read as news of a great son to come.
— Nidanakatha (Introduction to the Jataka), the conception of the Bodhisatta (paraphrase; cf. tr. T.W. Rhys Davids, Buddhist Birth Stories) [7]
In the Buddhist birth narrative, a dream arrives as a herald of joyful tidings and is answered with lavish hospitality.
The royal feast given in thanks for Maya’s dream is the very kind of ‘viruntu’ the Kural’s heroine wishes she could offer her dream.
Jainism
“He accepted the true meaning of the dreams, and honoured the interpreters of dreams with praise and plenty of food, flowers, perfumes, garlands, and ornaments.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §83 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
After Queen Trisala’s fourteen auspicious dreams foretell Mahavira’s birth, King Siddhartha thanks those who opened the dreams’ message with a lavish reception.
Jain scripture shows the gratitude the Kural’s heroine longs to express: a dream bearing good news is answered with a feast.
Sikhism
“If only someone would come, and lead me to meet my Darling Beloved; I would sell myself to him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 757, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das, speaking as the longing soul-bride, says she would give herself away to whoever brings her Beloved to meet her.
The Kural's wife wonders what feast could repay the dream that brought word of her husband; the Guru's bride likewise has nothing smaller to offer such a go-between than her whole self.
Greek Mythology
While Penelope sleeps in grief, Athena sends a phantom in the likeness of her sister Iphthime, who stands over her head and comforts her with the message that her absent son Telemachus will return safely.
— Homer, Odyssey 4.795-841 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer gives the anxious wife a dream-messenger sent from outside to carry word of an absent loved one.
Penelope’s dream-envoy is the Greek twin of the Kural’s dream ‘that came with my beloved’s messenger’.
Philosophy
In prison Socrates tells Crito he has dreamed of a fair woman in white who called him and spoke a line from Homer, telling him that on the third day he would come to fertile Phthia.
— Plato, Crito 44a-b (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato presents a dream as a gracious visitor delivering a message that Socrates trusts and acts upon.
Socrates’ dream-woman, like the Kural’s dream, arrives as a courier from beyond and is welcomed rather than dismissed.

Real Life Example

Thutmose IV sets up the Dream Stela at the Sphinx
c. 1400 BCE · Giza, Egypt
A granite stela set up in front of the Great Sphinx at Giza records that Prince Thutmose fell asleep in the Sphinx's shade and dreamed that the Sphinx, speaking as the sun god, promised him the throne if he would clear away the sand that buried it. After becoming king, Thutmose IV restored the Sphinx and erected the stela to record the dream. Scholars often read the inscription as royal propaganda supporting the legitimacy of his reign.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The stela is one of the oldest surviving records of a dream treated as a message worth honouring with action and a monument.
The kural's heroine wonders what feast she could give to the dream that brought her a message, and Thutmose IV repaid his dream-messenger with a restoration and a monument.
