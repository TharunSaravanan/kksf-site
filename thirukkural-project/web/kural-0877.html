Kural 877

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

நோவற்க நொந்தது அறியார்க்கு மேவற்க மென்மை பகைவர் அகத்து.
Novarka nondhadhu ariyaarkku mevarka menmai pakaivar akaththu.

Meaning

Do not tell your troubles to people who do not already know about them, and do not show your weak points to your enemies.

Elementary

If you are feeling hurt, you do not have to tell everyone you meet. And if someone is being mean to you, try not to show them where it hurts, because they might poke you there again. Share your worries with the people who really care and already understand.

University

The couplet has two prohibitions in parallel: nōvaṟka (‘do not lament’) to those who do not know one’s suffering, and mēvaṟka (‘do not take on, display’) meṉmai (மென்மை, ‘softness, weakness’) in the presence of enemies. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar take the first line as referring to friends who have not themselves perceived one’s distress: to complain to them is undignified and spreads knowledge of one’s weakness. Solomon Pappaiah reads the hidden object of both lines as ‘our lack of strength’, which should neither be told to unaware friends nor shown to enemies. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads the lines quite differently: do not grieve over those who do not understand grief, and do not covet the soft spot of enemies. Pope’s ‘To those who know them not, complain not of your woes; nor to your foeman’s eyes infirmities disclose’ follows the majority reading. The text is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
During their thirteenth year of exile, the Pandavas lived in disguise at King Virata’s court, enduring hardships and insults in silence so that the Kauravas could not discover them.
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva [3]
Revealing themselves before the year ended would have handed their enemies the advantage.
They lived out the Kural’s rule: keep your suffering hidden and give your enemies no glimpse of weakness.
Christianity
Jesus warns not to give what is holy to dogs or throw pearls before swine, lest they trample them and turn and tear you.
— Matthew 7:6 [4]
What is precious and vulnerable should not be exposed to those who will misuse it.
The Kural applies the same caution to one’s sorrows and weaknesses.
Islam
“I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allāh, and I know from Allāh that which you do not know.”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Sahih International); cf. 12:5 [5]
The prophet Jacob, grieving for Joseph, refuses to pour out his sorrow to those around him and takes it only to God; earlier in the same sura he warns Joseph not to tell his dream to his brothers lest they plot against him (12:5).
Together the verses mirror both halves of the Kural: do not air your grief to the unknowing, and do not expose yourself to hostile people.
Judaism
“That he told her all his heart, and said unto her. There hath not come a razor upon mine head; for I have been a Nazarite unto God from my mother’s womb: if I be shaven, then my strength will go from me, and I shall become weak, and be like any other man.”
— Judges 16:17 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Samson revealed the secret of his weakness to Delilah, who was working for his Philistine enemies, and was captured.
This is the classic disaster the Kural warns against: disclosing one’s weakness where enemies can use it.
Buddhism
In the Kacchapa Jataka, a talkative tortoise carried through the air on a stick by two geese cannot resist answering the mocking children below, opens his mouth, and falls.
— Kacchapa Jataka (Jataka No. 215) [6]
The Bodhisatta uses the tale to teach a king the danger of speaking at the wrong moment.
Like the Kural, it warns that speaking out in front of those who mock you can be one’s undoing.
Jainism
“If he suffers from the heat of hot things, or from the heat of his body, or from the heat of summer, he should not lament the loss of comfort. … All these troubles have been declared by the Kāśyapa. A monk should not be vanquished by them, when attacked by any anywhere.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.8 and closing passage (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Mahāvīra's teaching on the twenty-two hardships (parīṣaha) asks the monk to bear them without complaint and never to let them defeat him.
The Kural says not to pour out one's troubles or show weakness before enemies, and the Jain discipline likewise forbids lamenting one's sufferings and calls for firmness against every attack.
Sikhism
“Tell your troubles to the One who is the Source of all comfort.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 16, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak teaches that one's sorrows should be laid before God, the giver of all comfort, rather than spread before others.
The Kural says not to tell your troubles to those who do not already know them, nor show weakness before enemies; Guru Nanak likewise directs the telling of troubles to God alone.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus wakes on Ithaca, Athena disguises him as an old beggar and advises him to tell no one who he is and to endure in silence the insults of the suitors until the time is right.
— Homer, Odyssey 13 [9]
Odysseus keeps his identity and his grief hidden from both enemies and most of his household.
He follows the Kural to the letter, concealing pain and vulnerability from his foes.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises that in company one should avoid frequently and at length talking about one’s own actions and dangers, since others do not enjoy hearing them as much as one enjoys telling them.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 [10]
The Stoic counsels reserve about one’s own troubles in ordinary society.
This echoes the Kural’s first line about not airing sorrows to those who do not already know.

Real Life Example

The Ghost Army deceives the enemy in Europe
1944-1945 · France, Belgium, Luxembourg and Germany
The US Army's 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, a secret unit of about 1,100 men later called the Ghost Army, used inflatable tanks and vehicles, recorded battle sounds played through huge speakers, and fake radio traffic to make the enemy believe that large forces stood where there were few or none. In its largest operation, Operation Viersen in March 1945, it inflated more than 600 dummy vehicles to simulate two divisions while the Ninth Army crossed the Rhine. Its work stayed classified for decades, and its veterans were later awarded the Congressional Gold Medal.
— Sources: [11] [12]
By hiding gaps in the line and never letting the enemy see where it was thin, the Allies kept their opponents from exploiting their real weak points.
The kural says never to show weakness before enemies, and the Ghost Army made a whole craft of hiding where Allied lines were weak.
