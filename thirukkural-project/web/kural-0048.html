Kural 48

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

ஆற்றின் ஒழுக்கி அறனிழுக்கா இல்வாழ்க்கை நோற்பாரின் நோன்மை உடைத்து.
Aatrin ozhukki aranizhukkaa ilvaazhkkai, Norpaarin nonmai utaiththu.

Meaning

A family life that helps others stay on the right path, and never turns away from what is right itself, is stronger than the strict self-discipline of people who give up home life.

Elementary

One person sits alone on a hill and eats nothing for a week — that's hard. Another person gets up every day, stays honest, feeds the family, and gently keeps everyone else honest too — for fifty years. This kural says the second one is doing the harder, stronger thing.

University

The verse builds a careful two-part condition before making its comparison. ஆற்றின் ஒழுக்கி (aatrin ozhukki) is causative — “having caused [others] to conduct themselves on the path” — while அறனிழுக்கா (aran-izhukkaa) is negative and reflexive: “not itself slipping from virtue.” Only a household meeting both conditions is then said to possess நோன்மை (nonmai) exceeding that of நோற்பார் (norpaar, those who undertake நோன்பு, austerity/penance). The pun is the point: நோன்மை means both “endurance under penance” and “strength,” so Valluvar claims the householder wins the ascetic's own contest on the ascetic's own metric. This is the chapter's most polemical line and reflects a broader Tamil bhakti-era and post-Sangam argument against Jain and Buddhist monastic prestige.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Whatever action a great man performs, common men follow. And whatever standards he sets by exemplary acts, all the world pursues.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.21 (with 3.20 on Janaka) [4]
Krishna cites the householder-king Janaka, who attained perfection through action, to argue that a person of standing must keep working precisely in order to guide others by example.
This supplies the Kural's first condition exactly — the householder's virtue is measured by his capacity to keep others on the path.
Christianity
“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”
— Matthew 5:16 (KJV) [5]
Jesus places the moral value of visible, ordinary good conduct in its effect on others, immediately after warning against hiding a lamp under a bushel.
Both texts make guiding others by one's own consistent conduct the measure of a life, rather than private feats of self-denial.
Islam
“O you who have believed, protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, over which are [appointed] angels, harsh and severe…”
— Qur'an 66:6, At-Tahrim (Sahih International) [6]
The verse is traditionally read as commanding the head of a household to instruct and morally safeguard his dependants, not merely himself.
It matches the Kural's dual condition precisely: keep yourself from slipping and keep your household on the right path.
Judaism
“And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up.”
— Deuteronomy 6:7 (KJV, Tanakh) [5]
The Shema makes the ordinary rhythms of household life — sitting, walking, lying down, rising — the setting for transmitting the commandments to the next generation.
This is the Kural's ஆற்றின் ஒழுக்கி in practice: the household is where others are actually steered onto the right path.
Buddhism
“One should first establish oneself in what is proper; then only should one instruct others. Thus the wise man will not be reproached.”
— Dhammapada 158, Attavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
The Dhammapada's self-chapter insists on the order of operations: self-correction precedes the correction of others, or the teaching is worthless.
The Kural states the same two requirements in the same order — not slipping oneself, and guiding others along the path.
Jainism
“Some householders are superior to some monks in self-control; but the saints are superior to all householders in self-control.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahāvīra's teaching admits that a disciplined householder can surpass a lax monk in self-restraint, while still ranking the true saints above every layman.
The Kural says a virtuous household has more strength than the penance of ascetics, and the Uttarādhyayana grants the same point for some householders against some monks.
Sikhism
“How have you put off yogic garb and attired yourself in a household way.” … “You, while distancing and repudiating house hold life, go again to those householders for begging. Except their offerings you don’t get anything.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 1, Pauri 40 (tr. Dr Jodh Singh) [9]
Bhai Gurdas, the early Sikh scholar and scribe of the Adi Granth, describes the yogi Bhangar Nath at Achal Batala mocking Guru Nanak for living as a householder, and the Guru replying that renunciants who reject household life still live on householders' gifts.
It is the Sikh form of Valluvar's boldest claim: the householder who keeps to virtue is stronger than the ascetics, who depend on him for their food.
Greek Mythology
“To you, foolish Perses, I will speak good sense. Badness can be got easily and in shoals: the road to her is smooth, and she lives very near us. But between us and Goodness the gods have placed the sweat of our brows: long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the first.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 286-292 (Evelyn-White) [10]
Works and Days is framed as a farmer-householder correcting his idle brother, arguing that ordinary honest labour is the long steep road to virtue.
Hesiod's householder both walks the hard path himself and works to pull a wayward kinsman onto it — the Kural's two conditions in a single poem.
Philosophy
The Stoic Musonius Rufus argued in his lectures that marriage is no handicap to philosophy, that the philosopher must marry and raise children, and that the shared household is itself the arena in which self-control, justice and endurance are actually practised.
— Musonius Rufus, Lecture XIV, 'Is Marriage a Handicap for the Pursuit of Philosophy?' [11]
Against the Cynic model of the unencumbered sage, Musonius insisted that the demands of spouse, children and household are harder training in virtue than solitude.
This is the Kural's claim in Roman Stoic form: domestic life possesses more real endurance than the withdrawn ascetic's regimen.

Real Life Example

Susanna Wesley's household at Epworth
1690s-1735 · Epworth, Lincolnshire, England, United Kingdom
At the rectory in Epworth, Susanna Wesley raised the ten of her nineteen children who survived infancy and taught them herself on a strict daily timetable, with lessons from nine to noon and two to five, girls as well as boys learning to read from the age of five, and prayers at rising and bedtime. During her husband's long absences she also read prayers and sermons to neighbours at the rectory, gatherings that at one time drew around 200 people. Her sons John and Charles Wesley went on to found the Methodist movement, and she is often called the Mother of Methodism.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Susanna Wesley's ordered, devout household shaped two of the most influential religious leaders of the eighteenth century and reached out to her whole village.
The kural praises a household that keeps others on the right path while never slipping itself as stronger than the austerities of ascetics, and Susanna Wesley's home guided her children and neighbours alike.
