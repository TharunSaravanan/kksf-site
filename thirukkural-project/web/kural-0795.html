Kural 795

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

அழச்சொல்லி அல்லது இடித்து வழக்கறிய வல்லார்நட்பு ஆய்ந்து கொளல்.
Azhachcholli alladhu idiththu vazhakkariya vallaarnatpu aaindhu kolal.

Meaning

Find and keep friends who can make you cry with hard truths, scold you sharply when you are wrong, and understand how the world works.

Elementary

A good coach sometimes tells you, ‘That was wrong, do it again,’ and it can make you want to cry. But that coach makes you better. Valluvar says to choose friends like that coach, not friends who only say nice things.

University

Azhach cholli (அழச்சொல்லி) is ‘speaking so as to make one weep’ and idiththu (இடித்து) is ‘striking’, that is, rebuking hard; the same verb appears in kural 448 of the idikkum thunai, the counsellor who rebukes a king. Parimelazhagar distinguishes the two: the friend makes you weep with counsel before a wrong is done, and rebukes you sharply after it is done; Solomon Pappaiah follows this two-step reading. Vazhakku ariya valla (வழக்கறிய வல்ல) means ‘able to make one know the ways of the world’, adding practical wisdom to frankness. Both ytamizh and thirukkural.net print வல்லார்நடபு, which is missing its pulli; the sandhi-joined edition’s வல்லார்நட் பாய்ந்து confirms the reading வல்லார்நட்பு used here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“They are abundant, O king, that can always speak agreeable words. The speaker, however, is rare, as also the hearer, of words that are disagreeable but medicinal.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXVII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura tells the blind king Dhritarashtra that flatterers are everywhere, but people who speak painful, healing truth are rare, and so are those willing to hear it.
Vidura’s ‘disagreeable but medicinal’ words are the hard words of the friend the Kural tells you to keep.
Christianity
“Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (KJV) [5]
The proverb says a friend’s painful honesty can be trusted, while an enemy’s affection is a trick.
The ‘wounds of a friend’ are the Kural’s words that make you weep.
Islam
“The believer is the believer’s mirror, and the believer is the believer’s brother who guards him against loss and protects him when he is absent.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4918 (graded hasan by al-Albani) [6]
The Prophet says a fellow believer should show you your faults as a mirror does, while also protecting you.
The ‘mirror’ friend who shows your faults is the Kural’s friend who rebukes.
Judaism
“You shall not hate your kinsfolk in your heart. Reprove your kindred but incur no guilt on their account.”
— Leviticus 19:17 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, Sefaria) [7]
The Torah makes rebuke a duty: instead of hating someone silently, you must tell him plainly where he is wrong.
Both texts treat honest reproof as a mark of real love rather than a breach of it.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure. It is always better, and never worse, to cultivate such an association.”
— Dhammapada 76 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha tells his hearers to treat someone who points out their faults like a guide to buried treasure.
This is almost the Kural’s own advice: seek out and keep the friend who rebukes.
Jainism
“Wise, fearless monks consider even a rough instruction as a benefit, but the fools hate it, though it produces patience and purity of mind.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Uttarādhyayana teaches that hard words of correction are to be welcomed as a benefit, and that only fools resent them.
The Kural says we should seek friends who can rebuke us sharply enough to make us weep, and the Jain canon likewise values the harsh word that corrects us over pleasant flattery.
Sikhism
When Guru Gobind Singh, passing through Narayana in Rajasthan, saluted the shrine of the saint Dadu by raising an arrow to his forehead, the Khalsa objected and one Sikh, Man Singh, quoted the Guru's own verse forbidding the worship of tombs. The Guru at once offered to pay a fine; after some debate the Sikhs fixed it at Rs 125, which they spent on a kitchen tent.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, ed. Harbans Singh (Punjabi University, Patiala), entry 'Dadu Dial (1544-1603)' by Gurnek Singh (paraphrase) [10]
The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism says the Guru did this to test the conviction of his Sikhs, and he welcomed being rebuked by them when he appeared to break his own teaching.
The Kural tells us to seek friends who will rebuke us sharply and understand the world's ways; the Khalsa rebuking even its Guru, and the Guru accepting it, is the Sikh model of a fellowship that corrects rather than flatters.
Greek Mythology
As the Greeks are being driven back to their ships, Patroclus comes to Achilles in tears and rebukes him as pitiless for sitting out the battle in anger while his comrades die, then begs to lead the Myrmidons himself.
— Homer, Iliad 16.1-45 [11]
At the opening of Iliad 16, Achilles’ dearest friend is the one person who confronts him to his face over his wrath.
Patroclus is the friend who speaks hard words to stop a wrong, as the Kural describes.
Philosophy
“Plain speaking is a cause of trouble, if the result of it is resentment, which is poison of friendship; but compliance is really the cause of much more trouble, because by indulging his faults it lets a friend plunge into headlong ruin.”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia §24 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [12]
Laelius argues that a friend who only goes along with you does more harm than one whose frankness stings.
Cicero and Valluvar agree that the stinging, frank friend is the one worth keeping.

Real Life Example

Clementine Churchill's frank letter to Winston
27 June 1940 · London, United Kingdom
On 27 June 1940, in the weeks after the fall of France, Clementine Churchill wrote to her husband, now Prime Minister, to warn him that he was in danger of becoming unpopular with his colleagues and staff because of his rough, sarcastic and overbearing manner. She told him plainly that she herself had noticed his manner growing worse, and urged him to combine his great power with kindness and calm. The letter is preserved in the Churchill Archives Centre at Churchill College, Cambridge.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The letter is remembered as a model of loving but unsparing counsel, offered to the most powerful man in Britain at a moment of extreme strain.
Valluvar urges us to seek those who can speak hard words that sting and who understand the ways of the world, and Clementine's letter was exactly such counsel.
