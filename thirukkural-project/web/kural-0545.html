Kural 545

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

இயல்புளிக் கோலோச்சும் மன்னவன் நாட்ட பெயலும் விளையுளும் தொக்கு.
Iyalpulik Kolochchum Mannavan Naatta Peyalum Vilaiyulum Thokku.

Meaning

In the country of a king who rules by fair law, the seasonal rains come and the harvests are rich, both together.

Elementary

When a family shares fairly and nobody grabs, dinner time is happy and there is enough for everyone. The old Tamil poets believed a whole country works that way: when the ruler is fair, even the rain and the crops seem to join in.

University

இயல்புளி is இயல்பு with the instrumental ending உளி (= ஆல்), ‘by the proper nature/way’. Parimelazhagar glosses it as ruling according to the method laid down in the books of statecraft (நீதிநூல்), and Varadarasan (‘நீதி முறைப்படி’) and Pappaiah (‘அரச நீதிநூல்கள் கூறியபடி’) follow him. நாட்ட is a genitive plural predicate, ‘belong to his land’. பெயல் is seasonal rainfall and விளையுள் the abundant yield. The final தொக்கு (having gathered together) means the two are always found together there. Behind the couplet lies a belief found across ancient cultures, that the king’s righteousness governs the fertility of the land. Valluvar states the negative in the next chapter: முறைகோடி மன்னவன் செய்யின் உறைகோடி ஒல்லாது வானம் பெயல் (559). Kalaignar reads it as a simile, that just government is like timely rain bringing a good harvest, which softens the causal claim. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Whether it is the king that makes the age, or, it is the age that makes the king, is a question about which thou shouldst not entertain any doubt. The truth is that the king makes the age.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section LXIX (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Bhishma teaches that when the king rules fully by the science of just punishment the Krita age sets in and the earth yields crops in abundance, while under an unjust king the clouds do not pour seasonably and crops fail.
The same causal chain as the Kural: righteous rule brings rain and harvest together.
Christianity
“Behold, a king shall reign in righteousness, and princes shall rule in judgment. … Then judgment shall dwell in the wilderness, and righteousness remain in the fruitful field.”
— Isaiah 32:1, 16 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah foresees a righteous king, and a time when justice and righteousness make even the wilderness fruitful.
Just rule and fruitful land belong together, as the Kural says.
Islam
“And if only the people of the cities had believed and feared Allah, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth; but they denied [the messengers], so We seized them for what they were earning.”
— Qur’an 7:96 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says a community’s faith and God-consciousness would open blessings from sky and earth.
Blessings from heaven (rain) and earth (harvest) depend on moral order, as in the Kural.
Judaism
“If ye walk in My statutes, and keep My commandments, and do them; then I will give your rains in their season, and the land shall yield her produce, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit.”
— Leviticus 26:3–4 (JPS 1917) [6]
The Torah promises rain in season and a full harvest if Israel lives by God’s laws.
Rain in season and yield together, as a reward for living by the law, is the Kural’s பெயலும் விளையுளும் தொக்கு.
Buddhism
“At a time when kings are principled, royal officials become principled. … the heavens provide plenty of rain. When the heavens provide plenty of rain, the crops ripen well.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.70, Adhammika Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha traces a chain from righteous kings through officials and people to regular seasons, good rain and ripening crops.
This is almost the Kural’s claim itself: a principled king means good rain and good harvest together.
Jainism
“In the night in which the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra was brought into the family of the Gñâtris their silver increased, their gold increased; their riches, corn, majesty, and kingdom increased; their army, train, treasure, storehouse, town, seraglio, subjects, and glory increased”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Mahāvīra §90 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Because the realm prospered from the moment the future Tīrthaṅkara was conceived, his parents named him Vardhamāna, ‘the Increaser’.
The Kural links righteous rule to abundant harvests in the land, and the Kalpa Sūtra likewise tells of a royal house whose corn, treasure and subjects multiplied in the presence of great merit.
Sikhism
“The rain has fallen; I have found the Transcendent Lord God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 105, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan uses the coming of the rain as an image of grace and fulfilment.
The Kural says seasonal rain and rich harvests belong together in the land of a lawful king; Gurbani also links rain with blessing, but the rain is sent by God rather than earned by a ruler's justice.
Greek Mythology
“But they who give straight judgements to strangers and to the men of the land, and go not aside from what is just, their city flourishes, and the people prosper in it: … The earth bears them victual in plenty, and on the mountains the oak bears acorns upon the top and bees in the midst.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 225–233 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod teaches that Zeus rewards cities whose judges give straight verdicts with peace and a generous earth.
‘Straight judgements’ are the Greek செங்கோல், and plenty from the earth follows, just as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“But those officers whose land he sees lying idle and with but few inhabitants, owing either to the harshness of their government, their insolence, or their neglect, he punishes, and making them to cease from their office he appoints other rulers in their place.”
— Xenophon, Oeconomicus (The Economist), ch. IV, 8 (tr. H. G. Dakyns) [11]
Socrates tells Critobulus that the Persian king rewards governors whose districts are thickly peopled and richly cultivated, and removes those whose land lies waste through harsh or careless rule.
Like the Kural, Xenophon ties the fruitfulness of a land to the quality of its government, though through administration rather than seasonal rain.

Real Life Example

Todar Mal's revenue settlement under Akbar
c. 1580 · Northern India (Mughal Empire)
Under the Mughal emperor Akbar, the revenue minister Raja Todar Mal reorganised the land revenue of northern India. Land was carefully measured, using lengths of bamboo joined with iron rings in place of hemp ropes, and the government gathered ten years of local figures on crop yields and prices to set average rates for each crop in each revenue circle. Under this system, called zabt, the revenue demand was fixed according to the quality of the soil and the continuity of cultivation, at between one-third and one-half of the value of the produce.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Assessing taxes on measured land and long-term average harvests replaced arbitrary demands with a known rule, one reason Akbar's administration is remembered for its order.
The kural links rule by proper law with abundant harvests, and Todar Mal's settlement shows a government binding its demands to the real yield of the land rather than to the ruler's whim.
