Kural 297

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

பொய்யாமை பொய்யாமை ஆற்றின் அறம்பிற செய்யாமை செய்யாமை நன்று.
Poyyaamai poyyaamai aatrin aram pira Seyyaamai seyyaamai nandru.

Meaning

If you are truthful and never fail at it, that is enough, even if you do no other good deed.

Elementary

Imagine a report card where one subject counts so much that getting it right is enough to pass everything. For Valluvar, truthfulness is that subject: if you never fail at being honest, you’re doing well even if you haven’t done any other good deeds yet.

University

A celebrated example of Valluvar’s wordplay: poyyaamai poyyaamai (literally ‘non-lying, not failing’) and seyyaamai seyyaamai (‘not doing, not doing’). The first pair means practising non-falsehood unfailingly; the second is read by most commentators (Parimelazhagar, Pappaiah) as ‘not doing [other virtues] is itself not a lack’ — truth alone suffices. Varadarasan offers a softer reading (‘practising other virtues also becomes good’), and Kalaignar reads it as truthfulness surpassing the merit of mere abstentions. The dominant reading makes veracity a sufficient condition of the good life, the strongest claim in the chapter after 300.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“satya-pratiṣṭhāyāṁ kriyā-phala-āśrayatvam”
— Yoga Sutras of Patanjali 2.36 [3]
Patanjali teaches that when a person is firmly established in truthfulness, actions and their fruits come to rest on him — commentators say his words and deeds become effective by truth alone.
Truthfulness alone, fully established, makes all action bear fruit — like the Kural’s claim that truth alone suffices.
Christianity
“And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.”
— John 8:32 (KJV) [4]
Jesus promises that living in the truth brings freedom from sin and bondage.
Truth alone has the power to liberate — close to the Kural’s truth that is enough by itself.
Islam
“Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise. And a man keeps on telling the truth until he becomes a truthful person.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6094 [5]
The Prophet taught that steady truthfulness leads step by step to all righteousness and finally to Paradise, while lying leads to wickedness and the Fire.
Keep truthfulness unfailingly, and the rest of goodness follows — the Kural’s point exactly.
Judaism
“Keep thee far from a false matter; and the innocent and righteous slay thou not: for I will not justify the wicked.”
— Exodus 23:7 (KJV) [4]
The Torah does not merely forbid falsehood — it commands keeping far away from it, an unusually strong phrasing rabbinic tradition often notes.
Unfailing distance from falsehood as a core command echoes the Kural’s unfailing non-lying.
Buddhism
“For a liar who has violated the one law (of truthfulness) who holds in scorn the hereafter, there is no evil that he cannot do.”
— Dhammapada 176 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada calls truthfulness ‘the one law’: break it, and every other evil becomes possible.
The mirror image of the Kural: keep this one virtue and all is well; break it and all is lost.
Jainism
“Man, understand well the truth! exerting himself in the rule of truth a wise man overcomes Mâra.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.3.3.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884, p. 33); cf. the Jainworld rendering of the same passage [7]
In the Ācārāṅga, Mahāvīra urges the seeker to hold to truth, saying that by exerting himself in truth the wise man overcomes death (Māra). Jainworld renders the same passage as “O Self! Practice Truth, and nothing but Truth.”
Valluvar says that unbroken truthfulness is enough even without any other virtuous deed. The Ācārāṅga makes the rule of truth by itself sufficient to overcome death.
Sikhism
“So how can you become truthful? And how can the veil of illusion be torn away? O Nanak, it is written that you shall obey the Hukam of His Command, and walk in the Way of His Will.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Pauri 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Japji's first pauri rejects ritual bathing, silence, fasting and cleverness as paths to becoming truthful (sachiaara). It names one way only: living in the divine order.
The Kural says that if one practises truthfulness unfailingly, it is well even without other virtuous deeds. Japji likewise treats becoming truthful as the one thing needed and finds the many other practices unnecessary.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that when a god swears falsely by the waters of Styx he lies breathless for a year, is barred from nectar and ambrosia, and is cut off from the gods’ councils and feasts for nine more years.
— Hesiod, Theogony 775-806 (summary) [9]
Even immortals keep one law above all: their sworn word by the Styx must never be false.
Among the gods, truthfulness to one’s oath is the one indispensable law, as truthfulness is for Valluvar.
Philosophy
“From me you shall hear the whole truth.”
— Plato, Apology 17b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
At his trial Socrates refuses flattery and rhetoric, offering only the truth — and dies rather than abandon it.
Socrates staked his whole life on one commitment, truth, and considered it enough.

Real Life Example

Solzhenitsyn publishes Live Not by Lies on the day of his arrest
12 February 1974 · Moscow, Soviet Union
On 12 February 1974, the day KGB officers arrested him at his Moscow flat, Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn released his essay Live Not by Lies. It urged Soviet citizens not to attempt heroic protest but simply to refuse personal participation in lies, by not writing, signing, saying or voting for anything they did not believe. The next day he was stripped of his Soviet citizenship and deported to West Germany.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Solzhenitsyn argued that an ordinary person who did nothing else but steadfastly refuse to lie could help bring down a system that depended on lies.
The kural says that if one practises truthfulness without ever failing, it is well even if one does no other virtuous deed, which is almost exactly the programme Solzhenitsyn set out in his essay.
