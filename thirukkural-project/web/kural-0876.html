Kural 876

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

தேறினும் தேறா விடினும் அழிவின்கண் தேறான் பகாஅன் விடல்.
Therinum theraa vitinum azhivinkan theraan pakaaan vital.

Meaning

In a time of trouble, whether you have tested a person before or not, do not get too close to them and do not cut them off. Keep a careful distance.

Elementary

When something big goes wrong, it is not the time to suddenly make a new best friend or to start a new fight. The poet says: stay polite and keep a bit of distance, and wait until things are calm before deciding who to trust.

University

This is among the most compressed and debated couplets of the chapter. The verb tēṟu (தேறு, ‘to trust, be convinced about’) appears three times: tēṟiṉum tēṟā viṭiṉum (‘whether one has trusted him or not’), then tēṟāṉ (‘not trusting’) paired with pakāaṉ (பகாஅன், ‘not separating’, with the alapedai lengthening), ending in viṭal (‘leave him so’). Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as: whether you had earlier assessed a man or not, in a time of ruin neither trust him nor drop him; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar apply it specifically to an enemy, advising neither closeness nor open rupture; Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as ‘do not approach the untrustworthy in ruinous times’. Pope’s verse is loose, and his explanation ‘neither to join nor separate’ follows the traditional reading. The thirukkural.net English summary, ‘be wary of joining or opposing any’, captures the consensus: in crisis, keep a guarded neutrality. The text matches in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Before the Kurukshetra war, Balarama, bound by affection to both the Pandavas and Duryodhana, refused to fight for either side and left on pilgrimage along the Sarasvati.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva and Shalya Parva [3]
In the greatest crisis of his family, Balarama neither joined nor broke with either party.
It is a living example of the Kural’s guarded middle course in a time of ruin.
Christianity
“Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.”
— Matthew 10:16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus counsels his disciples to combine wariness with harmlessness in a hostile world.
The Kural’s advice is the same blend: neither trust blindly nor provoke a break.
Islam
“O you who have believed, take your precaution and [either] go forth in companies or go forth all together.”
— Qur’an 4:71 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an instructs believers facing danger to stay alert and take precautions.
The Kural similarly counsels caution rather than hasty trust in dangerous times.
Judaism
The Mishnah warns to be careful with the ruling powers, since they draw people close only for their own needs, seem like friends when it suits them, and do not stand by a person in the hour of distress.
— Pirkei Avot 2:3 [6]
Rabban Gamaliel’s teaching warns that apparent friends may vanish exactly when trouble comes.
This is why the Kural advises not committing to anyone untested in a time of ruin.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha describes false friends, including the one who is a companion only in pleasure and the one who helps only out of self-interest, and contrasts them with the friend who stands by you in misfortune.
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 [7]
The Buddha gives criteria for telling true friends from false ones.
The Kural assumes the same problem: in crisis one cannot yet know who is true, so one should stay guarded.
Jainism
“Benevolence (maitrī) towards all living-beings (sattva), joy (pramoda) at the sight of the virtuous (guṇādhika), compassion and sympathy (kāruṇya) for the afflicted (kliśyamāna), and tolerance (mādhyasthya) towards the insolent and ill-behaved (avineya) are the other observances.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.11, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Umāsvāti's sūtra prescribes mādhyasthya, a neutral stance toward the insolent. Pūjyapāda's commentary defines it as freedom from both attachment and revulsion.
The Kural advises keeping a careful middle distance in a crisis, neither committing to a doubtful person nor breaking with him, and Jain mādhyasthya is a stance of neither embrace nor rejection.
Sikhism
“One who is not affected by pleasure or pain, who looks upon friend and enemy alike - says Nanak, listen, mind: know that such a person is liberated.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 15), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the liberated person as one whose mind does not swing between friend and enemy.
The Kural advises keeping a careful middle distance in a crisis, neither committing to nor breaking with a person; Gurbani also holds friend and enemy in the same even regard, though for a different reason.
Greek Mythology
When the Trojans found the great wooden horse left by the Greeks, the priest Laocoön urged them not to trust it, saying he feared the Greeks even when they brought gifts.
— Virgil, Aeneid 2.40-56 [10]
At the decisive moment, an untested offer from a former enemy was the most dangerous thing in Troy.
The Kural warns against committing to the untested in a time of danger, which is what Troy failed to heed.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that the wish to be friends comes quickly but friendship does not; people cannot know each other until they have shared much over time.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.3 (1156b) [11]
Trust has to be tested by time and experience, not assumed.
The Kural applies this to crisis: trust that has not been tested should not be relied on when ruin looms.

Real Life Example

The first Non-Aligned summit meets in Belgrade
1-6 September 1961 · Belgrade, Yugoslavia (now Serbia)
In September 1961 leaders of about two dozen countries met in Belgrade for the first conference of the Non-Aligned Movement, founded by Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia, Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana and Sukarno of Indonesia. At the height of the Cold War, the member states declined to join either the American-led or the Soviet-led bloc. The conference adopted a declaration against colonialism and an appeal for peace addressed to both superpowers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Newly independent nations chose to keep a careful distance from both rival blocs, neither binding themselves to one nor making an open enemy of the other.
The kural counsels that in a time of crisis one should neither commit to nor break with either side, which is the stance non-alignment took during the Cold War.
