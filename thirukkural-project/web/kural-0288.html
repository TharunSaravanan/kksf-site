Kural 288

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

அளவறிந்தார் நெஞ்சத் தறம்போல நிற்கும் களவறிந்தார் நெஞ்சில் கரவு.
Alavarindhaar nenjath tharampola nirkum, Kalavarindhaar nenjil karavu.

Meaning

Goodness stays firmly in the hearts of people who know proper limits. In the same way, sneaky deceit stays in the hearts of people who know stealing.

Elementary

Your heart is like a room. If you practise being fair, fairness moves in and makes itself at home. If you practise cheating, sneakiness moves in instead — and whoever lives there decides how you act.

University

The couplet is built on precise parallelism — aḷavu aṟintār / kaḷavu aṟintār (“those who know measure / those who know fraud”) — with the verb niṟkum (“stands, abides”) serving both halves. The point is that virtue and deceit (karavu, “concealment”) are both settled dispositions formed by what one has come to “know” through practice. This is close to Aristotle’s claim that we become just or unjust through our dealings with others, and to the Gospel image of the heart as a treasury from which good or evil is brought out. It also explains 284 and 286: once fraud is ‘known’ in this habitual sense, concealment becomes the heart’s standing condition.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Among them, the open rogues (are those) who subsist by (cheating in the sale of) various marketable commodities, but the concealed rogues are burglars, robbers in forests, and so forth.”
— Manusmriti 9.257 (tr. G. Bühler) [5]
Manu classifies thieves the king must restrain and puts dishonest traders among them, treating fraud in the market as a settled way of life.
Manu’s ‘rogues who subsist by cheating’ are the Kural’s kaḷavu aṟintār, people for whom deceit has become a livelihood and a habit.
Christianity
“A good man out of the good treasure of the heart bringeth forth good things: and an evil man out of the evil treasure bringeth forth evil things.”
— Matthew 12:35 (KJV) [6]
Jesus describes the heart as a storehouse whose contents determine a person’s words and deeds.
Same two-sided structure as the Kural: virtue dwells in one kind of heart, deceit in the other.
Islam
“…and when a thief steals, then he is not a believer at the time of stealing…”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2475 [7]
The Prophet taught that faith and theft cannot occupy the heart at the same moment.
Like the Kural, it treats the heart as holding either faith/virtue or deceit, but not both together.
Judaism
“This is as Shmuel said: It is prohibited to deceive people, and even to deceive a gentile.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Chullin 94a (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [8]
The Talmud’s principle of geneivat da’at (‘theft of the mind’) treats creating false impressions as a form of theft, and lists many everyday examples.
The rabbis, like Valluvar, see deceit (karavu) as fraud’s inner form, forbidden even when no goods change hands.
Buddhism
“Think not lightly of evil, saying, ‘It will not come to me.’ Drop by drop is the water pot filled. Likewise, the fool, gathering it little by little, fills himself with evil.”
— Dhammapada 121 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Dhammapada says character fills up gradually, one small deed at a time.
Explains how kaḷavu aṟintār come to have deceit settled in the heart: little by little, through repeated practice.
Jainism
“When he is overcome by violent desire, takes another's property, and is not satisfied with those 'colours' and their possession, then his deceit and falsehood increase on account of his greed; yet he will not get rid of his misery.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
The Uttarādhyayana describes how one act of taking another's property leads to settled habits of deceit and falsehood, fed by greed.
The Kural says deceit lives steadily in the hearts of those who have learned fraud. The Jain verse shows how deceit grows and takes root in the one who takes what belongs to others.
Sikhism
“Those who have one thing in their heart, and something else in their mouth, are judged to be false.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In a hymn in Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid contrasts those whose love is true in the heart with those whose inner mind and outward words do not match.
The Kural says virtue abides in the hearts of those who know measure, while deceit abides in the hearts of those who have learned fraud. Farid likewise judges people by what is settled in the heart, not by what they show.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus’ maternal grandfather Autolycus is said to have surpassed all men in thievery and in the oath, a skill given to him by Hermes, whom he honoured with sacrifices.
— Homer, Odyssey 19.394-398 [12]
Homer presents skill in theft and tricky oaths as a settled excellence of character, almost a gift of the gods.
An example of the Kural’s kaḷavu aṟintār: for Autolycus deceit has become a stable second nature.
Philosophy
“by doing the acts that we do in our transactions with other men we become just or unjust”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) [13]
Aristotle teaches that moral character is formed by habit, and that justice or injustice is built up through how we deal with others.
The same two-sided account of habituation as the Kural: repeated honest dealing makes virtue settle in, and repeated fraud makes deceit settle in.

Real Life Example

Hwang Woo-suk's stem cell papers are found to be fabricated
December 2005 - January 2006 · Seoul National University, Seoul, South Korea
Korean scientist Hwang Woo-suk had published two celebrated papers in Science, in 2004 and 2005, claiming breakthroughs in cloned human embryonic stem cells. Investigations by Seoul National University found that a significant amount of the data in both papers had been fabricated, and Science retracted them. Hwang was dismissed from the university in March 2006.
— Sources: [14] [15]
The scandal revealed not a single lapse but deception repeated across separate papers, and it became one of the best-known cases of research fraud.
The kural says deceit settles permanently in the hearts of those who have learned fraud, and Hwang's fabrication recurred from one paper to the next.
