Kural 732

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

பெரும்பொருளால் பெட்டக்க தாகி அருங்கேட்டால் ஆற்ற விளைவது நாடு.
Perumporulaal pettakka thaaki arungettaal aatra vilaivadhu naadu.

Meaning

A true country is so rich that others want it, is free from terrible disasters, and grows plenty of crops.

Elementary

Imagine a garden so lovely and full of fruit that everyone in town wishes it were theirs. It is not flooded or eaten by pests, and every year it gives more than enough. That is what a good country is like.

University

Perumporul is ‘great wealth’, and pettakkadhu means ‘that which is desirable’; Parimelazhagar takes the desire to be that of other kingdoms, so the kural admits that a rich land will be coveted, which prepares for the next chapter on fortification (Aran). Arungettaal is read by Parimelazhagar as arum-kedu, ‘with destructive causes being absent’ (arumai here meaning scarcity or absence), and Solomon Pappaiah lists the traditional calamities meant: excessive rain, drought, wild beasts, harmful birds and misrule. Aatra vilaivadhu means ‘yielding abundantly’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read pettakkadhu more generally as ‘praised and desired by all’. The sandhi-joined text runs the words together as ‘பெட்டக்க தாகி யருங்கேட்டா லாற்ற’, with no change in meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him settle in a country which is open and has a dry climate, where grain is abundant, which is chiefly (inhabited) by Aryans, not subject to epidemic diseases (or similar troubles), and pleasant, where the vassals are obedient and his own (people easily) find their livelihood.”
— Manusmriti 7.69 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu tells the king what kind of country to make his home: one with plenty of grain, free from disease and other troubles, and pleasant to live in.
Manu’s abundant, pleasant and trouble-free country matches the Kural’s desirable, calamity-free land of great yield.
Christianity
“And Lot lifted up his eyes, and beheld all the plain of Jordan, that it was well watered every where, before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, even as the garden of the LORD.”
— Genesis 13:10 (KJV) [4]
When Abraham and Lot must separate, Lot looks at the fertile Jordan plain and chooses it because it is so rich and well watered.
The plain is the kind of land the Kural describes: so rich that others want it for themselves.
Islam
“And if only the people of the cities had believed and feared Allah, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth; but they denied [the messengers], so We seized them for what they were earning.”
— Qur’an 7:96 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says abundance from sky and earth would have come to towns that were faithful, while calamity came to those that were not.
The Kural’s land of great yield and no calamity is, in Qur’anic terms, a land on which blessings have been opened.
Judaism
“We came unto the land whither thou sentest us, and surely it floweth with milk and honey; and this is the fruit of it.”
— Numbers 13:27 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
The spies Moses sent into Canaan come back with huge clusters of fruit and report that the land is wonderfully rich.
Canaan is desired because of its fruit, which is what the Kural means by a land made desirable by its great wealth.
Buddhism
In the Mahasudassana Sutta the Buddha describes Kusavati, the royal city of an ancient righteous king, as rich, prosperous, crowded with people and well supplied with food, and never silent with the sounds of festivity.
— Digha Nikaya 17, Mahasudassana Sutta [7]
The Buddha tells Ananda that the small town where he is dying was once this splendid capital, and then teaches that all such splendour passes.
Kusavati is the Buddhist picture of the desirable land the Kural praises, full of wealth and food.
Jainism
“Knowing that the earth with its crops of rice and barley, with its gold and cattle, that all this put together will not satisfy one single man, one should practise austerities.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.49 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
King Nami, renouncing the kingdom of Mithila, answers Indra's call to enlarge his wealth by saying that even the whole earth's harvests and gold cannot satisfy one person's craving.
The Kural praises a land so rich that others desire it; the Jain answer is that such desire for land is limitless, so the right response to a rich country is restraint, not covetousness.
Sikhism
“The Creator has given me the nine treasures, wealth and spiritual powers, and I do not lack anything. Eating, spending and enjoying, I have found peace; the gifts of the Creator Lord continually increase. … Millions of obstacles have all been removed, and pain does not even approach me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 783-784, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a chhant celebrating the completion of the Amritsar pool, Guru Arjan describes a place overflowing with God's gifts, where abundance keeps growing and harm cannot come near.
The Kural's ideal land is rich, untouched by calamity and abundantly fruitful; Guru Arjan's hymn pictures the same unfailing plenty and freedom from trouble, received as divine grace.
Greek Mythology
“If they had had these they would have colonized the island, for it is a very good one, and would yield everything in due season.”
— Homer, Odyssey 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus describes a fertile island near the Cyclopes with meadows, deep soil and a safe harbour, which nobody works because the Cyclopes have no ships.
Homer’s island is the kind of land others would covet for its yield, which is how Parimelazhagar reads the Kural’s ‘desirable’.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates says that once a city wants luxuries beyond its needs, its land will no longer be enough, so it will have to cut off a slice of its neighbours’ land, and the neighbours will want a slice of its land too. That, he says, is the origin of war.
— Plato, Republic II, 373d-e (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato traces war back to one land coveting another land’s wealth.
This is the danger hidden in the Kural’s word ‘desirable’: a rich land draws envy, which is why the next chapter turns to fortresses.

Real Life Example

Augustus annexes Egypt and makes it Rome's granary
30 BCE onward · Egypt
After defeating Mark Antony and Cleopatra, Octavian, later called Augustus, arrived in Egypt in 30 BCE, ended the Ptolemaic monarchy and annexed the country as his personal domain, governed by a prefect of his choosing. Egypt's wealth was largely agricultural, and its grain supplied the city of Rome; its revenues were almost equal to those of Gaul. Historians count the addition of Egypt as a major supplier of the capital among Augustus's most important measures for Rome's food supply.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Egypt's dependable Nile harvests made it so valuable that Rome's ruler kept it under his own direct control.
The kural describes a true country as one so rich that others desire it, free of ruinous calamities and yielding abundant harvests, and Egypt was coveted and seized by Rome for exactly that wealth.
