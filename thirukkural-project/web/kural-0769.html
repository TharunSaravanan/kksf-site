Kural 769

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

சிறுமையும் செல்லாத் துனியும் வறுமையும் இல்லாயின் வெல்லும் படை.
Sirumaiyum sellaath thuniyum varumaiyum illaayin vellum padai.

Meaning

An army will win if its numbers are not shrinking, its soldiers do not hold a lasting grudge, and it is not short of supplies.

Elementary

A team does best when nobody keeps quitting, nobody is sulking and angry at the coach, and everyone has enough food and kit. Valluvar says an army is the same: keep it whole, happy and well supplied, and it will win.

University

Three defects are named: ciṟumai (சிறுமை, diminution, a shrinking of numbers or strength), cellāt tuṉi (செல்லாத் துனி, a resentment that will not go away, which Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar direct specifically against the king or commander), and vaṟumai (வறுமை, poverty, i.e. unpaid or ill-supplied troops). Solomon Pappaiah follows this reading, while Sivayogi Sivakumar renders ciṟumai as ‘confusion’, a looser gloss. The couplet’s realism about morale and pay matches Kautilya’s detailed discussion of an army’s ‘calamities’. Both sources agree (சந்தி: சிறுமையுஞ் ... வறுமையு / மில்லாயின்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya discusses the ‘calamities of the army’, among them troops that are unpaid, disaffected, reduced in numbers, or harbouring grievances against the king, and weighs which are hardest to remedy.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book VIII, Chapter 5 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
The treatise treats pay, morale and strength as conditions without which an army cannot fight well.
These are the very three defects, dwindling, discontent and want, that the Kural says an army must be free of.
Christianity
“Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house divided against itself shall not stand.”
— Matthew 12:25 (KJV) [4]
Jesus answers his critics with a proverb about internal division leading to ruin.
An army eaten by lasting resentment, as the Kural warns, is a house divided against itself.
Islam
“So if there are from you one hundred [who are] steadfast, they will overcome two hundred. And if there are among you a thousand, they will overcome two thousand by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the steadfast.”
— Qur’an 8:66 (Saheeh International) [5]
The verse acknowledges weakness among the believers and sets a realistic ratio for a steadfast force.
Like the Kural, it links victory to the inner condition of the troops rather than to numbers alone.
Judaism
“My father hath troubled the land; see, I pray you, how mine eyes are brightened, because I tasted a little of this honey. How much more, if haply the people had eaten freely to-day of the spoil of their enemies which they found? had there not been then a much greater slaughter among the Philistines?”
— 1 Samuel 14:29–30 (JPS 1917, Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Jonathan argues that Saul’s oath forbidding food left the troops faint and blunted their victory.
It shows the Kural’s point that an army in want cannot win as it should.
Buddhism
At Kosambi the monks fell into a bitter quarrel that would not be settled; the Buddha urged reconciliation, and when they would not listen he withdrew to dwell alone.
— Majjhima Nikaya 128, Upakkilesa Sutta (opening narrative) [7]
The episode shows how a community paralysed by lasting resentment loses its strength and purpose.
It is a monastic picture of the Kural’s cellāt tuṉi, the grievance that will not go away.
Jainism
“A houseless and poor monk who wanders from village to village may become tired of ascetic life: he should bear this trouble.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.14 (cf. 2.15) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Discontent (arati) is one of the twenty-two hardships, and the monk must overcome it and wander on 'perfectly passionless' (2.15).
The Kural names lasting discontent and want as things that ruin an army. The Jain text also treats discontent as a danger to be overcome, but it accepts poverty instead of curing it.
Sikhism
“I am so hungry, I cannot perform devotional worship. Here, Lord, take back Your mala.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 656, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In this frank hymn Kabir admits that real want undermines even devotion, and asks God for simple food, a bed and a quilt so that he can serve wholeheartedly.
The Kural says an army wins if it is free of want and lasting discontent; Kabir makes the same point about the devotee, whose service cannot last on an empty stomach.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Achilles, aggrieved at Agamemnon for taking his prize, withdraws himself and his Myrmidons from the fighting, and the Achaeans suffer heavy reverses until the quarrel is resolved.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 1 and 9 [10]
The epic is built on the cost of an unresolved grievance between a leader and his best warriors.
It is the classic illustration of the Kural’s cellāt tuṉi, lasting discontent with the commander.
Philosophy
“By Method and discipline are to be understood the marshalling of the army in its proper subdivisions, the gradations of rank among the officers, the maintenance of roads by which supplies may reach the army, and the control of military expenditure.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 1.10 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu counts supply lines and the control of military spending among the basic factors of war.
Keeping an army supplied and paid answers the Kural’s warning against vaṟumai.

Real Life Example

The Pennsylvania Line mutinies over pay and enlistments
1 January 1781 · Morristown and Princeton, New Jersey, United States
On the night of 1 January 1781 soldiers of the Pennsylvania Line in the Continental Army, long unpaid and poorly supplied and insisting their enlistments had expired, rose in mutiny at their winter camp near Morristown, and an officer was killed in the disorder. Some 1,500 men marched toward Princeton to press their demands on Congress, while rejecting British offers of pardon and back pay to change sides. The mutiny was settled by negotiation, with many men discharged and others furloughed.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Even loyal troops who refused to desert to the enemy could no longer function as an army once pay, supplies and goodwill toward their government had run out.
The kural says an army wins if it is free from dwindling numbers, lasting discontent and want, and the Pennsylvania Line shows what happens when discontent and poverty take hold.
