Kural 878

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

வகையறிந்து தற்செய்து தற்காப்ப மாயும் பகைவர்கண் பட்ட செருக்கு.
Vakaiyarindhu tharseydhu tharkaappa maayum pakaivarkan patta serukku.

Meaning

Know the right way to act, build up your strength, and protect yourself well. Then your enemies’ pride will fade away by itself.

Elementary

If a team keeps teasing yours, the best answer is not to shout back but to practise, get stronger, and play smart. When they see how ready you are, their boasting stops on its own.

University

Three participial steps lead to the result: vakai aṟintu (‘knowing the method’), taṟceytu (‘making oneself strong’), taṟkāppa (‘guarding oneself’); then māyum (‘will perish’) the cerukku (செருக்கு, ‘pride, arrogance, exultation’) that has arisen in the enemies. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read cerukku as the enemy’s pride, which collapses without a blow once one is prepared; Solomon Pappaiah adds that taṟkāppa includes guarding one’s secrets, linking back to 877; Pope’s explanation renders cerukku as the foes’ ‘joy’ and taṟceytu as ‘securing assistance’, a reading that ties the couplet to 875. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as knowing one’s qualifications and self-protection. The point is deterrence: preparation defeats an enemy’s confidence before any conflict. The text matches in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthashastra teaches that a king should first strengthen the constituent elements of his own state, such as treasury, army, fort and allies, since a well-fortified kingdom deters attack.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Books 6-7 [3]
Indian statecraft makes self-strengthening the foundation of security against enemies.
The Kural’s sequence of knowing the method, strengthening oneself, and self-protection follows the same logic.
Christianity
“Put on the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil.”
— Ephesians 6:11 (KJV) [4]
Paul urges believers to be fully equipped so that they can stand firm against their adversary.
Both texts teach that preparation and self-protection are what defeat an enemy’s schemes.
Islam
“And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allāh and your enemy...”
— Qur’an 8:60 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an commands believers to build up their strength so that enemies are deterred.
This is the Kural’s point exactly: preparation itself breaks the enemy’s confidence.
Judaism
“They which builded on the wall, and they that bare burdens, with those that laded, every one with one of his hands wrought in the work, and with the other hand held a weapon.”
— Nehemiah 4:17 (Tanakh, KJV translation); cf. 6:16 [4]
Mocked and threatened by Sanballat and his allies, Nehemiah’s builders worked and stood guard at once until the wall was finished, and their enemies lost heart.
They did what the Kural advises: knew what to do, made themselves strong, and guarded themselves, and the enemies’ arrogance collapsed.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada urges one to see the body as fragile as a clay pot, to make the mind secure like a fortified city, and to fight Mara with the weapon of wisdom.
— Dhammapada 40 [6]
The Buddha uses the image of a well-defended city for inner preparedness against the adversary.
The Kural’s self-fortification against outer foes has an inner counterpart here.
Jainism
“Making Faith his fortress, Penance and Self-control the bolt (of its gate), Patience its strong wall, so that guarded in three ways it is impregnable; making Zeal his bow, its string Carefulness in walking (iriyâ), and its top (where the string is fastened) Content, he should bend (this bow) with Truth, piercing with the arrow, Penance, (the foe's) mail, Karman--(in this way) a sage will be the victor in battle and get rid of the Samsâra.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.20–22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Asked by Indra to fortify his city, King Nami describes instead an inner fortress and an inner bow, built from the virtues, with which the sage defeats karma.
The Kural says to know the method, build up your strength and guard yourself so that the enemy's pride withers, and Nami's fortress-and-bow image gives the same three steps in Jain form, aimed against karma.
Sikhism
After the execution of Guru Arjan, Guru Hargobind trained in arms, built up a Sikh army and fortified his cities, built the Akal Takht at Amritsar in 1609 and the fort of Lohgarh, and later defeated Shah Jahan's forces four times.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Hargobind (paraphrase) [8]
Under pressure from Mughal power, the sixth Guru made his community strong and well defended, and is believed to have borne two swords at his succession for temporal (miri) and spiritual (piri) authority.
The Kural says to know the right method, build up your strength and guard yourself, and the enemy's arrogance will fade; Guru Hargobind's build-up of Sikh strength and defences shows that strategy at work.
Greek Mythology
Before facing Medusa, Perseus learned the way from the Graeae and the nymphs and equipped himself with winged sandals, a cap of invisibility, a pouch and a sickle, so that he could approach and escape safely.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.4.2 [9]
Perseus succeeded because he first learned the method and armed himself well.
This follows the Kural’s order: know the way, make yourself ready, protect yourself.
Philosophy
Sun Tzu teaches that skilled warriors of old first made themselves invincible and then waited for the enemy to become vulnerable; securing against defeat lies in one’s own hands.
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War, ch. 4 (tr. Lionel Giles) [10]
Strategy begins with one’s own preparation, not with attacking.
The Kural likewise places victory over an enemy’s arrogance in one’s own readiness.

Real Life Example

Meiji Japan ends the unequal treaties
1894-1911 · Tokyo, Japan, and London, United Kingdom
From the 1850s Western powers imposed treaties on Japan that gave their citizens extraterritorial legal privileges and fixed Japan's customs duties, and they refused revision until Japan's institutions matched Western models. The Meiji government built modern legal codes, a constitution, an army and a navy, and in 1894 Britain signed a treaty agreeing to end extraterritoriality, which was abolished in 1899. Japan regained full tariff autonomy in 1911.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Japan won equal treatment not by a single blow against the treaty powers but by patiently building its own strength until their claims of superiority could no longer be sustained.
The kural says that knowing the right method, making oneself strong and guarding oneself will make the enemy's arrogance fade by itself, which is how Japan ended the unequal treaties.
