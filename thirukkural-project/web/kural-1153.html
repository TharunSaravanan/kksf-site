Kural 1153

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

அரிதரோ தேற்றம் அறிவுடையார் கண்ணும் பிரிவோ ரிடத்துண்மை யான்.
Aridharo thaetram arivudaiyaar kannum pirivo ritaththunmai yaan.

Meaning

Even a wise and understanding lover leaves at some time. So it is hard to feel sure of anyone's promise never to leave.

Elementary

Suppose the kindest, smartest grown-up you know promises, ‘I’ll always be here.’ Then one day they have to go away for work. Next time someone promises you that, it is harder to believe, even if they mean it. The wife in this couplet feels the same: if even a wise, loving husband sometimes leaves, how can she trust any promise to stay?

University

Tēṟṟam (தேற்றம்) means ‘certainty, confidence’ and arito (அரிதரோ, with the emphatic particle aro) ‘is indeed hard’. Aṟivuṭaiyār kaṇṇum (அறிவுடையார் கண்ணும்), ‘even in those who have understanding’, refers to the husband, who knows how much his absence will hurt; pirivu ōr iṭattu uṇmaiyāṉ (பிரிவு ஓர் இடத்து உண்மையான்), ‘since separation exists at one time or another’, gives the reason. The printed form pirivō riṭattuṇmai is a sandhi run-together of pirivu ōr iṭattu uṇmai, and editions agree on the wording. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan take tēṟṟam to be her confidence in his ‘I will not leave’, while Solomon Pappaiah reads it as her inability to be sure of his love. Pope: ‘To trust henceforth is hard, if ever he depart, / E’en he, who knows his promise and my breaking heart.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Nala, a king famed for virtue who had won Damayanti and sworn devotion to her, abandons her while she sleeps in the forest after he loses his kingdom at dice.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase) [3]
In the Mahabharata’s Nala episode, even a good and wise husband, under the pressure of misfortune, leaves the wife who trusted him.
Nala is a clear example of the Kural’s fear: even an understanding husband may, at some point, part from his wife.
Christianity
“Peter said unto him, Though I should die with thee, yet will I not deny thee. Likewise also said all the disciples.”
— Matthew 26:35 (KJV) [4]
Peter and the other disciples sincerely swear never to forsake Jesus, and within hours they scatter and deny him.
As the Kural warns, even sincere and understanding people break their ‘I will never leave’, which makes such promises hard to rely on.
Islam
“Everyone upon it [i.e., the earth] will perish, And there will remain the Face of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor.”
— Qur’an 55:26-27 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that every created being passes away and only God endures.
If everything on earth is subject to passing, then, as the Kural concludes, no human promise of permanent presence can be fully trusted.
Judaism
The Mishnah teaches that any love that depends on some particular thing ceases when that thing ceases, while love that does not depend on anything never ceases; it contrasts Amnon’s love for Tamar with the love of David and Jonathan.
— Pirkei Avot 5:16 (paraphrase) [6]
Pirkei Avot distinguishes love that will not last from love that does, and suggests that it is hard to know from outside which is which.
The Kural’s heroine faces the same uncertainty: she cannot be sure whether her husband’s love will outlast the reasons that now call him away.
Buddhism
“‘I will grow different, separate from all that is dear and appealing to me.’”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.57, Upajjhatthana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha lists separation from all that is dear among five facts that every person, lay or ordained, should reflect on often.
This is the Kural’s premise stated as doctrine: separation comes to everyone, the wise included, so no promise can rule it out.
Jainism
“Time elapses and quickly pass the days; the pleasures of men are not permanent; they come to a man and leave him just as a bird leaves a tree void of fruit.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Brahmadatta admits to the monk Citra that worldly joys come and go and cannot be held on to.
The Kural’s heroine has learned the same thing about love: what comes to us also leaves, so no promise of permanence is certain.
Sikhism
“Union with Him, and separation from Him, come by His Will. We come to receive what is written in our destiny.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib (pauri 29), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Japji Guru Nanak teaches that meeting and parting alike come by the divine order (hukam).
The Kural's heroine finds it hard to trust any promise because parting comes in time even with a wise lover; the Japji agrees that separation is built into life, though it asks the Sikh to accept it as hukam rather than lose trust.
Greek Mythology
Abandoned by Theseus on the island of Naxos after he had sworn to marry her, Ariadne laments that from now on no woman should believe a man’s oath or trust that his words are faithful.
— Catullus, Poem 64 (Ariadne’s lament) (paraphrase) [10]
In Catullus’s retelling, Ariadne draws a general lesson about trust from one desertion.
She reaches the Kural’s conclusion: once even a trusted lover has left, confidence in any promise becomes hard.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that friendships based on pleasure or usefulness are easily dissolved, because when the pleasure or use ends, so does the bond.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.3 (paraphrase) [11]
Only friendship of character, he argues, is lasting; others depend on changing circumstances.
The Kural’s heroine has reason to doubt because circumstances, such as the need to earn, can pull even a loving partner away.

Real Life Example

Douglas Mawson misses his ship and spends an extra year in Antarctica
February 1913 to early 1914 · Cape Denison (Main Base), Commonwealth Bay, Antarctica, and Adelaide, Australia
Douglas Mawson led the Australasian Antarctic Expedition, leaving his fiancée, Francisca (Paquita) Delprat, behind in Australia. Returning alone from a sledging journey on which his two companions had died, he reached Main Base only about five hours after the relief ship Aurora had sailed, and he was forced to remain in Antarctica for another year. Soon after finally returning, he married Paquita on 31 March 1914.
— Sources: [12] [13]
However careful the plan, Mawson's promised return slipped by a whole year because of events no one could control.
The kural observes that even with a wise and loving partner separation comes at some time, so a promise of no parting or of a fixed return is hard to trust, and Mawson's lost year shows exactly how such a promise can fail.
