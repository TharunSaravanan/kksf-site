Kural 1128

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

நெஞ்சத்தார் காத லவராக வெய்துண்டல் அஞ்சுதும் வேபாக் கறிந்து.
Nenjaththaar kaadha lavaraaga veydhundal anjudhum vaepaak karindhu.

Meaning

Because my lover lives in my heart, I am afraid to eat anything hot, knowing it would burn him.

Elementary

Have you ever carried a baby bird very carefully so it wouldn't get hurt? The girl in this poem feels her love is living right inside her chest, so she won't even drink something hot in case it burns him.

University

The conceit moves from the eyes to the heart: nenjaththaar (நெஞ்சத்தார், 'he who is in my heart') is so literally present that veydhu undal (வெய்துண்டல், eating hot food) is feared (anjudhum, அஞ்சுதும்) because of vaepaakku (வேபாக்கு, his being scorched). The first person plural is the heroine's dignified 'we'. All four commentators on thirukkural.net and ytamizh.com agree on the sense; Kalaignar generalises it into a definition: true lovers are those so blended heart with heart that they fear hot food might burn the other. Pope: 'Within my heart my lover dwells; from food I turn / That smacks of heat, lest he should feel it burn.' Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O dearly beloved! Your lotus feet are so soft that we place them gently on our breasts, fearing that Your feet will be hurt.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.31.19 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, vedabase.io) [3]
In their song of separation the gopis tell Krishna they fear even their own bodies might hurt his tender feet, and worry about him on the stony forest paths.
Exactly as the Kural's heroine fears her own hot food might burn the lover within her, the gopis fear their own bodies might hurt their beloved.
Christianity
“That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith; that ye, being rooted and grounded in love,”
— Ephesians 3:17 (KJV) [4]
Paul prays that Christ will make his home in the believers' hearts.
The Kural's beloved also lives in the heroine's heart; Paul uses the same image of a dear one dwelling within.
Islam
“We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein”
— Qur'an 50:16 (Saheeh International) [5]
God declares that He is nearer to a person than the vein in his own neck.
The Kural's beloved is so near he is inside the heart; the Qur'an expresses a closeness even nearer than the body's own veins.
Judaism
The bride of the Song of Songs asks her beloved to set her as a seal upon his heart and upon his arm, for love is strong as death.
— Song of Songs 8:6 (paraphrase) [6]
The seal on the heart is the image of a beloved permanently imprinted within.
The Kural's heroine likewise carries her beloved inside her heart and guards him there.
Buddhism
“Even as a mother protects with her life / Her child, her only child, / So with a boundless heart / Should one cherish all living beings”
— Sutta Nipata 1.8, Karaniya Metta Sutta (tr. The Amaravati Sangha) [7]
The Metta Sutta takes a mother's protective love as the model for boundless kindness.
The Kural's heroine guards the beloved within her as anxiously as a mother guards her only child.
Jainism
The Acaranga Sutra teaches that fire-bodies, water-bodies and other tiny, unseen beings are alive, and that a careful person avoids harming them even in ordinary acts like cooking and eating.
— Acaranga Sutra I.1 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Jain ahimsa extends care to beings too small to be seen, shaping how one eats and drinks.
The Kural's heroine changes how she eats to avoid harming an unseen beloved; Jain practice changes how one eats to avoid harming unseen life.
Sikhism
“O Baba, the pleasures of other foods are false. Eating them, the body is ruined, and wickedness and corruption enter into the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 16, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak warns against foods eaten for pleasure that harm the body and disturb the mind, setting restraint in eating within a life centred on the Lord.
The Kural's heroine avoids hot food so as not to scorch the lover in her heart; Guru Nanak likewise guards what he eats for the sake of what dwells within the mind.
Greek Mythology
When Psyche secretly lights a lamp to look at her sleeping husband Cupid, a drop of hot oil falls on his shoulder and burns him, and he flies away.
— Apuleius, Metamorphoses (The Golden Ass) 5.22-24 (paraphrase) [10]
Psyche's moment of curiosity hurts the beloved she had never seen and costs her his presence.
The Kural's heroine fears exactly this: that something hot from her might burn the lover she carries.
Philosophy
“for he walks not upon the earth, nor yet upon skulls of men, which are not so very soft, but in the hearts and souls of both god, and men, which are of all things the softest: in them he walks and dwells and makes his home.”
— Plato, Symposium 195e, speech of Agathon (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
In Plato's Symposium, Agathon praises Love as the tenderest of gods, who lives only in soft and gentle hearts.
The Kural's lover also makes his home in the heart, and the heroine guards that tender place from anything that could burn him.

Real Life Example

Lady Dervorguilla keeps her husband's heart and founds Sweetheart Abbey
1268–1289 · New Abbey, Dumfries and Galloway, Scotland
When John Balliol died in 1268, his widow, Lady Dervorguilla of Galloway, had his heart embalmed and placed in a casket of ivory (one account says silver and ivory), which she kept with her. In 1273 she founded a Cistercian abbey in his memory, which the monks named Dulce Cor, Latin for sweet heart. When she died in 1289 she was buried before the abbey's high altar with her husband's heart.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The ruined abbey, still known as Sweetheart Abbey, remains one of Scotland's best-known monuments to married love.
The kural's heroine keeps her lover so truly within her heart that she fears hot food might burn him, and Dervorguilla kept her husband's heart close to her for more than twenty years and was buried holding it.
