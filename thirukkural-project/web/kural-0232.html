Kural 232

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

உரைப்பார் உரைப்பவை எல்லாம் இரப்பார்க்கொன்று ஈவார்மேல் நிற்கும் புகழ்.
Uraippaar uraippavai ellaam irappaarkku ondru Eevaarmel nirkum pukazh.

Meaning

All the praise that people speak comes to rest on those who give something to people who ask for help.

Elementary

When grown-ups tell stories about great people, the ones everyone loves to talk about are usually the helpers, the ones who shared. Kindness is what people remember and keep talking about.

University

உரைப்பார் உரைப்பவை, ‘what those who speak speak’, is read by Parimelazhagan as the praise uttered by poets and eulogists; the couplet claims that all of it ultimately ‘stands upon’ (மேல் நிற்கும்) givers. Renown is thus a social fact: it lives in the mouths of others and is conferred, not seized. The verb நிற்கும் (‘stands, abides’) implies durability; the praise settles and remains. Classical Tamil culture institutionalised exactly this in the bardic economy of Sangam patronage, where poets immortalised generous chieftains such as Pari in verse. The couplet therefore doubles as a sociology of reputation: fame follows the flow of gifts, a pattern echoed in the Buddha’s list of the visible fruits of generosity and in the Psalmist’s praise of the one who ‘hath given to the poor’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is for this, O king, that they who have great intelligence say that there is nothing higher and greater in the three worlds than gift!”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 199 (trans. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
The sage Markandeya, instructing Yudhishthira on charity, concludes that the wise unanimously declare giving to be the highest thing in all the worlds.
The Kural says all that speakers praise rests on givers; the Mahabharata likewise reports the verdict of the wise converging on the giver.
Christianity
“Wheresoever this gospel shall be preached in the whole world, there shall also this, that this woman hath done, be told for a memorial of her.”
— Matthew 26:13 (KJV) [4]
When a woman pours costly ointment on Jesus and is criticised for waste, he declares that her generous act will be told everywhere in her memory.
A single act of lavish giving becomes what people everywhere speak of, just as the Kural says all praise settles on the giver.
Islam
“You people are Allah's witnesses on earth.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1367 [5]
When a funeral passed and people praised the deceased, the Prophet said Paradise was affirmed for him, explaining that the community’s testimony about a person carries real weight.
Both texts treat what people say about someone as a meaningful verdict on that life; the Kural adds that such praise gathers around givers.
Judaism
“He hath dispersed, he hath given to the poor; his righteousness endureth for ever; his horn shall be exalted with honour.”
— Psalm 112:9 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
The Psalm describes the righteous person who distributes to the poor, promising that his righteousness lasts and his standing is raised in honour.
Giving to the poor leads to enduring honour: nearly the same chain of cause and effect as the Kural.
Buddhism
“The fine reputation of one who gives, who is a master of giving, is spread far & wide.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.34, Siha Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
Asked by General Siha for a fruit of generosity visible in this life, the Buddha lists, among others, that the generous person’s good reputation travels widely.
Almost a restatement of the Kural: renown is the visible, spoken-about fruit that settles on the giver.
Jainism
“In the sky sounded drums, intoxicating by their echoes, like bards proclaiming Śreyāṃsa’s happiness.” … The kings, townsmen, and the other people … said to Śreyāṃsa: “O Prince, you are fortunate, a crest-jewel of men, since you persuaded the Master to accept even a little cane-juice.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book I (Ādīśvaracaritra), ch. III, part 7 'The fast-breaking of Ṛṣabha' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [7]
When Prince Śreyāṃsa offered sugar-cane juice to break the first Tīrthaṅkara Ṛṣabha's year-long fast, gods and men together celebrated the giver. Jain tradition holds that the duty of giving on earth began with this act.
Valluvar says all the praise that speakers utter comes to rest on those who give; in this Jain story, the praise of gods, kings and townspeople likewise gathers on the giver Śreyāṃsa.
Sikhism
“DADDA: The One Lord is the Great Giver; He is the Giver to all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 257, Raag Gauree, Bavan Akhri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Bavan Akhri Guru Arjan teaches that there is only one true Giver, who provides for every being.
The Kural says all the praise of poets comes to rest on those who give; Guru Arjan directs that praise to the One who is the Giver of all, so the Sikh parallel moves the givers' glory to God.
Greek Mythology
“He that is righteous and deals righteously, the people tell of his praise among all lands, and many shall call him blessed.”
— Homer, Odyssey 19 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Penelope, preparing to honour a ragged stranger (the disguised Odysseus), reflects that the harsh are cursed while the righteous are praised everywhere.
Both texts describe praise spreading through people’s speech; Penelope says this while practising generous hospitality.
Philosophy
“The prize appointed for the noblest deeds; and this is honour; that is surely the greatest of external goods.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.3 (trans. W.D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle identifies honour as the greatest of the external goods, the recognition that is awarded for the finest actions.
Aristotle treats honour as conferred by others for noble deeds, just as the Kural says praise settles on those who give.

Real Life Example

Maecenas becomes the patron of Virgil and Horace
c. 38-8 BCE · Rome, Roman Republic and Empire
Gaius Maecenas, a wealthy adviser to Augustus who remained an equestrian and never held high office, supported a circle of poets that included Virgil and Horace. Virgil dedicated the Georgics to him, Horace dedicated several works to him, and Maecenas gave Horace an estate in the Sabine hills. His name became proverbial for a generous patron of the arts.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The praise of the greatest poets of the age settled on the man who supported them, so lastingly that his name became a common word for a patron.
The kural says that all the praise that poets utter comes to rest on those who give, and in Maecenas the poets' praise literally became the giver's name.
