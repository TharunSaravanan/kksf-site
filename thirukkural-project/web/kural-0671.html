Kural 671

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

சூழ்ச்சி முடிவு துணிவெய்தல் அத்துணிவு தாழ்ச்சியுள் தங்குதல் தீது.
Soozhchi mudivu thunivu eydhal aththunivu Thaazhchiyul thangudhal theedhu.

Meaning

The goal of thinking things over is to reach a firm decision. Once you have decided, letting that decision sit and wait is harmful.

Elementary

Imagine your team spends all of recess deciding which game to play, finally agrees on tag, and then just stands around until the bell rings. All that planning was wasted. This kural says: think carefully, decide, and then actually start.

University

The chapter opens by linking it to the preceding chapters on counsel (soozhchi, deliberation) and firmness in action (vinaith thitpam, ch. 67): the telos (mudivu) of counsel is thunivu, a settled resolve, not endless discussion. Thaazhchi means lowering, lagging or delay, and thangudhal 'to remain, to linger', so the second line pictures a resolve that has sunk into procrastination; theedhu (evil, harm) ranks such delay as a positive fault rather than mere inefficiency. Parimelazhagar glosses the kural as teaching that once the ministers' counsel has concluded in a decision, the king must not let time pass before execution, because delay lets circumstances and the enemy change. Older editions print the couplet with full sandhi (துணிவெய்த லத்துணிவு / தாழ்ச்சியுட் டங்குத றீது), as in thirukkural-thamizh.org, but the words are identical. The maxim closely matches the proverb Aristotle cites in Nicomachean Ethics VI.9: deliberate slowly, execute quickly.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Arjun said: O Infallible One, by Your grace my illusion has been dispelled, and I am situated in knowledge. I am now free from doubts, and I shall act according to Your instructions.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.73 [3]
After eighteen chapters of dialogue, Arjuna announces that his doubts are gone and he will now act, which ends the counsel and begins the battle.
The Gita's long deliberation ends exactly as the Kural prescribes: in a doubt-free resolve (thunivu) that is carried straight into action.
Christianity
“And Jesus said unto him, No man, having put his hand to the plough, and looking back, is fit for the kingdom of God.”
— Luke 9:61-62 (KJV) [4]
A would-be follower asks to delay his commitment to say goodbye at home, and Jesus answers that someone who starts ploughing and keeps looking back is not ready.
Both texts treat hesitation after a decision has been made as a real failure, not a harmless pause.
Islam
“So pardon them and ask forgiveness for them and consult them in the matter. And when you have decided, then rely upon Allāh.”
— Quran 3:159 (Sahih International) [5]
The Prophet is told to consult his companions, and once he has made up his mind, to put his trust in God and proceed.
This is the Kural's sequence exactly: consultation (soozhchi), then resolve (azm, thunivu), then action without wavering.
Judaism
“Say not: ‘when I shall have leisure I shall study;’ perhaps you will not have leisure.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:4 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Hillel warns against postponing a good resolution to some later, more convenient time, because that time may never come.
Like the Kural, Hillel treats a resolve that is left to wait for later as a resolve that will likely fail.
Buddhism
“Hasten to do good; restrain your mind from evil. He who is slow in doing good, his mind delights in evil.”
— Dhammapada 116 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha urges quick action on good intentions, because slowness gives the mind room to slide back into bad habits.
The Dhammapada and the Kural agree that delay after a good decision is not neutral; it actively opens the way to harm.
Jainism
“You have crossed the great ocean; why do you halt so near the shore? make haste to get on the other side; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 10.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira urges Gautama, who has already taken the decisive step of renunciation, not to linger at the last stage but to hasten to completion.
The kural's teaching that once a decision has been reached, delay is a harm is mirrored in this rebuke to halting when the goal is in reach.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, that which you have to do tomorrow - do it today instead; and that which you have to do now - do it immediately! Later on, you will not be able to do anything, when death hangs over your head.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Kabir (Salok 138), Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir, whose saloks the Gurus included in the scripture, warns that putting off what has been resolved is fatal, because the time to act can close without warning.
The Kural says that once a decision is reached, letting it sink into delay is a positive harm; Kabir likewise treats postponement of a settled duty as the real danger.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod tells his brother Perses not to put off work until tomorrow or the day after, because a man who delays never fills his barn and is always wrestling with ruin.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 410-413 [10]
In his farming poem Hesiod treats postponement as the main enemy of the careful farmer, who must act as soon as the work is due.
Hesiod's delaying farmer who wrestles with ruin is the Kural's theedhu: a decision left idle turns into damage.
Philosophy
“They say that one should carry out quickly the conclusions of one's deliberation, but should deliberate slowly.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.9 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle, discussing excellence in deliberation, cites the common saying that thinking should be slow but carrying out the result should be fast.
This is almost a Greek translation of Kural 671: counsel is meant to end in resolve, and resolve should not lag.

Real Life Example

Bell files his telephone patent hours before Elisha Gray
14 February 1876 · U.S. Patent Office, Washington, D.C., United States
On 14 February 1876 a patent application for Alexander Graham Bell's telephone was filed at the U.S. Patent Office. A few hours later the same day, the inventor Elisha Gray filed only a caveat, a declaration that he intended to patent a similar device within three months. Bell was granted U.S. Patent No. 174,465 on 7 March 1876, and in the legal disputes that followed, his earlier filing of a full application helped him prevail.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gray had worked out a comparable idea, but he had not yet acted on it fully, and a gap of a few hours meant that the patent, and the fame, went to Bell.
The kural says that once a decision is reached, letting it sit idle in delay is a harm, and Gray's choice to file a preliminary caveat rather than a full application lost him the race by a matter of hours.
