Kural 286

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

அளவின்கண் நின்றொழுகல் ஆற்றார் களவின்கண் கன்றிய காத லவர்.
Alavinkan nindrozhugal aatraar kalavinkan, Kandriya kaadha lavar.

Meaning

People with a deep-rooted desire for stealing cannot live within proper limits.

Elementary

A scale in a shop is supposed to measure fairly. Someone who loves cheating is like a scale with a broken spring — it can’t stay steady, and it keeps tipping the wrong way.

University

Aḷavu (“measure, limit”) is the key term of the second half of the chapter (286-289), and Valluvar plays on its near-rhyme with kaḷavu (“fraud, theft”). Commentators differ on what aḷavu means: Parimēlaḻakar takes it as the ascetic’s limited livelihood, Mu. Varadarasan as thrift or contentment, others as the rule of right conduct. The rhyme itself makes the point that fraud is a failure of measure, which is why honest weights and measures are the concrete test in Leviticus, Deuteronomy, the Qur’an and the Buddhist suttas. The couplet repeats kaṉṟiya kātal from 284, presenting fraud as a habit that eats away the ability to keep within limits.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who is regulated in his habits of eating, sleeping, recreation and work can mitigate all material pains by practicing the yoga system.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.17 [5]
Krishna teaches moderation (yukta) — a measured life — as the foundation of spiritual discipline.
The Gita’s ‘regulated’ person is the one who keeps within aḷavu; the Kural says the fraud-lover cannot.
Christianity
“But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition.”
— 1 Timothy 6:9 (KJV) [6]
Paul warns that a set determination to become rich traps people in desires that drag them beyond all restraint.
Like the Kural, it describes an entrenched desire for gain that destroys a person’s ability to live within bounds.
Islam
“Woe to those who give less [than due], Who, when they take a measure from people, take in full. But if they give by measure or by weight to them, they cause loss.”
— Qur’an 83:1-3 (Sahih International) [7]
Surat al-Mutaffifin condemns traders who demand full measure for themselves but short-change others.
The literal failure of measure (aḷavu) through fraud in weights, the concrete form of the Kural’s abstract point.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not have in thy bag diverse weights, a great and a small. Thou shalt not have in thy house diverse measures, a great and a small. A perfect and just weight shalt thou have; a perfect and just measure shalt thou have…”
— Deuteronomy 25:13-15 (JPS 1917) [8]
The Torah forbids even keeping two sets of weights, one for buying and one for selling.
The fraudster’s ‘great and small’ weights are exactly a refusal to stand by a single, fixed measure.
Buddhism
“He abstains from running messages… from buying and selling… from dealing with false scales, false metals, and false measures… from bribery, deception, and fraud.”
— Digha Nikaya 2, Samaññaphala Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [9]
Among the virtues of the monk, the Buddha lists refusal to use false scales and measures and refusal of deception and fraud.
Makes honest measure part of the definition of virtue, as the Kural does.
Jainism
“If there were numberless mountains of gold and silver, as big as Kailâsa, they would not satisfy a greedy man; for his avidity is boundless like space.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
King Nami, renouncing his kingdom, tells Indra that greed has no natural limit. No amount of wealth can satisfy it.
The Kural says those in whom the love of fraudulent gain has hardened cannot keep within measure (aḷavu). Nami's verse describes the same measureless quality of greed, “boundless like space.”
Sikhism
“Without contentment, no one is satisfied.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan describes a person who heaps up Maya and pleasures but never feels satisfied. He wears himself out chasing more, and only contentment (santokh) brings rest.
The Kural says those hardened in love of fraudulent gain cannot keep within measure. Guru Arjan names contentment as the missing measure, without which desire knows no limit.
Greek Mythology
Erysichthon cut down Ceres’ sacred oak for his own use. Ceres sent Famine to him, and he became insatiably hungry: he consumed all his wealth, repeatedly sold his daughter Mestra, and in the end devoured his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738-878 [12]
Someone who seized what was not his is punished with an appetite that has no limit.
An image of the Kural’s point that the fraud-lover cannot remain within any measure.
Philosophy
“…virtue must have the quality of aiming at the intermediate.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6 (tr. W. D. Ross) [13]
Aristotle defines moral virtue as hitting the mean between excess and deficiency.
Aristotle’s mean is a close counterpart to Valluvar’s aḷavu; the fraud-lover has lost the ability to aim at it.

Real Life Example

The Bre-X gold fraud collapses
1997 · Busang, East Kalimantan, Indonesia, and Calgary and Toronto, Canada
The Canadian company Bre-X Minerals claimed an enormous gold deposit at Busang in Indonesia, and its stock became a star of the Toronto Stock Exchange. In March 1997 tests by the mining company Freeport found insignificant amounts of gold, and an independent review by Strathcona Mineral Services concluded that the core samples had been tampered with by adding gold from elsewhere. Billions of dollars in market value vanished, Bre-X went bankrupt in November 1997, and no one was ever criminally convicted of the fraud.
— Sources: [14] [15]
Called the biggest fraud in mining history, Bre-X showed how a deception can keep inflating its claims far beyond any natural limit until it collapses.
The kural says those hardened in the love of fraud cannot keep their conduct within measure, and the Busang claims swelled without limit until they burst.
