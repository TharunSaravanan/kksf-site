Kural 355

Knowledge of the True · Meyyunarthal · மெய்யுணர்தல்

எப்பொருள் எத்தன்மைத் தாயினும் அப்பொருள் மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு.
Epporul eththanmaith thaayinum apporul Meypporul kaanpadhu arivu.

Meaning

Whatever a thing is, and however it looks, wisdom is seeing the real truth inside it.

Elementary

Two chocolates can look exactly the same, but one might have caramel inside and the other a nut. You only know by looking inside. This kural says a wise person doesn’t stop at how things look on the outside but finds out what they really are.

University

This is the chapter’s most quoted couplet, and it works as a definition: arivu (wisdom) is meypporul kaanpadhu, ‘seeing the true thing’ in every porul, whatever its apparent nature (eththanmaith thaayinum). The chain of repetitions (epporul, apporul, meypporul) performs the act it describes, peeling ‘thing’ back to ‘true thing’. Mu. Varadarasanar’s gloss makes the point explicit: one must not be dazzled by how a thing appears but know its real nature. Modern readers often extend it to critical thinking in general, meaning not being taken in by surface, reputation, or rhetoric. The ideal is close to the Buddhist yathabhuta-ñanadassana (‘knowing and seeing things as they really are’) and to the Greek project, from Heraclitus to Plato, of distinguishing a thing’s real nature (phusis) from how it seems (doxa).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The door of the True is covered with a golden disk. Open that, O Pûshan, that we may see the nature of the True.”
— Isha Upanishad 15 (trans. Max Müller) [3]
The seeker prays that the dazzling golden covering over reality be removed so that the truth behind it can be seen.
The Kural’s demand to look past a thing’s appearance to its ‘meypporul’ matches the prayer to remove the golden lid and see the True.
Christianity
“Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment.”
— John 7:24 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his critics not to judge by surface impressions but by what is truly right.
Same principle as the Kural: see through appearance to reality.
Islam
“But perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allāh knows, while you know not.”
— Qur’an 2:216 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an reminds believers that how something seems to them can be the opposite of what it really is; only God knows its true nature.
Both texts insist that a thing’s appearance can mislead and its true nature must be sought.
Judaism
“For the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart.”
— 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV) [4]
When Samuel is about to anoint the impressive-looking Eliab, God tells him that outward appearance is not the measure: God sees the inner reality.
The Kural asks human wisdom to see as God sees here, past the outward form to the true substance.
Buddhism
“‘All conditioned things are impermanent’ — when one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering. This is the path to purification.”
— Dhammapada 277 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that wisdom means seeing the real characteristics of things (impermanent, unsatisfactory, not-self) behind their seeming permanence. The tradition calls this yathabhuta, seeing things as they really are.
The Kural’s ‘meypporul kaanpadhu arivu’ is essentially the Buddhist definition of wisdom as seeing things as they truly are.
Jainism
“He who understands the true nature of all substances by means of all proofs (pramâṇa) and nayas, believes by a complete course of study.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 28.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 155) [7]
Jain epistemology examines every object through valid means of knowledge (pramāṇa) and partial standpoints (naya). No single appearance or angle is taken for the whole truth of a thing.
Valluvar defines wisdom as seeing the true reality in each thing, however it appears. The Jain method of pramāṇa and naya, the basis of anekāntavāda, is a disciplined way of getting past appearances to a thing's true nature.
Sikhism
“Wherever I look, there I see the Lord pervading.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 193, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes the vision of one who has been taught by the Guru: God is seen pervading everything.
The Kural defines wisdom as seeing the true reality within each thing, whatever it appears to be; Guru Arjan describes seeing the Lord within whatever he looks at.
Greek Mythology
To learn the truth, Menelaus must seize the shape-shifting sea-god Proteus and hold on while he turns into a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, running water and a tree. Only when Proteus returns to his own form does he speak truly.
— Homer, Odyssey 4.351–570 [9]
The truth is available only to the one who refuses to be fooled by changing appearances and holds on until the real form shows itself.
A perfect myth of the Kural’s teaching: whatever form a thing takes, wisdom grasps its true nature.
Philosophy
“For, though all things come to pass in accordance with this Word, men seem as if they had no experience of them, when they make trial of words and deeds such as I set forth, dividing each thing according to its kind and showing how it truly is.”
— Heraclitus, Fragment 1 (DK B1; trans. John Burnet) [10]
Heraclitus describes his philosophical task as showing ‘how each thing truly is’, something most people miss even though it is all around them. (He also says, in Fragment 123, that ‘Nature loves to hide.’)
Heraclitus’s aim of showing ‘how it truly is’ is the Kural’s ‘meypporul kaanpadhu’ almost word for word.

Real Life Example

Oskar Pfungst discovers the secret of Clever Hans
1904-1907 · Berlin, German Empire (now Germany)
Clever Hans, a horse owned by the Berlin schoolteacher Wilhelm von Osten, appeared to answer arithmetic and other questions by tapping his hoof, and in 1904 a group of experts who examined him found no deliberate trickery. The psychology student Oskar Pfungst then ran careful experiments and showed that the horse answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and was visible to him. In his 1907 report Pfungst concluded that Hans was reacting to tiny, unintentional movements of the questioner rather than doing sums.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Pfungst succeeded where expert observers had failed because he tested what was really happening instead of trusting how the performance looked; the Clever Hans effect is still taught as a warning in science.
The kural defines wisdom as seeing the true reality within a thing, whatever it appears to be, and Pfungst looked past the appearance of a counting horse to the real cause of its answers.
