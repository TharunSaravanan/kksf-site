Kural 383

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

தூங்காமை கல்வி துணிவுடைமை இம்மூன்றும் நீங்கா நிலனான் பவர்க்கு.
Thoongaamai kalvi thunivudaimai immoondrum Neengaa nilanaan bavarkku.

Meaning

Being alert and never slow, being learned, and being brave and firm: these three must never leave those who rule a land.

Elementary

A good lifeguard never falls asleep on duty, knows how to swim and rescue, and jumps in bravely when needed. A ruler needs the same three things: stay awake, keep learning, and be brave enough to act.

University

Thoongaamai literally means ‘not sleeping’, and the commentators read it as freedom from sloth and delay — prompt action when action is due. Kalvi (learning) and thunivu (decisive courage) complete the triad, and the verb neengaa (‘must not depart’) again stresses that these are permanent dispositions, not occasional performances. The combination is significant: learning without resolve produces a hesitant ruler, and resolve without learning produces a reckless one, while both fail without vigilance. Kautilya’s Arthashastra makes the same point in its timetable for the king, which divides the day and night into tasks and insists that the king ‘shall ever be wakeful’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“IF a king is energetic, his subjects will be equally energetic. If he is reckless, they will not only be reckless likewise, but also eat into his works. Besides, a reckless king will easily fall into the hands of his enemies. Hence the king shall ever be wakeful.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.19 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya says the people copy the king’s energy or laziness, so the king must always stay alert.
“Ever be wakeful” is almost a literal rendering of the Kural’s thoongaamai (sleeplessness/vigilance).
Christianity
“...he that giveth, let him do it with simplicity; he that ruleth, with diligence; he that sheweth mercy, with cheerfulness.”
— Romans 12:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the church that whoever has the gift of leading must lead diligently, not lazily.
Diligence in ruling parallels thoongaamai — the ruler’s refusal to be slothful.
Islam
The Prophet taught that the strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak one, and urged believers to strive eagerly for what benefits them, seek God’s help, and not give in to helplessness (Sahih Muslim 2664).
— Sahih Muslim 2664 (paraphrased) [5]
Muslims are told to be strong and keen, and never to give up or become passive.
Echoes the Kural’s pairing of unflagging effort with firm resolve.
Judaism
“And it shall be with him, and he shall read therein all the days of his life: that he may learn to fear the LORD his God, to keep all the words of this law and these statutes, to do them:”
— Deuteronomy 17:19 (KJV) [4]
The Torah requires Israel’s king to write his own copy of the law and study it every day of his life.
Makes lifelong learning (kalvi) a legal duty of kingship, as the Kural makes it a quality that must never leave the ruler.
Buddhism
“I am never content with exerting myself or with despatching business. Truly, I consider the welfare of all to be my duty, and the root of this is exertion and the prompt despatch of business.”
— Ashoka, Rock Edict VI (tr. S. Dhammika) [6]
The Buddhist emperor Ashoka says he never stops working hard and dealing quickly with the business of state, because the welfare of all depends on it.
“Prompt despatch of business” is exactly the no-delay alertness that thoongaamai demands.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Mahavira's sermon to his chief disciple Gautama repeats 'be careful all the while' (do not be heedless, pramada, even for a moment) as a refrain for all thirty-six verses. Sloth and delay are the danger he warns against most.
Valluvar's first requirement is thoongaamai, a wakefulness that never puts off what must be done. Mahavira's refrain is the Jain version of the same never-sleeping vigilance, though he aims it at the soul's liberation rather than at governing a land.
Sikhism
“Those who remain awake and aware are saved, while those who sleep are plundered.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 34, Siree Raag, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das warns that only those who stay alert are protected, while the careless sleeper is robbed of everything of value.
The Kural says sleepless vigilance must never leave those who rule; Guru Amar Das gives the same warning that whoever stops keeping watch loses what they hold.
Greek Mythology
“To sleep the whole night through beseemeth not a man that is a counsellor, to whom a host is entrusted, and upon whom rest so many cares.”
— Homer, Iliad 2.24-25 (tr. A. T. Murray) [9]
A dream sent by Zeus scolds King Agamemnon: a leader responsible for an army should not sleep all night.
A near-literal statement of thoongaamai — a ruler must not ‘sleep’ on his responsibilities.
Philosophy
“Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one... cities will never have rest from their evils.”
— Plato, Republic 5.473c-d (tr. Jowett) [10]
Plato argues that cities will suffer until rulers become truly learned and wise.
Supports the Kural’s insistence that kalvi (learning) must never depart from those who rule.

Real Life Example

Peter the Great works in foreign shipyards during the Grand Embassy
1697-1698 · Zaandam and Amsterdam, Dutch Republic; Deptford, England
In 1697 Tsar Peter I of Russia travelled west with a large diplomatic mission, the Grand Embassy, under the assumed name of Pyotr Mikhailov. In the Dutch Republic he studied shipbuilding and worked as a ship's carpenter in the Dutch East India Company's yard, and in England he worked at the Royal Navy dockyard at Deptford and visited factories, arsenals and other institutions. On his return he began sweeping reforms and built up a Russian navy.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Peter combined a tireless drive to act with a determination to learn practical skills himself, and then had the resolve to impose far-reaching changes on his country.
The kural says a ruler must never lose alertness, learning and firm resolve, and Peter's hands-on study abroad followed by bold reform displays all three.
