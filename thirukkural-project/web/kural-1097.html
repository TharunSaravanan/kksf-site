Kural 1097

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

செறாஅச் சிறுசொல்லும் செற்றார்போல் நோக்கும் உறாஅர்போன்று உற்றார் குறிப்பு.
Seraaach chirusollum setraarpol nokkum uraaarpondru utraar kurippu.

Meaning

Little harsh words that hold no anger, and looks like an enemy's, are signs of people who love each other while acting like strangers.

Elementary

Have you ever seen two best friends tease each other, saying ‘You’re so annoying!’ while grinning? Their pretend-grumpy faces are really a sign of how close they are. Sometimes people who like each other act as if they don’t.

University

The couplet balances ceṟāac ciṟucol (செறாஅச் சிறுசொல், ‘little words that are not angry’) against ceṟṟār pōl nōkku (செற்றார்போல் நோக்கு, ‘a look like that of enemies’), and names both the kuṟippu of uṟṟār (உற்றார், intimates, lovers) who behave uṟāar pōnṟu (like those not intimate). Mu. Varadarasanar reads ciṟucol as kaṭuñcol, harsh words that hold no hostility; Kalaignar speaks of lovers ‘acting’ like strangers outwardly while loving within; Solomon Pappaiah frames it as the hero now understanding her earlier coldness. The couplet completes the thought of 1096 by naming the signs. Pope: ‘The slighting words that anger feign, while eyes their love reveal, / Are signs of those that love, but would their love conceal.’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Shakuntala (to herself): “I would never leave him if I could help myself.” … Shakuntala (aloud): “I am not your servant any longer. I will go when I like.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
Shakuntala speaks sharply and threatens to leave, while privately admitting she cannot bear to go from the king’s presence.
Her cross words that hide affection are the Kural’s ‘little words without anger’ of a lover acting aloof.
Christianity
“For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth.”
— Hebrews 12:6 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews teaches that God’s correction, though painful, is a sign of his love.
Sternness that is really a sign of love is the Kural’s point, transposed from lovers to God and his children.
Islam
“And if We willed, We could show them to you, and you would know them by their mark; but you will surely know them by the tone of [their] speech.”
— Qur’an 47:30 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says the Prophet would recognise people’s true inner state from the tone of their speech, whatever they say outwardly.
The Kural too says the inner heart is read from tone and look rather than from the literal words.
Judaism
“And Joseph saw his brethren, and he knew them, but made himself strange unto them, and spoke roughly with them.”
— Genesis 42:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Joseph recognises his brothers but treats them like strangers and speaks harshly, though he loves them and later weeps over them.
Acting like a stranger and speaking roughly while loving within is exactly the Kural’s ‘intimates who seem like strangers’.
Buddhism
In the Abhayarajakumara Sutta the Buddha says he will speak even unwelcome and disagreeable words if they are true and beneficial, comparing himself to a nurse who pulls a dangerous object from a baby’s throat even if it draws blood, out of compassion.
— Majjhima Nikaya 58, Abhayarajakumara Sutta [7]
Harsh-sounding speech can come from compassion rather than ill will.
Like the Kural’s angerless sharp words, the Buddha’s hard words carry no hostility.
Jainism
“I am the daughter of the Bhoga-king, and you are an Andhakavrishni; being born in a noble family let us not become like Gandhana-snakes; firmly practise self-control!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.43 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati speaks sternly to Rathanemi, not from hatred but to recall him to his vows.
Her severe words without enmity resemble the Kural’s angerless sharp words, though their purpose is renunciation, not love.
Sikhism
“his father teaches him, and scolds him so many times, but still, he hugs him close in his embrace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 624, Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan compares God to a father whose child keeps making mistakes: he scolds the child many times, yet his scolding hides no anger, and he takes the child back into his arms.
The Kural reads sharp words that hold no anger as a sign of hidden love; Guru Arjan's father likewise speaks harshly while his heart stays loving.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus at last stands before Penelope, she treats him coolly as if he were a stranger, and Telemachus rebukes her hard heart; she answers that if he is truly Odysseus they will know each other by secret signs known only to the two of them.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.85-110 [10]
Penelope’s apparent coldness hides a deep love that is waiting for its sign.
A beloved who acts like a stranger while loving within is the Kural’s exact picture.
Philosophy
Plutarch argues that a true friend’s frankness may sting like medicine while a flatterer’s sweetness harms, so the harshness of a friend should be read by its intent, not its surface.
— Plutarch, Moralia, How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend [11]
Plutarch teaches how to tell real affection behind sharp words from false affection behind sweet ones.
The Kural similarly says that sharp words without anger are a sign of real closeness.

Real Life Example

John Adams lists Abigail Smith's faults
7 May 1764 · Braintree and Weymouth, Massachusetts, British America
During their courtship, John Adams, signing himself Lysander, wrote to Abigail Smith listing her supposed faults in a mock-serious voice: she did not play cards, had never learned to sing, sat with her legs crossed, and walked with her toes turned in. Abigail, signing herself Diana, replied two days later in the same teasing spirit, accepting some faults and rejecting others. They married in October 1764.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The exchange shows the playful affection that ran through one of the most famous marriages in American history.
The kural says small sharp words with no anger in them are a sign of hidden love; John's list of faults and Abigail's witty reply were exactly that.
