Kural 24

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

உரனென்னும் தோட்டியான் ஓரைந்தும் காப்பான் வரனென்னும் வைப்பிற்கோர் வித்து.
Uranennum thottiyaan oraindhum kaappaan, Varanennum vaippirkor viththu.

Meaning

A person who controls the five senses with a strong mind, the way a hook controls an elephant, is a seed for the best world of all, freedom from being born again.

Elementary

A person riding a huge elephant carries a small hooked stick, and with that one little stick they can steer an animal far stronger than they are. This kural says your five senses are like five elephants, and a steady mind is the little hook that steers them — and steering them is how something wonderful gets planted.

University

The image is precise and technical: “thotti” is the ankusa, the mahout's hooked goad, and the “oraindhu,” the five, are the sense-faculties imagined as five rutting elephants. Against their bulk Valluvar sets “uran,” mental firmness or resolve — the smallest instrument mastering the largest force, which is the whole argument of the verse in one picture. The payoff clause is theologically loaded: “varan” (from Sanskrit vara, the choicest) names the supreme estate, and “vaippu” is a deposit or treasure-store, so the ascetic becomes “or viththu,” a single seed, for that store. The seed metaphor matters — the sense-controller is not said to have arrived, but to be the germ from which the highest state grows, which keeps the emphasis on practice rather than attainment. The Tamil Shaiva tradition heard this verse as close kin to Tirumular's Tirumandiram, which likewise sets the goad of knowledge against the elephants of the senses.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Know the Self to be sitting in the chariot, the body to be the chariot, the intellect (buddhi) the charioteer, and the mind the reins. The senses they call the horses, the objects of the senses their roads.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.3-4 (trans. Max Müller, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15) [4]
The Katha Upanishad's chariot allegory assigns each part of the person a role in a single vehicle, and makes everything depend on whether the driver holds the reins firmly enough to control the animals.
The same claim with a different draught animal: a small instrument of resolve governing the powerful senses is what carries a person to the highest place.
Christianity
“But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:27 (KJV) [5]
Paul borrows the vocabulary of athletic training to describe a deliberate, forcible mastery of his own appetites, undertaken because he does not consider himself exempt from failing.
Like the Kural, it treats bodily self-restraint not as an optional austerity but as the very condition of reaching the prize.
Islam
“But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge.”
— Qur'an 79:40-41, An-Nazi'at (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an sets out a two-step condition — reverent awareness, then active restraint of the soul's appetite — and attaches Paradise directly to it.
This is the Kural's causal chain exactly: restraint of appetite now is what makes the highest estate one's destination.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city’ (Proverbs 16:32).”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Ben Zoma redefines strength itself: real power is not what a person can do to the world but what a person can do to the yetzer, the appetitive drive inside.
Both texts locate the decisive strength in the small inner instrument that governs appetite, not in outward force.
Buddhism
“Formerly this mind wandered about as it liked, where it wished and according to its pleasure, but now I shall thoroughly master it with wisdom as a mahout controls with his ankus an elephant in rut.”
— Dhammapada 326, Nagavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's Elephant chapter pictures the untrained mind as a musth elephant and wisdom as the mahout's hooked goad that brings it under control.
The Kural's exact image — goad, rutting elephant, faculty of restraint — appears here in an independent tradition.
Jainism
“Having heard these well-spoken words of the virtuous lady, he returned to the Law like an elephant driven by the hook. Protected in thoughts, words, and acts, subduing his senses and keeping the vows, he practised true Sramanahood throughout life.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.46-48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895); vv. 42-46 recur in Dasavaikalika Sutra 2 [9]
The monk Rathanemi, overcome by desire on seeing the nun Rajimati, is brought back by her firm rebuke as a mahout's hook brings back an elephant. He then masters his senses, and the lecture says both of them became omniscient (kevalins) and reached perfection.
This is the Kural's own image almost word for word: the goad (thotti, the ankusa) of firm resolve turns the elephant of the senses back, and the person so controlled becomes the seed of the highest estate, which in this story ends in liberation.
Sikhism
“The mind is the elephant, the Guru is the elephant-driver, and knowledge is the whip. Wherever the Guru drives the mind, it goes. O Nanak! Without the whip, the elephant wanders into the wilderness, again and again.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 516, Raag Gujri, Gujri ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das uses the mahout's picture: the mind is a powerful elephant, steered by the Guru with the goad of knowledge.
This is almost the Kural's own image: an elephant held in check by a small goad as the way to the highest state.
Greek Mythology
Warned by Circe, Odysseus has his crew stop their ears with wax and orders them to lash him upright to the mast and to tighten the ropes if he begs to be freed, so that he can hear the Sirens' song and survive it.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.165-200 [11]
Odysseus does not try to resist the pull of sensory pleasure by willpower in the moment; he binds his future self in advance, using a deliberate physical restraint as a substitute for restraint of desire.
It is the Greek world's clearest picture of the Kural's claim that a deliberate instrument of restraint applied to the senses is what saves a person.
Philosophy
“The human charioteer drives his in a pair; and one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed; and the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him.”
— Plato, Phaedrus 246a-b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
In Plato's allegory the soul is a chariot whose driver, reason, must control an obedient horse and an unruly appetitive one if the soul is to rise toward the truth.
Same claim as the Kural: the ascent to the highest state depends on a governing faculty keeping the appetitive powers in hand.

Real Life Example

Eric Liddell declines to run on Sunday at the Paris Olympics
1924 · Paris, France
When the programme for the 1924 Paris Olympics was issued, the Scottish sprinter Eric Liddell learned that the heats of the 100 metres, his strongest event, would be run on a Sunday. As a devout Christian who would not compete on the Sabbath, he withdrew and trained instead for the 200 and 400 metres. He won bronze in the 200 metres and gold in the 400 metres in an Olympic record time of 47.6 seconds.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Liddell gave up his best chance of glory because of a principle he had settled in advance, and still triumphed in an event that was not his speciality.
The kural praises the one who controls the five senses with the goad of firm resolve, and Liddell's steady resolve held back the strongest desire an athlete has, the desire to win in his best event.
