Kural 405

Ignorance · Kallaamai · கல்லாமை

கல்லா ஒருவன் தகைமை தலைப்பெய்து சொல்லாடச் சோர்வு படும்.
Kallaa oruvan thagaimai thalaippeydhu Sollaadach chorvu padum.

Meaning

An uneducated person’s high opinion of themselves fades away as soon as they meet and talk with learned people.

Elementary

Someone might think they’re very smart — until they start talking with people who have actually studied. Then, very quickly, it becomes clear how little they know, and their proud image falls apart.

University

Thagaimai is estimation or worth — here the self-estimation (or reputation) that the unlearned man carries; thalaippeydhu means ‘meeting, coming face to face’ (with the learned), and chorvu padum ‘will droop, fail’. The kural completes the logic of 402–403: the man who does not keep silent will be exposed in conversation. This is the drama of the Socratic elenchus, where confident interlocutors are shown in dialogue to lack what they claimed; Plato’s Phaedrus names the result ‘the show of wisdom without the reality’. The Mundaka and Katha Upanishads describe fools ‘wise in their own conceit’, and 1 Corinthians 8:2 compresses the diagnosis into a sentence. Dhammapada 63’s ‘fool who thinks himself wise’ is exactly the figure whose pride collapses here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Mundaka Upanishad describes fools who live in ignorance yet consider themselves wise and learned; they wander about, afflicted, like the blind led by the blind.
— Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.8 [4]
People who believe themselves learned while remaining ignorant stumble from one error to another.
Both portray the false self-estimation of the unlearned, which cannot survive contact with reality.
Christianity
“And if any man think that he knoweth any thing, he knoweth nothing yet as he ought to know.”
— 1 Corinthians 8:2 (KJV) [5]
Paul warns that the person who is sure of his own knowledge has not yet begun to know as he should.
The Kural’s unlearned man thinks highly of himself; Paul explains why that confidence is empty.
Islam
“Here you are - those who have argued about that of which you have [some] knowledge, but why do you argue about that of which you have no knowledge? And Allah knows, while you know not.”
— Qur’an 3:66 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an rebukes people who push their arguments into areas where they have no knowledge at all.
Both picture the unlearned entering debate and being shown up for lacking the knowledge they pretend to.
Judaism
Pirkei Avot lists seven marks of the wise person — among them, he does not speak before one greater in wisdom, does not interrupt, is not hasty to answer, and admits when he has not heard something — while the unformed person (golem) does the opposite.
— Pirkei Avot 5:7 [7]
The Mishnah contrasts how the wise and the unformed behave in conversation with the learned.
The Kural’s unlearned man behaves like the golem and is exposed in the very kind of conversation the Mishnah describes.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The worst kind of fool is the one who believes he is wise.
That self-believing fool is exactly the man whose esteem collapses when he talks with the learned.
Jainism
Indrabhuti Gautama, a renowned Vedic scholar with 500 disciples, went with bruised pride to debate Mahavira. Mahavira greeted him by name and answered his unspoken doubt about the existence of the soul. Indrabhuti's pride gave way, and he became Mahavira's first and chief disciple, Gautamaswami.
— Jain narrative tradition of Indrabhuti Gautama's conversion, as retold in 'Gautamswami' (jainworld.com Jain stories) [9]
Jain tradition remembers the chief ganadhara's conversion as the moment a proud man's self-esteem melted in the presence of one who truly knew.
Valluvar says an unlearned man's self-esteem withers once he talks with the learned. Indrabhuti's pride likewise dissolved the moment he spoke with Mahavira.
Sikhism
“In the Company of the Holy, egotism is eliminated.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 271, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan lists the fruits of the Saadh Sangat and says pride dissolves in the company of the holy.
The Kural says the unlearned man's self-esteem withers when he meets the learned; Guru Arjan says self-importance is undone in the company of the truly wise and holy.
Greek Mythology
When King Midas, judging a music contest, loudly declared Pan’s rustic piping superior to Apollo’s lyre, Apollo gave him the ears of an ass to show the quality of his judgment.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.146–193 [11]
Midas spoke up confidently among those who truly knew music, and his lack of understanding was made visible to all.
Like the Kural’s unlearned man, Midas’s standing collapses the moment he offers his opinion in a company of experts.
Philosophy
“They will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing; they will be tiresome company, having the show of wisdom without the reality.”
— Plato, Phaedrus 275a–b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
King Thamus warns that writing will let people seem knowledgeable without real understanding.
‘The show of wisdom without the reality’ is exactly the unlearned man’s self-image that wilts in real conversation — which, for Plato, is where real knowledge is tested.

Real Life Example

A royal commission tests Mesmer's animal magnetism
1784 · Paris, France
Franz Anton Mesmer, a physician who had moved his practice from Vienna to Paris, drew crowds of patients with treatments based on an invisible fluid he called animal magnetism. In 1784 King Louis XVI appointed a commission whose members included Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier; after tests that included what is probably the first blind trial, it reported that the effects came from the patients' imagination and not from any magnetic fluid. Some 20,000 copies of the report were published, the mesmerist movement declined, and Mesmer left Paris.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The investigation is remembered as a landmark in testing medical claims, and it showed how quickly a fashionable reputation could collapse once examined by the learned.
The kural says the self-esteem of the unlearned withers as soon as they meet the learned, and Mesmer's celebrated standing faded once Franklin, Lavoisier and their colleagues examined his claims.
