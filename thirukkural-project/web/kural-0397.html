Kural 397

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

யாதானும் நாடாமால் ஊராமால் என்னொருவன் சாந்துணையுங் கல்லாத வாறு.
Yaadhaanum naadaamaal ooraamaal ennoruvan Saanthunaiyum kallaadha vaaru.

Meaning

For a learned person, every country and every town becomes their own. So why does anyone stay without learning right up to the day they die?

Elementary

A good doctor or teacher can move to any town and still be welcomed and needed. What you learn goes with you everywhere and makes you at home anywhere. So why would anyone ever stop learning?

University

The kural is built as a rhetorical question. Its premise is that for the learned, யாதானும் நாடாம், ஊராம் (any land is their land, any town is their town), because learning makes a person welcome and useful anywhere. Its conclusion asks why anyone would remain unlearned சாந்துணையும், 'even until death'. That phrase quietly adds the idea of lifelong learning: there is no age at which study becomes pointless. In the Porul section's concern with the practical world, this is an argument from utility. Land and local status can be lost, but learning is portable citizenship. The first idea matches the Sanskrit maxim that a scholar is honoured everywhere, and the second matches Solon's line about growing old while always learning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A well-known Sanskrit subhashita states: “svadeśe pūjyate rājā, vidvān sarvatra pūjyate”. A king is honoured in his own land, but a learned person is honoured everywhere.
— Sanskrit subhashita (traditionally included in Chanakya Niti collections; attribution varies) [3]
The proverb contrasts the local, limited honour of political power with the universal honour of learning.
A near-perfect match to the Kural's first half: the learned person is at home and respected in every land and town.
Christianity
“As for these four children, God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom: and Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams.”
— Daniel 1:17 (KJV) [4]
Daniel and his friends, exiles in a foreign empire, rise to high honour in the Babylonian court purely because of their learning and wisdom.
A narrative example of the Kural's claim: through learning, the exile makes a foreign land his own.
Islam
“[Joseph] said, “Appoint me over the storehouses of the land. Indeed, I will be a knowing guardian.”
— Qur'an 12:55 (Sahih International) [5]
Joseph (Yusuf), sold as a slave into Egypt, becomes the trusted steward of a foreign kingdom because of his knowledge.
Like the Kural, it shows knowledge turning a stranger's land into the place where he is honoured and entrusted.
Judaism
“Say not: ‘when I shall have leisure I shall study;’ perhaps you will not have leisure.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:4 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Hillel warns against putting off study to a later time that may never come, since life is uncertain.
This parallels the Kural's second half, its challenge to anyone who stays unlearned until death: do not postpone learning.
Buddhism
“Better it is to live one day wise and meditative than to live a hundred years foolish and uncontrolled.”
— Dhammapada 111 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that a long life without wisdom is worth less than a single day lived wisely.
Like the Kural's rebuke of those who stay unlearned until death, it counts a lifetime without wisdom as wasted.
Jainism
“The enlightened and liberated monk should control himself, whether he be in a village or a town, and he should preach to all the road of peace; Gautama, &c.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain monk is homeless and keeps moving. Wherever his wandering takes him, village or town, he lives by the same discipline and shares his learning with everyone, and the refrain urges him to stay vigilant all his life.
Valluvar says every land and every town belongs to the learned, so no one should stop learning until death. The Jain ideal of the wandering learned monk, at home and of use in any village or town, matches the first half of the kural.
Sikhism
“No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that since he found the company of the holy, no one seems an enemy or a stranger to him and he is at home with all.
The Kural says that to the learned every land and every town becomes their own; Guru Arjan describes the same freedom from foreignness, in which no one is a stranger.
Greek Mythology
The Odyssey opens by describing its hero as a man who saw the cities of many peoples and learned their minds.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.1–4 [10]
Odysseus' defining trait is knowledge gained through wandering. It lets him survive, and even win honour, in one foreign land after another.
Like the Kural's learned person, Odysseus becomes a citizen of many cities through what he knows.
Philosophy
Solon, the Athenian lawgiver, is reported in Plato's Laches as saying that he grew old always learning many things. The Cynic Diogenes, asked where he came from, called himself 'a citizen of the world' (kosmopolitēs).
— Plato, Laches 188b (Solon); Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.63 (Diogenes) [11]
Solon models lifelong learning right up to old age. Diogenes models the thinker whose home is the whole world rather than one city.
Together they cover both halves of the Kural: every land is the learned person's home, and learning should never stop until death.

Real Life Example

Al-Idrisi makes his home at the court of Roger II of Sicily
c. 1145-1154 · Palermo, Kingdom of Sicily (now Italy)
Muhammad al-Idrisi, a Muslim scholar born in Ceuta in North Africa and educated in Cordoba, entered the service of the Norman Christian king Roger II of Sicily around 1145. He lived and worked at the court in Palermo for the rest of his life, gathering reports from travellers and earlier geographers. Shortly before Roger's death in 1154 he completed a world map with an accompanying geography, known in Arabic as the Book of Roger.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Al-Idrisi's learning made him welcome and honoured in a foreign kingdom of a different faith, where he produced one of the great geographical works of the Middle Ages.
The kural says every land becomes the learned person's own land, and al-Idrisi found a lasting home at a foreign court because of what he knew.
