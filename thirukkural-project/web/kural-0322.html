Kural 322

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

பகுத்துண்டு பல்லுயிர் ஓம்புதல் நூலோர் தொகுத்தவற்றுள் எல்லாந் தலை.
Pakuththundu palluyir oampudhal nooloar Thokuththavatrul ellaam thalai.

Meaning

Sharing your food with others and protecting all kinds of living things is the greatest of all the good deeds that writers of books on right living have listed.

Elementary

If you have a lunchbox and a hungry friend, you split your sandwich. If you see a bird with a hurt wing, you help it. Valluvar says that sharing and taking care of every living thing is the number-one rule in all the good books ever written.

University

The couplet fuses two duties — sharing food (pakuttuṇṭal) and protecting the manifold life of creatures (palluyir ōmputal) — and ranks them as “the head” (talai) of everything the authors of moral treatises (nūlōr) have compiled. Its placement in the chapter on non-killing shows that for Valluvar non-violence is not merely a prohibition but has a positive face: actively sustaining life. The appeal to the collected wisdom of the nūlōr is also a characteristic move, presenting the Kural as a distillation of an entire ethical literature. The pairing of generosity and care for life anticipates modern ethics of care that see nurture, not only abstention from harm, as the core of morality.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The devotees of the Lord are released from all kinds of sins because they eat food which is offered first for sacrifice. Others, who prepare food for personal sense enjoyment, verily eat only sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.13 (As It Is) [4]
Krishna teaches that food must first be offered and shared within the cycle of sacrifice; eating only for oneself is itself sinful.
Matches the Kural’s first half — sharing food (pakuttuṇṭal) is a central religious duty.
Christianity
“He that hath two coats, let him impart to him that hath none; and he that hath meat, let him do likewise.”
— Luke 3:11 (KJV) [5]
John the Baptist tells the crowds that true repentance means sharing clothing and food with those who lack them.
Same instruction as the Kural’s pakuttuṇṭal — divide what you have to eat with those in need.
Islam
“And they give food in spite of love for it to the needy, the orphan, and the captive,”
— Qur’an 76:8 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an describes the righteous as those who give away food they themselves desire, seeking only God’s face.
Both texts rank sharing food with the vulnerable among the highest virtues.
Judaism
“That which is hateful to you do not do to another; that is the entire Torah, and the rest is its interpretation. Go study.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [7]
Hillel summarizes the whole Torah for a would-be convert in a single ethical principle.
Like Valluvar ranking one virtue as the head of all that the learned have compiled, Hillel names one principle as the sum of all scripture.
Buddhism
“If beings knew, as I know, the results of giving & sharing, they would not eat without having given, nor would the stain of selfishness overcome their minds.”
— Itivuttaka 26 (Thanissaro Bhikkhu trans.) [8]
The Buddha teaches that even one’s last mouthful should be shared if there is someone to receive it.
Almost a direct echo of pakuttuṇṭal — eating only after sharing.
Jainism
Jain householder ethics joins sharing food with protecting life: the vow of sharing with guests (atithi-saṃvibhāga) is one of the householder's supplementary vows, and the classical Jain list of gifts places the gift of food (āhāra-dāna) alongside the gift of fearlessness or protection of life (abhaya-dāna).
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.21 (Digambara numbering; atithi-saṃvibhāga vow), with the traditional fourfold dāna scheme in later Jain śrāvakācāra literature [9]
For the Jain layperson, generosity with food and active protection of living beings are the positive counterparts of the vow of non-violence.
The Kural ranks sharing one's food and protecting many lives as the chief of all virtues; Jain lay ethics pairs exactly these two, food-giving and life-protecting, as core duties.
Sikhism
Every gurdwara keeps a langar, a free communal kitchen where food is served to all who come, seated together in a line so that no one claims higher or lower status. Britannica notes that under Guru Amar Das visitors ate in the casteless langar, and that the langar originated as a protest against the caste system.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'langar' (with linked coverage in 'Guru Amar Das' and 'Sikhism: The rejection of caste') (paraphrase) [10]
The langar puts vand chhakna, sharing what one has, at the centre of Sikh worship: no one who comes is turned away hungry.
The Kural ranks sharing one's food first among the virtues the learned have gathered; the Sikh langar makes that sharing a permanent institution open to all.
Greek Mythology
Triptolemus, who received grain from Demeter and spread agriculture to humankind, was credited with three laws: honour your parents, honour the gods with fruits, and spare animals.
— Porphyry, On Abstinence 4.22 (citing Xenocrates) [11]
Porphyry reports this Eleusinian tradition, linking the gift of shared grain with a command not to harm animals.
Combines the Kural’s two themes — sharing food and protecting living creatures — in a single mythic lawgiver.
Philosophy
Plutarch, in On the Eating of Flesh, protests that for the sake of a little flesh humans deprive animals of the sun, of light, and of the span of life that is theirs by nature.
— Plutarch, Moralia, De esu carnium I.4 (994E) [12]
Plutarch argues that sustaining our own lives does not justify cutting short the lives of other creatures.
Supports the Kural’s palluyir ōmputal — the duty to preserve the many lives around us.

Real Life Example

The Oxford Committee for Famine Relief is founded
5 October 1942 · University Church of St Mary the Virgin, Oxford, England, United Kingdom
A group of Oxford citizens, including the vicar T. R. Milford and Professor Gilbert Murray, met in the Old Library of the University Church and formed the Oxford Committee for Famine Relief. They acted because famine in German-occupied Greece could not be relieved while an Allied naval blockade stopped food imports. The committee raised money for relief through the Greek Red Cross and campaigned for food to be allowed through; it later broadened its work and became Oxfam.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
A small local committee grew into one of the world's best-known relief organisations, built on the idea that feeding the hungry is a duty that crosses the lines of war.
The kural ranks sharing one's food and protecting the many forms of life as the highest of virtues, and the committee was founded to do exactly that for starving strangers.
