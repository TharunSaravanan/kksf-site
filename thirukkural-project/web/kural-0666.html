Kural 666

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

எண்ணிய எண்ணியாங்கு எய்துப எண்ணியார் திண்ணியர் ஆகப் பெறின்.
ENNiya eNNiyaangu eydhupa eNNiyaar ThiNNiyar aagap peRin.

Meaning

If people who make plans stay firm in carrying them out, they will get what they planned, and in just the way they planned it.

Elementary

If you decide to learn to ride a bike and you keep practising every day, even after wobbling, you will ride just the way you imagined. Wishing is the start; sticking with it is what makes the wish come true.

University

The couplet plays on eṇṇu (to think, plan) four times: eṇṇiya (what was planned), eṇṇiyāṅku (as it was planned), eṇṇiyār (those who planned), set against tiṇṇiyar (the firm), which chimes with tiṭpam in the chapter title. Parimelazhagar reads it as a promise conditional on firmness in execution: planners who are also firm doers obtain both the object and the manner they intended; Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah follow him. On the text: ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print eytu (எய்து), but the sandhi-joined edition at thirukkural-thamizh.org has eytupa (எய்துப, ‘they will attain’, future plural), and thirukkural.net’s own musical setting of the couplet by P. A. Muthukrishnan also quotes எய்துப; that reading, which the metre and grammar need, is adopted here. The couplet is one of the most often quoted in the Porul section, as a maxim that resolve turns intention into result.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Bhagīratha undertakes long austerities to bring the celestial Gaṅgā down to earth for his ancestors, persisting through each obstacle until Śiva agrees to bear her fall and the river reaches its goal.
— Śrīmad Bhāgavatam 9.9 (Bhaktivedanta Vedabase) [4]
His persistence is so proverbial that ‘Bhagīratha prayatna’ (Bhagiratha’s effort) is a common Indian phrase for relentless, successful effort.
Bhagīratha attains exactly what he set out to do because he stays firm, as the Kural promises.
Christianity
“For verily I say unto you, That whosoever shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed, and be thou cast into the sea; and shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass; he shall have whatsoever he saith.”
— Mark 11:23 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that an undivided, undoubting heart obtains what it asks for.
Like the Kural, it ties getting what one intends to an unwavering inner firmness.
Islam
“And that there is not for man except that [good] for which he strives”
— Quran 53:39 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran states that a person gains only what he has actively striven for.
Both texts tie attainment to sustained effort toward the intended end.
Judaism
“...along the path a person wishes to proceed, one leads and assists him.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Makkot 10b (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [7]
Rabbi Elazar draws from Torah, Prophets and Writings the principle that a person is helped along whatever path he firmly chooses.
As in the Kural, a settled intention is carried to fulfilment along the course it has chosen.
Buddhism
“Gladly would I let the flesh & blood in my body dry up, leaving just the skin, tendons, & bones, but if I have not attained what can be reached through human firmness, human persistence, human striving, there will be no relaxing my persistence.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 70, Kitagiri Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha describes the resolve (adhiṭṭhāna) of a committed disciple, who will not relax effort until the goal is reached, and says such a disciple can expect the fruit.
‘Human firmness’ leading to attainment is the Kural’s tiṇṇiyar reaching what they planned.
Jainism
“But he who has renounced pleasure, will not miss his aim”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.25-26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
In the parable of the ram, the Uttaradhyayana contrasts the one who, though taught the right way, goes astray again and again with the one who has renounced pleasure and therefore does not miss his aim.
Like the kural's promise that the firm will attain what they planned, this verse makes steadiness of purpose the condition for reaching one's goal.
Sikhism
“Make the effort, and you shall live; practicing it, you shall enjoy peace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 522, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan teaches that sustained effort (udam) and practice bring the fruit that is sought.
The Kural promises that planners who are firm in carrying out their plans will attain what they aimed at; Guru Arjan likewise ties attainment to steady effort and practice.
Greek Mythology
“Nevertheless, I want to get home, and can think of nothing else. If some god wrecks me when I am on the sea, I will bear it and make the best of it.”
— Homer, Odyssey 5 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Offered immortality by Calypso, Odysseus holds to his single aim of returning home, and in the end he gets there.
His fixed intention, held firmly through every setback, reaches its goal just as he meant it to.
Philosophy
Seneca writes that our plans miscarry because they have no fixed aim, and that when a man does not know which harbour he is making for, no wind is the right wind.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 71 (trans. R. M. Gummere) [12]
For Seneca, a firmly fixed purpose is the precondition for any course of action to succeed.
He matches the Kural’s point that attainment follows a settled, firmly held intention.

Real Life Example

The Eiffel Tower is completed in time for the 1889 Exposition
26 January 1887 - 31 March 1889 · Paris, France
For the 1889 Exposition Universelle, marking the centenary of the French Revolution, the organisers chose the design submitted by Gustave Eiffel's firm from more than 100 proposals. Construction began on 26 January 1887 and the tower was finished on 31 March 1889, in two years, two months and five days, with a small workforce and some 2.5 million rivets. It served as the entrance to the Exposition, which opened that May, and was visited by about two million people during the fair.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
An unprecedented iron structure was built exactly to plan and on time, becoming a lasting symbol of engineering achievement.
The kural says that planners who are firm in carrying out their plans attain what they aimed at in just the way they aimed at it, and Eiffel's team delivered the tower as designed and in time for the event it was built for.
