Kural 789

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

நட்பிற்கு வீற்றிருக்கை யாதெனின் கொட்பின்றி ஒல்லும்வாய் ஊன்றும் நிலை.
Natpirku veetrirukkai yaadhenin kotpindri ollumvaai oondrum nilai.

Meaning

Where does friendship sit like a king on a throne? In always being ready to hold up a friend in every way you can.

Elementary

Think of a walking stick that an old person leans on. It doesn’t move around or give up; it’s always there to take their weight. Valluvar says the very best friendship is like that stick: steady, never changing its mind, and always ready to hold you up however it can.

University

VīRRirukkai (வீற்றிருக்கை) means a throne or seat of majesty, so the question is ‘where is friendship’s throne?’, that is, what is its highest state. The answer has three parts: kotpu inRi (கொட்பின்றி, ‘without kotpu’, a word meaning whirling or wavering, so ‘without change of heart’); ollum vāy (ஒல்லும்வாய், ‘wherever and however possible’); and ūnRum nilai (ஊன்றும் நிலை, ‘the state of propping up, supporting’, as a staff supports). Parimelazhagar explains ollumvāy as helping in every way available toward the friend’s virtue and wealth, and kotpinri as never wavering in mind, whatever the times. Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah gloss kotpinri as ‘always, without change’. Both web sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As a father tolerates the impudence of his son, a friend the impertinence of a friend, or a husband the familiarity of his wife, please tolerate the wrongs I may have done You.”
— Bhagavad Gita 11.44 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Arjuna asks Krishna for the patient, unwavering forbearance that a friend shows toward a friend.
The friend’s steady tolerance is the Kural’s kotpinri, a support that does not waver or change its mind.
Christianity
“Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends.”
— John 15:13 (KJV) [4]
Jesus calls giving one’s life for one’s friends the greatest love.
Laying down one’s life is the furthest limit of the Kural’s support ‘in every way one can’.
Islam
When the Emigrants arrived in Medina, the Prophet paired each with a Helper as a brother; the Helper Sa‘d ibn al-Rabi‘ offered to share half of all his property with ‘Abd al-Rahman ibn ‘Awf, who thanked him and asked only to be shown the market.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3780 [5]
The Helpers supported newly arrived strangers with everything they had, steadily and without being asked.
Sa‘d’s offer is the Kural’s ‘holding up in every way one can’, offered as a settled commitment.
Judaism
Though his father Saul is trying to kill David, Jonathan keeps faith with his friend, warns him of the danger, renews their covenant, and sends him away safely with the signal of the arrows.
— 1 Samuel 20 [6]
Jonathan supports David at great cost to his own position as heir.
Jonathan’s loyalty does not waver even under pressure from his own father, and he helps by every means he can.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who is the same in happiness and sorrow be understood as a warm-hearted friend: (i) he reveals his secrets, (ii) he conceals one’s own secrets, (iii) in misfortune he does not forsake one, (iv) his life even he sacrifices for one’s sake.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha describes the friend who stays the same in good times and bad, even to the point of giving his life.
‘The same in happiness and sorrow’ is the Kural’s kotpinri, friendship without wavering.
Jainism
“When affection continues affection, then is friendship preserved, like the flower on the stalk, which, being full-blown, closes not again. Those who resemble the lotus, which, having once blown, closes again its petals, know neither affection nor friendship.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 5 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The stanza says true friendship stays constant like a flower that never closes once it opens, unlike the lotus that opens and closes again.
The Kural places friendship's throne in steady support that never wavers, and the Naladiyar likewise makes constancy without wavering the test of genuine friendship.
Sikhism
“My associates and companions have all deserted me; no one remains with me. Says Nanak, in this tragedy, the Lord alone is my Support.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1429, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 55), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In this salok attributed to Guru Tegh Bahadur, human companions fall away in adversity, and only God stays as an unwavering support.
The Kural places friendship's throne in steady, unwavering support; this salok shows by contrast how worldly companions waver, and finds that steady prop in God alone.
Greek Mythology
When the tyrant Dionysius condemned the Pythagorean Phintias (Pythias) to death, Damon stood surety for his life so that Phintias could settle his affairs; Phintias returned on the appointed day, and the tyrant, amazed, asked to be admitted as a third friend.
— Cicero, De Officiis 3.45 [10]
Damon offered his own life as a pledge, and Phintias kept faith by returning to die.
This is the Kural’s enthroned friendship: support given without hesitation and kept without wavering.
Philosophy
“there is nothing which so fits in with our nature, or is so exactly what we want in prosperity or adversity.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 5 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Laelius says friendship suits human nature better than anything else and is what we need in both good times and bad.
Cicero’s friendship for ‘prosperity or adversity’ is the Kural’s support that holds steady in all seasons.

Real Life Example

Anne Sullivan's decades of support for Helen Keller
1887-1936 · Tuscumbia, Alabama, and elsewhere in the United States
On 3 March 1887 Anne Sullivan, a former pupil of the Perkins Institution, arrived at the Keller home in Tuscumbia to teach six-year-old Helen Keller, who could neither see nor hear, by spelling words into her hand. Sullivan stayed with Keller long after the famous breakthrough at the water pump, and when Keller entered Radcliffe College in 1900 Sullivan spelled out all the lectures and unbrailled texts for her, at great cost to her own weak eyesight. Keller graduated with honours in 1904, and Sullivan remained her interpreter and constant companion for decades afterwards.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Sullivan's steady, many-sided support made possible Keller's education and her later life as an author and public figure.
The kural places friendship on its throne when it stands firm without wavering and holds the other up in every way it can, and Sullivan's decades of unwavering support are as complete an example as history offers.
