Kural 1019

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

குலஞ்சுடும் கொள்கை பிழைப்பின் நலஞ்சுடும் நாணின்மை நின்றக் கடை.
Kulanjudum kolgai pizhaippin nalanjudum naaninmai nindrak kadai.

Meaning

Slipping from good behaviour burns away the good name of your family. But once you become shameless, it burns away every good thing you have.

Elementary

If you break a rule once, people might think less of your family for a while. But if you stop caring at all whether you do wrong, it's like a fire that keeps spreading and burns up everything good about you.

University

The kural ranks two fires with the same verb, sudum (சுடும், 'burns, destroys'). If kolgai (கொள்கை, 'one's settled conduct', glossed by the commentators as ozhukkam) pizhaippin (பிழைப்பின், 'should fail'), it burns the kulam (குலம், 'family, lineage'). But naaninmai (நாணின்மை, 'shamelessness') nindra kadai (நின்றக் கடை, 'once it becomes settled') burns all nalam (நலம், 'every good'). Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read the second fire as more total than the first; Kesikan says the first ruins only the family's standing while the second consumes everything. Kalaignar connects the lines: failing to feel shame at one's lapse lets ruin spread. Pope: ‘'Twill race consume if right observance fail; 'Twill every good consume if shamelessness prevail.’ The text (with the doubled consonant in நின்றக் கடை) is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“With the destruction of the dynasty, the eternal family tradition is vanquished, and thus the rest of the family becomes involved in irreligion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 1.39 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Arjuna fears that once a family's dharma breaks down, adharma overtakes the whole line.
The Gita shares the Kural's concern that a lapse in conduct ruins the kulam.
Christianity
“Speaking lies in hypocrisy; having their conscience seared with a hot iron”
— 1 Timothy 4:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes deceivers whose conscience has been cauterised until it no longer feels.
The Kural uses the same image of burning (sudum) for what happens when shame is lost.
Islam
“Haya' (pious shyness from committing religeous indiscretions) does not bring anything except good.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6117 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that haya' produces only good.
The positive counterpart of the Kural: where shame brings only good, shamelessness burns all good away.
Judaism
“one who does not have the capacity to be shamefaced, it is known that his forefathers did not stand at Mount Sinai.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 20a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud treats shamelessness as evidence that a person's lineage lacks the formative experience of Sinai.
Like the Kural, it links a person's shamelessness to the standing of his whole lineage.
Buddhism
“One who destroys life, utters lies, takes what is not given, goes to another man's wife, and is addicted to intoxicating drinks — such a man digs up his own root even in this world.”
— Dhammapada 246-247 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Dhammapada says that persistent wrongdoing uproots a person's own foundations, here and now.
Like the Kural's fire of shamelessness, settled misconduct destroys every good a person has.
Jainism
“The daughter of the best king, true to self-control and her vows, maintained the honour of her clan and family, and her virtue”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.40, with 22.43 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati, refusing Rathanemi's advances, guards both her family's honour and her own virtue, and reminds him that those born into a noble family must not fall.
The episode joins the Kural's two concerns: conduct guards the kulam, and shame guards one's whole good.
Sikhism
“Modesty has left her home, and honor has gone away with her.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1243, Raag Sarang, Sarang ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak pictures modesty walking out of the house and honour following her.
The Kural says that once shamelessness settles in it burns away every good thing; Guru Nanak shows the same chain, with honour going the moment modesty leaves.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus' Oresteia the crimes of Atreus, including the feast he served his brother Thyestes, bring a curse on the house that passes from generation to generation: Agamemnon's sacrifice of Iphigenia, his murder by Clytemnestra, and Orestes' revenge.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1090-1097, 1583-1602 [10]
In Greek tragedy, one shameless outrage blights an entire family line.
This is the Kural's first line on a tragic scale: a breach of conduct burns the kulam.
Philosophy
“And does not the legislator and every one who is good for anything, hold this fear in the greatest honour? This is what he terms reverence, and the confidence which is the reverse of this he terms insolence; and the latter he always deems to be a very great evil both to individuals and to states.”
— Plato, Laws I, 646e-647a (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
The Athenian Stranger defines shame as the fear of being thought evil for a dishonourable word or deed. Its opposite, a shameless boldness, he calls a very great evil for persons and cities alike.
Plato, like the Kural, treats the loss of shame as a ruin that goes deeper than any single lapse.

Real Life Example

Boris Onishchenko's rigged epee gets the Soviet pentathlon team thrown out
19 July 1976 · Montreal, Canada
During the fencing stage of the modern pentathlon at the 1976 Montreal Olympics, officials found that Soviet pentathlete Boris Onishchenko's epee had been modified with a hidden switch that could register a hit without any touch. He was disqualified from the Games and banned from the sport, and his disqualification also put the whole Soviet team out of the competition.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single act of dishonesty ruined a decorated athlete's career and cost his teammates, who had done nothing wrong, their place in the event.
The kural says a lapse in conduct burns the name of one's 'family', and Onishchenko's cheating brought down his team along with himself.
