Kural 1224

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

காதலர் இல்வழி மாலை கொலைக்களத்து ஏதிலர் போல வரும்.
Kaadhalar ilvazhi maalai kolaikkalaththu aedhilar poala varum.

Meaning

When my love is not here, evening comes at me like enemies marching onto a killing field.

Elementary

Imagine a game where a scary character slowly walks toward you and you cannot run. When the wife is alone, the evening feels like that: it creeps closer and closer, and she dreads it.

University

The simile is military and stark: kolaikkaḷam is a ‘field of slaughter’ or execution ground, and ētilar are strangers or foes, so evening approaches the lonely wife like men coming to kill. Kesikan glosses ētilar as highway robbers (āṟalaippār) at a place of murder, Varadarasanar as enemies, and Karunanidhi shifts the image to an enemy’s raised sword; Solomon Pappaiah adds the contrast implicit in the chapter, that the same evening once made her life flourish when her husband was home. Pope’s couplet runs: ‘When absent is my love, the evening hour descends, / As when an alien host to field of battle wends.’ Both web sources give ētilar; a song setting by P. A. Muthukrishnan quoted on thirukkural.net sings it as ētilār, a lyrical lengthening rather than a textual variant.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ashoka grove of Lanka, Sita, separated from Rama, is surrounded by fierce rakshasi guards who threaten to kill and devour her if she will not yield to Ravana, and in her despair she longs for death.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, the rakshasis’ threats to Sita (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki places the separated wife literally among would-be killers, her grief for Rama sharpened by their menace.
The Kural’s wife only feels the evening as such killers; Sita lives the image, alone and threatened while her husband is far away.
Christianity
“Prepare ye war against her; arise, and let us go up at noon. Woe unto us! for the day goeth away, for the shadows of the evening are stretched out.”
— Jeremiah 6:4 (KJV) [4]
In Jeremiah, invaders preparing to attack cry out as the evening shadows lengthen, linking the fall of evening with the onset of assault.
The Kural’s evening also arrives with the lengthening shadows of an attacking enemy.
Islam
“When night falls (or it is evening), keep your children close to you for the devils spread out at that time.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3304 [5]
The Prophet treats nightfall as a dangerous hour when harmful forces spread abroad.
The Kural likewise feels the coming of evening as the arrival of hostile powers.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not be afraid of the terror by night, Nor of the arrow that flieth by day; Of the pestilence that walketh in darkness, Nor of the destruction that wasteth at noonday.”
— Psalms 91:5-6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Psalm 91 names the terror that walks in the dark and promises that one sheltered by God need not fear it.
The Kural’s wife experiences just such a ‘terror by night’ approaching her; the Psalm answers it with trust in divine protection.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada compares death to a great flood that sweeps away a sleeping village, carrying off the person whose mind clings to children and cattle.
— Dhammapada 287 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhism pictures death advancing suddenly on those absorbed in their attachments.
The Kural’s evening comes like killers to a heart wholly bound up in an absent love, the clinging mind the Dhammapada warns about.
Jainism
“The mind of those who always live in unfrequented lodgings, who eat low food, and who subdue their senses, will not be attacked by the foe, Love, who is vanquished as disease is by medicine.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana calls Love a foe that attacks the undisciplined mind.
The Kural’s wife feels herself attacked at evening as if by enemies; Jain teaching names the real attacker as love itself.
Sikhism
“Fareed, that body, within which love of the Lord does not well up - look upon that body as a cremation ground.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 36) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid uses the grim image of the cremation ground for a body untouched by the longing of separation from God.
The Kural's wife feels the evening come at her like enemies onto a field of slaughter; Farid also reaches for a place of the dead, though he applies it to the heart that feels no longing at all.
Greek Mythology
In Hesiod’s Theogony, Night (Nyx) gives birth to hateful Doom, black Fate and Death, and to Sleep and the tribe of Dreams.
— Hesiod, Theogony 211-212 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Greek myth makes Night the mother of the powers that destroy human life.
The Kural’s evening, coming like killers, is the same dark power that Hesiod sees as the mother of Death.
Philosophy
Lucretius says that as children tremble and fear everything in the blind darkness, so we adults in daylight sometimes fear things no more real than what children dread in the dark.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 2.55-58 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.H.D. Rouse) [11]
The Epicurean poet treats dread of the dark as a product of the mind, to be dispelled by understanding.
The Kural’s wife dreads the evening as if it were an assassin; Lucretius would say the terror comes from within, not from the hour.

Real Life Example

The Blitz begins with an evening of bombing over London
7 September 1940 · London, United Kingdom
On Saturday 7 September 1940, starting around 4 pm, hundreds of German planes attacked the docks of east London, beginning the Blitz. London was then bombed for 57 nights in a row; blackout rules dimmed the city's lights, and within weeks about 120,000 people a night were sheltering in Underground stations.
— Sources: [12] [13]
For Londoners in 1940 nightfall became the hour when the enemy arrived, so the coming of evening itself was something to dread.
The kural says that, without her beloved, evening approaches the wife like enemies advancing on a killing ground, and in the Blitz the coming of evening literally meant the coming of an enemy.
