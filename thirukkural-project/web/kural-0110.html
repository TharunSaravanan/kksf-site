Kural 110

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

எந்நன்றி கொன்றார்க்கும் உய்வுண்டாம் உய்வில்லை செய்ந்நன்றி கொன்ற மகற்கு.
Ennandri kondraarkkum uyvundaam uyvillai Seynnandri kondra magarku.

Meaning

People who destroy any other kind of goodness may still be saved. But there is no saving the person who is ungrateful for help they were given.

Elementary

People can make lots of mistakes and still fix them. But this kural says the one thing that is almost impossible to fix is being ungrateful to someone who helped you, so that is the one mistake you should never make.

University

The chapter closes with its severest verdict. Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah, following the traditional commentary, read the first நன்றி as அறம் in general ('whoever has killed any virtue'), against which செய்ந்நன்றி கொன்ற மகன், the man who has 'killed' a benefit by forgetting it or returning harm for it, stands uniquely without உய்வு (escape, deliverance). The idea has deep roots in Tamil and Sanskrit tradition: the Sangam anthology Puranānūru (poem 34) is commonly cited alongside this kural for declaring that there is no escape for those who destroy a benefit, and Valmiki's Kishkindha Kanda (4.34.12) says atonement is ordained for cow-slayers, drinkers, thieves and vow-breakers but not for the kṛtaghna, the ingrate. Outside South Asia, Cicero held that no duty is more imperative than proving one's gratitude (De Officiis 1.47), and Hume chose ingratitude as the most horrid and unnatural of crimes (Treatise 3.1.1). This agreement suggests that ingratitude was treated not as one vice among many but as the destruction of the reciprocity on which moral community rests.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The savants have ordained atonement for slayers of cows, for drinkers of liquors, thus for thieves and infringers of solemnity, but no atonement is available to a treacherous person.”
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 4.34.12 (Desiraju Hanumanta Rao trans.) [3]
Lakshmana, rebuking Sugriva for forgetting his promise to Rama, quotes an old verse: even killers, drunkards and thieves can atone, but there is no atonement for someone who is ungrateful (kṛtaghna, rendered 'treacherous' by this translator).
Almost word-for-word the Kural's claim: every other sinner has a way out, but the ingrate has none.
Christianity
“And Jesus answering said, Were there not ten cleansed? but where are the nine? There are not found that returned to give glory to God, save this stranger.”
— Luke 17:17-18 (KJV) [4]
Jesus heals ten lepers, but only one, a Samaritan foreigner, comes back to thank him, and Jesus asks sadly where the other nine are.
The story singles out ingratitude after a life-changing kindness as something that grieves even Jesus.
Islam
“And [remember] when your Lord proclaimed, 'If you are grateful, I will surely increase you [in favor]; but if you deny, indeed, My punishment is severe.'”
— Qur'an 14:7 (Saheeh International) [5]
God promises more blessings to the grateful and warns of severe punishment for those who deny His favours.
Ingratitude (kufr of blessings) carries grave consequences, echoing the Kural's warning that it has no escape.
Judaism
“Whoso rewardeth evil for good, evil shall not depart from his house.”
— Proverbs 17:13 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that anyone who repays good with evil will never be free of trouble in their household.
'Evil shall not depart' is a close match for the Kural's 'there is no escape' for the one who kills a kindness.
Buddhism
In the Silavanaga Jataka, the Bodhisatta, born as a noble elephant, rescues a lost forester and carries him to safety. The man returns three times to saw off the elephant's tusks, which are freely given; on his way home the third time the earth splits open and swallows him.
— Jataka No. 72, Silavanaga Jataka (E. B. Cowell ed., vol. 1) [6]
A man keeps taking the tusks of the elephant that saved his life, and after his third betrayal the ground itself opens and swallows him.
The ingrate who repays rescue with injury meets a doom from which there is no escape, the Kural's verdict told as a story.
Jainism
“What crime against you was committed by the Lord, when he showed the snake being burned inside the log, if you were prevented from sin at that time? Good advice then led to your hostility, villain, like rain-water on saline soil.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book IX, ch. III, part 9 'Attacks by Meghamālin' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Kaṭha, reborn as the demon Meghamālin, repays Pārśva's kindly correction with lifelong hostility. Confronted by Dharaṇa, he is terrified: “I can not remain in the three worlds because of the crime against him … Where shall I go for a refuge?”
Valluvar holds that whoever kills a benefit has no escape, and Hemacandra's ingrate himself finds no refuge anywhere in the three worlds for returning good with harm.
Sikhism
“No one can save such an ungrateful person; he is thrown into the most horrible hell.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1086, Raag Maaroo, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan names the akirtghan, the one who forgets what has been done for him, as a person whom nothing can save.
The Kural says any other sin may find expiation but not the killing of gratitude; Gurbani likewise singles out ingratitude as leaving one beyond rescue.
Greek Mythology
Pindar tells how Ixion, whom Zeus had purified and received as a guest among the gods, tried to seduce Hera; bound forever to a winged, turning wheel, he proclaims to mortals that they must repay a benefactor with kind returns.
— Pindar, Pythian Ode 2.21-48 [9]
Ixion was forgiven and welcomed by Zeus, then betrayed him, and was punished for all eternity as a warning to everyone.
Ixion is Greek myth's model ingrate, and his punishment is endless, the Kural's 'no escape'.
Philosophy
“Of all crimes that human creatures are capable of committing, the most horrid and unnatural is ingratitude, especially when it is committed against parents, and appears in the more flagrant instances of wounds and death.”
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 3.1.1.24 [10]
The Scottish philosopher David Hume uses ingratitude as his example of the crime everyone agrees is the worst, especially ingratitude toward parents.
Hume ranks ingratitude at the very top of human crimes, matching the Kural's claim that it alone has no escape.

Real Life Example

Brutus joins the conspiracy against Julius Caesar
48 - 44 BCE · Rome, Roman Republic (modern Italy)
Marcus Junius Brutus fought on Pompey's side against Julius Caesar, but after Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus in 48 BCE Caesar pardoned him, made him a highly honoured companion, and later put him in charge of Cisalpine Gaul and gave him high civic office. In 44 BCE Brutus joined Cassius and other conspirators in assassinating Caesar in Rome. In the Inferno, written in the early fourteenth century, Dante placed Brutus and Cassius, as betrayers of their benefactor, beside Judas in the lowest region of Hell.
— Sources: [11] [12]
For centuries Brutus's name became shorthand for betrayal of a benefactor, and Dante gave him the harshest place in his entire vision of punishment.
The kural says there is no escape for one who destroys gratitude for a kindness received; Dante's verdict on Brutus expresses the same judgment in literary form.
