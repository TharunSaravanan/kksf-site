Kural 939

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

உடைசெல்வம் ஊண்ஒளி கல்விஎன்று ஐந்தும் அடையாவாம் ஆயங் கொளின்.
Udaiselvam oon-oli kalvi-endru aindhum adaiyaavaam aayang kolin.

Meaning

If a person takes up gambling, these five things will never come to them: clothes, riches, food, fame and learning.

Elementary

Think of five good things: clothes to wear, money, food, a good name, and learning. Valluvar says that if you make gambling your habit, all five walk away from you.

University

The couplet lists five goods: udai (உடை, clothing), selvam (செல்வம், wealth), oon (ஊண், food), oli (ஒளி, 'light', i.e. fame or renown) and kalvi (கல்வி, learning). Aayam (ஆயம்) here means gambling, and aayang kolin means 'if one takes it up'. Adaiyaavaam (அடையாவாம்) says these 'will not come to' him, so the stress is on goods that are never gained, not only goods lost. Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kesikan list the five in reverse order (fame, learning, wealth, food, clothing), presumably following Parimelazhagar's ranking from highest to lowest, while Pope keeps the Kural's order. Kesikan adds 'even if one does it thinking it mere sport' (வேடிக்கை), and Sivayogi Sivakumar takes oli as 'self-knowledge'. The text is the same on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After Nala loses his kingdom at dice to Pushkara, he wanders into the forest with Damayanti wearing a single cloth; starving, he throws that garment over some birds to catch them, and they fly away with it, leaving him naked, hungry and without a kingdom.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana, trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
The gambler-king is stripped in turn of wealth, food, clothing and royal glory.
Nala's fall is almost a list of the Kural's five lost goods.
Christianity
“...They parted my raiment among them, and for my vesture they did cast lots. These things therefore the soldiers did.”
— John 19:24 (KJV) [4]
Soldiers gamble for the clothes of a condemned man.
Clothing changing hands by lot echoes the first of the Kural's five losses, seen from the winners' side.
Islam
“...But their sin is greater than their benefit. And they ask you what they should spend. Say, ‘The excess [beyond needs].’”
— Qur'an 2:219 (Saheeh International) [5]
The verse condemns gambling and in the same breath teaches spending only what is beyond one's needs.
The contrast protects the basic needs, such as food and clothing, that the Kural says the gambler loses.
Judaism
“Rather, those who play dice are disqualified because they are not involved in settling the world, i.e., in productive occupations that demand hard work.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 24b (Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
Rav Sheshet sees the dice-player as someone outside productive life.
Someone who produces nothing will not gain the food, clothing and wealth the Kural lists.
Buddhism
“(v) he is despised by his friends and associates, (vi) he is not sought after for matrimony; for people would say he is a gambler and is not fit to look after a wife.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), trans. Narada Thera [7]
The gambler loses respect and the chance of marriage, because people judge him unable to provide.
This is the Kural's lost renown (oli) and inability to secure basic goods.
Jainism
“He will be born among men where there is wealth, beauty, glory, fame, long life, and eminent happiness.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.27 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana says the one who has renounced pleasure will be reborn where wealth, glory, fame and happiness are found.
Valluvar says that clothing, wealth, food, renown and learning never come to one who takes up gambling; the Jain verse gives the reverse case, in which a similar list of goods comes to the one who has given up pleasure.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang, Saarang ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak’s short salok sums up two pillars of Sikh life, kirat karni (honest work) and vand chhakna (sharing). Food, clothing and standing are to come from honest labour, never from chance or someone else’s loss.
The Kural says the gambler never gets clothing, wealth, food, renown or learning, and Guru Nanak names the honest, sharing path by which a person does gain them.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus relates that Pisistratus' army overran the Athenians while some of them had gone off to dice after their midday meal.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.63 (trans. George Rawlinson) [10]
Men at their dice lost their city and with it their freedom and standing.
The loss of everything a citizen has echoes the Kural's sweeping list of goods that will not come.
Philosophy
“Both, then, since they are willing to make gain from wrong sources, are sordid lovers of gain; therefore all such forms of taking are mean.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle concludes that the gambler's way of getting is mean and dishonourable.
Such meanness rules out the renown (oli) that the Kural counts among the five goods.

Real Life Example

Dostoevsky gambles away his travel money in Wiesbaden and pawns his belongings in Baden-Baden
1865 and 1867 · Wiesbaden and Baden-Baden, Germany
In August 1865 Fyodor Dostoevsky gambled away all his travel money at the Wiesbaden casino and was stranded at his hotel for two months, where he was eventually given nothing to eat but tea and the staff stopped serving him, until friends and a Russian Orthodox priest sent money for him to leave. In 1867, having just married Anna Snitkina, he went to Baden-Baden hoping to escape his debts by gambling, and by the end of that summer he had pawned many of his and his wife's belongings and lost the gifts sent by friends to rescue them. He stopped gambling altogether only in 1871.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Even a writer of genius, at work on Crime and Punishment at the time, was reduced by roulette to hunger and pawned possessions.
The kural says that clothing, wealth, food, renown and learning never reach one who takes up gambling, and Dostoevsky's gambling left him without food in Wiesbaden and without his and his wife's belongings in Baden-Baden.
