Kural 139

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

ஒழுக்க முடையவர்க்கு ஒல்லாவே தீய வழுக்கியும் வாயாற் சொலல்.
Ozhukkam udaiyavarkku ollaave theeya Vazhukkiyum vaayaal solal.

Meaning

People with good behaviour cannot speak bad words, not even by mistake.

Elementary

Some people are so used to being kind that even when they are angry or surprised, mean words just don't come out. Being good becomes so natural that it even protects your mouth.

University

The key word is vazhukkiyum, ‘even when slipping’ or ‘even by inadvertence’. Valluvar does not say the disciplined hold back evil speech by effort. He says it is ollaa for them, impossible or unfitting: conduct has become so much second nature that even an unguarded moment is safe. This is a strong claim about habituation, close to Aristotle's view that the virtuous person does the right thing with pleasure and without inner struggle. Tamil ethics gives speech special attention, with chapters on sweet speech (10) and on avoiding useless speech (20). Parallel traditions do too: the Gita counts truthful, non-distressing speech as an austerity, rabbinic ethics condemns lashon hara, and Buddhism places right speech on the Eightfold Path.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Words that do not cause distress, are truthful, inoffensive, and beneficial, as well as regular recitation of the Vedic scriptures—these are declared as austerity of speech.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna names speech that never wounds as a form of spiritual discipline.
Both treat freedom from harmful speech as a mark of disciplined character.
Christianity
“Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.”
— Ephesians 4:29 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers that no corrupt word should come from their mouths at all.
Both require that no evil speech escape a person of good conduct.
Islam
“anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should talk what is good or keep quiet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6018 [5]
The Prophet makes good speech, or silence, a sign of real faith.
Like the Kural, it makes clean speech a direct expression of inner character.
Judaism
“Keep thy tongue from evil, and thy lips from speaking guile.”
— Psalm 34:13 (KJV) [4]
The Psalmist names guarding the tongue as the path to a good life, a verse central to the later Jewish laws against lashon hara (evil speech).
Both make freedom from evil speech a core part of right conduct.
Buddhism
“He who utters gentle, instructive and truthful words, who imprecates none — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 408 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha includes gentle, truthful speech that curses no one among the marks of a holy person.
Both treat blameless speech as the sign of perfected conduct.
Jainism
“A monk should avoid untruth, nor should he speak positively (about future things, his plans, &c.); he should avoid sinful speech, and always keep free from deceit. He should not tell anything sinful or meaningless or hurtful, neither for his own sake nor for anybody else's, nor without such a motive.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.24–25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
The disciplined monk avoids harmful speech whatever the circumstances, even when he has no motive at all.
Valluvar says people of right conduct cannot utter evil words even by a slip; the Jain rule likewise rules out hurtful speech 'without such a motive', that is, even when it would be idle or unintended.
Sikhism
“So speak the Truth, in righteousness, and do not speak falsehood.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Asa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sheikh Farid's hymn in the Guru Granth Sahib tells the seeker to speak only truth and righteousness.
The Kural says the disciplined cannot utter evil words even by a slip; Farid gives the positive rule of speech such people live by.
Greek Mythology
Queen Niobe boasts that her fourteen children make her greater than the goddess Leto, who has only two. Apollo and Artemis kill her children, and Niobe, frozen in grief, is turned to stone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.146–312 [9]
One arrogant speech brings total ruin on Niobe.
It is the reverse image of the Kural: the harm that evil words bring.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises being silent for the most part, or saying only what is necessary in few words, and avoiding talk that is vulgar or that speaks ill of others.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 [10]
The Stoic sage controls speech as part of a disciplined character.
This matches the Kural's picture of the well-conducted person whose tongue does not slip into evil.

Real Life Example

The Chofetz Chaim publishes his code on the laws of harmful speech
1873 · Radun, Russian Empire (now Belarus)
In 1873 Rabbi Yisrael Meir Kagan, then about thirty-five and living in Radun, published a book that systematically set out the Jewish laws forbidding lashon hara, harmful speech about others, and gossip. The work, titled Chafetz Chaim, became the authoritative text on the ethics of speech, and its author became known everywhere by its name. He also wrote a companion ethical treatise on guarding the tongue, Shemirat HaLashon.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Kagan treated careful speech not as occasional politeness but as a discipline to be trained until it becomes habit, and his book still shapes Jewish teaching on speech.
The kural says people of right conduct cannot speak evil even by a slip of the tongue; Kagan devoted his first book to building exactly that habituated guard over speech.
