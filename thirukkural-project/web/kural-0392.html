Kural 392

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

எண்ணென்ப ஏனை எழுத்தென்ப இவ்விரண்டும் கண்ணென்ப வாழும் உயிர்க்கு.
Ennenba enai ezhuthenba ivvirandum Kannenba vaazhum uyirkku.

Meaning

What we call numbers and what we call letters are said to be the two eyes of every living person.

Elementary

You have two eyes to see the world. Valluvar says your mind also has two eyes: numbers and letters. Maths and reading let you understand things you could never see without them.

University

This kural reduces the whole of learning to two basic disciplines, எண் (en, number and calculation) and எழுத்து (ezhuthu, letters and literacy), and calls them the two eyes of human life. Commentators widened the terms. Parimelazhagar took en to cover arithmetic and the sciences such as astronomy, and ezhuthu to cover grammar and literature. The modern commentator Solomon Pappaiah even glosses them as science and the arts. The repeated என்ப ('they say') presents this as settled wisdom, not personal opinion. The image of eyes prepares for the next kural, where lacking learning is compared to having sores instead of eyes. Remarkably, Aeschylus' Prometheus names the very same pair, numbers and the combining of letters, as the founding gifts of civilization.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Mundaka Upanishad teaches that two kinds of knowledge must be known, the higher (para) and the lower (apara). The lower includes the four Vedas together with phonetics, grammar, etymology, metre, ritual and astronomy.
— Mundaka Upanishad 1.1.4–5 [3]
The Upanishad sorts all learning into a twofold scheme. The 'lower' disciplines, which include the sciences of letters (phonetics and grammar) and of calculation (astronomy), are the tools that come before and serve the higher knowledge.
Like the Kural, it treats learning as a pair of essential faculties. Its lower knowledge includes exactly the letter-based and number-based sciences that Valluvar calls the two eyes.
Christianity
“Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path.”
— Psalm 119:105 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes God's written word as the light that lets him see where he is walking.
The Kural's image of learning as eyes, the organs of sight, matches the Psalm's image of the written word as light for the path.
Islam
“Who taught by the pen - Taught man that which he knew not.”
— Qur'an 96:4–5 (Sahih International) [5]
In the first verses revealed to the Prophet, God is praised as the One who taught humanity through the pen, meaning through writing. Literacy is presented as a divine gift that opens up knowledge.
The Qur'an honours ezhuthu, letters and writing, as the channel by which people come to know what they did not know. This mirrors the Kural's claim that letters are an eye of life.
Judaism
“The calculation of the equinoxes and gematria are the desserts of wisdom.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:18 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Eliezer Hisma names astronomical calculation and gematria (the numerical values of letters) as the delightful extras that round out Torah learning.
Here numbers and letters meet, since gematria is literally letters read as numbers. The Mishnah treats both as part of complete wisdom, though as supplements rather than 'eyes'.
Buddhism
“To have much learning, to be skillful in handicraft, well-trained in discipline, and to be of good speech — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Mangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.4 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
In the Discourse on Blessings, the Buddha lists extensive learning (bahusacca) and practical skill (sippa) among the highest blessings of a good life.
Like the Kural, it treats basic learning and skills as essential to living well, not as optional ornaments.
Jainism
Jain texts say that Rishabhanatha, the first Tirthankara and first king, taught his daughter Brahmi the art of writing, so that the Brahmi script bears her name. He taught his other daughter Sundari the science of numbers (anka-vidya).
— Jain tradition on Rishabhanatha, Brahmi and Sundari, as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Rishabhanatha' [8]
In Jain sacred history, letters and numbers are the first gifts of civilisation, handed down together by the first teacher of humankind to his two daughters.
Valluvar calls numbers and letters the two eyes of every living person. The Jain story of Rishabha teaching Brahmi letters and Sundari numbers makes the same pair the foundation of all human learning.
Sikhism
Guru Angad, the second Sikh Guru, is remembered as the standardizer of the Gurmukhi script in which much of the Adi Granth is written, and Sikh tradition credits him with setting up schools to teach young people to read and write in their own language, Punjabi, rather than classical Sanskrit.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Angad' (paraphrase) [9]
By fixing a script for the people's own language, Guru Angad made literacy and direct access to the Gurus' hymns possible for ordinary Sikhs.
The Kural calls letters and numbers the two eyes of every living person; Guru Angad's work on Gurmukhi and the schools linked to him reflect the same conviction that basic literacy belongs to everyone.
Greek Mythology
“Aye, and numbers, too, chiefest of sciences, I invented for them, and the combining of letters, creative mother of the Muses' arts, wherewith to hold all things in memory.”
— Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 459–461 (trans. H. W. Smyth) [10]
Chained to his rock, the Titan Prometheus lists the gifts he gave to helpless early humans. Numbers and writing come near the top as the foundations of all other arts.
An exact pairing: Aeschylus names numbers and letters as the twin gifts that made human life truly human, just as Valluvar calls them life's two eyes.
Philosophy
Galileo writes that philosophy is written in the great book of the universe. That book cannot be understood unless one first learns its language and reads its letters, and it is written in the language of mathematics.
— Galileo Galilei, Il Saggiatore (The Assayer), 1623 [11]
For Galileo, reading and mathematics together are the only way to 'see' nature. Without them a person wanders as if in a dark labyrinth.
This parallels the Kural closely: letters and numbers are the faculties through which reality becomes visible.

Real Life Example

Al-Khwarizmi writes on the Hindu art of reckoning
c. 825 · Baghdad, Abbasid Caliphate (now Iraq)
Working at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad under the caliph al-Ma'mun, the mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote a treatise explaining the Hindu place-value numerals, including zero as a placeholder, and how to calculate with them. The Arabic original is lost, but a Latin translation, Algoritmi de numero Indorum, carried these numerals and methods into Europe. The Latin form of his name, Algoritmi, is the origin of the word algorithm.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single book on how to write and work with numbers gave much of the world the counting system it still uses for trade, science and computing.
The kural calls number one of the two eyes of human life, and al-Khwarizmi's treatise opened that eye for whole civilisations.
