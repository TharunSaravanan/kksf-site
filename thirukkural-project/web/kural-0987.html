Kural 987

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

இன்னாசெய் தார்க்கும் இனியவே செய்யாக்கால் என்ன பயத்ததோ சால்பு.
Innaasey thaarkkum iniyave seyyaakkaal enna payaththadho saalbu.

Meaning

What use is perfect character if it does not do kind things even to people who have hurt you?

Elementary

Being nice to people who are nice to you is easy. Valluvar asks a harder question: what if someone was mean to you? He says that being kind back to them is what really shows you are a good person.

University

The couplet is a rhetorical question: if one does not do iniyavai (இனியவை, 'pleasant, kind things') even to those who did innaa (இன்னா, 'painful, harmful things'), of what use (enna payaththadho, என்ன பயத்ததோ) is saalbu? The -um of thaarkkum (தார்க்கும், 'even to those who did') carries the force. It restates for the noble householder the teaching of Innaa Seyyaamai (ch. 32), especially Kural 314 (inna seydhaarai oruththal avar naana nannayam seydhu vidal), and all modern commentators (Varadarasanar, Pappaiah, Karunanidhi, Kesikan) agree on the plain sense. Note that the English section for this couplet on ytamizh.com is misaligned: its 'Couplet Explanation' belongs to Kural 946 on overeating and the line printed as the couplet is Pope's prose explanation, so it should not be relied on here. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not envious but is a kind friend to all living entities, who does not think himself a proprietor and is free from false ego, who is equal in both happiness and distress, who is tolerant, always satisfied, self-controlled, and engaged in devotional service with determination, his mind and intelligence fixed on Me – such a devotee of Mine is very dear to Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.13-14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna describes the dear devotee as one who bears no hatred toward any being and is friendly and forgiving.
This matches the Kural's call for kindness extended to all, including those who give offence.
Christianity
“But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you;”
— Matthew 5:44 (KJV) [4]
Jesus commands his followers to love and do good to those who hate and harm them.
This is almost exactly the Kural's demand to do kindness to those who have done harm.
Islam
“Repel, by [means of] what is best, [their] evil. We are most knowing of what they describe.”
— Qur'an 23:96 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an tells the Prophet to answer the evil done to him with what is best.
This matches the Kural's teaching of returning good for harm.
Judaism
“If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat; If he is thirsty, give him water to drink. You will be heaping live coals on his head, And GOD will reward you.”
— Proverbs 25:21-22 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs instructs that an enemy in need should be fed and given drink.
This is a concrete form of the Kural's demand to do good even to those who have done harm.
Buddhism
“Overcome the angry by non-anger; overcome the wicked by goodness; overcome the miser by generosity; overcome the liar by truth.”
— Dhammapada 223 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that each kind of wrongdoing should be met with its opposite virtue.
Like the Kural, it says that the wicked are to be met with goodness, not with retaliation.
Jainism
In the Jain account of Mahavira's ascetic years, the venomous serpent Chandakaushika bites the meditating Mahavira; instead of anger, Mahavira responds with calm compassion and words of awakening, and the snake gives up violence.
— Hemacandra, Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra, Parva X (Life of Mahavira) (tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
The story shows the Jina returning only compassion to a creature that tried to kill him, and thereby transforming it.
This dramatises the Kural's question: what use is perfection if it does not do good even to one who does harm?
Sikhism
“Fareed, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid's salok, kept in the Guru Granth Sahib, tells the devotee to return good for evil and not to let anger take hold. Sikh tradition honours the same ideal in Bhai Kanhaiya, who gave water to the wounded of both sides.
The Kural asks what perfection is worth if it will not do kindness even to those who have done harm, and Farid's command to answer evil with goodness is that same demand.
Greek Mythology
“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to anyone, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”
— Plato, Crito 49c (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Awaiting execution, Socrates tells Crito that it is never right to return wrong for wrong.
This shares the Kural's rejection of retaliation, though the Kural goes further in demanding active kindness.
Philosophy
“(It is the way of the Tao) to act without (thinking of) acting; to conduct affairs without (feeling the) trouble of them; to taste without discerning any flavour; to consider what is small as great, and a few as many; and to recompense injury with kindness.”
— Laozi, Tao Te Ching 63 (trans. James Legge) [11]
Laozi lists returning kindness for injury as part of the way of the Tao.
This matches the Kural's demand to do good to those who have done harm.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela invites his Rivonia Trial prosecutor to lunch
November 1995 · Pretoria, South Africa
Percy Yutar was the chief prosecutor at the 1963-64 Rivonia Trial, at which Nelson Mandela and his co-accused were sentenced to life imprisonment; South African History Online notes that he had wanted the death sentence for Mandela. In November 1995, as President of South Africa, Mandela invited Yutar to lunch at his presidential residence in Pretoria and told him he had only been doing his duty as a prosecutor. Some of Mandela's former co-accused, including Walter Sisulu and Ahmed Kathrada, declined to attend.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mandela used the power of his office not to settle scores but to extend hospitality to the man who had sought his destruction.
The kural asks what use perfect character is if it does not do kind things even to those who have done harm, and Mandela offered kindness to the prosecutor who had helped send him to prison for decades.
