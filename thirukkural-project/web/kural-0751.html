Kural 751

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

பொருளல் லவரைப் பொருளாகச் செய்யும் பொருளல்லது இல்லை பொருள்.
Porulal lavaraip porulaakach cheyyum porulalladhu illai porul.

Meaning

Nothing but money can make people who count for nothing into people who count for something. There is nothing else like it.

Elementary

Imagine a boy nobody picks for the team. One day he brings a brand-new football, and suddenly everyone wants to play with him. He is the same boy, but now people notice him. Valluvar says money works like that ball: it makes people who were ignored get noticed.

University

The chapter opens the Kural’s treatment of koozh (கூழ், treasury or wealth), one of the six limbs of the state named in kural 381, and it begins with a deliberately startling claim. The couplet plays on three senses of porul: ‘thing of account’, ‘worth’, and ‘wealth’, so that porulallavar are ‘those who are no-thing’, and the second line says there is no porul (thing) except porul (wealth). Parimelazhagar treats the verse as a plain statement of how the world behaves rather than a moral endorsement, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar likewise read it as a description of social esteem, not of true merit. The ytamizh text divides பொருளல்லது இல்லை, while the sandhi-joined text at thirukkural-thamizh reads பொருளல்ல தில்லை; the words are the same.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He that has wealth has friends. He that has wealth has kinsmen. He that has wealth is regarded as a true man in the world. He that has wealth is regarded as a learned man.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section VIII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna urges the grieving Yudhishthira not to renounce the kingdom, arguing that wealth is what makes a person count in the eyes of the world.
Arjuna and Valluvar make the same observation: wealth makes a person be seen as someone of worth.
Christianity
“The poor is hated even of his own neighbour: but the rich hath many friends.”
— Proverbs 14:20 (KJV) [4]
The proverb observes that the poor are shunned even by those nearest them, while the rich attract a crowd of friends.
Like the Kural, it describes how wealth turns an ignored person into a sought-after one.
Islam
“And He found you poor and made [you] self-sufficient.”
— Qur’an 93:8 (Sahih International) [5]
God reminds the Prophet that he once lacked means and that God raised him to sufficiency, as one of several favours listed in the sura.
The verse shares the Kural’s picture of someone raised from nothing by provision, but it credits God rather than wealth itself.
Judaism
“They make a banquet for revelry; wine makes life merry, and money answers every need.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:19 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet notes, with his usual dry realism, that money is what meets every demand of life.
Kohelet and the Kural share the same unsentimental claim that wealth is the one thing that answers for everything else.
Buddhism
“The second thing, having got wealth by legitimate means, is the wish: ‘May fame come to me, together with my family and mentors.’”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.61, Pattakamma Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha tells Anathapindika that the second hard-won desire of a householder is fame, which follows once wealth has been legitimately gained.
The sutta, like the Kural, sees standing and reputation as things that come after wealth.
Jainism
“The value of penance has become visible, birth appears of no value! Look at the holy Harikêśa, the son of a Śvapâka, whose power is so great.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
After the outcaste-born monk Harikesa is first despised and then revered, the onlookers conclude that worth comes from austerity and virtue, not from birth or outward standing.
The Kural says only wealth can turn people of no account into people of account; the Jain story makes the opposite claim, that it is penance that raised a man counted as nothing into one honoured by all.
Sikhism
“Nanak seeks the company of the lowest of the low class, the very lowest of the low. Why should he try to compete with the great?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak deliberately turns away from the esteem the world gives to the wealthy and powerful, and places himself among those whom society counts as nothing.
The Kural observes that only wealth turns people of no account into people who count; Guru Nanak answers that observation by choosing the company of those who count for nothing in the world's eyes.
Greek Mythology
“But if you work, the idle will soon envy you as you grow rich, for fame and renown attend on wealth.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells his brother Perses that honest work leads to riches, and that riches bring fame and renown with them.
Hesiod gives the same reason as Valluvar: esteem goes wherever wealth goes.
Philosophy
In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Adam Smith describes how people are naturally disposed to admire and almost worship the rich and great while neglecting persons of poor and mean condition, and he calls this disposition the great cause of the corruption of our moral sentiments.
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Part I, Section III, Chapter 3 [11]
Smith explains that we tend to honour the wealthy because we sympathise more easily with their good fortune, even when they do not deserve it.
Smith explains why the Kural’s observation holds, while warning that the esteem wealth brings is not moral worth.

Real Life Example

Jacques Coeur, a furrier's son, is ennobled by Charles VII
1441 · Bourges, France
Jacques Coeur, born around 1395 in Bourges as the son of a furrier, built a great trading business that dealt in salt, silks and many other goods. He became argentier (steward of royal expenditure) to King Charles VII, sat on the king's council, was put in charge of tax collection, and was ennobled in 1441. His fortune helped fund the king's reconquest of Normandy in 1450.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A merchant's son with no rank of his own was raised by his wealth into the king's council and the nobility of France.
The kural says nothing but wealth turns those who count for nothing into people who count, and Coeur's fortune carried him from a furrier's household to the king's side.
