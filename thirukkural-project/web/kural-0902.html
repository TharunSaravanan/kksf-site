Kural 902

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

பேணாது பெண்விழைவான் ஆக்கம் பெரியதோர் நாணாக நாணுத் தரும்.
Penaadhu penvizhaivaan aakkam periyadhor naanaaka naanuth tharum.

Meaning

If a man forgets his duties and lives only for his desire for his wife, whatever success he has will bring him great shame that everyone can see.

Elementary

If a class president stops doing the job and just lets his best friend decide everything, the whole class feels let down, and he ends up embarrassed too. Holding a job means doing it yourself.

University

Penaadhu (பேணாது, 'without guarding/caring for') is read by Parimelazhagar as neglecting one's own aanmai (ஆண்மை, manliness, i.e. capacity for independent action), while pen-vizhaivaan (பெண்விழைவான்) is one who desires the 'femininity' of his wife. The aakkam (ஆக்கம், prosperity) he holds becomes a periyadhor naan (பெரியதோர் நாண், a great shame) first for men generally and then for himself; Parimelazhagar explains that because the wife effectively controls the giving and enjoyment of that wealth, the public sees the man's agency as transferred to her. Manakkudavar reads penaadhu as neglecting aram and porul (virtue and wealth). Kalaignar's modern gloss shifts the emphasis to a man who abandons his principles to chase a woman, illustrating the trend among twentieth-century commentators to recast the chapter as a critique of infatuation rather than of women's agency. The text is uniform across ytamizh, thirukkural.net, thirukkural.com and kuraltheni. Contemporary critics note that the verse's notion of 'shame to all men' reflects a gendered honour code of its era.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Dasharatha, bound by boons he had granted his beloved queen Kaikeyi, is compelled to exile his heir Rama, to the dismay of his court and people.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda [5]
A king's indulgence of a favoured queen leads him to act against his own judgment and his kingdom's welfare.
As the Kural warns, the king's power becomes a source of grief and public dismay when it is steered by private attachment.
Christianity
King Lemuel records his mother's counsel not to give his strength to women nor his ways to that which destroys kings.
— Proverbs 31:3 [6]
A queen mother advises her royal son that dissipated attachments ruin rulers.
The same concern as the Kural's address to the ruler; notably, here the warning itself comes from a woman.
Islam
“Beautified for people is the love of that which they desire - of women and sons, heaped-up sums of gold and silver, fine branded horses, and cattle and tilled land. That is the enjoyment of worldly life, but Allāh has with Him the best return.”
— Qur'an 3:14 (Saheeh International) [7]
The Qur'an lists attractions of worldly life that people naturally love, while pointing to a higher return.
The Kural's man is captured by one such attraction to the point of neglecting his responsibilities.
Judaism
“For it came to pass, when Solomon was old, that his wives turned away his heart after other gods: and his heart was not perfect with the LORD his God, as was the heart of David his father.”
— 1 Kings 11:4 (KJV) [6]
The narrator of Kings attributes Solomon's decline to his attachment to his many foreign wives.
As in the Kural, a powerful man's attachment to his wives leads to the loss of his integrity and standing.
Buddhism
“Those who are lust-infatuated fall back into the swirling current (of samsara) like a spider on its self-spun web.”
— Dhammapada 347 (Buddharakkhita) [8]
Infatuation traps a person in a web of his own making.
The Kural's man is likewise caught in a predicament he spun himself, ending in shame.
Jainism
“Getting up in the night they lull the baby asleep like nurses; and though they are ashamed of themselves, they wash the clothes like washermen.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.2.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The text pictures a man who fell for a woman and now does every household chore, aware of his own shame but unable to stop.
This matches Kural 902, where a man who neglects his own standing out of desire for his wife ends up publicly ashamed.
Sikhism
“O sexual desire, you lead the mortals to hell; you make them wander in reincarnation through countless species.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sehskritee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan addresses kaam (lust), one of the five thieves, as a force that drags people away from their true purpose.
The Kural says the man who drops his duties out of desire for his wife brings shame on himself, and Gurbani likewise treats being ruled by desire as a downfall.
Greek Mythology
Hector rebukes his brother Paris, who prefers Helen's chamber to the battlefield, as a disgrace to his family and city.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 3 and 6 [11]
Paris's devotion to Helen while his people fight makes him an object of reproach even to his own brother.
This is the Kural's picture of a man whose attachment brings shame on himself and others.
Philosophy
Plutarch portrays Mark Antony neglecting command and statecraft for Cleopatra, culminating in his flight after her at Actium.
— Plutarch, Life of Antony [12]
The moralist-biographer presents Antony as a capable man whose judgment was captured by passion.
Antony is a classical example of the Kural's warning that power steered by attachment ends in shame.

Real Life Example

Nur Jahan's name appears on Mughal coins
c. 1611-1627 · Mughal Empire, India
The Mughal emperor Jahangir married Nur Jahan in 1611, and in the later years of his reign she took charge of many of his duties and issued farmans, or sovereign orders, in her own name. She was the only Mughal queen to have coins issued in her name, and museum and reference sources describe her as the power behind the throne while Jahangir was frequently incapacitated.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
The emperor's wealth and authority came to be seen, even on the coinage, as directed by his wife rather than by himself.
The kural says the prosperity of a man who abandons his own responsibilities to his wife becomes a public matter of shame, and Nur Jahan's coins made that transfer of agency visible across the empire.
