Kural 1159

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

தொடிற்சுடின் அல்லது காமநோய் போல விடிற்சுடல் ஆற்றுமோ தீ.
Thotirsudin alladhu kaamanoi pola vitirsudal aatrumo thee.

Meaning

Fire burns only when you touch it. Can fire, like love-sickness, burn you even when you move away from it?

Elementary

A campfire only hurts if you touch it; step back and you are safe. But missing someone you love is a strange kind of fire: it hurts more the farther away they are. This couplet points out how odd and how painful that is.

University

The couplet is a vētṟumai (contrast) built on fire: toṭiṉ cuṭiṉ allatu (தொடின் சுடின் அல்லது), ‘other than burning when touched’, set against viṭiṉ cuṭal (விடின் சுடல்), ‘burning when let go of, when apart’. Kāmanōy (காமநோய்), ‘love-sickness’, is the subject that has this power, and āṟṟumō (ஆற்றுமோ), ‘is it able?’, makes it a rhetorical question to which the answer is no. Commentators agree on the reading; Kalaignar expands it to the lovers neither seeing nor touching each other, and Solomon Pappaiah renders it simply. The image is one of the most quoted in the Kāmattuppāl. Pope: ‘Fire burns the hands that touch; but smart of love / Will burn in hearts that far away remove.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda, Radha, separated from Krishna, feels sandal paste, cool breezes and moonlight, normally soothing, as burning fire, and her friend reports to Krishna that she is wasting in the heat of separation.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, cantos on Radha’s viraha (paraphrase) [3]
The Gita Govinda uses the classical viraha theme, in which everything cool burns the separated lover.
Radha experiences the paradox of the Kural: in separation, love burns from a distance, and even cooling things become fire.
Christianity
“For love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame.”
— Song of Solomon 8:6 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs describes love as a fire of the fiercest kind.
The Kural’s love-sickness is just such a fire, so fierce that it burns even at a distance.
Islam
“This plaint of the flute is fire, not mere air. / Let him who lacks this fire be accounted dead!”
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book I, Prologue (tr. E.H. Whinfield) [5]
Rumi says the reed’s lament for its lost home is made of the fire of love, a fire every true lover carries.
The reed burns because it is separated, which is exactly the Kural’s fire that burns at a distance.
Judaism
“Many waters cannot quench love, Neither can the floods drown it.”
— Song of Songs 8:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs says love’s fire cannot be put out by any amount of water.
If love cannot be quenched by floods, it certainly cannot be quenched by distance, which is why it burns the Kural’s heroine from afar.
Buddhism
“There is no fire like lust; there is no grip like hatred; there is no net like delusion; there is no river like craving.”
— Dhammapada 251 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha ranks passion as the greatest of fires.
The Kural agrees that love’s fire surpasses real fire; the Dhammapada sees this as reason to put it out.
Jainism
“Happy are we, happy live we who call nothing our own; when Mithilâ is on fire, nothing is burned that belongs to me.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Nami, having renounced his kingdom, is untouched when told his city is burning, because he no longer claims anything as his own.
Nami is the reverse of the Kural’s lover: through non-attachment, fire cannot burn him even up close, while love burns her even from afar.
Sikhism
“The separation of my beloved is not only appearing in my body, like the fire of jungle, but all these dainty dishes and dresses instead of giving me comfort are acting like oil in raising the intensity of fire and consequently my sufferings.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabitt Savaiye 668, English translation as given in the BaniDB database [9]
Bhai Gurdas describes separation from the Beloved as a forest fire inside the body, which comforts only feed like oil. His Kabitt Savaiye are early exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural says ordinary fire burns only when touched, but love-sickness burns when one is far away; Bhai Gurdas likewise describes separation itself as a fire that blazes all the more in the Beloved's absence.
Greek Mythology
Sappho describes the symptoms of love at the sight of the beloved: her tongue breaks, a thin fire runs beneath her skin, her eyes see nothing, her ears hum, and she feels close to death.
— Sappho, Fragment 31 (paraphrase) [10]
Sappho’s poem is the classical description of love as a fire felt through the whole body.
Sappho’s fire burns at the sight of the beloved; the Kural adds that it burns even more in her absence.
Philosophy
Plato’s Socrates describes the lover’s soul when separated from the beloved: the passages through which the wings grow dry up and close, and the soul is stung and goaded in agony until the sight of the beloved brings relief.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251c-e (paraphrase) [11]
In the Phaedrus, the absence of the beloved is a painful, burning torment, and his presence is the only cure.
Plato gives the Kural’s paradox a full account: love wounds most when the beloved is away.

Real Life Example

Nizami of Ganja writes Layla and Majnun
1188 · Ganja (now in Azerbaijan)
In 1188 the Persian poet Nizami of Ganja, working under a commission from the Shirvanshah court, completed Layla and Majnun as part of his Khamsa, or Quintet. The poem tells of two young lovers in the Arabian desert who are forcibly separated when Layla's father refuses the marriage. Qays, the hero, flees into the desert, where he wanders singing of his love and longing and becomes known as Majnun, the one driven mad by love.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nizami's poem became one of the best-known love stories of the Persian and Islamic world, and its central image is a love that burns hotter the farther the lovers are kept apart.
The kural says that fire burns only those who touch it, while love burns even those who move away from it, and Majnun, consumed by love far off in the desert, is that idea told as a story.
