Kural 1003

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

ஈட்டம் இவறி இசைவேண்டா ஆடவர் தோற்றம் நிலக்குப் பொறை.
Eettam Ivari Isaiventaa Aatavar Thotram Nilakkup Porai

Meaning

People who only want to pile up money and do not care about a good name are just a weight on the earth.

Elementary

Think of a squirrel that hides a thousand nuts and never lets another animal have one, and no one in the forest has a kind word for it. Valluvar says people who only collect and collect, and never do anything good that others would remember, are just heavy weight on the ground.

University

The couplet sets two objects of desire against each other: eettam (ஈட்டம், 'accumulation', from ஈட்டு- 'to earn, amass') and isai (இசை, 'fame, renown'), which in the Kural is earned chiefly by giving (compare Pugazh, ch. 24, beginning 'ஈதல் இசைபட வாழ்தல்'). Those who 'cling to accumulation and do not want renown' have a thoatram (தோற்றம், 'appearance, birth, existence') that is poRai (பொறை, 'burden, weight') to the earth; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read தோற்றம் as 'being born and living', Pope as appearing 'on the stage of being'. Solomon Pappaiah adds a competitive nuance: they want to amass more than others. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads இசை as 'interest/inclination' (இசைவு) beyond acquiring wealth, a minority interpretation. The 'burden of the earth' image echoes Kural 239, where the land bearing a body without fame loses its yield. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna says that one who does not take part in the cycle of sacrifice and sharing set in motion for the world, but lives only for the satisfaction of the senses, lives in vain.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.16 [3]
A life that only takes and never contributes back to the common order is declared futile.
Like the Kural, it judges the purely acquisitive life a dead weight on the world.
Christianity
Jesus tells of a fig tree that bore no fruit for three years; the owner orders it cut down, asking why it should take up the ground, and the gardener pleads for one more year.
— Luke 13:6–9 (Parable of the Barren Fig Tree) [4]
A barren life that produces nothing for others merely occupies space.
The parable's tree that only 'cumbers the ground' is the Kural's man who is a burden to the earth.
Islam
The Qur'an warns that rivalry in piling up worldly increase distracts people until they reach their graves, when they will surely come to know its emptiness.
— Qur'an 102:1–4 (At-Takathur) [5]
Competitive accumulation is portrayed as a diversion that fills a whole life and leaves nothing worthwhile.
It matches the Kural's men obsessed with accumulation and indifferent to true honour.
Judaism
Rabbi Shimon teaches that there are three crowns, of Torah, of priesthood and of royalty, but the crown of a good name surpasses them all.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 4:13 [6]
A good name earned by one's deeds is ranked above every inherited dignity.
The Kural condemns those who prize hoarding over exactly this good name.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that misers do not go to heavenly realms and fools do not praise generosity, but the wise person rejoices in giving and is happy hereafter.
— Dhammapada 177 [7]
The verse sets the stingy fool against the generous wise person whose giving brings joy and good standing.
The Kural's hoarders who care nothing for renown are the Dhammapada's misers who scorn generosity.
Jainism
“Those who have, to the best of their ability, practised to any extent trifling acts of charity, will in another birth become great; while those who, when they have become wealthy, say, We will give alms some time or other, shall be punished and shall perish from all the sea-surrounded earth.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 28 ('Illiberality'), st. 2 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain poet contrasts small, real generosity with the wealthy man's endless postponement of giving.
Like the Kural, it sees the wealthy non-giver as having no rightful place on the earth.
Sikhism
“Earning a thousand, he runs after a hundred thousand. Satisfaction is not obtained by chasing after Maya.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278-279, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan describes the restless accumulator who earns a thousand and at once runs after a hundred thousand, never satisfied.
The Kural condemns people greedy only for piling up wealth; Guru Arjan pictures that endless piling up and shows that it never brings contentment.
Greek Mythology
A fragment of the epic Cypria tells that Zeus, seeing the broad-bosomed Earth weighed down by the countless tribes of men, took pity on her and resolved to lighten her burden through the Trojan War.
— Cypria, fragment 1 (preserved in the scholia to Iliad 1.5) [10]
Greek myth imagines humanity itself as a weight the Earth can groan under.
It shares the Kural's image of people whose existence is a burden to the earth, though not its moral about hoarding.
Philosophy
Cicero argues that liberality and beneficence win people's goodwill and lasting glory, while wealth hoarded or spent only on oneself earns none.
— Cicero, De Officiis II.52–64 [11]
For Cicero, true and lasting renown is gained by using one's means to help others.
The Kural blames exactly those who pursue accumulation and neglect the renown that giving would bring.

Real Life Example

Verres plunders Sicily and is driven into exile
73-70 BCE · Sicily and Rome, Roman Republic (now Italy)
Gaius Verres governed the Roman province of Sicily from 73 to 71 BCE and used his office to extort money and seize works of art and other valuables from its people. In 70 BCE the Sicilians engaged Marcus Tullius Cicero to prosecute him for extortion; Cicero gathered evidence across the island in a few weeks and opened the case so forcefully that Verres's advocate, Hortensius, gave up the defence. Verres left Rome for exile before the trial ended, and Cicero's published speeches made his name a byword for official greed.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Verres pursued gain with no concern for his reputation, and what survives of him is a lasting record of disgrace.
The kural says that men greedy only for piling up wealth, with no care for an honourable name, are a burden to the earth, and Verres enriched himself at the expense of an entire province.
