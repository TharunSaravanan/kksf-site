Kural 1256

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

செற்றவர் பின்சேறல் வேண்டி அளித்தரோ எற்றென்னை உற்ற துயர்.
Setravar pinsaeral vaendi aliththaroa etrennai utra thuyar.

Meaning

How strange is this sorrow that has taken hold of me! It makes me want to follow the very one who turned away from me.

Elementary

Sometimes when a friend goes away, we feel so sad that we want to run after them, even if they did not say a kind goodbye. The heroine wonders at this sadness: it is so strong that it makes her want to follow him anyway.

University

The heroine marvels at her own grief: eṟṟu (‘of what kind?’) and the exclamatory aḷittarō, which can be heard ironically as ‘how gracious!’, frame a sorrow (tuyar) that makes her wish to go after (piṉ cēṟal) ceṟṟavar, the one who has scorned or left her. Pope’s rendering on ytamizh.com (‘My grief how full of grace, I pray you see! / It seeks to follow him that hateth me’) captures this irony. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan treat tuyar as the kāmanōy (love-sickness) itself, and Solomon Pappaiah calls it exceedingly cruel because it drives her to follow. The couplet continues 1255: having denied that she has the dignity not to follow, the heroine now wonders at the grief that compels her. The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Krishna suddenly disappears from the rasa dance, the gopis, stricken with grief, search the forest for him, asking the trees and creepers whether they have seen him pass.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.30 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Bhagavata shows women whose grief at their beloved’s withdrawal sends them looking for him everywhere.
Like the Kural’s heroine, the gopis are driven by grief to follow the one who has left them.
Christianity
“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone: my soul failed when he spake: I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Solomon 5:6 (KJV) [4]
When the bride finally opens the door, her beloved has gone, and she goes searching and calling for him.
The Kural’s heroine also longs to follow the one who has turned away, and wonders at the grief that drives her.
Islam
“Barira’s husband was a slave called Mughith, as if I am seeing him now, going behind Barira and weeping with his tears flowing down his beard. The Prophet (ﷺ) said to ’Abbas, ‘O ’Abbas! are you not astonished at the love of Mughith for Barira and the hatred of Barira for Mughith?’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5283 (sunnah.com) [5]
After Barira, newly freed, chose to leave her marriage, her husband Mughith followed her through the streets of Medina in tears.
Mughith is the Kural’s lover exactly: grief makes him follow the one who has turned away, and the Prophet himself remarks on the wonder of it.
Judaism
“The watchmen that go about the city found me, they smote me, they wounded me; the keepers of the walls took away my mantle from me.”
— Song of Songs 5:7 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Searching through the city for the beloved who has gone, the woman of the Song is beaten and stripped of her mantle, yet keeps searching.
The Kural’s heroine also marvels at a grief that makes her follow the one who left, whatever it costs her.
Buddhism
“From lust springs grief, from lust springs fear. For one who is wholly free from craving there is no grief; whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 215 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha traces grief back to desire and attachment.
The Kural’s heroine experiences what this verse names: her grief comes from a longing that drives her after the one who has gone.
Jainism
“Râgîmatî thought: ‘Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him! it is better I should turn nun.’”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Forsaken by Arishtanemi on their wedding day, Rajimati follows him into renunciation and later sets out for Mount Raivataka, where he had gone.
Rajimati’s grief, like the heroine’s, makes her follow the one who left her, though she follows him on the path of renunciation.
Sikhism
“My mind and body are so thirsty for the Blessed Vision of His Darshan. Won't someone please come and lead me to him, O my mother.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 135, Raag Maajh, Barah Maha, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha, Guru Arjan describes a longing so strong that the bride begs anyone at all to take her to her Beloved.
The Kural's wife marvels at the grief that makes her long to follow the very one who left her; Guru Arjan's bride is likewise driven by her ache to seek out the absent Beloved.
Greek Mythology
Ovid’s Medea, falling in love with Jason against her duty to her father, argues with herself and confesses that she sees the better course and approves it, yet follows the worse.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.17-21 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid captures a lover driven by a force she recognizes as harmful to herself.
The Kural’s heroine also wonders at a grief that drives her where her dignity tells her not to go.
Philosophy
Pausanias says that a lover may beg and pray, swear oaths, sleep on the beloved’s doorstep and submit to a slavery no slave would accept, and yet gods and men excuse him because he is in love.
— Plato, Symposium 183a-b (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.R.M. Lamb) [11]
Plato’s speaker describes how lovers do humiliating things that would disgrace anyone not in love.
The Kural’s heroine wonders at the same humbling power: her grief makes her want to follow one who has turned away.

Real Life Example

Charlotte Bronte's letters to Constantin Heger
1844-1845 · Haworth, England, and Brussels, Belgium
After returning home from Brussels in 1844, Charlotte Bronte wrote a series of letters to Constantin Heger, her former teacher there. He replied rarely and, by the end of 1845, had stopped answering, while her letters grew more desperate; four survive, the last dated 18 November 1845. The letters were torn up at some point and later mended, and Heger's children gave them to the British Museum in 1913; they are now in the British Library.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters show a proud and private writer unable to stop reaching out to someone who had withdrawn from her.
The kural's heroine marvels at a strange grief that makes her long to follow the one who has turned away; Bronte's letters to a silent Heger are a documented case of just such longing.
