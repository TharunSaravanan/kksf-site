Kural 296

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

பொய்யாமை அன்ன புகழில்லை எய்யாமை எல்லா அறமுந் தரும்.
Poyyaamai anna pugazhillai eyyaamai Ellaa aramum tharum.

Meaning

No praise is as great as the praise for never lying. Without any extra effort, it brings every other good quality with it.

Elementary

Being known as someone who never lies is the best reputation you can have. And it’s like a magnet — once you are truthful, other good habits, like fairness and kindness, come to you easily, without you even trying hard.

University

The couplet makes two claims: truthfulness is the supreme source of pugazh (fame, cf. ch. 24), and it ‘yields every dharma’ (ellaa aramum tharum). The pivot word eyyaamai is read in two ways: Parimelazhagar-line commentators take it as ‘without suffering/strain’ — truthfulness produces all virtue effortlessly, unlike painful austerity; Varadarasan and Pappaiah read it as ‘without one’s knowing it,’ i.e., the virtues accrue unnoticed. Either way the claim is that veracity is a generative, master virtue from which the others follow — a thesis close to the Islamic hadith that truthfulness leads to righteousness.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The true prevails, not the untrue; by the true the path is laid out, the way of the gods.”
— Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.6 (tr. F. Max Müller) [3]
The Mundaka Upanishad — source of India’s national motto ‘Satyameva Jayate’ — says truth wins, and that truth itself lays down the road to the divine.
Truth that triumphs and opens the path to every good echoes the Kural’s truth that brings unmatched fame and all virtues.
Christianity
“Wherefore putting away lying, speak every man truth with his neighbour: for we are members one of another.”
— Ephesians 4:25 (KJV) [4]
Paul makes truth-telling the first step of the new life, because believers belong to one another like parts of one body.
Truthfulness as the foundation from which the rest of the moral life grows.
Islam
“…the truthful men and truthful women… for them Allāh has prepared forgiveness and a great reward.”
— Qur’an 33:35 (Sahih International) [5]
Among the qualities God praises in believing men and women, truthfulness appears alongside patience, charity and fasting, all promised a great reward.
Truthfulness earns the highest honour — divine praise and reward.
Judaism
“Rabban Shimon ben Gamaliel used to say: on three things does the world stand: On justice, on truth and on peace.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:18 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah names truth as one of the three pillars that hold up the whole world.
Truth as a foundation supporting everything else — the Kural’s ‘brings all virtues.’
Buddhism
“He who utters gentle, instructive and truthful words, who imprecates none — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 408 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha gives truthful, gentle speech as one mark of the true brahmin, the person worthy of the highest honour.
Truthful speech earns the highest title of respect.
Jainism
“Truthfulness indeed is tapa (penance). In truthfulness do reside self-restraint and all other virtues. Just as the fish can live only in the sea, so can all other virtues reside in Truthfulness alone.”
— Bhagavatī Ārādhanā 842 (Śivārya), English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [8]
The Bhagavatī Ārādhanā calls truthfulness the element that all the other virtues live in, as fish live in the sea.
Valluvar says that never lying brings every other virtue with it, without any strain. The Jain verse says that all virtues live in truthfulness, which is the same claim.
Sikhism
“Truth is the medicine for all; it removes and washes away our sins.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 468, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the same salok of Asa di Vaar on what true living means, Guru Nanak calls Truth a cure for every ill, cleansing the person of wrongdoing.
The Kural says truthfulness brings every other virtue with it, without strain. Guru Nanak makes truth the single remedy that heals everything else.
Greek Mythology
“Muse, you and the daughter of Zeus, unforgetting Truth: with the hand that puts things right, keep from me the blame for lying, for wronging my friend.”
— Pindar, Olympian 10.3-6 (tr. Diane Arnson Svarlien) [10]
Pindar calls on Aletheia (Truth), daughter of Zeus, to protect his reputation from the blame of a broken promise.
In Greek thought Truth is a goddess who guards a person’s good name — the Kural’s link between truth and fame.
Philosophy
“And falsehood is in itself mean and culpable, and truth noble and worthy of praise.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.7 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle states that truthfulness is by its very nature noble and deserves praise.
Truth is ‘worthy of praise’ in itself — the Kural’s ‘no fame like truthfulness.’

Real Life Example

Raja Harishchandra, the story of the truthful king, opens India's film industry
3 May 1913 · Coronation Cinema, Girgaon, Bombay (Mumbai), India
Dadasaheb Phalke's silent film Raja Harishchandra, widely regarded as the first full-length Indian feature film, was released at the Coronation Cinema in Bombay on 3 May 1913. It told the legend of King Harishchandra, who gave up his kingdom and family to keep his word to the sage Vishwamitra and was finally restored. Because no women would take the roles, Phalke cast men in the female parts, and the film was a great success; only fragments survive today.
— Sources: [12] [13]
When India's first feature film needed a story that every audience would know and honour, it turned to the legend of a king remembered above all for never breaking faith with the truth.
The kural says there is no fame like that of never lying, and the enduring fame of the truthful Harishchandra was strong enough to launch an entire national cinema.
