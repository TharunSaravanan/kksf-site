Kural 16

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

விசும்பின் துளிவீழின் அல்லால்மற் றாங்கே பசும்புல் தலைகாண்பு அரிது.
Visumbin thulivizhin allaal matraange, Pasumpul thalaikaanbu aridhu.

Meaning

Unless rain falls from the sky, you will hardly see even the tip of a blade of green grass on the earth.

Elementary

Put a seed in a pot of soil and give it no water at all. Nothing happens — not even a tiny green shoot. This kural says that is what the whole world would look like without rain: not one blade of grass anywhere.

University

விசும்பு (visumbu) is the open expanse of sky rather than the cloud, so the drop is imagined falling from the whole heavens at once. The argument is a fortiori and depends on Tamil biological classification: பசும்புல் (pasumpul), green grass, is the standard example of an ஓரறிவு உயிர் — a one-sense living being, the humblest grade of life in the Tamil-Jain scheme — so if even that cannot appear, nothing above it can. தலை here means the tip or head of the blade, sharpening the claim from "no grass" to "not even the tip of grass." The verse's spare, almost photographic minimalism has made it one of the most quoted lines of the chapter in Tamil environmental writing.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Forth burst the winds, down come the lightning-flashes: the plants shoot up, the realm of light is streaming. Food springs abundant for all living creatures, what time Parjanya quickens earth with moisture.”
— Rigveda 5.83.4 (Griffith translation) [4]
The Rigveda's hymn to Parjanya, god of the rain-cloud, describes plants springing up the moment the storm breaks and moisture reaches the soil.
It states the positive form of the Kural's negative claim: the sprouting of plants begins exactly when the drops fall.
Christianity
“And every plant of the field before it was in the earth, and every herb of the field before it grew: for the LORD God had not caused it to rain upon the earth, and there was not a man to till the ground.”
— Genesis 2:5 (KJV) [5]
Genesis explains the pre-vegetative state of the newly made world by a single fact: it had not yet rained.
This is precisely the Kural's proposition — no drops from above, therefore no green thing below.
Islam
“And you see the earth barren, but when We send down upon it rain, it quivers and swells and grows [something] of every beautiful kind.”
— Qur'an 22:5, Al-Hajj (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an contrasts the visibly dead, barren ground with the same ground stirring into growth the moment rain reaches it.
Both verses make the visible presence or absence of green growth the direct index of whether rain has fallen.
Judaism
“There is not a single blade of grass that does not have a constellation in the firmament that strikes it and says to it: 'Grow.'”
— Bereshit Rabbah 10:6 [7]
Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi's midrash makes the growth of every individual blade of grass depend on a specific influence descending from the heavens.
Like the Kural it insists that even the humblest blade of grass is entirely dependent on something coming down from the sky.
Buddhism
“cows graze on the lush meadow grass … Right then a vast cloud rained forth, soaking the uplands and valleys.”
— Dhaniya Sutta, Sutta Nipāta 1.2 (Bhikkhu Sujato translation) [8]
In the Dhaniya Sutta a wealthy cattleman and the Buddha trade verses each ending "so rain forth, heavens, if you wish," and at the close a great cloud does exactly that, soaking the grazing land the herdsman depends on.
It shares the Kural's chain of rain to green pasture, though the sutta's real interest is in what makes a person ready for the rain.
Jainism
“'For in the rainy season living beings, grass, seeds, mildew, and sprouts frequently come forth.'”
— Kalpa Sutra, Rules for Yatis §55 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The Kalpa Sutra's rules for the monsoon retreat tell monks to inspect the ground with special care in the rains, because that is when grass and countless small lives spring up everywhere.
It confirms from the Jain side the Kural's observation that grass appears only when rain falls, and for Jains each such blade is itself a living, one-sensed soul to be protected.
Sikhism
“First, there is life in the water, by which everything else is made green.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 472, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Arguing against ideas of ritual impurity, Guru Nanak notes that life begins in water and that water is what turns everything green.
This is the Kural's point in one line: without water from the sky, not even a blade of grass turns green.
Greek Mythology
“a most dreadful and cruel year for mankind over the all-nourishing earth: the ground would not make the seed sprout, for rich-crowned Demeter kept it hid”
— Homeric Hymn 2, To Demeter, lines 305–309 (Evelyn-White translation) [11]
Grieving for Persephone, Demeter suspends the earth's fertility entirely, so that sown seed simply does not come up.
It is the Kural's image of an earth on which not even a green shoot can be seen.
Philosophy
“Lo, the rains perish which Ether-father throws / Down to the bosom of Earth-mother; but then / Upsprings the shining grain, and boughs are green / Amid the trees, and trees themselves wax big / And lade themselves with fruits”
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 1.250–254 (W. E. Leonard translation, 1916) [12]
Lucretius uses rainfall to demonstrate his atomist thesis that nothing is annihilated: the rain vanishes, but reappears transformed as crops and foliage.
It gives the Kural's claim a physical mechanism — the green blade is literally the rain in another form.

Real Life Example

The Sahel drought
1968-1974 · West African Sahel (including Mauritania, Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso and Chad)
From 1968 the rains in the West African Sahel deteriorated, and only in the 1974 rainy season did near-normal conditions return. The long drought led to the near-total failure of crops and the loss of an estimated 50 to 70 percent of cattle, with the pastoral grazing zones of the northern Sahel hit hardest. Large numbers of people died or were displaced by the resulting food shortages.
— Sources: [13] [14]
When the rains failed, the grasslands that supported herds across the Sahel withered, and a whole pastoral way of life collapsed with them.
The kural says that without drops from the sky not even the tip of a blade of grass will be seen, and the Sahel's dying pastures showed the humblest green life vanishing first.
