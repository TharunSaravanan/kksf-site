Kural 861

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

வலியார்க்கு மாறேற்றல் ஓம்புக ஓம்பா மெலியார்மேல் மேக பகை.
Valiyaarkku maaretral ombuka ombaa meliyaarmel meka pakai.

Meaning

Do not pick fights with people stronger than you. But do not be slow to stand against people who are weaker.

Elementary

Imagine a small boat and a giant ship. A smart small boat does not try to bump the giant ship, because it would sink. Valluvar says: pick your contests wisely, and never start a fight you cannot possibly win.

University

The chapter title pakai maatchi (பகை மாட்சி) means the ‘excellence’ or right conduct of enmity: not whether a ruler will have enemies, but which enmities are worth having. The couplet turns on a deliberate wordplay between ombuka (ஓம்புக, ‘guard against, avoid’) and ombaa (ஓம்பா, ‘without avoiding’), and on meka (மேக, from mevu, ‘desire, take up’). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read it as plain statecraft: avoid opposing the stronger, and willingly take up enmity against the weaker; Solomon Pappaiah sharpens it to ‘if weaker, attack at once’. Mu. Karunanidhi (Kalaignar) notably inverts the moral, praising the one who spares the weak and fights the strong as the true glory of enmity, a modern ethical rereading rather than the traditional gloss. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthashastra (Book 7) lays down the basic rule of interstate policy: a king who is inferior in power to another should make peace with him, while one who is superior should make war.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 7.1 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
The Arthashastra treats war and peace as calculations of relative strength rather than matters of pride.
It is the same rule as the Kural: avoid the stronger, take on the weaker.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand? Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace.”
— Luke 14:31-32 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses the image of a sensible king who measures his strength before battle and seeks peace if he is outnumbered.
The weaker king in the parable does exactly what Valluvar advises: he does not challenge the stronger.
Islam
At Hudaybiyyah (628 CE) the Prophet Muhammad accepted a treaty with the Quraysh on terms many of his companions felt were unfavourable, choosing a truce over a confrontation at that moment.
— Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari 2731-2732 (Book of Conditions) [5]
The early Muslim community, not yet in a position to prevail at Mecca, accepted peace, and the truce later opened the way to its growth.
Like the Kural, it shows the wisdom of not pressing a contest at an unfavourable moment.
Judaism
Jeremiah urges King Zedekiah to submit to the yoke of the king of Babylon and live, rather than rebel against an overwhelmingly stronger empire; Zedekiah’s revolt ends in the fall of Jerusalem.
— Jeremiah 27:12-13; 2 Kings 25 (Tanakh) [6]
The prophet counsels against defying a power that cannot be resisted, and the king who ignored him brought ruin on his city.
It is a tragic illustration of the first half of the Kural: do not set yourself against the stronger.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that victory breeds enmity and the defeated live in pain, while the peaceful live happily, having given up both victory and defeat.
— Dhammapada 201 [7]
The Buddha looks past the calculation of winning and losing to the suffering that every conflict produces.
It offers a contrasting view: where Valluvar teaches which fights to pick, the Buddha questions picking fights at all.
Jainism
“O king, bring into subjection all princes who do not acknowledge you; thus you will be a true Kshattriya.” … “Though a man should conquer thousands and thousands of valiant (foes), greater will be his victory if he conquers nobody but himself.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.32 and 9.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Indra, disguised as a Brahmin, urges the renouncing King Nami to subdue the weaker princes around him as a true warrior should. Nami refuses and says the only conquest worth seeking is conquest of oneself.
The Kural advises a ruler to pick fights only with weaker foes, and the Jain text puts that same advice in Indra's mouth and has the saint reject it, so the passage answers the Kural's strategy rather than echoing it.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir defines the true hero not by the strength of the foe he chooses but by the cause he fights for.
The Kural advises avoiding stronger foes and taking on weaker ones; Gurbani measures the warrior instead by the righteousness of his cause, whatever the odds.
Greek Mythology
In the Melian Dialogue the Athenian envoys tell the people of Melos that, between unequal powers, the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must; Melos resists and is destroyed.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 5.84-116 [10]
Thucydides dramatises the harsh logic of power: a small state that defies a great one is crushed.
Melos is the classic case of a weaker party ignoring the Kural’s first warning.
Philosophy
Sun Tzu advises that a general equal in strength may give battle, one slightly inferior should avoid the enemy, and one unequal in every way should withdraw.
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War III.9-10 (tr. Lionel Giles) [11]
The Art of War makes the decision to fight depend entirely on a sober comparison of forces.
Sun Tzu and Valluvar give the same rule: never engage a stronger foe.

Real Life Example

Ranjit Singh accepts the Sutlej frontier with the British
25 April 1809 · Amritsar, Punjab, India
Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Sikh kingdom of Punjab, at first rejected the East India Company's terms and crossed the Sutlej to take territory on its southern bank, and both sides began preparing for war when the British sent a force under David Ochterlony to Ludhiana. He then signed the Treaty of Amritsar, which fixed his frontier broadly along the Sutlej while leaving him free to act north of the river. Britannica notes that the episode gave him great respect for the Company's disciplined troops and a determination never to fight them, and that his later conquests were to the west and north.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A rising ruler chose not to fight a stronger power, and used the security of that frontier to expand where his rivals were weaker.
The kural advises avoiding enmity with the stronger while not shrinking from contests with the weaker, which is exactly the course Ranjit Singh followed after 1809.
