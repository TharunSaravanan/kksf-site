Kural 371

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

ஆகூழால் தோன்றும் அசைவின்மை கைப்பொருள் போகூழால் தோன்றும் மடி.
Aakoozhaal thondrum asaivinmai kaipporul Pokoozhaal thondrum madi.

Meaning

When fate is ready to bring you riches, it makes you work hard without tiring. When fate is ready to take riches away, it makes you lazy.

Elementary

Imagine two kids with the same homework. One suddenly feels full of energy and finishes it; the other feels sleepy and gives up. This kural says that even the energy or the sleepiness you feel can be part of your fate: good fortune shows up as hard work, and bad fortune shows up as laziness.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a subtle psychological claim: fate (ஊழ், uzh) does not merely deliver outcomes from outside but works through the agent’s own dispositions. ஆகூழ் (aakuzh, ‘fate of gaining’) manifests as அசைவின்மை (unflagging perseverance), while போகூழ் (pokuzh, ‘fate of losing’) manifests as மடி (sloth). This neatly reconciles the chapter with the Kural’s many praises of effort elsewhere (e.g. the later chapters on ஆள்வினையுடைமை): effort is praised, yet the very capacity to make effort is itself read as a sign of a favourable destiny. Commentators such as Parimelazhagar treat ஊழ் as the ripened fruit of past deeds (பழவினை), which makes the idea closer to karmic causation than to arbitrary luck. The structure anticipates the Stoic ‘co-fated’ reply to the Lazy Argument: outcome and effort are fated together, so fatalism is no excuse for idleness.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The body, the doer (soul), the various senses, the many kinds of efforts, and Divine Providence—these are the five factors of action.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.14 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna lists five causes behind every action; human effort is one of them, but daiva (Providence or fate) is named as the decisive fifth.
Like the Kural, the Gita places effort and fate side by side: effort is real, but it operates inside a larger, destined frame.
Christianity
“For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure.”
— Philippians 2:13 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers that even their wanting to act and their actual doing are worked in them by God.
The Kural says fate shows up as inner energy; Paul says the very will to act is given from above.
Islam
“As for he who gives and fears Allāh and believes in the best [reward], We will ease him toward ease. But as for he who withholds and considers himself free of need and denies the best [reward], We will ease him toward difficulty.”
— Qur'an 92:5-10 (Saheeh International) [5]
God ‘eases’ each person along the path matching their disposition, making one path smooth and the other hard.
Both texts describe a higher power smoothing a person’s road toward either flourishing or decline.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that everything is in the hands of Heaven except the fear of Heaven.
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 33b [6]
The Sages hold that circumstances such as wealth and fortune are set by Heaven, while a person’s reverence and moral choice remain their own.
A partial contrast: the Kural puts even effort and sloth under fate, whereas the Talmud keeps inner moral choice outside it.
Buddhism
The Buddha rejects the view that whatever a person experiences is entirely caused by what was done in the past, arguing that such a view would leave no room for desire, effort, or the sense of what should and should not be done.
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.61 (Titthayatana Sutta) [7]
The Buddha argues that pure past-action determinism destroys the motivation to act rightly now, so he rejects it.
A deliberate contrast: the Kural lets fate produce effort or sloth, while the Buddha refuses to let past karma explain away present effort.
Jainism
“The second–lābha antarāya–hinders him from attaining any gain even though he sets his heart on it. … The fifth–vīrya antarāya–prevents effort or exertion, although he wants to make an effort or exert himself.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 8.13, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Jain karma theory has an 'obstructive' karma (antarāya), one subtype of which blocks gain and another blocks energy or exertion. When it ripens, a person can want to strive and still find the effort will not come. In Jainism this karma is subtle matter the soul itself has bound, not a decree from outside.
Valluvar says the fate of gain shows itself as tireless effort and the fate of loss as idleness. Pūjyapāda likewise explains failures of both gain and exertion by karma working within the person's own capacities.
Sikhism
“Make the effort, and you shall live; practicing it, you shall enjoy peace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 522, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Gujri ki Vaar Guru Arjan urges udam, steady effort on the path of the Name, and promises that such effort brings true life and peace.
The Kural says a fortune bent on giving shows itself as tireless effort; Guru Arjan likewise treats unflagging effort as the way blessings arrive, though he presents it as a duty to take up rather than a sign that fortune has already decided.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod describes the three Moirai, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos, as the powers who give mortals at their birth both good and evil to have.
— Hesiod, Theogony 217-219 [10]
In Greek myth, each person’s share (moira) of good and bad is spun and allotted at birth.
Like ஆகூழ் and போகூழ், the Moirai hand out a lot of gain or loss before any effort is made.
Philosophy
Cicero reports the ‘Lazy Argument’: if recovering from illness is fated, calling the doctor is pointless. Chrysippus answered that some events are ‘co-fated’: recovery and calling the doctor are fated together.
— Cicero, De Fato 28-30 [11]
The Stoics held that fate works through human actions, not around them, so fate is no excuse for doing nothing.
Valluvar makes the same move: fate comes to pass through effort (or through its absence).

Real Life Example

Ino Tadataka begins his survey of Japan at fifty-five
1800-1816 · Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku and Kyushu, Japan
In 1800, at the age of 55, Ino Tadataka obtained the shogunate's permission to survey the southeastern coast of Hokkaido, partly in order to work out the length of a degree of latitude. His maps impressed the shogunate, which sent him on survey after survey along the coasts of Japan over the next sixteen years, during which he spent more than 3,700 days on the road and covered tens of thousands of kilometres on foot. He died in 1818, and the completed map of Japan based on his surveys was presented to the shogunate in 1821.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ino began the work for which he is remembered at an age when most men of his time had retired, and his tireless persistence produced the first accurate map of Japan.
The kural says that when fortune is set on bringing gain it shows itself as unflagging effort, and Ino's late-life energy, which carried him around the whole country, is the kind of perseverance the couplet describes.
