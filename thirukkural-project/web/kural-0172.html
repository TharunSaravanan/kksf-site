Kural 172

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

படுபயன் வெஃகிப் பழிப்படுவ செய்யார் நடுவன்மை நாணு பவர்.
Padupayan vehkip pazhippaduva seyyaar Naduvanmai naanu pavar.

Meaning

People who are ashamed to be unfair will never do shameful things just because they want the profit those things could bring.

Elementary

Suppose you could win a game by quietly peeking at someone’s cards. A person who would feel embarrassed to cheat just won’t do it, even if it means winning. This kural says that feeling of shame protects you from doing wrong for a prize.

University

Where kural 171 threatened consequences, 172 names the internal safeguard: நாண் (naan), a moral sense of shame or modesty, here specifically shame at நடுவன்மை (naduvanmai, “non-equity”). The phrase படுபயன் (padupayan) denotes the gain that “falls” to one, the benefit that would accrue from taking another’s property, and பழிப்படுவ (pazhippaduva) are acts that incur public blame. Valluvar’s psychology is notable: the person of integrity is restrained less by fear of punishment than by an internalised aversion to injustice, a theme developed fully in chapter 102 (நாணுடைமை). This aligns with Gita ethics, in which modesty (Sanskrit hri) and the reflex of weighing gain against righteousness are marks of a mature moral agent.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…compassion and freedom from covetousness; gentleness, modesty and steady determination … these transcendental qualities, O son of Bharata, belong to godly men endowed with divine nature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna lists the qualities of a divinely-natured person; two of them are freedom from covetousness (aloluptvam) and modesty, or a sense of shame at wrongdoing (hri).
The Kural pairs the same two things: shame at wrongdoing, and therefore no coveting.
Christianity
“But fornication, and all uncleanness, or covetousness, let it not be once named among you, as becometh saints.”
— Ephesians 5:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers that covetousness is so unfitting for holy people that it should not even be mentioned among them.
Both texts appeal to a sense of what is fitting or shameful: good people recoil from covetousness itself.
Islam
“And do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly or send it [in bribery] to the rulers in order that [they might aid] you [to] consume a portion of the wealth of the people in sin, while you know [it is unlawful].”
— Qur’an 2:188 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an forbids scheming, including bribery, to get other people’s wealth, noting that people do this even while knowing it is wrong.
The Kural says the person with a conscience will not do blameworthy deeds for profit; the Qur’an names exactly those blameworthy schemes and forbids them.
Judaism
“Behold, here I am: witness against me before the LORD, and before his anointed: whose ox have I taken? or whose ass have I taken? or whom have I defrauded? whom have I oppressed? or of whose hand have I received any bribe to blind mine eyes therewith?”
— 1 Samuel 12:3 (KJV) [4]
At the end of his career Samuel publicly challenges the people to name anything he took unjustly; no one can.
Samuel is the portrait of the person the Kural describes: someone so ashamed of injustice that he never profited from it.
Buddhism
“He is indeed virtuous, wise, and righteous who neither for his own sake nor for the sake of another (does any wrong), who does not crave for sons, wealth, or kingdom, and does not desire success by unjust means.”
— Dhammapada 84 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha defines the righteous person as someone who will not seek wealth or success through unjust means.
Nearly the Kural’s statement: the righteous person does not pursue gain through injustice.
Jainism
“Queen Kamalāvatī to King Ishukāra: “A man who returns, as it were, to the vomit, is not praised; but you want to confiscate the property left by the Brâhmaṇa. If the whole world and all treasures were yours, you would still not be satisfied, nor would all this be able to save you.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.38–39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
When the king prepares to seize the estate abandoned by the family that renounced the world, the queen shames him by likening such greed to eating vomit, and the king himself then renounces.
Valluvar says those with a sense of shame at unfairness will not stoop to disgraceful acts for gain, and the queen's rebuke shows shame checking a king who was about to take another's property.
Sikhism
“They accept bribes, and block justice.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 951, Raag Raamkalee (Ramkali ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok of Ramkali ki Vaar, Guru Nanak condemns those who sit in judgment and make a show of piety, but sell justice for a bribe.
The Kural says those who feel shame at unfairness will never do disgraceful things for gain; Guru Nanak shows the shameless opposite, people who trade fairness for a bribe.
Greek Mythology
“Do not get base gain: base gain is as bad as ruin.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 352 (tr. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod warns his brother that dishonest profit is no profit at all; it is equivalent to disaster.
The Kural says the honourable will not do blameworthy deeds for gain; Hesiod gives the same rule in one line.
Philosophy
“Now, suppose a wise man had just such a ring, he would not imagine that he was free to do wrongly any more than if he did not have it; for good men aim to secure not secrecy but the right.”
— Cicero, De Officiis (On Duties), Book III, §38 (tr. Walter Miller (Loeb, 1913)) [10]
Retelling Plato’s story of Gyges’ ring of invisibility, Cicero argues that a good person would not use a guarantee of impunity to take what is not his, because what restrains him is the wrongness of the act and not fear of being found out.
This matches the Kural’s inner safeguard: the person who is ashamed of injustice turns down disgraceful gain even when nothing outside him would stop him.

Real Life Example

The insulin team sells its patent to the University of Toronto for one dollar each
1922-1923 · Toronto, Ontario, Canada
After Frederick Banting, Charles Best, James Collip and their colleagues developed insulin at the University of Toronto, Banting objected to taking out patents and charging royalties for its manufacture. Banting, Best and Collip assigned their patent rights on insulin and its method of preparation to the University of Toronto for one dollar each, so that no one could secure a profitable private monopoly. The university then licensed manufacturers and used the royalties to fund further research.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The discoverers of a life-saving drug could have grown rich from it but chose to place it in public trust.
The kural says those who feel shame at unfairness will not do disgraceful things for gain, and the insulin team refused to profit privately from the suffering of diabetic patients.
