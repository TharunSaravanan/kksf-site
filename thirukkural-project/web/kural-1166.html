Kural 1166

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

இன்பம் கடல்மற்றுக் காமம் அஃதடுங்கால் துன்பம் அதனிற் பெரிது.
Inpam kadalmatruk kaamam aqdhadungaal thunpam adhanir peridhu.

Meaning

The joy of love is as big as the sea. But when love turns to pain, its sadness is even bigger than the sea.

Elementary

When your best friend is with you, the fun feels as big as the ocean. But when they move far away, the missing-them feeling can seem even bigger than that ocean. The wife says love works like that: the happy part is huge, and the sad part is huger still.

University

The couplet sets inpam (இன்பம், joy) against tunpam (துன்பம், sorrow) with kaṭal (கடல், sea) as the unit of measure: love’s joy is a sea, and its pain, when it aṭuṅkāl (அடுங்கால், ‘when it torments’), is periṭu (பெரிது), greater than that. Maṟṟu (மற்று) marks the turn, ‘but then’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan take the contrast as union versus separation, which fits the chapter’s setting. The sea of 1164 thus grows: there it was a sea without a raft, here the sorrow exceeds the sea. Pope: ‘A happy love’s sea of joy; but mightier sorrows roll / From unpropitious love athwart the troubled soul.’ The text is identical on both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, Radha, separated from Krishna after their joy together, is described as wasting with longing, feeling even moonlight and cool breezes as burning, while her friend pleads with Krishna to return.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, songs of Radha’s separation (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s poem moves from the bliss of union to an anguish of separation that seems to fill the whole world.
Radha experiences the Kural’s arithmetic: love’s joy is great, but the pain of its loss feels greater still.
Christianity
“Even in laughter the heart is sorrowful; and the end of that mirth is heaviness.”
— Proverbs 14:13 (KJV) [4]
The proverb warns that joy can end in a sorrow that outweighs it.
The Kural’s heroine has lived this: love’s mirth has ended in a heaviness greater than the joy.
Islam
In Nizami’s Layla and Majnun, the brief joy of the young lovers’ first meeting gives way, after they are separated, to Majnun’s lifelong grief, which drives him to wander the desert.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (paraphrase) [5]
Nizami’s romance, read in Sufi tradition as an allegory of longing for the divine, makes the sorrow of separation far exceed the joy of union.
Majnun’s story is the Kural’s claim told as a life: love’s sorrow outgrows love’s joy.
Judaism
“For love is strong as death, Jealousy is cruel as the grave; The flashes thereof are flashes of fire, A very flame of the LORD. Many waters cannot quench love, Neither can the floods drown it.”
— Song of Songs 8:6-7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song measures love against death, the grave and the floods, and finds love the stronger.
Like the Kural, the Song uses the largest measures it knows, death and the floods, to show how vast love’s power is, including its power to hurt.
Buddhism
“If one, longing for sensual pleasure, achieves it, yes, he’s enraptured at heart. The mortal gets what he wants. But if for that person, longing, desiring, the pleasures diminish, he’s shattered, as if shot with an arrow.”
— Sutta Nipata 4.1, Kama Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Kama Sutta sets the rapture of getting what one desires against the shattering pain of losing it.
This is the Kural’s two-part comparison: love’s joy is great, but its loss strikes harder still.
Jainism
“Pleasures bring only a moment’s happiness, but suffering for a very long time, intense suffering, but slight happiness; they are an obstacle to the liberation from existence, and are a very mine of evils.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The two sons of the priest Bhrigu tell their father that the pain pleasures bring far outweighs the pleasure itself.
The Kural’s heroine makes the same reckoning from inside love: its sorrow is greater than its joy.
Sikhism
“Emotional attachment to Maya is a treacherous ocean of pain and poison, which cannot be crossed.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1417, Salok Vaaran te Vadheek, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das calls attachment (moh) an ocean of pain so vast it cannot be crossed by one's own effort.
The Kural says love's joy is as wide as the sea but its sorrow is wider still; Guru Amar Das likewise measures the sorrow born of attachment as an uncrossable ocean.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls Eros the ‘limb-loosener’ and names him ‘bittersweet’, a creature against whom there is no defence.
— Sappho, Fragment 130 (paraphrase) [10]
Sappho’s coined word holds love’s sweetness and its bitterness in a single breath.
The Kural measures the same two sides of love and finds the bitter part the larger.
Philosophy
Schopenhauer argues that pain is positive and felt directly, while pleasure is merely the absence of pain, so that in any honest reckoning the suffering in life outweighs its enjoyment.
— Schopenhauer, ‘On the Sufferings of the World’ (Studies in Pessimism) (paraphrase) [11]
For Schopenhauer, sorrow is always the heavier side of the scale.
The Kural’s heroine reaches the same verdict about love: its joy is a sea, but its sorrow is greater.

Real Life Example

The 1586 letter of Won's mother to her dead husband
1586 (discovered 1998) · Andong, Korea
In 1998, when a grave in Andong, South Korea, was being moved, archaeologists found a letter laid on the body of Yi Eung-tae, a member of the Goseong Yi clan who died in 1586. His pregnant wife had written it, addressed to Won's father. In it she said she could not go on without him, asked how she should bear her grief while raising their child, and begged him to come to her in her dreams.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter preserves, in one woman's words, the moment when the joy of a shared life turned into grief too great to bear.
The kural says that love's joy is as vast as the sea but its sorrow, when love brings pain, is greater still, and the Andong letter shows that greater sorrow overwhelming a happy marriage.
