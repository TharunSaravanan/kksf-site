Kural 1305

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

நலத்தகை நல்லவர்க்கு ஏஎர் புலத்தகை பூஅன்ன கண்ணார் அகத்து.
Nalaththakai nallavarkku aeer pulaththakai pooanna kannaar akaththu.

Meaning

Even for good men with fine qualities, the real beauty is the sulking that rises in the hearts of their wives with flower-like eyes.

Elementary

A kind, good person is like a fine house. What makes the house feel truly loved is that someone inside cares enough to be a little upset when you are late. That small fuss is a sign of how much you matter.

University

Nalattakai nallavar are ‘good men of excellent quality’; their ēr (‘beauty, ornament’) is the pulattakai (‘the excellence of sulking’) that arises akattu (‘within,’ in the heart) of pū aṉṉa kaṇṇār, ‘those with flower-like eyes.’ The paradox is that the husband’s virtues are crowned not by praise but by his beloved’s pique, which proves her attachment; Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it this way, Solomon Pappaiah speaks of the abundance of sulking in the wife’s mind, and Kalaignar says such pique ‘adds beauty’ to noble lovers. Pope translates ‘Even to men of good and worthy mind, the petulance / Of wives with flowery eyes lacks not a lovely grace.’ ytamizh.com divides நல்லவர்க்கு ஏஎர் and பூஅன்ன, while thirukkural.net prints the sandhi forms நல்லவர்க் கேஎர் and பூவன்ன.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Harivamsa, Satyabhama, roused to jealousy when Krishna gives a celestial flower to Rukmini, retires in sulking anger, and Krishna, the ideal husband, appeases her by promising to bring her the whole Parijata tree from heaven.
— Harivamsa, Parijataharana episode (paraphrase) [3]
Krishna’s greatness is displayed precisely in how he honours and answers his queen’s pique.
As the Kural says, a good man’s glory is shown in the loving pique of his wife and in his response to it.
Christianity
“Likewise, ye husbands, dwell with them according to knowledge, giving honour unto the wife, as unto the weaker vessel, and as being heirs together of the grace of life; that your prayers be not hindered.”
— 1 Peter 3:7 (KJV) [4]
The epistle tells husbands to live with understanding and to honour their wives as co-heirs.
The Kural likewise measures a good husband by how he receives his wife’s feelings, including her pique.
Islam
The Prophet is reported to have said that the best of people are those who are best to their families, and that he was the best of them to his own family.
— Jami at-Tirmidhi 3895 (paraphrase) [5]
A man’s true excellence is measured inside his home, by how he treats his wife.
The Kural similarly locates a good man’s real beauty in his relationship with his wife and her feelings toward him.
Judaism
“Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my bride; Thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes, With one bead of thy necklace.”
— Song of Songs 4:9 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover is overcome by a single glance of his bride’s eyes.
The Kural too gives the power to the beloved’s eyes, likened to flowers, whose moods shape her husband’s happiness.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta, the Buddha teaches that a husband should minister to his wife by honouring her, not disparaging her, being faithful, handing over authority in the home and providing her with adornments.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
A good layman’s virtue is shown in how respectfully he treats his wife.
The Kural locates a good man’s ornament in his wife’s feelings; the sutta shows the respect that makes such loving pique safe to express.
Jainism
The Tamil Jain epic Jivaka Chintamani follows its hero through a series of courtships and marriages, portraying each bride’s feelings with care before Jivaka finally renounces the world.
— Tiruttakkatevar, Jivaka Chintamani (general paraphrase) [8]
Jain literature can depict married love vividly, though it treats it as a stage on the way to renunciation.
The Kural celebrates a wife’s pique as a husband’s ornament, a valuation Jain narrative would admire as poetry but not as a final goal.
Sikhism
“Wear these three robes, O sister, and you will captivate your Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Farid, Bhagat Shaikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Following his lines on humility, forgiveness and sweet speech, Farid says these virtues are the soul-bride's true garments.
The Kural calls a wife's loving pique the ornament of a good husband; Farid names the soul-bride's true ornaments as the virtues that win the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
On the walls of Troy, Andromache reproaches Hector for his recklessness in battle and pleads with him to stay; he answers her tenderly, holding their son and comforting her.
— Homer, Iliad 6 (paraphrase) [10]
Hector’s nobility shines most clearly in the scene where his wife scolds him out of love.
As in the Kural, the hero’s best ornament is the loving reproach of his wife and his gentle reply.
Philosophy
Terence has a character remark that the quarrels of lovers are the renewal of love.
— Terence, Andria, Act III, sc. 3 (amantium irae amoris integratio est; paraphrase) [11]
The Roman playwright turns lovers’ anger into a sign of love’s vitality.
The Kural similarly treats a wife’s pique as something that adorns, rather than diminishes, a good man’s love.

Real Life Example

Clementine Churchill warns Winston about his manner
27 June 1940 · London, United Kingdom
In June 1940, with France defeated and a German invasion feared, Clementine Churchill wrote to her husband, Prime Minister Winston Churchill, that a devoted member of his staff had told her he risked being disliked by colleagues and subordinates because of his rough, sarcastic and overbearing manner. She told him she had noticed the change herself and that he was not as kind as he used to be. She urged him to combine his great power with courtesy, kindness and calm; it is the only known letter between them from 1940.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Clementine’s loving rebuke, written at the height of national crisis, came from someone who cared enough about her husband to tell him what no one else dared.
The kural says that the true ornament of good men is the loving pique of their wives, and Clementine’s reproachful letter is a famous case of a wife’s affectionate displeasure helping to make a great man better.
