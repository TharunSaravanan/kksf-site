Kural 498

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

சிறுபடையான் செல்லிடம் சேரின் உறுபடையான் ஊக்கம் அழிந்து விடும்.
Sirupadaiyaan sellidam serin urupadaiyaan Ookkam azhinthu vidum.

Meaning

If a leader with a small army stands on ground where it can fight well, a leader with a big army will lose his push and his advantage.

Elementary

In a narrow hallway, one kid can block the way even if ten kids are trying to get through, because only one can squeeze in at a time. A small team in the right spot can stop a big team.

University

‘Sellidam’ is the key word: ‘the place where one’s power prevails’ (sellu, to be current or valid, as with a coin that ‘passes’). Parimelazhagar reads it as ground favourable to the small army, where it can fight on its own terms. ‘Urupadaiyaan’ is the commander of a large or mighty army, and ‘ookkam’, his drive and self-confidence (the subject of chapter 60), is what gets destroyed. Solomon Pappaiah gives a different reading: when the large army goes after the small one into the place where the small one lies, the large army’s prestige is lost. The sense stays the same, but the grammar of ‘sellidam’ is read in another way.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One bowman, placed on a rampart, is a match in battle for one hundred (foes), one hundred for ten thousand; hence it is prescribed (in the Sastras that a king will posses) a fortress.”
— Manusmriti 7.74 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu puts a number on the advantage of position: a single archer on a wall equals a hundred attackers.
This is the small force on its own ground overcoming the large one, as the Kural describes.
Christianity
“Out of weakness were made strong, waxed valiant in fight, turned to flight the armies of the aliens.”
— Hebrews 11:34 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews recalls heroes such as Gideon, Barak and David who, weak in numbers, routed much larger forces.
It celebrates the few defeating the many, the outcome this kural describes.
Islam
“How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of Allāh. And Allāh is with the patient.”
— Quran 2:249 (Sahih International) [5]
Before facing Goliath’s army, the faithful few under Saul (Talut) remind themselves that small forces have often beaten large ones.
The Kural gives the practical means, favourable ground, by which a small force can win.
Judaism
“The children of Israel pitched before them like two little flocks of kids; but the Syrians filled the country.”
— 1 Kings 20:23–28 (KJV) [4]
The Arameans thought Israel’s God ruled only the hills and chose to fight on the plain, yet the small Israelite force still won.
The passage is openly about choosing terrain against a small army, though it overturns the Aramean assumption.
Buddhism
In the Tacchasūkara Jātaka, wild boars terrorised by a tiger are organised by a clever boar (the Bodhisatta). They dig pits and take up positions on prepared ground, so that when the tiger leaps, it falls into a pit and is killed.
— Jātaka No. 492, Tacchasūkara Jātaka (ed. E. B. Cowell) [6]
Weak animals defeat a far stronger predator by preparing the ground where the fight happens.
The small force on its own prepared ground breaks the strong one’s spirit, which is the kural’s scenario.
Jainism
“Though a man should conquer thousands and thousands of valiant (foes), greater will be his victory if he conquers nobody but himself.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Nami tells Indra that a single victory over oneself outweighs the conquest of countless warriors.
The kural shows a small force standing on its own ground defeating a great army; Nami's saying likewise makes the single self, on its own ground, greater than thousands of foes, though Jainism moves the battle inward.
Sikhism
At Chamkaur in December 1705, Guru Gobind Singh, with only a small band of Sikhs, took shelter in a walled, double-storeyed house with a single entrance. He posted eight Sikhs to guard each of its four sides and two at the gate, and through a whole day the much larger imperial army surrounding it failed to storm it.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (Punjabi University, Patiala, ed. Harbans Singh), 'Chamkaur Sahib', via The Sikh Encyclopedia (paraphrase) [8]
The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism describes how a handful of defenders, using a defensible house as a temporary citadel, held off repeated assaults until their arrows and ammunition ran out.
The Kural says a small force on ground where its strength counts saps the drive of a large army; at Chamkaur a few Sikhs on well-chosen ground held off a far larger host.
Greek Mythology
At Thermopylae, a few thousand Greeks led by Leonidas and his 300 Spartans held the narrow pass for days against Xerxes’ huge army. Repeated Persian assaults, including the elite Immortals, failed until a traitor showed the Persians a mountain path around the pass.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.210–213 (tr. A. D. Godley) [9]
In the narrows, Persian numbers counted for nothing, and the Great King’s confidence was badly shaken.
This is the classic case of ‘sirupadaiyaan sellidam’, the small army on its own ground ruining the large army’s drive.
Philosophy
“With a superior force, make for easy ground; with an inferior one, make for difficult ground.”
— Wu Tzu, quoted in Chang Yu’s commentary on Sun Tzu III.17 (Giles, The Art of War, 1910) [10]
Wu Tzu, quoted by Chang Yu in Giles’s notes, advises the smaller army to seek broken terrain where numbers cannot be deployed.
This is the Kural’s rule for the small army put as a maxim.

Real Life Example

Henry V's smaller army wins at Agincourt
25 October 1415 · Agincourt (Azincourt), northern France
Henry V's English army, much smaller than the French force facing it, took position on a recently ploughed field bounded by woods on both sides, which narrowed the front. The English archers drove sharpened stakes into the ground before them, and days of rain had turned the field to deep mud. The heavily armoured French men-at-arms, advancing through the mud and crowded together in the narrow space, lost their advantage of numbers and suffered a disastrous defeat.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Agincourt is a classic case of a small army choosing ground where the enemy's greater numbers became a burden rather than a strength.
The kural says that if a commander with a small army stands on ground where his strength prevails, the commander of a large army loses his drive and advantage, as the French did at Agincourt.
