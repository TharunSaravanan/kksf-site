Kural 1108

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

வீழும் இருவர்க்கு இனிதே வளியிடை போழப் படாஅ முயக்கு.
Veezhum iruvarkku inidhe valiyidai pozhap padaaa muyakku.

Meaning

Sweet to two people in love is a hug so close that not even a breath of wind can pass between them.

Elementary

Have you ever hugged someone so tightly that you felt like one person? The verse says that for two people who love each other, the sweetest hug is one so close that not even a tiny puff of air could slip in between.

University

Vīḻum iruvar (வீழும் இருவர், ‘two who desire each other’) stresses that the love is mutual, and the embrace (muyakku) is one that vaḷi iṭai pōḻap paṭāa (‘is not split apart in the middle by the wind’). Paṭāa carries the metrical lengthening (aḷapeṭai) also seen in teṟūum of kural 1104. Every gloss on both sites (Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar) reads it as an embrace so close that even air cannot enter. Pope, characteristically, turns the image moral: ‘Sweet is the strict embrace of those whom fond affection binds, / Where no dissevering breath of discord entrance finds’, treating the wind as discord, a reading the Tamil commentators do not share. Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Now as a man, when embraced by a beloved wife, knows nothing that is without, nothing that is within, thus this person, when embraced by the intelligent (prâgña) Self, knows nothing that is without, nothing that is within.”
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.3.21 (tr. F. Max Müller, SBE 15) [3]
The Upanishad uses the embrace of loving spouses, in which nothing outside or inside is felt, as an image of union with the Self.
The Kural’s embrace that lets nothing come between is the same image of complete union.
Christianity
“Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.”
— Genesis 2:24 (KJV) [4]
Genesis says that husband and wife cling together and become one flesh.
The Kural’s embrace with no gap between the two is a picture of becoming one.
Islam
“They are a clothing for you and you are a clothing for them.”
— Qur’an 2:187 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes husband and wife as garments for each other, as close as clothing to the skin.
Like the Kural’s embrace that admits no wind, the garment image leaves nothing between the spouses.
Judaism
“When our love was strong, we could have slept on a bed that was the width of a sword. Now that our love is not strong, a bed of sixty cubits is not sufficient for us.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 7a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud quotes a popular saying: loving spouses need almost no room between them, but estranged ones cannot be far enough apart.
This is the Kural’s point exactly: true lovers leave no space at all between them.
Buddhism
In the Bhallatiya Jataka a pair of kinnaras (forest spirits) still weep for the one night, seven hundred years earlier, when a flooded stream kept them apart.
— Jataka 504, Bhallatiya Jataka (tr. ed. E. B. Cowell, vol. IV) [7]
The couple’s love is so close that a single night’s separation is mourned for centuries, and the story leads the king to value his own wife.
Like the Kural, the tale shows lovers for whom even the smallest gap is unbearable.
Jainism
“But a man who is indifferent to ‘colours,’ is free from sorrows; though still in the Samsâra, he is not affected by that long succession of pains, just as the leaf of the Lotus (is not moistened) by water.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira holds up the lotus leaf, which stays untouched by the water around it, as the model of a detached soul.
The Kural praises an embrace where nothing comes between; Jainism praises the opposite, a soul that nothing clings to.
Sikhism
“They alone are called husband and wife, who have one light in two bodies.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 788, Raag Suhi, Suhi ki Vaar (salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Suhi, Guru Amar Das says that true marriage is not just living side by side, but two people sharing one inner light.
The Kural praises an embrace so close that not even a breath of air can come between the two lovers; Guru Amar Das describes a union so close that two bodies share a single light.
Greek Mythology
Ovid tells how Alcyone, grieving her drowned husband Ceyx, is changed into a bird together with him, and the two stay faithful mates even as birds.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.710-748 [10]
Even transformation and death do not separate the loving pair.
The Kural’s lovers allow not even a breath of wind between them; Ceyx and Alcyone remain together beyond death.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium Aristophanes imagines Hephaestus asking two lovers lying side by side whether they wish to be wholly one, day and night in each other’s company, and offering to melt them into one and let them grow together.
— Plato, Symposium 192d-e (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Aristophanes, love is the longing of two halves to become one whole.
The Kural’s embrace that leaves no room even for air is the same wish to be wholly one.

Real Life Example

Gustav Klimt paints The Kiss
1908–1909 · Vienna, Austria
The Austrian painter Gustav Klimt painted The Kiss, an image of a couple locked in an embrace, in oil on canvas with gold leaf. It was first shown at the Kunstschau exhibition in Vienna in 1908, and the Austrian state bought it there for 25,000 kronen. It is regarded as the high point of Klimt's Golden Period and hangs in the Belvedere museum in Vienna.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The painting became one of the best-known images of love in Western art.
The kural praises an embrace so close that not even a breath of wind can pass between the two lovers, and Klimt's couple are painted so tightly enfolded that they seem to become a single shape.
