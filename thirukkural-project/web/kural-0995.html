Kural 995

Courtesy · Panpudaimai · பண்புடைமை

நகையுள்ளும் இன்னா திகழ்ச்சி பகையுள்ளும் பண்புள பாடறிவார் மாட்டு.
Nakaiyullum innaa thikazhchchi pakaiyullum panpula paadarivaar maattu.

Meaning

Making fun of someone hurts, even as a joke. So people who understand others stay polite even toward their enemies.

Elementary

Calling someone a silly name 'just for fun' can still make them cry inside. Kids who really understand feelings don't do it, not even to a kid they're cross with. Valluvar says being polite even to someone you don't like is a sign of real wisdom.

University

The first line states a psychological fact: igazhchi (இகழ்ச்சி, 'contempt, ridicule') is innaa (இன்னா, 'painful') even within nagai (நகை, 'laughter, jest'). The second draws the ethical conclusion: among paadarivaar (பாடறிவார், 'those who know the nature or proper manner', from பாடு, 'way, condition'), panbu remains even within pagai (பகை, 'enmity'). Mu. Varadarasanar reads the paadarivaar as those who act knowing others' nature; Pappaiah expands that such people do not ridicule even foes; Pope renders 'Contempt is evil though in sport. They who man's nature know, / E'en in their wrath, a courteous mind will show.' The jingle nagaiyullum ... pagaiyullum binds jest and enmity as the two tests of courtesy. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
The Gita defines the discipline of speech as words that are true and pleasant and that do not disturb others' minds.
Speech that never agitates another rules out the hurtful jest the Kural warns against.
Christianity
“As a mad man who casteth firebrands, arrows, and death, So is the man that deceiveth his neighbour, and saith, Am not I in sport?”
— Proverbs 26:18–19 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs compares the one who harms a neighbour and then says he was only joking to a madman throwing deadly arrows.
This is a near one-to-one match for the Kural's warning that ridicule wounds even in jest.
Islam
“O you who have believed, let not a people ridicule [another] people; perhaps they may be better than them; nor let women ridicule [other] women; perhaps they may be better than them. And do not insult one another and do not call each other by [offensive] nicknames.”
— Qur'an 49:11 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an forbids mocking, insulting and name-calling, reminding the mocker that the mocked may be better than he.
It directly forbids the ridicule that the Kural says hurts even when meant as fun.
Judaism
“Anyone who humiliates another in public, it is as though he were spilling blood.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 58b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud teaches that shaming someone so that the blood drains from his face is comparable to bloodshed.
The rabbis, like Valluvar, treat the wound of contempt as real injury, not a harmless matter.
Buddhism
“Speak not harshly to anyone, for those thus spoken to might retort. Indeed, angry speech hurts, and retaliation may overtake you.”
— Dhammapada 133 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Dhammapada warns that harsh speech wounds others and breeds retaliation.
It shares the Kural's insight that wounding words, toward anyone at all, cause real pain.
Jainism
“He should not tell anything sinful or meaningless or hurtful, neither for his own sake nor for anybody else's, nor without such a motive.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on discipline forbids hurtful speech for any reason, including speech with no motive at all.
Ruling out hurtful words even 'without a motive' covers the idle or joking ridicule the Kural condemns.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all. Do not break anyone’s heart; these are all priceless jewels.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, salok 129 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Farid's saloks forbid even one harsh word because God lives in every person, and call every heart a priceless jewel that must not be broken. The next salok repeats that harming anyone's mind is never good.
The Kural warns that mockery hurts even in jest and so the wise keep courtesy even toward enemies, and Farid likewise forbids any wounding word to anyone, since God dwells in all.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Thersites jeers at Agamemnon to make the Greek soldiers laugh, and Odysseus beats him with the sceptre while the army laughs at him in turn.
— Homer, Iliad 2.211–277 [10]
Homer's portrait of Thersites shows mockery used for laughter that ends in humiliation and pain.
The episode illustrates the Kural's warning that ridicule, even when aimed at a laugh, wounds and breeds hostility.
Philosophy
“Those who carry humour to excess are thought to be vulgar buffoons, striving after humour at all costs, and aiming rather at raising a laugh than at saying what is becoming and at avoiding pain to the object of their fun.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.8 (tr. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive) [11]
Aristotle condemns the buffoon whose jokes ignore the pain they cause their target, and praises tactful wit.
Aristotle names exactly the Kural's concern: a joke that wounds its object is a failure of good character.

Real Life Example

Australian airmen bury the Red Baron with full military honours
22 April 1918 · Bertangles, Somme, France
Manfred von Richthofen, Germany's most famous fighter pilot, known as the Red Baron, was shot down and killed over the Somme on 21 April 1918. The next day men of No. 3 Squadron, Australian Flying Corps, buried him in the cemetery at Bertangles with full military honours: men of the squadron fired a volley over the grave, and wreaths sent by British squadrons were laid on it. The Australian War Memorial holds film and photographs of the funeral.
— Sources: [12] [13]
In the middle of a bitter war, his enemies chose to honour a fallen opponent rather than celebrate over him.
The kural says that those who understand people keep their courtesy even toward enemies, and the Australian airmen showed respect to the enemy airman who had shot down so many of their comrades.
