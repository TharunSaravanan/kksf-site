Kural 686

The Envoy · Thoodhu · தூது

கற்றுக்கண் அஞ்சான் செலச்சொல்லிக் காலத்தால் தக்கது அறிவதாம் தூது.
Katrukkan anjaan selachchollik kaalaththaal Thakkadhu arivadhaam thoodhu.

Meaning

A good envoy is well-learned, is not scared by angry looks, speaks so the message sinks in, and knows what is right for the moment.

Elementary

Sometimes when you explain something important, the other person frowns at you. A brave messenger doesn’t run away or start stammering. They keep calm, say it clearly so it is understood, and think about what will work best right then.

University

Parimelazhagar rearranges the clauses: kaṟṟu (having studied the works on polity), cela colli (speaking so the enemy king takes the message to heart), kaṇ añcāṉ (not fearing his glare, ceyirttu nōkku), and kālattāl takkatu aṟivatu (knowing the means suited to the time). He explains why learning comes first: it teaches the means (upāyam), and if the moment calls for another method, the envoy must be able to change course. Manakkudavar gives kaṟṟu a narrower sense, having learned how to calm a foreign king who becomes angry. Pappaiah calls the timely skill a strategic path (tantira vazhi). ‘Kaṇ añcāṉ’ goes back to the Sangam ideal of the unflinching gaze (vaṉkaṇ), which returns in 689. On the text: ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net read காலத்தால் / தக்கது அறிவதாம்; the sandhi-joined text reads கற்றுக்கண் ணஞ்சான் … காலத்தாற் / றக்க தறிவதாந் தூது, the same wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Captured and brought bound before Ravana, Hanuman speaks without fear. He declares himself Rama’s envoy and warns Ravana to return Sita. When Ravana orders his death, Vibhishana objects that an envoy may not be killed.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, sargas 50-52 [5]
Hanuman delivers his master’s warning to the face of an enraged king, with learning and nerve.
He embodies the Kural’s envoy who ‘does not fear the eye’ and makes his words sink in.
Christianity
“And for me, that utterance may be given unto me, that I may open my mouth boldly, to make known the mystery of the gospel, For which I am an ambassador in bonds: that therein I may speak boldly, as I ought to speak.”
— Ephesians 6:19-20 (KJV) [6]
In prison, Paul calls himself an ‘ambassador in bonds’ and asks for boldness to speak his message as he should.
Paul’s bold speech under hostile power is the Kural’s kaṇ añcāṉ, the envoy who does not quail.
Islam
Before the battle of al-Qadisiyya, the Muslim envoy Rib‘i ibn ‘Amir rode into the Persian commander Rustam’s richly carpeted tent. Unimpressed by its splendour, he calmly stated his side’s terms and aims.
— al-Tabari, Tarikh al-rusul wa-l-muluk (History of al-Tabari, vol. 12: The Battle of al-Qadisiyyah) [7]
Early histories remember Rib‘i as unafraid of the display and power of the Persian court.
He fits the Kural’s envoy who is not cowed by a hostile ruler’s gaze and delivers his message whole.
Judaism
“And Pharaoh said unto him, Get thee from me, take heed to thyself, see my face no more; for in that day thou seest my face thou shalt die. And Moses said, Thou hast spoken well, I will see thy face again no more.”
— Exodus 10:28-29 (KJV) [6]
Threatened with death if he appears again, Moses answers Pharaoh calmly and without fear.
Moses does not fear the king’s angry face, which is the Kural’s kaṇ añcāṉ almost word for word.
Buddhism
“They don’t tremble when arriving at an assembly of fierce debaters. … and they don’t tremble when questioned.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 8.16, Dūteyya Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [8]
In the verses on the qualified envoy, the Buddha says such a monk is not shaken by a hostile assembly or by hard questions.
This is the Buddhist form of the Kural’s kaṇ añcāṉ: steadiness in front of hostile listeners.
Jainism
“Suvega, clinging to his courage though disturbed in mind, got up and left the council-hall, watched by the kings whose eyes were red with anger…”
— Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra 1.5, Part 1, 'Negotiations with Bahubali' (Hemachandra, tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS 1931) [9]
After delivering Bharata's demand, the envoy Suvega leaves Bahubali's court under the furious glares of the princes and bodyguards, keeping hold of his courage.
The Kural's envoy 'does not flinch at angry looks'; Suvega delivers his whole message and walks out steady under eyes red with anger.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else - says Nanak, listen, mind: call him spiritually wise.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahalla 9 (Salok 16), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Tegh Bahadur makes fearlessness, together with not intimidating others, a mark of true wisdom; he himself went to his death in Delhi in 1675 rather than yield.
The Kural's envoy does not flinch at angry looks and knows what the moment needs; Guru Tegh Bahadur's ideal is exactly such composed fearlessness grounded in wisdom.
Greek Mythology
King Priam goes alone to the tent of Achilles, the man who killed his son. He faces Achilles’ temper, kisses his hands, and, choosing his moment and his words, persuades him to give back Hector’s body.
— Homer, Iliad 24.468-620 [11]
Priam, acting as his own envoy, faces a dangerous and angry enemy and wins his request through courage and well-judged speech.
He shows the Kural’s trio: no fear of the fierce gaze, words that sink in, and a sense of what the moment needs.
Philosophy
At his trial, Socrates refuses to flatter or plead before an angry Athenian jury. He says he would rather die than speak in a way unworthy of himself.
— Plato, Apology 38d-39b [12]
Socrates speaks the truth plainly to a hostile audience and ignores the threat to his life.
Fearlessness before angry listeners is the Kural’s kaṇ añcāṉ.

Real Life Example

Adlai Stevenson confronts Valerian Zorin at the UN Security Council
25 October 1962 · UN Security Council, New York, United States
During the Cuban Missile Crisis, the United States ambassador to the UN, Adlai Stevenson, pressed the Soviet representative Valerian Zorin in an open session of the Security Council to say whether the Soviet Union had placed missiles in Cuba. When Zorin would not answer directly, Stevenson had large reconnaissance photographs of the missile sites displayed to the Council. The JFK Library notes that the normally courteous Stevenson confronted Zorin aggressively that day.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Stevenson chose the moment and the evidence so that his case reached not only the Soviet delegate but the whole Council and the watching world.
The kural asks for an envoy who speaks so that his words sink in, who does not flinch under a hostile glare, and who knows what the moment requires, which is what Stevenson did in the Council chamber.
