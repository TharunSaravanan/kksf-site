Kural 568

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

இனத்தாற்றி எண்ணாத வேந்தன் சினத்தாற்றிச் சீறிற் சிறுகும் திரு.
Inaththaatri ennaadha vaendhan sinaththaatrich Seeril sirugum thiru.

Meaning

A king who does not think things through with his advisers, and then gets angry and blames them, will see his good fortune shrink.

Elementary

Imagine a group leader who never asks the team for ideas, and then, when the project goes wrong, shouts at everyone. The team stops helping, and things get worse and worse. The kural says a leader who skips advice and then explodes in anger loses what they have.

University

The couplet is syntactically compact and has two established readings. Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah take இனத்து ஆற்றி எண்ணாத as ‘not deliberating together with his circle (ministers)’, so the fault is failure to consult followed by angry outbursts; Pope, following the older commentary, reads it as handing affairs to ministers without himself reflecting, and then raging at them when things fail. On either reading the pattern is the same: neglect of counsel, then சினத்து ஆற்றி சீறுதல், being carried along by anger and flaring at subordinates. The result is that திரு (Śrī, royal fortune) சிறுகும், dwindles. Here ‘dread’ is directed at the king’s own ministers, extending the chapter’s concern from subjects to the inner court. The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.63 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna traces a chain from anger to delusion to loss of judgment to ruin.
The raging king of this Kural loses judgment in exactly this chain, and with it his fortune.
Christianity
“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.”
— James 1:19–20 (KJV) [4]
James urges listening first and restraining anger, because anger does not produce right outcomes.
‘Swift to hear, slow to wrath’ is the exact reverse of the king who neither deliberates nor restrains his anger.
Islam
“And those who avoid the major sins and immoralities, and when they are angry, they forgive, … and whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves.”
— Quran 42:37–38 (Sahih International) [5]
In consecutive verses the Quran praises believers who forgive when angry and who settle their affairs by consultation (shura).
Consultation and restraint of anger are the two virtues whose absence the Kural blames for the shrinking of royal fortune.
Judaism
“Without counsel purposes are disappointed: but in the multitude of counsellors they are established.”
— Proverbs 15:22 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that plans made without counsel fail, while those made with many advisers stand.
The Kural’s king who does not deliberate with his circle is the one whose purposes are disappointed.
Buddhism
“He who checks rising anger as a charioteer checks a rolling chariot, him I call a true charioteer. Others only hold the reins.”
— Dhammapada 222 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha praises the one who can halt anger as it rises, as a skilled driver stops a runaway chariot.
The Kural’s king is carried along by anger (சினத்து ஆற்றி); the Buddha calls for the opposite mastery.
Jainism
“Anger destroys love, pride destroys modesty, deceit destroys friendship; greed is destructive of everything.”
— Saman Suttam 135 (English tr., jainworld.com; from Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.38) [7]
This verse, which comes from the Dasavaikalika Sutra, names anger as the passion (kashaya) that destroys affection.
The Kural's king who rages at his counsellors instead of deliberating with them sees his fortune shrink; the Jain verse explains the mechanism, since anger destroys the goodwill his rule depends on.
Sikhism
“Tatta: In anger, you are burnt, you fool. T'hat'ha: That place where you live, is cursed.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 435, Raag Aasaa (Patti), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In his acrostic Patti, Guru Amar Das warns the learned man that anger burns him up and spoils the very place where he dwells.
The Kural says a king who acts without deliberating with his counsellors and lashes out in anger will see his fortune shrink; Guru Amar Das likewise shows anger consuming the angry man and his surroundings.
Greek Mythology
At the opening of the Iliad, Agamemnon, stung by the seer Calchas’ advice, rages at Achilles and seizes his prize; Achilles withdraws from the war and the Greeks suffer disaster after disaster.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 1 [9]
A leader who answers counsel with fury alienates his best ally and brings losses on his whole cause.
Agamemnon is the Kural’s king who lashes out at those around him and watches his fortunes shrink.
Philosophy
Seneca tells of the governor Gnaeus Piso, who in anger ordered a soldier executed, then, when the supposedly murdered comrade turned up alive, condemned the soldier, the returned man, and the centurion as well, turning his own error into three deaths.
— Seneca, De Ira 1.18 [10]
Seneca uses the story to show how anger in a ruler refuses correction and multiplies injustice.
Piso is the Kural’s ruler who does not deliberate, then rages at subordinates, to his own discredit.

Real Life Example

Ibrahim Lodi alienates his nobles and loses the Delhi Sultanate
1517-1526 · Delhi and Panipat, India
Ibrahim Lodi, sultan of Delhi from 1517, tried to rule more autocratically than his father and became increasingly suspicious and harsh toward his Afghan nobles, some of whom rebelled. Resenting his attempts to curb their authority, Dawlat Khan Lodi, governor of the Punjab, and Alam Khan Lodi, Ibrahim's uncle, appealed to Babur, the ruler of Kabul, for help. Babur invaded, and Ibrahim was defeated and killed at the First Battle of Panipat in April 1526, ending the Lodi dynasty.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
A sultan who ruled without carrying his nobles with him and treated them with suspicion and harshness found his own governors inviting a foreign enemy against him.
The kural says a king who does not deliberate with his circle and then flares at them in anger will see his fortune shrink, and Ibrahim's quarrels with his nobles led directly to the loss of his kingdom.
