Kural 46

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

அறத்தாற்றின் இல்வாழ்க்கை ஆற்றின் புறத்தாற்றில் போஒய்ப் பெறுவ தெவன்?
Araththaatrin ilvaazhkkai aatrin puraththaatril, Pooip peruva thevan?

Meaning

If a person lives family life in the right way, what more could they gain by leaving it for some other path?

Elementary

If the water in your own well is clean and cold, why walk ten miles to another village's well? The answer is: there's no reason to. Living well at home already gives you what you would go looking for elsewhere.

University

The couplet is framed as a rhetorical question — பெறுவது எவன்? (“what is there to obtain?”) — and the expected answer is “nothing.” புறத்தாற்றில் (puraththaatril, “on the outside path”) is the polite euphemism for the ascetic's road, the துறவறம் that Valluvar treats sympathetically in chapters 3 and 35. The argument is not that renunciation is false but that it is redundant *for someone already doing the domestic path properly*, the operative qualifier being அறத்தாற்றின் (“by the way of virtue”). Note the doubled ஆறு/ஆற்று root — path, path, outside-path — which makes the verse a small meditation on the metaphor of the road itself. This is the pivot on which the whole chapter, and arguably the whole of Book I's householder section, turns.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is unattached to the fruits of his work and who works as he is obligated is in the renounced order of life, and he is the true mystic, not he who lights no fire and performs no duty.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.1 [4]
Krishna redefines sannyāsa as an inner condition available to anyone doing their duty, explicitly denying that extinguishing the household fire makes one a renunciant.
This is the Kural's argument almost verbatim: the household path rightly followed already contains whatever the outside path was supposed to supply.
Christianity
“Now the Spirit speaketh expressly, that in the latter times some shall depart from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits, and doctrines of devils; … Forbidding to marry, and commanding to abstain from meats, which God hath created to be received with thanksgiving of them which believe and know the truth.”
— 1 Timothy 4:1-3 (KJV) [5]
The Pastoral Epistles attack an early ascetic movement that treated marriage and food as impediments to holiness, calling the doctrine demonic.
Both texts push back against the assumption that leaving domestic life is a higher spiritual road.
Islam
“Are you the same people who said so-and-so? By Allah, I am more submissive to Allah and more afraid of Him than you; yet I fast and break my fast, I do sleep and I also marry women. So he who does not follow my tradition in religion, is not from me.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5063, Book of Wedlock (Nikah) [6]
Three men had resolved to pray all night, fast perpetually, and never marry; the Prophet rebuked them, making moderate married life the normative religious path.
It answers the Kural's rhetorical question with a flat 'nothing' — there is no superior road outside the ordinary married life.
Judaism
The Talmud records Rabbi Elazar HaKappar's view that the nazirite who abstains from wine must bring a sin-offering because he denied himself something permitted, and that anyone who afflicts himself by further such abstentions is called a sinner.
— Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 10a [7]
Rabbinic Judaism is structurally suspicious of voluntary self-denial: creation's permitted pleasures are to be sanctified in ordinary life, not renounced.
Both traditions deny that stepping outside ordinary domestic life earns any additional merit.
Buddhism
“Neither going about naked, nor matted locks, nor filth, nor fasting, nor lying on the ground, nor smearing oneself with ashes and dust, nor sitting on the heels (in penance) can purify a mortal who has not overcome doubt.”
— Dhammapada 141, Dandavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [8]
The Buddha, who had himself tried extreme austerity, denies that any external ascetic practice purifies anyone by itself.
It grants the Kural's premise that the outside path holds no automatic advantage, though it does not go on to prefer the householder.
Jainism
“Bark and skin (of a goat), nakedness, twisted hair, baldness--these (outward tokens) will not save a sinful ascetic. … A sinner, though he be a mendicant (friar), will not escape hell; but a pious man, whether monk or householder, ascends to heaven.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.21–22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The Uttarādhyayana denies that the outward marks of renunciation are worth anything in themselves: a virtuous householder reaches heaven just as a virtuous monk does.
When the Kural asks what a virtuous householder would gain by taking the 'other path', the Jain sūtra answers in part: the ascetic's insignia gain nothing on their own.
Sikhism
“O Nanak! Meeting the True Guru, one comes to know the Perfect Way. While laughing, playing, dressing and eating, he is liberated.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 522, Raag Gujri, Gujri ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 5, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan teaches that liberation is not reserved for those who leave the world; it is found in the middle of ordinary life, by one who follows the Guru's way.
Valluvar asks what more anyone could gain by leaving a virtuous home for another path; the Guru answers that even liberation is reached within everyday life.
Greek Mythology
Held for seven years on Ogygia by the goddess Calypso, who offers to make him immortal and ageless if he stays as her husband, Odysseus refuses and weeps on the shore, choosing his mortal wife Penelope and his house on Ithaca over deathlessness.
— Homer, Odyssey V (Calypso and Odysseus) [11]
The episode is Greek epic's sharpest statement that domestic life with mortal kin is worth more than a transcendent condition outside it.
Odysseus answers the Kural's question in narrative form: even godhood on another path is not worth leaving a rightly ordered home.
Philosophy
“he who is unable to live in society, or who has no need because he is sufficient for himself, must be either a beast or a god”
— Aristotle, Politics 1253a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Aristotle holds that human flourishing is constitutively social, beginning in the household; the solitary self-sufficient life is not a higher human option but a non-human one.
Both deny that stepping outside the household and its bonds leads anywhere better for a human being.

Real Life Example

Martin Luther marries Katharina von Bora
13 June 1525 · Wittenberg, Germany
Martin Luther, a former Augustinian friar, married Katharina von Bora, a former Cistercian nun who had left her convent in 1523, on 13 June 1525. According to Britannica, Luther was convinced that marriage was an honourable order of creation and chose to take a public stand against clerical celibacy. The couple raised six children in the former monastery building in Wittenberg, where Katharina ran the household and its finances, which also came to include her relative, six of Luther's sister's children and student lodgers.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Luther's marriage made the family home of a former monk a model of married life that many later clergy followed.
The kural asks what more a person could gain by another path if household life is lived in virtue, and Luther left the monastic path to make a household the setting of his religious life.
