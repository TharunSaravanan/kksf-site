Kural 763

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

ஒலித்தக்கால் என்னாம் உவரி எலிப்பகை நாகம் உயிர்ப்பக் கெடும்.
Oliththakkaal ennaam uvari elippakai naagam uyirppak kedum.

Meaning

So what if a crowd of rats roars like the sea? One hiss from a cobra and they all run away and are destroyed.

Elementary

Imagine a crowd of mice squeaking very loudly in a barn. It sounds scary, but the moment a snake hisses, they all scurry away. Valluvar says a big, noisy army with no real courage is like those mice, and a small, brave army is like the snake.

University

The couplet is a celebrated uvamai (simile): uvari (உவரி, ‘the salt sea’) describes the roar of a vast but timid enemy, while nākam (நாகம், ‘cobra’) and uyirppa (உயிர்ப்ப, ‘as it breathes, hisses’) picture a small force of real valour. Parimelazhagar reads it as a statement that numbers without courage count for nothing against a brave army, and Solomon Pappaiah explicitly makes the cobra a small army and the rats a large one ‘rat-like in mind’. Kalaignar applies it to a single hero rising in anger. The text is the same in both sources apart from sandhi (ஒலித்தக்கா லென்னா முவரி யெலிப்பகை).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, the Pandavas field seven akshauhinis against the Kauravas’ eleven, yet the larger host is defeated at Kurukshetra.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga and Bhishma Parvas (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The epic repeatedly stresses that the numerically superior Kaurava army lacked the righteousness and leadership that sustained the smaller force.
It is the Kural’s point on an epic scale: a great noisy host gives way before a smaller, stronger one.
Christianity
When the king of Aram surrounds Dothan with horses and chariots, Elisha tells his frightened servant that those with them are more than those with the enemy, and his eyes are opened to see the hills full of horses and chariots of fire.
— 2 Kings 6:15–17 [4]
The story teaches that a vast visible host does not decide the outcome.
Like the rats roaring like the sea, the besieging army looks overwhelming yet comes to nothing.
Islam
“How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the patient.”
— Qur’an 2:249 (Saheeh International) [5]
As Talut’s (Saul’s) small band faced Jalut (Goliath) and his soldiers, the believers among them affirmed that a small company can overcome a large one.
This is the cobra and the rats: size of host matters less than steadfast courage.
Judaism
“Come and let us go over unto the garrison of these uncircumcised; it may be that the LORD will work for us; for there is no restraint to the LORD to save by many or by few.”
— 1 Samuel 14:6 (JPS 1917, Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Jonathan and his armour-bearer alone climb up to a Philistine garrison, and the whole Philistine camp falls into panic and melts away.
A tiny, fearless force scattering a large host is exactly the Kural’s cobra among the rats.
Buddhism
In the Padhana Sutta, Mara’s ‘army’ of desire, discontent, hunger, craving, sloth and fear gathers around the Bodhisatta, who declares that he will not be driven from his place and defeats it by insight.
— Sutta Nipata 3.2, Padhana Sutta [7]
A vast and clamorous host is overcome by one steadfast mind.
It mirrors the Kural’s image: a roaring multitude is undone by a single, truly strong presence.
Jainism
“A man believes himself a hero as long as he does not behold the foe, as did Śiśupâla (before he beheld) the valorously-fighting, great warrior.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.3.1.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The verse mocks bravado that lasts only until the real foe appears, and the next verse adds that once the fight begins such men are cut down (1.3.1.2).
Like the rats whose sea-like roar comes to nothing when the cobra hisses, the boaster's courage collapses the moment he faces true valour.
Sikhism
At Chamkaur in December 1704, after the evacuation of Anandpur, Guru Gobind Singh, his two elder sons and about forty Sikhs held a mud fortress against a far larger Mughal-led force. The Guru's elder sons Ajit Singh and Jujhar Singh died fighting there.
— Wikipedia, 'Battle of Chamkaur' (paraphrase of traditional and historical accounts) [9]
Sikh memory treats Chamkaur as the classic case of a tiny band of Khalsa holding off a vast besieging army through sheer resolve.
The Kural says a host of rats roaring like the sea scatters at one breath of the cobra; Chamkaur is remembered in the same terms, with a handful of committed fighters standing against a multitude.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus tells how the Athenians at Marathon, heavily outnumbered, charged the Persian army at a run and put it to flight.
— Herodotus, Histories 6.112–113 [10]
The Persians thought the Athenians mad to attack so few against so many, yet the smaller, determined force prevailed.
It is a historical instance of the Kural’s rats scattered at the cobra’s breath.
Philosophy
Xenophon, rallying the Greek officers after their generals were seized, tells them that it is neither numbers nor strength that wins battles, but that whichever side advances with stouter hearts, the enemy generally will not stand against it.
— Xenophon, Anabasis 3.1.42 [11]
Stranded deep in Persian territory, Xenophon argues that morale, not size, decides the issue.
Valluvar’s cobra and rats make the same claim about courage versus numbers.

Real Life Example

Robert the Bruce's smaller army defeats Edward II at Bannockburn
23-24 June 1314 · Bannockburn, near Stirling, Scotland, United Kingdom
Edward II of England led a large army, estimated at some 13,000 infantry and about 3,000 cavalry, to relieve the besieged Stirling Castle. Robert the Bruce met it with a smaller force of perhaps 7,000 infantry, mostly pikemen, and a few hundred light horse, using woodland and dug ditches to channel the attack. Repeated English cavalry charges failed to break the Scottish spear formations, and the English army was routed, with Edward barely escaping.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A disciplined, determined smaller force overthrew a far larger and more splendid host, in what Britannica calls a decisive battle in Scottish history.
The kural asks what it matters if a host of rats roars like the sea when a single breath of the cobra scatters them, and at Bannockburn the larger army's numbers counted for little against the resolve of the smaller one.
