Kural 155

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

ஒறுத்தாரை ஒன்றாக வையாரே வைப்பர் பொறுத்தாரைப் பொன்போற் பொதிந்து.
Oruththaarai ondraaga vaiyaare vaippar, Poruththaaraip ponpor podhindhu.

Meaning

People think nothing of those who pay back every wrong. But they treasure those who stay patient, like gold kept safe and wrapped up.

Elementary

Two kids get pushed in the lunch line. One shoves back hard, and the other says, 'It's okay.' Later, which one do people trust and want as a friend? This kural says people treasure the patient one like a piece of gold kept safe in a box, while the one who always hits back isn't valued at all.

University

ஒறுத்தார் ('those who punished' the offender) and பொறுத்தார் ('those who bore it') differ by a single initial consonant, and Valluvar uses the near-rhyme to make the moral contrast audible. ஒன்றாக வையார் means such people are not counted as anything at all, while பொன்போற் பொதிந்து வைப்பர் ('they wrap and keep like gold') suggests a treasure bundled up and stored with care. The judgement is a social one: honor goes not to the person who wins the exchange of blows but to the one who refuses it. That reverses a warrior-culture instinct that leaving a slight unavenged is shameful. Aristotle is a useful contrast: he praises the good-tempered man who 'tends to make allowances,' but warns that putting up with every insult can look 'slavish.' Valluvar settles that tension firmly on the side of forbearance.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Forgiveness is a virtue of the weak, and an ornament of the strong.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXIII, Vidura's counsel (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura admits that people sometimes mistake the forgiving for weak, but insists that forgiveness is great power, a jewel worn by the strong.
'Ornament' matches the kural's gold. The forbearing person is prized and adorned, not despised.
Christianity
“For what glory is it, if, when ye be buffeted for your faults, ye shall take it patiently? but if, when ye do well, and suffer for it, ye take it patiently, this is acceptable with God.”
— 1 Peter 2:20 (KJV) [5]
Peter tells believers that the real glory is patiently enduring mistreatment you didn't deserve.
Both texts say where true honor lies: with the one who bears wrong patiently, not the one who hits back.
Islam
“And whoever is patient and forgives - indeed, that is of the matters [worthy] of resolve.”
— Qur'an 42:43 (Sahih International) [6]
Right after allowing proportionate retaliation, the Qur'an praises patience and forgiveness as a higher, more resolute path.
Both texts rank the one who forbears above the one who retaliates.
Judaism
“It is the discretion of a man to be slow to anger, And it is his glory to pass over a transgression.”
— Proverbs 19:11 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs calls overlooking an offence a person's glory, that is, their honor and splendor.
'His glory' corresponds to the kural's 'treasured like gold': forbearance is what earns honor.
Buddhism
“Who can blame such a one, as worthy as a coin of refined gold? Even the gods praise him; by Brahma, too, is he praised.”
— Dhammapada, verse 230 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
In the Dhammapada's chapter on anger, the self-controlled person of flawless character is called as precious as refined gold and praised even by the gods.
It uses the same gold image as the kural for the person who has mastered anger.
Jainism
“The young man's self-restraint is self-restraint. The gift of him who owns no stores of wealth is gift indeed. When man has means and might to punish every fault, if he forbear, call him the patient man.”
— Nālaṭiyār 65 (ch. 7, 'The Absence of Anger: Meekness'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [9]
In its chapter on meekness, the Jain-authored Nālaṭiyār says that real forbearance belongs to the man who has the power to punish and holds back.
Valluvar contrasts those who punish (ஒறுத்தார்), whom the world counts as nothing, with those who forbear (பொறுத்தார்), whom it treasures like gold, and the Nālaṭiyār uses the same pair of verbs (ஒறுக்கும் … பொறுக்கும்) to praise the man who could punish but forbears.
Sikhism
“When something is placed on the balancing scale and weighed, the side which descends is heavier.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak uses the scale to show that the one who bows low is found to weigh more.
The Kural says people count the punisher as nothing and treasure the forbearing like wrapped gold; Guru Nanak likewise says the one who yields proves the more valuable when weighed.
Greek Mythology
Achilles, insulted when Agamemnon takes his prize Briseis, withdraws from the war in rage. His unappeased anger brings countless deaths on the Greeks.
— Homer, Iliad, Book I (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
The Iliad opens by naming Achilles' wrath as its subject, and it shows that refusing to let an insult pass wins the hero no lasting honor, only grief for his friends and for himself.
A counter-example: the person who must avenge every slight ends up diminished, not treasured.
Philosophy
“For the good-tempered man is not revengeful, but rather tends to make allowances.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle says good temper is praised, and that the good-tempered man is not vengeful but makes allowances. He also warns that tolerating every insult can look 'slavish.'
Aristotle praises the forbearing over the revengeful, as the kural does, though with more reservations.

Real Life Example

Emperor Taizong of Tang makes his rival's adviser Wei Zheng his trusted critic
626-643 · Chang'an (now Xi'an), China
Wei Zheng had served as an adviser to Crown Prince Li Jiancheng and had urged him to strike first against his younger brother Li Shimin. After Li Shimin killed his brothers in 626 and became Emperor Taizong, Wei Zheng was not punished but entered the new emperor's close circle as a counsellor. For years he criticised the emperor frankly, at one point pointing out more than 200 of his mistakes, which Taizong accepted and corrected; after Wei Zheng's death Taizong wrote that, in losing him, he had lost a mirror in which to see his own failings.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Taizong's tolerance of a former enemy turned blunt critic is one reason he is remembered as one of the greatest emperors in Chinese history, and Wei Zheng became the model of the honest minister.
The kural says people count the punisher as nothing but treasure the forbearing like gold; Taizong's forbearance won him an adviser he valued as a precious mirror and a lasting reputation as a model ruler.
