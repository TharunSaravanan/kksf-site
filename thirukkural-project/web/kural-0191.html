Kural 191

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

பல்லார் முனியப் பயனில சொல்லுவான் எல்லாரும் எள்ளப் படும்.
Pallaar muniyap payanila solluvaan, Ellaarum ellap padum.

Meaning

A person who keeps talking about useless things until many listeners are fed up will be looked down on by everyone.

Elementary

Imagine someone in class who keeps talking and talking about nothing while everyone is trying to listen to the teacher. Soon nobody wants to sit next to them. This kural says that people who chatter about useless things make everyone tired of them, and then everyone stops respecting them.

University

The chapter opens with a social rather than a metaphysical sanction: the idle talker is punished by the listening community itself. The verb முனிய (muniya, “to be vexed, to loathe”) names the listeners’ reaction, and எள்ளப்படும் (ellappadum, “will be scorned”) names the outcome, so the couplet traces a causal chain from speech to irritation to contempt. The contrast of பல்லார் (“the many” who hear him) with எல்லாரும் (“everyone”) is deliberate: annoyance starts with the immediate audience but reputation spreads to the whole world. Coming straight after the chapter on backbiting (புறங்கூறாமை), the kural widens the ethics of speech from malicious words to merely empty ones, treating wasted words as a real, if quieter, wrong. Plutarch’s essay on garrulousness makes the same diagnosis, that the chatterer seeks affection and wins hatred.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him not seek after many words, for that is mere weariness of the tongue.”
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV.4.21 (trans. F. Max Müller, SBE 15) [3]
The Upanishad tells the seeker who has found the Self to practise wisdom instead of piling up words, which only tire the tongue.
Both texts treat piling up words as fruitless. The Kural stresses how it wears on the listeners, and the Upanishad how it wears out the speaker.
Christianity
“The words of a wise man’s mouth are gracious; but the lips of a fool will swallow up himself. … A fool also is full of words.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:12, 14 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher contrasts gracious speech with a fool’s endless words, which end up destroying the fool himself.
In both texts the babbler is the one who pays: the Kural’s talker ends up scorned, and the Preacher’s fool is swallowed by his own lips.
Islam
“Allah has hated for you three things: -1. Vain talks, (useless talk) that you talk too much or about others.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1477 (trans. Muhsin Khan, sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet lists qīla wa qāla (‘it was said and he said’, meaning idle chatter) first among three things God dislikes for people.
In the Kural the whole world scorns the idle talker, and in the hadith God himself dislikes idle talk. Both condemn it plainly.
Judaism
“He that useth many words shall be abhorred; and he that taketh to himself authority therein shall be hated.”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 20:8 (KJV Apocrypha) [6]
Ben Sira, a Second-Temple Jewish sage, warns that the person who talks too much will be loathed by those around him.
This is almost the Kural’s own claim: too many words lead to being abhorred, just as the Kural’s talker is scorned by all.
Buddhism
“They blame those who remain silent, they blame those who speak much, they blame those who speak in moderation. There is none in the world who is not blamed.”
— Dhammapada 227 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha tells Atula that people criticise everyone, including those who talk too much.
Both texts say that the heavy talker is criticised. The Buddha, however, adds that the silent are blamed too, which softens the Kural’s sharp contrast.
Jainism
“As a bitch with sore ears is driven away everywhere, thus a bad, insubordinate, and talkative (pupil) is turned out.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The opening lecture of the Uttarādhyayana compares the talkative, unruly pupil to a mangy dog that is chased away from every door.
Valluvar says the man who keeps up useless talk until his listeners are sick of him ends up scorned by everyone, and the Jain simile shows the talkative pupil driven out wherever he goes.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, speaking insipid words, the body and mind become insipid. He is called the most insipid of the insipid; the most insipid of the insipid is his reputation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa (Asa ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Asa ki Vaar, Guru Nanak warns that fikaa (tasteless, graceless) speech hollows out the speaker and leaves him with a reputation as hollow as his words.
The Kural says the man who keeps pouring out useless talk ends up scorned by everyone; Guru Nanak likewise says the insipid talker becomes known to all by that insipidness.
Greek Mythology
“A man of many words, and those unseemly; a monger of sedition, a railer against all who were in authority, who cared not what he said, so that he might set the Achaeans in a laugh.”
— Homer, Iliad 2.212 ff. (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Thersites harangues the Greek assembly at Troy. The soldiers are ‘angry and disgusted’, and when Odysseus beats him, the whole army laughs at him.
This is the Kural in narrative form: a man whose many words disgust the many is scorned by all.
Philosophy
“In the case of babblers … they are hated when they wish to be liked, that they cause annoyance when they wish to please, that they are laughed at when they think they are admired.”
— Plutarch, Moralia, On Talkativeness (De garrulitate) 16, 510c-d (trans. W. C. Helmbold, Loeb) [11]
Plutarch diagnoses garrulousness as a disease whose sufferers get the opposite of what they want from their audience.
Plutarch’s babbler, like the Kural’s, annoys his listeners and is despised by them.

Real Life Example

The Senate censures Senator Joseph McCarthy
2 December 1954 · Washington, D.C., United States
In spring 1954 the Army-McCarthy hearings, carried in full on national television, showed Senator Joseph McCarthy badgering witnesses and ignoring procedure and ordinary courtesy, and the Senate Historical Office records that by June he had greatly damaged his image with the American people. The Senate, which its historians describe as weary of his embarrassing antics, then referred a censure resolution to a bipartisan select committee. On 2 December 1954 it voted 67 to 22 to censure him for his conduct towards two Senate committees, and after the vote he lost his power and standing.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A senator who had dominated the press and the Senate for four years lost his influence once the public had watched him at length and grown tired of him.
The kural traces a chain from wearying talk to the irritation of the many listeners and then to contempt from everyone, which is close to the path from the televised hearings to the Senate's formal censure.
