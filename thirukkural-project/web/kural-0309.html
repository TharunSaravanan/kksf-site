Kural 309

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

உள்ளிய தெல்லாம் உடனெய்தும் உள்ளத்தால் உள்ளான் வெகுளி எனின்.
Ulliyadhellaam udaneydhum ullaththaal Ullaan vekuli enin.

Meaning

If a person never lets even a thought of anger into their mind, they will right away get everything they hope for.

Elementary

When your mind isn't full of angry thoughts, it has lots of room for good ideas and plans. People also like to help calm, kind people. So a mind without anger can reach its goals much more easily.

University

The couplet plays on the root ul- ('to think', 'the mind'): ulliyadhu ('what one has thought of'), ullaththaal ('in the mind'), and ullaan ('does not think of'). Valluvar sets the standard at the level of thought itself: it is not enough to avoid angry acts or words; one must not entertain (ullaan) anger inwardly. The promised reward, attaining all one intends 'at once' (udan), is striking, and commentators variously read it as worldly success (a clear mind and goodwill from others) or spiritual attainment. The couplet thus links inner purity from anger to efficacy in the world, a pattern also found in the Gita's promise of liberation for those free of desire and anger.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who are free from anger and all material desires, who are self-realized, self-disciplined and constantly endeavoring for perfection, are assured of liberation in the Supreme in the very near future.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.26 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna promises that freedom from anger and desire brings the highest goal near at hand.
Close to the kural: the one free of anger in mind attains what is sought, and attains it soon ('abhitaḥ', 'near at hand', like Valluvar's 'udan', 'at once').
Christianity
“Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.”
— Matthew 5:5 (KJV) [4]
In the Beatitudes, Jesus promises the whole earth to the meek, those gentle and slow to anger.
Like the kural, the non-angry are promised a surprisingly large reward.
Islam
“And hasten to forgiveness from your Lord and a garden as wide as the heavens and earth, prepared for the righteous ... who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good;”
— Quran 3:133-134 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran lists restraining anger and pardoning others among the qualities of those promised a vast paradise.
Like the kural, freedom from anger is tied directly to receiving the greatest reward.
Judaism
“With regard to whoever forgoes his reckonings with others for injustices done to him, the heavenly court in turn forgoes punishment for all his sins.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Rosh Hashanah 17a (William Davidson/Koren-Steinsaltz trans., via Sefaria) [6]
Rava teaches that one who lets go of grievances receives complete forgiveness; the passage then tells of Rav Huna recovering from near-death because he never insisted on his rights.
Like the kural, the person who does not hold anger in mind receives an extraordinary, all-encompassing reward.
Buddhism
“One should give up anger, renounce pride, and overcome all fetters. Suffering never befalls him who clings not to mind and body and is detached.”
— Dhammapada 221 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The opening verse of the Dhammapada's chapter on anger promises freedom from suffering to one who gives up anger and clinging.
As in the kural, giving up anger leads to the fulfilment one seeks.
Jainism
“By conquering anger he obtains patience; he acquires no Karman productive of anger, and destroys the Karman he had acquired before.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.67 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In the catalogue of spiritual exertions, Mahāvīra says that overcoming anger (krodhavijaya) produces patience, stops new anger-producing karma, and wears away the old.
Valluvar promises that someone who never lets anger into his mind will gain whatever he desires. The Jain text also promises a reward for conquering anger, but the reward is freedom from karma rather than fulfilled wishes.
Sikhism
“Fareed, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger. Your body shall not suffer from any disease, and you shall obtain everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381-1382, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks Sheikh Farid tells the seeker to repay harm with good and keep anger out of the mind, promising wholeness and fulfilment in return.
The Kural promises that one who never lets even the thought of anger into the mind will attain everything he sets his heart on. Farid makes the same promise in nearly the same terms.
Greek Mythology
Back in his own house in disguise, Odysseus sees the suitors and disloyal maids and is seized with fury, but he strikes his breast and commands his heart to endure, recalling that it endured worse in the Cyclops' cave; this restraint lets him eventually regain his home and family.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 20 [10]
Odysseus' mastery of rage at the critical moment is what makes his homecoming possible.
Keeping anger from ruling the mind leads to attaining what the heart has long desired, as the kural promises.
Philosophy
“Ninth, consider that a good disposition is invincible, if it be genuine, and not an affected smile and acting a part.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.18 (trans. George Long) [11]
Marcus argues that true gentleness cannot be defeated, since the aggressor only harms himself.
Like the kural, a mind genuinely free of anger is presented as unstoppable in achieving its ends.

Real Life Example

Bjorn Borg, the Ice Man of tennis, wins five straight Wimbledon titles
1976-1980 · Wimbledon, London, England
Swedish player Bjorn Borg won the Wimbledon singles championship five years in a row from 1976 to 1980 and six French Open titles, and was known as the Ice Man for never letting opponents see what he was feeling on court. Borg later recalled that he had been suspended as a 13-year-old for bad behaviour and afterwards kept his emotions inside, receiving penalty points only once in his professional career. He retired at 26 with 11 major singles titles.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Borg's refusal to let anger take hold during matches was regarded by himself and others as a central strength of his game.
The kural promises that one who never lets anger enter the mind will attain what he sets his heart on, and Borg's famous composure accompanied one of the most successful careers in tennis.
