Kural 1099

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

ஏதிலார் போலப் பொதுநோக்கு நோக்குதல் காதலார் கண்ணே உள.
Edhilaar polap podhunokku nokkudhal kaadhalaar kanne ula.

Meaning

Looking at each other with a plain, ordinary look, as if they were strangers, is a habit found only among people in love.

Elementary

Two good friends might act normal in front of everyone, as if they hardly know each other, but they still understand each other perfectly. Only close friends can play that ‘we’re just strangers’ game.

University

Ētilār (ஏதிலார்) means ‘strangers, people with no connection’, and potu nōkku (பொதுநோக்கு) is a ‘common’ or neutral look, the kind one gives anyone in a crowd. Kātalār kaṇṇē uḷa (காதலார் கண்ணே உள) asserts, with the emphatic -ē, that this is found only among lovers. Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as lovers outwardly looking like strangers while loving within; Kalaignar specifies that they do this in public places; Puliyur Kesikan applies it to both lovers looking at each other. The couplet draws a general rule from the hero’s experience in 1096-1098. Pope: ‘The look indifferent, that would its love disguise, / Is only read aright by lovers’ eyes.’ Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Although she does not speak to me, / She listens while I speak; / Her eyes turn not to see my face, / But nothing else they seek.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
The king observes that Shakuntala seems not to look at him or speak to him, yet her whole attention is on him.
An outwardly indifferent look that hides full attention is the Kural’s lovers’ ‘stranger’s look’.
Christianity
“O that thou wert as my brother, that sucked the breasts of my mother! when I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; yea, I should not be despised.”
— Song of Solomon 8:1 (KJV) [4]
The beloved wishes she could show her affection openly in public without reproach, as one may with a brother.
Because lovers must look like mere acquaintances in public, the Kural says their neutral looks are a sign of love.
Islam
“An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign.”
— Qur’an 2:273 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says that the dignified poor hide their need, but the perceptive recognise them by their sign.
The Kural likewise says the outward look misleads others, while the one who knows reads the hidden sign.
Judaism
“Esther had not made known her people nor her kindred; for Mordecai had charged her that she should not tell it. And Mordecai walked every day before the court of the women’s house, to know how Esther did.”
— Esther 2:10-11 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Esther and Mordecai hide their close bond in public, yet he watches over her every day.
Two people who behave like strangers in public while staying deeply bound is the pattern the Kural describes.
Buddhism
In the Dhammapada commentary, Patacara, a rich man’s daughter, falls in love with a household servant and hides the bond from her family until the two secretly leave together.
— Dhammapada Commentary on verse 113 (story of Patacara) [7]
A hidden love, kept from others’ eyes, is part of the background of the nun Patacara’s story.
Lovers concealing their bond in front of others reflects the Kural’s ‘stranger’s look’, though the Buddhist story leads to grief and renunciation.
Jainism
“Though those who possess the three Guptis, cannot be disturbed even by well-adorned goddesses, still it is recommended to monks to live by themselves, as this is wholesome in every way.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The disciplined monk remains outwardly and inwardly unmoved even before great beauty.
The Jain monk’s unmoved look is genuine composure, while the Kural’s lovers only pretend to be unmoved, a telling contrast.
Sikhism
“Only those who taste it know its sweet taste, like the mute, who eats the candy, and only smiles.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 635, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak says the sweetness of the Lord's Name is known only to those who have tasted it, and they cannot explain it to anyone else.
The Kural says the cool look that lovers give each other in public is understood only by lovers; Guru Nanak too says only those who have known the love can read it.
Greek Mythology
“No relatives had guessed their secret love, / for all their converse was by nods and signs.”
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.63-64 (tr. Brookes More, 1922) [10]
Pyramus and Thisbe keep their love hidden from their families by speaking only through nods and signs.
Outsiders see nothing, but the lovers understand each other perfectly, which is the Kural’s point.
Philosophy
Pascal writes that the heart has its reasons which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées 277 (Brunschvicg numbering; tr. W. F. Trotter) [11]
For Pascal, the heart perceives and acts on things that detached reasoning cannot see.
Lovers’ seemingly neutral looks carry a meaning that only the heart, not an observer’s reasoning, can read.

Real Life Example

Tchaikovsky and Nadezhda von Meck agree never to meet
1877–1890 · Russia
The wealthy widow Nadezhda von Meck became the patron of the composer Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky after first writing to him in December 1876, and she later paid him an allowance that let him leave teaching and compose full time. For about fourteen years they exchanged more than 1,200 letters, yet by her wish they agreed never to meet in person. She ended the relationship and the allowance in 1890.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Their friendship is one of the most famous relationships in music history, intimate on paper yet kept at a distance in person.
The kural says that keeping up the appearance of strangers is something only those who are close do; Tchaikovsky and von Meck kept apart as strangers in person while sharing deep intimacy in letters.
