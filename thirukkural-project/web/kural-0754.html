Kural 754

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

அறன்ஈனும் இன்பமும் ஈனும் திறனறிந்து தீதின்றி வந்த பொருள்.
Aran eenum inbamum eenum thiranarindhu theedhindri vandha porul.

Meaning

Money earned the right way, without doing anything wrong, brings both goodness and happiness.

Elementary

Imagine two children selling lemonade. One makes it carefully and charges a fair price. The other sneaks sugar from a neighbour and cheats customers. Only the first can enjoy the money without worry and use it to do kind things. Valluvar says honest money brings both goodness and happiness.

University

The verb eenum (ஈனும், ‘brings forth, gives birth to’) is repeated for both aram (virtue) and inbam (pleasure or happiness), so wealth is pictured as the parent of the other two purusharthas. The conditions are thiran arindhu, ‘knowing the proper means’, and theedhu indri, ‘without evil’. Parimelazhagar takes the proper means to be those fitting one’s station and the law, and the absence of evil to mean acquiring without harm or deceit to others. Sivayogi Sivakumar adds that it means seeking wealth with knowledge of one’s own ability and harming no one. The sandhi-joined text reads அறனீனு மின்பமு மீனுந், which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Manusmriti opens its rules for the householder by telling him to gain his living by a blameless occupation that causes little or no pain to others, and to gather wealth only enough for his proper needs.
— Manusmriti 4.2-3 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
The dharma texts treat earning as a duty that must be carried out without harming other beings.
This is the Kural’s theedhindri vandha porul, wealth that comes without harm, as the basis of a righteous household.
Christianity
“Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth.”
— Ephesians 4:28 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the former thief to earn honestly so that he can be generous to people in need.
Paul, like Valluvar, links honestly earned wealth to the ability to do good.
Islam
“O you who have believed, spend from the good things which you have earned and from that which We have produced for you from the earth.”
— Qur’an 2:267 (Sahih International) [5]
Believers are told to give in charity from the good and lawful things they have earned.
The Qur’an and the Kural agree that lawfully earned wealth is what virtue is built on.
Judaism
“You shall enjoy the fruit of your labors; you shall be happy and you shall prosper.”
— Psalm 128:2 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalm promises that someone who walks in God’s ways will enjoy what their own hands have earned.
Honest labour producing happiness is the inbam half of the Kural’s promise.
Buddhism
“And what is the happiness of ownership? It’s when a gentleman owns legitimate wealth that he has earned by his own efforts and initiative, built up with his own hands, gathered by the sweat of the brow.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.62, Anana Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha tells Anathapindika that a layperson finds real happiness in owning wealth that was legitimately earned, and further happiness in using it to make merit.
The sutta’s legitimate wealth that brings both happiness and merit is the Kural’s wealth that yields both inbam and aram.
Jainism
“Nyayasampannavibhavah : Possessed of honestly earned wealth.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, no. 1, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, ‘Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code’ (jainworld.com) [8]
Hemacandra places honestly earned wealth first among the thirty-five qualities of the ideal Jain householder, so lawful earning is the foundation of lay virtue.
The Kural says wealth gained by right means and without wrongdoing gives rise to virtue and happiness, and the Jain householder code makes exactly such honestly earned wealth the first mark of a good layman.
Sikhism
“To take what rightfully belongs to another, is like a Muslim eating pork, or a Hindu eating beef.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh (Maajh ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Maajh ki Vaar, Guru Nanak declares that wealth taken from another's rightful share is as forbidden as the most abhorrent food in each faith.
The Kural says wealth gained by right means and without wrongdoing gives birth to virtue and happiness; Guru Nanak draws the boundary from the other side, condemning wealth taken unjustly as utterly unclean.
Greek Mythology
“Through work men grow rich in flocks and substance, and working they are much better loved by the immortals.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod teaches that honest work brings wealth and also the favour of the gods.
For Hesiod as for Valluvar, rightly gained wealth brings prosperity and divine favour together.
Philosophy
In On the Happy Life, Seneca argues that a philosopher may own ample wealth provided it was taken from no one, is not stained with another’s blood, and was gained without wronging anyone.
— Seneca, De Vita Beata, chapters 21-23 [11]
Seneca answers critics of his riches by saying wealth is no disgrace to a wise man if it was honestly acquired and used well.
Seneca’s conditions match the Kural’s theedhindri, wealth that comes without evil.

Real Life Example

Shibusawa Eiichi publishes his book on ethics and business
1916 · Tokyo, Japan
Shibusawa Eiichi, who helped found Japan's First National Bank in 1873 and went on to help establish hundreds of companies, set out his business philosophy in the book Rongo to Soroban in 1916. Drawing on classical Chinese moral teaching, he argued that morality and economic activity are inseparable. He held that business should make profit by honest means and that its profits should serve the public good.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Shibusawa, often called the father of Japanese capitalism, insisted that wealth earned without ethics was no true success.
The kural says wealth gained by knowing the right ways and without doing wrong gives birth to both virtue and happiness, which is almost exactly the union of moral teaching and the abacus (soroban) that Shibusawa taught.
