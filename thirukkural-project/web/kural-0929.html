Kural 929

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

களித்தானைக் காரணம் காட்டுதல் கீழ்நீர்க் குளித்தானைத் தீத்துரீஇ அற்று.
Kaliththaanaik kaaranam kaattudhal keezhneerk kuliththaanaith theeththureei atru.

Meaning

Trying to reason with a drunk person is like taking a burning torch under deep water to look for someone who has sunk.

Elementary

Imagine your friend has dived to the bottom of a pool and you try to find him by shining a candle from the side. The water puts the flame out and you can't reach him. The Kural says giving good reasons to someone who is drunk is like that: your words can't get through.

University

Kaaranam kaattudhal (காரணம் காட்டுதல், 'showing reasons') is set against keezhneer kuliththaan (கீழ்நீர்க் குளித்தான், 'one who has plunged into deep water'), and the attempt is theeththureei atru (தீத்துரீஇ அற்று, 'like searching with fire'). Thuru (துருவுதல்) means to probe or search, and the -eei is an alapedai lengthening. The image works twice over: fire cannot burn under water, and a drowned man is beyond help. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah read it as trying to sober or reform the drinker with arguments. Puliyur Kesikan takes kaaranam kaattudhal as pointing to another drunk as a warning, which leads into Kural 930. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Kishkindha Kanda, Sugriva, restored to his throne, sinks into drink and pleasure and forgets his promise to Rama; earlier counsel has no effect, and only Lakshmana's furious arrival at the gates rouses him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda [3]
Reasoning cannot reach a king lost in revelry; only a shock brings him back.
As the Kural says, reasons do not reach someone sunk in drink.
Christianity
“Whoredom and wine and new wine take away the heart.”
— Hosea 4:11 (KJV) [4]
Hosea says wine takes away the ‘heart’, which in Hebrew thought is the seat of understanding.
Once understanding is gone, there is nothing left for reasons to work on, which is why the Kural's torch is useless.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not approach prayer while you are intoxicated until you know what you are saying …”
— Qur'an 4:43 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an recognises that an intoxicated person does not know what he is saying, so prayer has to wait until he is sober.
If the drinker cannot follow his own words, others' reasons will not reach him either, as the Kural says.
Judaism
“You will be like one lying in bed on high seas, Like one lying on top of the rigging. ‘They struck me, but I felt no hurt; They beat me, but I was unaware; …’”
— Proverbs 23:34-35 (JPS Tanakh 1985, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs pictures the drunkard as someone lost at sea who feels nothing.
The sea image matches the Kural's man under water whom no light or reason can reach.
Buddhism
“Who is drunk, poor, destitute, still thirsty whilst drinking, frequents the bars, sinks in debt as a stone in water, swiftly brings disrepute to his family.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (Narada Thera trans.) [7]
The Buddha describes the drinker sinking ‘as a stone in water’, still thirsty even while drinking.
The sinking stone matches the Kural's drowned man, beyond reach.
Jainism
“He who has not renounced pleasure, will miss his aim (i.e. the true end of his soul); for though he has been taught the right way, he will go astray again and again.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana observes that a person still held by pleasure keeps going astray even after being shown the right path, so instruction alone cannot save him.
Valluvar says reasoning with a drunk man is like searching with a torch for someone sunk deep under water; the Jain text likewise says that teaching cannot reach one who is still bound to his pleasures.
Sikhism
“Don't argue with fools.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1 (before Pauri 19), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak’s salok ends with plain advice not to quarrel with a fool, since argument does not reach a mind that has lost its judgment.
The Kural says reasoning with a drunk is like searching with a torch under deep water, and Guru Nanak likewise judges argument with the senseless to be wasted effort.
Greek Mythology
At the wedding of Pirithous, the drunken centaurs led by Eurytus try to carry off the bride; no appeal stops them, and the feast becomes the battle of the Lapiths and Centaurs.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book 12 [10]
Once the centaurs are drunk, only force can stop them.
As the Kural says, reasoning is useless against the drunk.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that people in a state like drunkenness can repeat proofs or recite verses of Empedocles without their knowledge actually working, like those asleep, mad or drunk.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VII.3 (W. D. Ross trans.) [11]
The drunk may hear words, even repeat them, but the reasoning does not engage.
The torch is lit, but it cannot work under water.

Real Life Example

The Bronte family fails to rescue Branwell Bronte from drink
1845-1848 · Haworth Parsonage, Yorkshire, England
After he was dismissed from his post as a tutor at Thorp Green in July 1845, Branwell Bronte, the only brother of Charlotte, Emily and Anne, returned to Haworth and over the next three years sank into dependence on alcohol and opium. His family's distress is recorded in Charlotte's letters, the local doctor recommended only that he abstain from alcohol, and in his last year his father, the Reverend Patrick Bronte, took him into his own room to watch over him at night. Branwell's health collapsed and he died on 24 September 1848, aged thirty-one, probably of tuberculosis.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A family of exceptional intelligence and devotion could neither argue nor watch Branwell out of his addiction.
The kural compares reasoning with a drunkard to searching with a torch for someone sunk deep under water, and all the advice and care of Branwell's family could not reach him.
