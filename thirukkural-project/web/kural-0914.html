Kural 914

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

பொருட்பொருளார் புன்னலந் தோயார் அருட்பொருள் ஆயும் அறிவி னவர்.
Porutporulaar punnalan thoyaar arutporul aayum arivi navar.

Meaning

Wise people, who look for the true treasure of kindness, will not touch the cheap charms of women whose only goal is money.

Elementary

If you are saving up for something really valuable, like a bicycle, you do not spend it all on sweets that are gone in a minute. Wise people treat their hearts the same way: they look for real kindness, not for ‘friendships’ that are only about money.

University

The couplet turns on a pun on porul: porut-porulaar (பொருட்பொருளார்) are ‘those whose object (porul) is wealth (porul)’, set against arut-porul (அருட்பொருள்), ‘the wealth that is arul (grace, compassion)’, which the wise aayum (ஆயும், examine and pursue). Punnalam (புன்னலம், ‘petty or base beauty’) is the low pleasure they decline, and thoyaar (தோயார்) means ‘will not immerse themselves in or touch’. Mu. Varadarasanar treats arul itself as the highest wealth, while Solomon Pappaiah reads arut-porul as ‘wealth sought together with compassion’, a reading in keeping with Parimelazhagar’s concern that riches be earned and used with arul. The echo of chapter 25 (Aruludaimai) is deliberate. Ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree, with punnalan(th) sandhi spelled புன்னலந்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Katha Upanishad, Yama teaches Nachiketas that the good (shreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both come to a person; the wise examine them and choose the good, while the foolish choose the pleasant for the sake of getting and keeping.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2 [3]
The Upanishad says real wisdom is discerning lasting good from passing pleasure.
The aayum arivinar of Kural 914 are exactly those who examine and choose shreyas (arut-porul) over preyas (punnalam).
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt... But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven...”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts perishable earthly wealth with lasting spiritual treasure.
Valluvar’s wise man prefers arut-porul, the wealth of grace, to the petty pleasures money can buy.
Islam
“Wealth and children are [but] adornment of the worldly life. But the enduring good deeds are better to your Lord for reward and better for [one's] hope.”
— Qur’an 18:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an ranks lasting righteous deeds above worldly adornments.
The same choice of lasting good (arut-porul) over passing charm runs through Kural 914.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot...”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines wealth as contentment rather than possessions.
Like Valluvar, the Mishnah moves true wealth from money to an inner good.
Buddhism
“Riches ruin only the foolish, not those in quest of the Beyond. By craving for riches the witless man ruins himself as well as others.”
— Dhammapada 355 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says wealth harms only those who crave it, not those seeking the higher goal.
In Kural 914 the money-minded ruin themselves, while the seekers of arul stay untouched.
Jainism
Jain tradition remembers the monk Sthulabhadra, who had once loved the courtesan Kosha. As a monk he asked to spend the rainy season in her house, and at its end his teacher Sambhutavijaya praised him above two disciples who had spent it in a lion's cave and on a wheel.
— Hemachandra, Parisistaparvan VIII.109-169, as summarised from Jinaprabhasuri's Vividhatirthakalpa (OpenEdition / wisdomlib.org) [8]
The story is told to show that a man devoted to the spiritual path can stand amid a courtesan's charms without being moved by them.
It illustrates Kural 914: the wise, who seek the wealth of grace (arut-porul), will not touch the cheap charms (punnalam) of women for whom money is everything.
Sikhism
“The Sovereign Lord is the nine treasures for me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1158, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says that the Lord alone is his whole wealth, while the possessions and spouse people cling to belong to God and go with no one at death. Real riches, for Kabir, are the divine treasure, not what the senses chase.
As the Kural’s wise seek the wealth of grace and ignore cheap charms, Kabir counts the Lord as his true treasure and lets lesser attractions go.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads the young Heracles meets two women: Vice, who promises an easy road of pleasures, and Virtue, who offers a harder road to lasting honor; he chooses Virtue.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 (Prodicus’ Choice of Heracles) [10]
Prodicus’ fable, retold by Xenophon, became the classic Greek picture of a moral choice.
Kural 914’s wise man makes the same choice, turning from punnalam toward arut-porul.
Philosophy
In his defense speech, Socrates says his whole mission was to persuade Athenians, young and old, not to care first for their bodies or property but for the greatest improvement of the soul.
— Plato, Apology 30a-b [11]
Socrates ranks the soul’s good above wealth and bodily comfort.
Valluvar’s aayum arivinar likewise put the soul’s wealth, arul, above money-bought pleasure.

Real Life Example

Augustine's conversion in the garden at Milan
August 386 · Milan, Italy
Augustine, then a teacher of rhetoric in Milan, had long struggled to give up what his Confessions describe as the slavery of the desires of the flesh. In a garden in Milan he heard a child's voice chanting take up and read, opened the letters of Paul and read the passage in Romans 13 that warns against revelling and wantonness and urges putting on Christ. He resolved to change his life and was baptised by Ambrose at Easter 387.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
One of the most influential thinkers in Western history turned decisively from a life of pleasure to the pursuit of spiritual grace.
The kural says the wise who seek the true wealth of grace will not touch cheap pleasures, and Augustine's conversion is a famous example of choosing grace over such pleasures.
