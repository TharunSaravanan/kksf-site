Kural 871

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

பகைஎன்னும் பண்பி லதனை ஒருவன் நகையேயும் வேண்டற்பாற்று அன்று.
Pakaiennum panpi ladhanai oruvan nakaiyeyum ventarpaatru andru.

Meaning

Being enemies with others has nothing good in it. No one should want it, not even as a game or a joke.

Elementary

Some kids pretend to be enemies just for fun, teasing and picking fights as a joke. The poet says even pretend enmity is a bad game, because a joke fight can quickly turn into a real one that nobody can stop.

University

The chapter opens by naming its subject with a moral label: pakai (பகை, enmity) is panpiladhu (பண்பிலது), ‘that which is devoid of good quality’. The emphatic nakaiyeyum (நகையேயும், ‘even in jest / even in play’) carries the force of the couplet: enmity is not to be courted even in sport, still less in earnest. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as an evil without merit that should not be desired even as a pastime, and Kalaignar explains that enmity runs contrary to good character; Puliyur Kesigan adds that this is the settled conclusion of the ethical treatises. The placement is notable: in a chapter that goes on to give hard-headed advice about handling enemies, Valluvar first insists that enmity itself is never desirable. The text is identical in ytamizh and thirukkural.net, which titles the chapter பகைத்திறந் தெரிதல் with a word break.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavad Gita’s list of divine qualities, Krishna includes adroha, freedom from malice or hostility, alongside forgiveness, fortitude, and purity.
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 [3]
The Gita treats freedom from hostility as part of the character of one born to a divine nature, not just a matter of policy.
Like the Kural, it treats enmity as a lack of good character, something never to be cultivated.
Christianity
“If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.”
— Romans 12:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Roman believers to do everything in their power to avoid hostility with anyone.
Both texts make avoiding enmity a standing rule, not an occasional courtesy.
Islam
“Satan only wants to cause between you animosity and hatred through intoxicants and gambling and to avert you from the remembrance of Allāh and from prayer. So will you not desist?”
— Qur’an 5:91 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that pastimes such as gambling are a doorway through which enmity and hatred enter a community.
The Kural likewise warns that enmity can creep in through play and should not be invited even there.
Judaism
“As a mad man who casteth firebrands, arrows, and death, So is the man that deceiveth his neighbour, and saith, Am not I in sport?”
— Proverbs 26:18-19 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Proverbs compares the person who harms a neighbour and then claims it was only a joke to a madman throwing deadly missiles.
Both texts refuse to excuse hostility on the grounds that it was meant in fun.
Buddhism
In the opening chapter of the Dhammapada the Buddha teaches that hatred is never appeased by hatred, but only by non-hatred, and calls this an eternal law.
— Dhammapada 5 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
Enmity feeds on itself; once started, it only grows by being answered in kind.
This explains why the Kural says not to start enmity even playfully: it does not end easily once begun.
Jainism
“Everything that happens to somebody, affects him personally; therefore, knowing the creatures' love of their own self, do not deprive them of their life, but cease from endangering and combating them.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com); cf. the Prakrit kṣamāpanā verse ‘khāmemi savve jīvā’ of the Āvaśyaka/pratikramaṇa liturgy [7]
Because every being cherishes its own life, Mahāvīra's teaching forbids not only killing but all hostility toward other creatures. The daily Jain confession (pratikramaṇa) says the same in its well-known formula: friendship toward all beings and enmity toward none.
The Kural calls enmity a thing with no goodness in it, not to be wanted even in jest, and the Jain teaching rules out enmity toward any creature in any form at all.
Sikhism
“Farid, the Creator is in the Creation, and the Creation abides in God. Whom can we call bad? There is none without Him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 75, Mahalla 5), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok placed among Farid's, Guru Arjan reasons that since God dwells in every creature, there is no one we may write off as bad.
The Kural says enmity has no good in it and should not be courted even in jest; Guru Arjan removes the ground for enmity altogether, since no one is without God.
Greek Mythology
Eris, goddess of strife, threw a golden apple inscribed for the fairest among the goddesses at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis; the contest it provoked led to the Judgment of Paris and ultimately the Trojan War.
— Apollodorus, Epitome E.3.2 [9]
A mischievous gesture, almost a prank, set off rivalries that ended in a ten-year war.
It is a mythic warning that enmity started lightly can grow beyond anyone’s control, as the Kural implies.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes the buffoon as one who will not spare himself or anyone else if he can raise a laugh, and who says things a refined person would never say.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.8 [10]
For Aristotle, humour that wounds others is a vice of excess, not harmless fun.
The Kural similarly refuses to treat hostility as acceptable just because it is done in jest.

Real Life Example

The Pig War on San Juan Island
15 June 1859 · San Juan Island, Washington Territory, United States
On 15 June 1859 the American settler Lyman Cutlar shot a pig belonging to the British Hudson's Bay Company that was rooting in his garden on San Juan Island, whose ownership both Britain and the United States claimed. When British authorities threatened to arrest him, American troops under Captain George Pickett landed, British warships arrived, and by the end of August hundreds of soldiers and dozens of guns faced each other. General Winfield Scott and Governor James Douglas agreed a joint military occupation, and the dispute was settled peacefully by arbitration in 1872, with the pig as the only casualty.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A petty quarrel over a garden and a pig came close to dragging two great powers into war, showing how easily trifling hostility can grow beyond anyone's control.
The kural warns that enmity has no good in it and should not be courted even in jest, and the Pig War shows how a trivial grievance nearly became a real war.
