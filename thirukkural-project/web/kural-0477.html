Kural 477

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

ஆற்றின் அளவறிந்து ஈக அதுபொருள் போற்றி வழங்கு நெறி.
Aatrin alavarindhu eega adhuporul Potri vazhangu neri.

Meaning

Know how much you have, and give in a sensible way. That is how you keep your money safe while still giving.

Elementary

Being generous is good, but give in a way that fits what you actually have. That way you can keep helping people and still keep your household going.

University

Here the chapter turns from military to economic power, since for Valluvar wealth is also a form of vali. Aatrin means ‘in the proper way’, or in some readings ‘according to one’s capacity’. Alavu arindhu means knowing the measure of one’s wealth. The couplet reconciles two duties that could conflict: potri (preserving) and vazhangu (distributing). Measured giving is not stinginess. It is the method (neri) that makes generosity sustainable. Some web editions print aravarindhu here, but the standard Parimelazhagar text and most editions read alavarindhu, ‘knowing the measure’, which fits the chapter’s theme.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Taittirīya Upaniṣad’s parting charge to graduating students, the teacher lays down how gifts are to be given: with faith, not without faith; śriyā deyam (in keeping with one’s prosperity); with modesty, with awe and with understanding.
— Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.11.3 [4]
The Upaniṣad regulates the manner and proportion of giving, not only the act.
If śriyā deyam is read as ‘give in keeping with one’s means’, it parallels the Kural’s alavu arindhu eega.
Christianity
“For if there be first a willing mind, it is accepted according to that a man hath, and not according to that he hath not. For I mean not that other men be eased, and ye burdened.”
— 2 Corinthians 8:12–13 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells the Corinthians that a gift is measured by what the giver has, and that generosity is not meant to relieve others by crushing the giver.
This is very close to the Kural’s instruction to give knowing one’s measure, so that giving and preserving go together.
Islam
“And do not make your hand [as] chained to your neck or extend it completely and [thereby] become blamed and insolvent.”
— Qur’an 17:29 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an forbids both miserliness (a hand chained to the neck) and giving everything away (a hand fully outstretched) that leaves one destitute.
It matches the Kural’s path of giving by measure, which preserves one’s wealth while still distributing it.
Judaism
“Every man shall give as he is able, according to the blessing of the LORD thy God which he hath given thee.”
— Deuteronomy 16:17 (KJV) [5]
The Torah ties the festival offering to each person’s actual means.
Like the Kural, it makes one’s own measure the standard for giving.
Buddhism
The Buddha advises the young householder Sigāla to divide his earnings into four parts: one for his own use and the support of others, two for investment in his work, and the fourth set aside against misfortune.
— Dīgha Nikāya 31, Sigālovāda Sutta (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
Buddhist lay ethics sets a budget in which spending and giving are measured shares of income, not unlimited outflows.
It parallels the Kural’s way of giving while preserving wealth: distribution fitted to one’s measure.
Jainism
“Charity (dāna) is the giving of one's objects to another for mutual benefit–anugraha.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.38, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Pujyapada explains that a gift benefits both sides, bringing merit to the giver and right knowledge to the recipient. He also recalls that charity is to be practised according to one's capacity (sutra 6.24).
The Kural teaches that rightly measured giving preserves the giver's wealth, and the Jain definition of dana likewise sees a properly made gift as good for the giver as well as the receiver.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang, Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak joins honest earning (kirat karni) with sharing (vand chhakna): one gives some of what one has earned, not all of it.
The Kural says to give in the right way, knowing the measure of one's means, so that wealth is preserved while given; Guru Nanak commends giving a part of honest earnings.
Greek Mythology
The poor elderly couple Baucis and Philemon welcome disguised Zeus and Hermes with what little they have: cabbage, a bit of smoked pork, simple wine. They give generously within their means and even try to kill their one goose for the guests.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618–724 [10]
Their hospitality is generous yet in proportion to their modest household, and the gods reward it.
It illustrates giving from one’s real measure, as the Kural advises.
Philosophy
“liberality resides not in the multitude of the gifts but in the state of character of the giver, and this is relative to the giver’s substance.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1, 1120b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle defines liberality as giving the right amounts to the right people, relative to one’s resources. He adds that the liberal person does not neglect his own property, since he wants to be able to keep helping others.
This is the Kural’s doctrine in philosophical terms: generosity measured against one’s means, so that giving and preserving go together.

Real Life Example

Jakob Fugger founds the Fuggerei
1521 · Augsburg, Bavaria, Germany
The merchant and banker Jakob Fugger endowed a walled settlement of homes for needy Catholic citizens of Augsburg, setting the annual rent at one Rhenish guilder and providing in the foundation deed that it should last forever. Funded by the Fugger foundations' endowment, the Fuggerei still houses about 150 people, and its annual base rent remains the equivalent of one guilder, about 88 euro cents.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Because the gift was set up within the means of a lasting endowment, it has kept giving for five centuries without being used up.
The kural says giving in the right way and within one's measure is how wealth is preserved even while it is given, and the Fuggerei is a gift that has outlived its giver by 500 years.
