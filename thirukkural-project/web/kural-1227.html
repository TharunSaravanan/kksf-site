Kural 1227

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

காலை அரும்பிப் பகலெல்லாம் போதாகி மாலை மலரும்இந் நோய்.
Kaalai arumpip pakalellaam poadhaaki maalai malarumin noai.

Meaning

This love-sickness starts as a tiny bud in the morning, grows bigger all day, and bursts into full flower in the evening.

Elementary

Watch a flower: first a tiny bud, then a bigger bud, then it opens wide. The wife says her missing-him feeling grows the same way each day: small in the morning, bigger at noon, and wide open by evening.

University

One of the most admired images in the Kāmattuppāl, the couplet maps the growth of nōy (the love-sickness) onto the three stages of a flower: arumpu (bud) in the morning, pōtu (the full bud on the point of opening; Varadarasanar and Kesikan gloss it as pērarumpu, ‘large bud’) through the day, and malar (open bloom) at evening. The irony is pointed: the flower, symbol of love’s beauty, becomes the shape of love’s pain, and its daily cycle explains why the evening is worst. Solomon Pappaiah calls the disease ‘this flower of love-suffering’, and Karunanidhi generalises it to love itself. Pope: ‘My grief at morn a bud, all day an opening flower, / Full-blown expands in evening hour.’ The text is identical on both web sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When You go off to the forest during the day, a tiny fraction of a second becomes like a millennium for us because we cannot see You.”
— Bhagavata Purana 10.31.15 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami, via vedabase.io) [3]
In their song of separation, the gopis tell Krishna that every moment of the day without him swells into an age.
Like the Kural’s love-sickness that grows through the day toward evening, the gopis’ longing expands hour by hour while Krishna is away.
Christianity
“I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love.”
— Song of Solomon 5:8 (KJV) [4]
The bride in the Song of Songs names her longing as a sickness.
The Kural too calls love-longing a nōy, a disease, and traces its growth through the day.
Islam
“They said, ‘By Allah, you will not cease remembering Joseph until you become fatally ill or become of those who perish.’”
— Qur’an 12:85 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s sons warn that his unending remembrance of his lost son will grow into a fatal illness.
The Kural likewise describes longing for an absent loved one as a sickness that keeps growing.
Judaism
“Hope deferred maketh the heart sick; But desire fulfilled is a tree of life.”
— Proverbs 13:12 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that longing which is put off becomes a sickness of the heart, while its fulfilment is like a living, flowering tree.
The Kural’s wife suffers the first half of the proverb, and pictures her deferred longing as a plant that buds and blooms into sickness.
Buddhism
“Whoever is overcome by this wretched and sticky craving, his sorrows grow like grass after the rains.”
— Dhammapada 335 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares the sorrow that comes from craving to vegetation springing up after rain.
The Kural also pictures love-sorrow as a growing plant, though as a flower that opens by evening.
Jainism
“When he is not satisfied with those ‘colours,’ and his craving for them grows stronger and stronger, he will become discontented, and unhappy by dint of his discontent”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana describes how craving, once unsatisfied, grows stronger and brings ever-deeper unhappiness.
The Kural’s love-sickness grows from bud to bloom in exactly this way, stronger and stronger through the day.
Sikhism
“Many talk of the pain and suffering of separation; O pain, you are the ruler of all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 36) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid crowns the pain of separation (birha) as a sovereign whose rule nothing escapes.
The Kural traces love-sickness budding in the morning and blooming fully by evening; Farid too sees the pain of separation grow until it rules over everything.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls Eros the limb-loosener who shakes her, a bittersweet creature against which there is no defence.
— Sappho, fragment 130 (paraphrase) [10]
Greek lyric presents love as a force that overpowers the body, both sweet and painful.
The Kural’s love-sickness, which blooms into full pain by evening, is the same bittersweet power Sappho names.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates describes how the soul in love begins to grow its feathers again: the growth throbs and aches like teething, and when the beloved is absent the sprouting points are pinched shut and the soul is stung with pain.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251b-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato pictures love as an organic growth within the soul, painful when the beloved is away.
The Kural’s love-sickness also grows like a living thing, budding and opening, and hurts most in absence.

Real Life Example

Nizami completes the romance of Layla and Majnun
1188 (some sources give 1192) · Ganja, in the Caucasus (now Azerbaijan)
The Persian poet Nizami Ganjavi completed his verse romance Layla and Majnun around 1188 as part of his Khamsa (Quintet), at the request of the Shirvanshah ruler. In the story Qays loves Layla so completely that he becomes known as Majnun, meaning madman; when her father refuses the match he flees into the desert, the thought of Layla keeps him awake at night, and after she dies of heartbreak he dies at her grave.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem became the most influential love story of the Persian and wider Islamic world, the classic picture of love as a sickness that grows until it consumes the lover.
The kural shows love-sickness growing from bud to full flower as the day goes on, and Nizami's poem traces Majnun's love growing in the same way until it overwhelms him.
