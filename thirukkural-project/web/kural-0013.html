Kural 13

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

விண்இன்று பொய்ப்பின் விரிநீர் வியனுலகத்து உள்நின்று உடற்றும் பசி.
Vin indru poyppin virineer viyanulagaththu, Ul nindru udatrum pasi.

Meaning

If the sky fails to send rain, hunger will settle inside this wide world circled by the sea and make every living thing suffer.

Elementary

Imagine standing right next to a swimming pool while you are terribly thirsty — all that water, and not a drop you can drink. This kural says the world is surrounded by ocean, but if rain stops falling, people will still go hungry, and hunger will hurt from the inside.

University

The verb பொய்ப்பின் (poyppin) literally means "if it lies" — Valluvar personifies the sky as a party that can break faith, which is why English renderings often speak of clouds that "deceive." The phrase விரிநீர் வியனுலகம் (virineer viyanulagam), "the broad world of spreading waters," sets up a deliberate irony: a planet ringed by ocean can still starve, because salt sea is not rain. உடற்றும் (udatrum) is a strong verb of tormenting or harrying, and உள்நின்று (ul nindru, "standing within") places the affliction inside the body rather than outside it. The couplet is frequently cited in modern Tamil discussion of drought and food security precisely because it distinguishes water-in-general from usable fresh water.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Rāmāyaṇa, the kingdom of Anga under King Romapada suffers a devastating drought and famine; on the advice of his counsellors the king brings the young ascetic Rishyasringa into the land, and with his arrival the rains return and the famine ends.
— Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa 9–10 (the Rishyasringa episode) [4]
The episode treats a failed monsoon as a national emergency whose remedy is sought through a holy person, and it opens the Rāmāyaṇa's account of Dasharatha's sacrifice.
It dramatises the Kural's claim that when the sky fails, hunger becomes the defining condition of an entire kingdom.
Christianity
“And Elijah the Tishbite, who was of the inhabitants of Gilead, said unto Ahab, As the LORD God of Israel liveth, before whom I stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, but according to my word.”
— 1 Kings 17:1 (KJV); famine described at 1 Kings 18:2 [5]
Elijah announces a multi-year drought as judgement on Ahab; the narrative that follows describes a severe famine gripping Samaria until the rains return.
It is the same sequence the Kural compresses into two lines: the sky withholds, and hunger takes hold of the land.
Islam
“Say, 'Have you considered: if your water was to become sunken [into the earth], then who could bring you flowing water?'”
— Qur'an 67:30, Al-Mulk (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an closes Surah al-Mulk with a challenge: human beings have no fallback if the water that sustains them simply disappears.
Both verses press the reader to imagine the world's water failing and to recognise that nothing human can substitute for it.
Judaism
“And also I have withholden the rain from you, when there were yet three months to the harvest … So two or three cities wandered unto one city, to drink water; but they were not satisfied: yet have ye not returned unto me, saith the LORD.”
— Amos 4:7–8 (KJV, rendering the Hebrew Tanakh) [5]
Amos describes a partial drought — rain on one town, none on the next — and the desperate migration of thirsty, hungry populations that followed.
It gives concrete social texture to the Kural's abstract claim that a failed sky means hunger settling into a whole land.
Buddhism
According to the Pali commentarial tradition, the city of Vesālī was struck by drought, which brought famine, then corpses in the streets, then plague; the Buddha was invited, walked the city reciting the Ratana Sutta, and a great rain fell that washed the city clean and ended the crisis.
— Ratana Sutta (Sn 2.1 / Khp 6) and its commentarial background narrative on the famine at Vesālī [7]
The story is the traditional origin narrative for one of Theravada Buddhism's most widely chanted protective discourses.
Its causal order is the Kural's own: rain fails first, and hunger and suffering follow from that failure.
Jainism
Digambara tradition holds that a twelve-year famine in north India drove the teacher Bhadrabahu to lead an exodus of monks south to Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, joined, the tradition says, by Chandragupta Maurya, the first Mauryan king, who had become a Jain monk.
— Bhadrabahu–Chandragupta famine-migration tradition (Digambara), as summarised in Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Bhadrabahu I' [8]
In Digambara memory a famine scattered the whole monastic community and moved Jainism south. Some scholars treat the migration as the start of the Digambara–Svetambara split, and Svetambara accounts instead send Bhadrabahu to Nepal.
It is a Jain memory of just what the Kural warns: when the rains fail, hunger seizes the land and overturns even the life of a religious order.
Sikhism
“The hunger of the hungry is not appeased, even by piling up loads of worldly goods.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Pauri 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the opening stanza of Japji, Guru Nanak says that heaping up material goods never satisfies hunger. The deeper hunger is only met by obeying God's hukam (will).
The Kural pictures a world ringed by oceans that still starves without rain. Japji likewise says that surrounding yourself with plenty does not end hunger.
Greek Mythology
“Often even a whole city suffers for a bad man who sins and devises presumptuous deeds, and the son of Cronos lays great trouble upon the people, famine and plague together, so that the men perish away, and their women do not bear children, and their houses become few”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 238–245 (Evelyn-White translation) [10]
Hesiod warns that Zeus punishes collective injustice with collective calamity, and famine heads his list of instruments.
It matches the Kural's picture of hunger afflicting an entire population from within, though Hesiod traces the cause to human wrongdoing.
Philosophy
“Famine seems to be the last, the most dreadful resource of nature.”
— Thomas Robert Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), Chapter VII [11]
Malthus argues that when milder checks on population fail, hunger is the ultimate and most terrible constraint that reasserts the limits of the earth's productivity.
Both texts treat hunger as the inescapable consequence when the land's capacity to produce food gives out.

Real Life Example

The North China Famine
1876-1879 · Shandong, Shanxi, Henan, Zhili and Shaanxi provinces, China
A long drought struck large parts of northern China from 1876, spreading from Shandong westward through Shanxi, Henan and beyond. Harvests failed and famine reached its peak in Shanxi and Henan in 1878; official accounts stated that Shanxi lost about half of its population. Estimates of the total death toll range from about nine to thirteen million people.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It was one of the deadliest famines of the 19th century, and it was triggered by rains that simply did not come.
The kural warns that if the sky breaks faith and withholds rain, hunger will take up residence in the wide world, and the North China Famine is that warning carried out on a vast scale.
