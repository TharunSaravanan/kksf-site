Kural 944

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

அற்றது அறிந்து கடைப்பிடித்து மாறல்ல துய்க்க துவரப் பசித்து.
Atradhu arindhu kadaippidiththu maaralla thuykka thuvarap pasiththu.

Meaning

Make sure your last meal is digested. Then stick to foods that suit your body, and eat them only when you are really hungry.

Elementary

A plant needs water, but only when its soil has dried, and only clean water, not juice or soda. Valluvar says eat when you are really hungry, and choose the food that suits your body.

University

The couplet adds two refinements to the rule of digestion. Maaralla (மாறல்ல, 'things that are not contrary') are foods that do not disagree with one's constitution; Pappaiah extends this to foods unsuited to the body and to the season (உடம்பிற்கும் காலத்திற்கும் ஒவ்வாத). Kadaippidiththu (கடைப்பிடித்து) means holding firmly to such foods as a fixed practice. Thuvarap pasiththu (துவரப் பசித்து), 'being thoroughly hungry', makes real hunger, not appetite or habit, the signal to eat. Pope's translation renders the sense as 'when hunger prompteth thee, with constant care, the viands choose that well agree'. The idea of wholesome and unwholesome food relative to the person (Sanskrit satmya) is shared with Ayurveda, and the Parimelazhagar tradition reads the couplet as practical dietetics. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Foods in the mode of goodness increase the duration of life, purify one's existence and give strength, health, happiness and satisfaction. Such nourishing foods are sweet, juicy, fattening and palatable.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.8 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, As It Is, 1972) [3]
The Gita classes foods by their effect, praising those that are agreeable and promote life, strength and health.
The Kural's 'foods that agree with the body' correspond to the Gita's sattvic foods.
Christianity
The Preacher calls a land blessed whose princes eat in due season, for strength and not for drunkenness.
— Ecclesiastes 10:17 (KJV), paraphrase [4]
Ecclesiastes praises rulers who eat at the proper time and for the right purpose.
Eating in due season for strength parallels the Kural's eating only at true hunger.
Islam
God tells the Children of Israel to eat of the good things He has provided and not to transgress therein.
— Qur'an 20:81, paraphrase [5]
The Qur'an pairs eating what is good with a warning against going beyond bounds.
Both texts call for wholesome food taken within limits.
Judaism
“They are as follows: a person should never eat unless he is hungry, nor drink unless thirsty.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:1 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides' first practical rule of health is to eat only from real hunger.
This is the Kural's 'eat when thoroughly hungry' stated as law.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches monks to reflect wisely when they take almsfood: not for fun, intoxication or beautification, but only to sustain the body, to end the discomfort of hunger and to support the spiritual life.
— Sabbasava Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 2, paraphrase [7]
The daily reflection on food makes the relief of hunger and the support of the body the only proper reasons for eating.
The Kural likewise makes real hunger, not pleasure, the occasion for eating.
Jainism
“A Sramana engaged in austerities, who longs for righteousness, should eat the proper quantity of allowed food, should select a companion of right understanding, and should live in a place suited to seclusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain monk is to eat only permitted food, and only in the proper quantity.
Choosing allowed food in proper quantity echoes the Kural's choosing agreeable food at the right time, with the Jain criterion being ahimsa and discipline rather than bodily constitution.
Sikhism
“O Baba, the pleasures of other foods are false. Eating them, the body is ruined, and wickedness and corruption enter into the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 16, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a Sri Raag hymn, Guru Nanak warns against eating for pleasure foods that harm the body and disturb the mind, setting them against the true nourishment of the Name.
The Kural tells one to keep firmly to foods that agree with the body; Guru Nanak likewise warns against foods that, once eaten, ruin the body.
Greek Mythology
At Alcinous' palace the grieving Odysseus says there is nothing more shameless than the belly, which commands a man to eat even in his deepest sorrow.
— Homer, Odyssey 7.215-221 (tr. Samuel Butler), paraphrase [10]
Homer's hero admits that hunger rules even a hero's heart.
The Kural accepts hunger's claim but tames it: eat only when hunger is real, and eat what suits you.
Philosophy
Xenophon reports that Socrates ate only as much as he could eat with relish, and came to his meals so ready that the appetite itself was his sauce.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia I.3.5 (tr. E. C. Marchant), paraphrase [11]
Socrates let genuine hunger, not rich dishes, make food pleasant.
This is the Kural's rule to eat when thoroughly hungry, lived out by a philosopher.

Real Life Example

Willem Dicke shows that wheat harms children with coeliac disease
1950 · Utrecht, Netherlands
Dutch paediatrician Willem Karel Dicke presented a doctoral thesis at the University of Utrecht in 1950 showing that wheat flour and rye flour caused the symptoms of coeliac disease in children, while purified wheat starch did not. He had suspected wheat since the 1930s, and observations during the wartime Dutch famine of 1944-45, when bread was scarce, reinforced his view. His work, followed by the identification of gluten as the harmful part of the grain, made the gluten-free diet the standard treatment.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dicke showed that a food that nourishes most people can be harmful to some, so that health depends on choosing foods that suit one's own body.
The kural tells us to keep firmly to foods that agree with the body, and Dicke's patients recovered once a food that disagreed with their constitution was removed.
