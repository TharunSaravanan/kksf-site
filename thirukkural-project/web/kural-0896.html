Kural 896

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

எரியால் சுடப்படினும் உய்வுண்டாம் உய்யார் பெரியார்ப் பிழைத்தொழுகு வார்.
Eriyaal sutappatinum uyvuntaam uyyaar periyaarp pizhaiththozhuku vaar.

Meaning

A person caught in a fire may still survive, but people who keep doing wrong to the great will not escape.

Elementary

Getting burnt by a campfire hurts, but you can heal. Valluvar says that hurting truly great people is even more dangerous than fire, because there may be no getting better from it.

University

The comparison is a fortiori: eri (எரி, fire) can burn a man and still uyvu uNDaam (உய்வு உண்டாம், there may be escape, survival), but uyyaar (உய்யார், they will not survive) who periyaarp pizhaiththu ozhuguvaar (பெரியார்ப் பிழைத்து ஒழுகுவார், live in offence against the great). The chapter title itself comes from this root, pizhai (பிழை, fault, offence). Pope’s explanation, reflecting the traditional commentary, glosses the fire as a forest fire and the great as devotees or ascetics, whose spiritual power is pictured as more consuming than physical flame. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read the great as those of surpassing power generally. The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The sixty thousand sons of King Sagara, searching for the sacrificial horse, accuse and assault the sage Kapila in his hermitage, and are reduced to ashes by his power.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda (sargas 39-40, the sons of Sagara) [3]
Their offence against a great sage destroyed them instantly and completely.
Here the sage’s power literally acts as fire, matching the Kural’s claim that offending the great is deadlier than fire.
Christianity
“It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.”
— Hebrews 10:31 (KJV) [4]
The writer warns that those who scorn what is holy face a judgment more terrible than any human punishment.
The Kural also ranks offending the great as worse than being burnt by fire.
Islam
When Abraham’s people cast him into a fire for opposing their idols, God commanded the fire to be coolness and safety for him, and he came out unharmed.
— Qur’an 21:68-70 [5]
Abraham survived literal fire because he stood with God, while those who opposed him failed.
It echoes the Kural’s point that fire can be survived, while the real danger lies in opposing the truly great.
Judaism
“Warm yourself before the fire of the wise, but beware of being singed by their glowing coals, for their bite is the bite of a fox, and their sting is the sting of a scorpion, and their hiss is the hiss of a serpent, and all their words are like coals of fire.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:10 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Rabbi Eliezer teaches that the sages give warmth to those who approach rightly but burn those who offend them.
This is a remarkably close parallel: the great are like fire, and offending them burns worse than flame.
Buddhism
“Like fine dust thrown against the wind, evil falls back upon that fool who offends an inoffensive, pure and guiltless man.”
— Dhammapada 125 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Harm aimed at a pure person blows back on the one who throws it.
As in the Kural, offending the virtuous great is a fault from which the offender does not escape.
Jainism
“He is the man of great fame and might, of awful piety and power; do not injure him who cannot be injured, lest he consume you all by the fire (of his virtue).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Bhadrā tells the youths attacking Harikeśa that a sage of such power cannot truly be harmed, and that wronging him risks being consumed by the fire of his virtue.
The Kural says a man burnt by fire may survive but those who offend the great will not escape, and the Jain verse pictures offending a sage as calling down a fire that would consume them all.
Sikhism
“Slandering the Saints, one shall not escape the Messenger of Death.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says there is no escape for one who wrongs the holy.
The Kural says one burnt by fire may survive but those who offend the great will not escape, and Gurbani uses the same language of no escape.
Greek Mythology
Ajax the Lesser, hated by Athena, escapes shipwreck with Poseidon’s help, but when he boasts that he has escaped despite the gods, Poseidon splits the rock and he drowns.
— Homer, Odyssey 4.499-511 [10]
He survived the physical danger of the sea but not his defiance of the gods.
Like the Kural’s man who survives fire, Ajax survived the storm but could not survive offending the great.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates recalls that the poet Stesichorus was struck blind for speaking ill of Helen and regained his sight only after composing a recantation.
— Plato, Phaedrus 243a-b [11]
Even a famous poet suffered for defaming a revered figure, and was restored only by making amends.
It reflects the Kural’s warning that offending the great brings a penalty one cannot simply walk away from.

Real Life Example

Galileo is tried by the Inquisition
12 April - 22 June 1633 · Rome, Papal States (Italy)
In 1624 Pope Urban VIII allowed Galileo to write about systems of the universe on condition that the Copernican theory be treated only as a hypothesis. Galileo's Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, published in 1632, did not do so, and the pope convened a commission that found he had disobeyed a 1616 injunction. Tried by the Inquisition in 1633, Galileo was condemned, the Dialogue was banned, and he spent the rest of his life under house arrest near Florence until his death in 1642.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Even the most famous scientist in Europe could not escape the consequences of crossing the conditions set by the most powerful authority of his world.
The kural says one may survive a fire but not the anger of the great one has offended, and Galileo, though spared harsher punishment, never regained his freedom.
