Kural 143

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

விளிந்தாரின் வேறல்லர் மன்ற தெளிந்தாரில் தீமை புரிந்துதொழுகு வார்.
Vilindhaarin verallar mandra thelindhaaril, Theemai purindhozhugu vaar.

Meaning

People who do wrong with the wife of someone who trusts them completely are surely no better than dead.

Elementary

Suppose a friend trusts you so much that they let you into their home anytime. If you used that trust to try to take what is most precious to their family, you would be like a broken, empty person inside, with nothing good left alive in you. That is what this kural says.

University

The key word தெளிந்தார் means 'those who are clear' or 'those who trust without suspicion': the victims here are friends who have let the man into their home precisely because they do not doubt him. Valluvar's verdict, விளிந்தாரின் வேறல்லர் ('not different from the dead'), is a moral rather than literal death: the betrayer has extinguished what makes a person trustworthy and therefore socially alive. Commentators note the intensifier மன்ற ('certainly'), which removes any softening. The couplet thus adds betrayal of trust to the chapter's charge of folly, anticipating the ancient idea, shared by Stoics and scriptural traditions, that faithlessness destroys the essence of a human being.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For in this world there is nothing so detrimental to long life as criminal conversation with another man's wife.”
— Manusmriti 4.134 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
The Laws of Manu say nothing shortens a man's life more than an affair with another man's wife.
Both texts link this wrong with death: Manu speaks of shortened life, Valluvar of a man who is already as good as dead.
Christianity
“But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed. Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death.”
— James 1:14-15 (KJV) [4]
James describes how desire grows into sin and how sin, once fully grown, produces death.
The Kural's 'no different from the dead' matches James's picture of lust ending in death.
Islam
“And she, in whose house he was, sought to seduce him. She closed the doors and said, ‘Come, you.’ He said, ‘[I seek] the refuge of Allah. Indeed, he is my master, who has made good my residence. Indeed, wrongdoers will not succeed.’”
— Qur'an 12:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
When his master's wife tries to seduce him, the prophet Joseph (Yusuf) refuses, pointing to the kindness and trust his master has shown him.
Yusuf refuses to do exactly what the Kural condemns, betraying the household of someone who trusted him, and says such wrongdoers cannot succeed.
Judaism
“Behold, my master wotteth not what is with me in the house, and he hath committed all that he hath to my hand; There is none greater in this house than I; neither hath he kept back any thing from me but thee, because thou art his wife: how then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?”
— Genesis 39:8-9 (KJV) [4]
Joseph tells Potiphar's wife that his master has trusted him with everything, and that betraying that trust with her would be a great wickedness.
This is the Kural's exact situation: the one who is fully trusted in a household refuses to wrong that household.
Buddhism
“Just as rust arising from iron eats away the base from which it arises, even so, their own deeds lead transgressors to states of woe.”
— Dhammapada 240 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada compares wrongdoing to rust that eats away the very iron it grows on.
The Kural says the betrayer is hollowed out and lifeless; the Dhammapada says wrong deeds consume the doer from within.
Jainism
“In the assembly he pronounces holy (words), yet secretly he commits sins; but the wise know him to be a deceiver and great rogue.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.4.1.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In the lecture on 'Knowledge of Women', the Sūtrakṛtāṅga condemns the man who keeps a respectable face in public while secretly sinning with women, and says the wise see through him.
Valluvar condemns the man who plots against the home of friends who trust him without suspicion, and the Sūtrakṛtāṅga likewise condemns the hidden betrayal of a man who looks trustworthy, though it does not share the Kural's verdict that such a man is 'no different from the dead'.
Sikhism
In the Charitropakhyan of the Dasam Granth, the narrator recalls being taught never, even by mistake or in a dream, to go to another's wife's bed, and warns that such love is like a sharp dagger and an invitation to death.
— Dasam Granth, Charitropakhyan (Charitar 21), verses 51-53 as numbered on BaniDB (attributed to Guru Gobind Singh; authorship contested) [8]
The passage presents adultery as something that kills the one who commits it, not only the one wronged.
The Kural says those who plot against the home of a trusting friend are no different from the dead; this text likewise treats such desire as death already working in the body.
Greek Mythology
“Look at Aegisthus; he must needs make love to Agamemnon's wife unrighteously and then kill Agamemnon, though he knew it would be the death of him; for I sent Mercury to warn him not to do either of these things.”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Zeus (Jove) explains that Aegisthus seduced King Agamemnon's wife while the king was away at war, and was warned that it would cost him his life.
Aegisthus wronged the household of an absent king and was, in the Kural's words, already a dead man.
Philosophy
“How shall I consider you, man? As a neighbour, as a friend? What kind of one? As a citizen? Wherein shall I trust you? So if you were an utensil so worthless that a man could not use you, you would be pitched out on the dung heaps.”
— Epictetus, Discourses II.4 (trans. George Long) [10]
Speaking to a man caught in adultery, Epictetus says the adulterer has made himself impossible to trust, and so useless as a neighbour, friend, or citizen.
Valluvar says the betrayer of trust is 'no different from the dead'; Epictetus says he is a useless thing to be thrown away, for the same reason.

Real Life Example

Richard Wagner and Cosima von Bulow
1863-1870 · Munich, Bavaria, and Tribschen near Lucerne, Switzerland
Cosima von Bulow, a daughter of Franz Liszt, was married to the conductor Hans von Bulow, one of Richard Wagner's foremost champions, who conducted the premiere of Wagner's Tristan und Isolde in Munich in 1865. Cosima's relationship with Wagner began while she was still married to Bulow, and she had children with Wagner during the marriage; in 1868 she left her husband and went to live with Wagner at Tribschen in Switzerland. Bulow granted her a divorce in 1870, and she married Wagner later that year.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The affair is one of the best-known episodes in musical history, remembered partly because the man betrayed was a friend who had devoted his talents to Wagner's work.
The kural condemns those who plot against the household of people who trust them; Bulow had welcomed Wagner into his professional and domestic life and was deceived within it.
