Kural 104

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

தினைத்துணை நன்றி செயினும் பனைத்துணையாக் கொள்வர் பயன்தெரி வார்.
Thinaiththunai nandri seyinum panaiththunaiyaak Kolvar payantheri vaar.

Meaning

People who know the value of help see even a tiny favour, as small as a millet seed, as big as a palm tree.

Elementary

A millet seed is tiny, smaller than a grain of rice. A palm tree is huge. A grateful person takes even a tiny kindness, like someone lending a pencil, and treats it like something huge.

University

தினை (foxtail millet, among the smallest grains in the Tamil diet) and பனை (the palmyra, the tallest and most useful tree of the Tamil landscape) form one of the Kural's most memorable scale contrasts; the same pair recurs in Kural 433, where those who fear disgrace treat a fault the size of millet as if it were the size of a palm. The subject பயன்தெரிவார், 'those who discern the fruit or benefit', makes gratitude a cognitive virtue: the grateful person perceives consequences that the literal size of the gift conceals. Kalaignar's gloss exploits the palmyra's many uses (fruit, leaf, timber, sap) to suggest that a small help ramifies into manifold benefit. The same inversion of scale appears in the Gospel's widow's mite, in the Talmud's 'good guest' of Berakhot 58a, and in Seneca's argument that a benefit lies in the intention rather than in the object given.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If one offers Me with love and devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, I will accept it.”
— Bhagavad Gita 9.26 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna says he gladly accepts even a single leaf or a little water when it is offered with love, treating the tiny gift as precious.
The One who needs nothing treats the smallest offering as great, the same 'millet seen as palm' attitude the Kural praises (compare Krishna's welcome of Sudama's handful of rice in the Bhagavata Purana).
Christianity
“And he called unto him his disciples, and saith unto them, Verily I say unto you, That this poor widow hath cast more in, than all they which have cast into the treasury:”
— Mark 12:43 (KJV) [4]
Jesus watches a poor widow drop two tiny coins into the temple box and says she gave more than all the rich donors.
Jesus sees a millet-sized gift as palm-sized because he looks at what it really means, just like the discerning people in the Kural.
Islam
“So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it,”
— Qur'an 99:7 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says that even the tiniest good deed, the weight of an atom, is noticed and counts on the Day of Judgment.
No good deed is too small to matter; the Kural asks the grateful person to see small kindnesses with that same attention.
Judaism
“A good guest, what does he say? How much effort did the host expend on my behalf, how much meat did the host bring before me.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 58a (Koren-Steinsaltz trans.) [6]
The Talmud contrasts a good guest, who counts up everything the host did for him, with a bad guest, who says he only ate one piece of bread and the host did it all for his own family anyway.
The good guest is exactly the Kural's பயன்தெரிவார்: someone who sees a modest meal as a great kindness.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada commentary tells of Radha, a poor old brahmin whom no monk would ordain. The Buddha asked whether anyone remembered a kindness from him; Sariputta recalled that Radha had once given him a single ladleful of rice, and out of gratitude he ordained and trained Radha, who became an arahant. The Buddha praised Sariputta's gratitude.
— Dhammapada Commentary on verse 76 (Radha Thera story) [7]
The Buddha's chief disciple remembered that a poor man had once given him one spoonful of rice, and repaid it by becoming his teacher.
One ladle of rice treated as a debt worth years of teaching is a perfect 'millet seen as a palm tree'.
Jainism
“Those who are at the bottom in (the scale of) friendship are like the areca-tree; those others who are in the middle are like the cocoanut-tree. The friendship of those who have experience of the past is like the palmyra-tree, (whose uses are) difficult to reckon.”
— Nālaḍiyār ch. 22 'The choice of friends', st. 6 (= st. 216), English translation in The Indian Antiquary (wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaḍiyār grades friends by trees, placing at the top those who remember the past, whose benefits are as uncountable as the palmyra's.
Both texts use the palmyra as the image of immeasurable worth, but the Kural's point is that the grateful magnify a millet-sized favour into a palm, while the Nālaḍiyār only ranks friendships by the palm, so the parallel is mainly in the imagery.
Sikhism
“Countless sins are erased, by even a tiny particle of the Lord's Name.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1283, Raag Malaar, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das uses a scale contrast: a mere particle of the Naam outweighs countless sins.
The Kural's millet seed seen as a palm tree is a similar contrast of tiny and vast; Gurbani applies it to the immense worth of the smallest gift of grace.
Greek Mythology
In the Aesopic fable of the Lion and the Mouse, a lion scoffs at a mouse's promise to repay him for sparing its life, but when the lion is later caught in a hunter's net the mouse gnaws through the ropes and frees him.
— Aesop's Fables, The Lion and the Mouse (Perry Index 150; G. F. Townsend trans.) [10]
The lion thought a mouse could never help him, but the little creature's small help ended up saving the king of beasts.
It teaches, like the Kural, that small help can be enormous in value to those wise enough to see it.
Philosophy
Seneca argues that a benefit does not consist in the thing given (money, a meal, an office) but in the goodwill of the giver, so a small gift given with a good heart can be a greater benefit than a lavish one.
— Seneca, De Beneficiis 1.5-6 (Aubrey Stewart trans.) [11]
The Stoic says what makes a favour great is the kind intention behind it, not how much it costs.
Those who 'know the value' in the Kural look past the millet seed's size to the goodwill it carries.

Real Life Example

Gail Halvorsen, the Candy Bomber of Berlin
1948 - 1949 · Tempelhof airfield, West Berlin, Germany
During the Berlin Airlift, US Air Force pilot Gail Halvorsen met children at the fence of Tempelhof airfield and shared two sticks of gum with them, then began dropping chocolate and gum tied to small handkerchief parachutes from his transport plane, at first without permission. The airlift commander, Lt. Gen. William H. Tunner, approved and expanded the drops as Operation Little Vittles, and an estimated 46,000 pounds of candy was dropped on more than 250,000 parachutes. Halvorsen later commanded the American air base at Tempelhof from 1970 to 1974, and in 1974 the West German government awarded him its Order of Merit.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Sweets dropped to hungry children were tiny compared with the food and coal of the airlift, yet Berliners and the wider world remembered the Candy Bomber for the rest of his life.
The kural says those who understand kindness treat a millet-seed favour as if it were a palm tree; a few sweets on handkerchief parachutes were honoured by a whole nation decades later.
