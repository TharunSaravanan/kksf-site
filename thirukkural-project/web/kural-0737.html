Kural 737

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

இருபுனலும் வாய்ந்த மலையும் வருபுனலும் வல்லரணும் நாட்டிற்கு உறுப்பு.
Irupunalum vaaindha malaiyum varupunalum vallaranum naattirku uruppu.

Meaning

The parts of a country are water from rain and from springs, hills in the right places, rivers that flow from them, and a strong fort.

Elementary

A castle in a story often sits by a river, with hills behind it, springs nearby and high walls around it. Water keeps people alive, and hills and walls keep them safe. Valluvar says a country needs all of these, like a body needs arms and legs.

University

Uruppu means ‘limb’ or ‘organ’, so the kural presents the natural features of a land as parts of a living body. Parimelazhagar reads irupunal (‘the two waters’) as water from below (springs, wells) and water from above (rain), and varupunal (‘the water that comes’) as rivers flowing from the hills; the hills themselves are vaaindha (well situated) because they feed the rivers and shelter the land. Kalaignar instead reads irupunal as river and sea, and varupunal as rain. Vallaran, the ‘strong fortress’, anticipates the next chapter, Aran, where hills, water and forest reappear as natural defences. The sandhi text reads ‘வல்லரணு நாட்டிற் குறுப்பு’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him build (there) a town, making for his safety a fortress, protected by a desert, or a fortress built of (stone and) earth, or one protected by water or trees, or one (formed by an encampment of armed) men or a hill-fort. Let him make every effort to secure a hill-fort, for amongst all those (fortresses mentioned) a hill-fort is distinguished by many superior qualities.”
— Manusmriti 7.70-71 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu lists the kinds of fortress a king may build, protected by desert, earth, water, forest, men or hills, and says the hill-fort is best.
Manu’s water, hill and forest forts are the same natural defences the Kural names as limbs of a country.
Christianity
“There is a river, the streams whereof shall make glad the city of God, the holy place of the tabernacles of the most High. God is in the midst of her; she shall not be moved.”
— Psalm 46:4-5 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist sings of Jerusalem as a city refreshed by a river and made unshakable by God’s presence.
A river that gives life and a city that ‘shall not be moved’ match the Kural’s pairing of water and strong defence.
Islam
“And He has cast into the earth firmly set mountains, lest it shift with you, and [made] rivers and roads, that you may be guided.”
— Qur’an 16:15 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an names mountains and rivers among the signs of God’s care for people on earth.
Mountains and rivers are two of the Kural’s limbs of a land, here presented as God’s gifts.
Judaism
The Psalms say that as the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the LORD surrounds his people from now and for ever.
— Psalm 125:2 [6]
The hills around Jerusalem are used as a picture of God’s protection.
The psalm takes for granted what the Kural says outright: surrounding hills are a land’s natural defence.
Buddhism
“There is the case where the royal frontier fortress has large stores of grass, timber & water for the delight, convenience, & comfort of those within, and to ward off those without.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes a royal frontier fortress with a deep moat, high ramparts and stores of water and food, which cannot be taken by enemies. He uses it as a picture of a well-guarded mind.
The fortress needs water and strong walls together, as the Kural’s country needs its waters and its vallaran.
Jainism
“Indra: “Erect a wall, gates, and battlements; dig a moat; construct sataghnîs: then you will be a Kshattriya.” Nami: “Making Faith his fortress, Penance and Self-control the bolt (of its gate), Patience its strong wall, so that guarded in three ways it is impregnable”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.18 and 9.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Urged to fortify Mithila with walls, moat and engines, the renouncing King Nami turns the fortress into a picture of the inner life, with faith, penance, self-control and patience as its walls and bolt.
The Kural lists a strong fortress among the limbs of a country; the Jain text knows the same royal ideal of fortification but moves the real stronghold inside the soul.
Sikhism
“… wind, water, fire and the nether regions - in the midst of these, He established the earth as a home for Dharma.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 7, Japji Sahib (pauri 34), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the thirty-fourth pauri of Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak describes the elements and regions of creation and says that God set the earth among them as a dharamsal, a place where righteous living is practised.
The Kural lists the waters, hills and defences that form the limbs of a country; Japji Sahib likewise names the natural elements that frame the earth, but sees in them the setting for dharma.
Greek Mythology
“It is rugged and not a good driving country, but it is by no means a bad island for what there is of it. It grows any quantity of corn and also wine, for it is watered both by rain and dew ... and there are watering places where the water never runs dry.”
— Homer, Odyssey 13 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Athena, in disguise, describes Ithaca to the returning Odysseus: rocky and hilly, but watered by rain and by springs that never fail.
Rain from above and springs that never run dry are exactly Parimelazhagar’s ‘two waters’, set in a hilly land.
Philosophy
“It should afford easy egress to the citizens, and at the same time be inaccessible and difficult of capture to enemies. There should be a natural abundance of springs and fountains in the town, or, if there is a deficiency of them, great reservoirs may be established for the collection of rainwater.”
— Aristotle, Politics VII.11 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle sets out the ideal site for a city: hard for enemies to reach, with plentiful springs, or cisterns for rainwater.
Aristotle, like Valluvar, names spring water, rain water and natural defence as essentials of a good territory.

Real Life Example

King Kassapa I builds his capital on the rock of Sigiriya
c. 477 to 495 CE · Sigiriya, Central Province, Sri Lanka
King Kassapa I made Sigiriya his capital, building a palace on the summit of a granite rock that rises about 180 metres above the surrounding plain and intending it as a safeguard against his enemies. Visitors entered through a gigantic brick and plaster lion whose paws still frame the stairway. At the foot of the rock lay elaborate water gardens with ponds and terraces, surrounded by a moat.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Sigiriya combined a natural rock fortress, carefully managed water and surrounding lands into a single royal stronghold that is now a UNESCO World Heritage site.
The kural lists water, well-placed hills and a strong fortress as the limbs of a country, and Sigiriya, a hill fortress ringed by water gardens and a moat, brings these features together in one place.
