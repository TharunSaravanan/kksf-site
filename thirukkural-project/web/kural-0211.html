Kural 211

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

கைம்மாறு வேண்டா கடப்பாடு மாரிமாட்டு என்ஆற்றுங் கொல்லோ உலகு.
Kaimmaaru vendaa kadappaadu maarimaattu En aatrung kollo ulagu.

Meaning

Real helping does not expect anything back. What does the world ever give back to the rain cloud that waters it?

Elementary

The rain falls on everyone's fields and never sends a bill. Nobody pays the clouds back, and the clouds never ask. This kural says good people help others the same way — just because it is right, not to get something back.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter by naming the virtue kadappaadu — literally ‘what is owed, what cannot be set aside’ — and then startles the reader by saying this obligation expects no repayment. The rhetorical question (en aatrum kollo, ‘what does it do?’) is read by Parimelazhagar as an emphatic ‘nothing at all’: living beings return nothing to the clouds, so those who resemble clouds do not calculate returns either. Manakkudavar glosses kadappaadu directly as oppuravu, defining it as giving suited to one's means to the deserving without seeking recompense. The image deliberately recalls Chapter 2 (Vaan Sirappu, the Excellence of Rain), turning a cosmological fact into an ethical model: the benefactor is a natural force of sustenance, not a party to a contract. The paradox — a duty that is binding yet unrepaid — anticipates later debates about whether generosity can be obligatory without becoming exchange.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Therefore, without being attached to the fruits of activities, one should act as a matter of duty, for by working without attachment one attains the Supreme.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.19 [4]
Krishna tells Arjuna to do what is right because it is his duty, not because of what he will get out of it.
Like the Kural's kadappaadu, duty here is binding precisely because it ignores any reward.
Christianity
“But love ye your enemies, and do good, and lend, hoping for nothing again; and your reward shall be great, and ye shall be the children of the Highest: for he is kind unto the unthankful and to the evil.”
— Luke 6:35 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells his followers to do good and lend without expecting to be paid back, imitating God who is kind even to the ungrateful.
Same ethic of giving that ‘hopes for nothing again,’ modeled on a divine generosity that, like rain, falls on all.
Islam
“[Saying], ‘We feed you only for the countenance of Allah. We wish not from you reward or gratitude.’”
— Qur'an 76:9 (Sahih International) [6]
The righteous who feed the poor, orphan, and captive say they want neither payment nor even thanks from the people they help.
Mirrors the Kural's cloud that asks nothing back — not even gratitude — from those it nourishes.
Judaism
“Do not be like servants who serve the master in the expectation of receiving a reward, but be like servants who serve the master without the expectation of receiving a reward, and let the fear of Heaven be upon you.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:3 (Sefaria, trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Antigonus of Socho teaches that right action should be done for its own sake, not for a payoff.
Shares the Kural's insistence that genuine duty does not bargain for recompense.
Buddhism
“A person responsive to requests, sympathetic to all beings, delighting in distributing alms ... As a cloud—resounding, thundering—rains, filling with water, drenching the plateaus & gullies: a person like this is like that.”
— Itivuttaka 75 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha sorts people into three kinds: a cloud that never rains, one that rains only in some places, and one that rains everywhere. The best giver is like the cloud that soaks the whole land.
Uses the very same rain-cloud image as the Kural to describe the ideal, all-embracing benefactor.
Jainism
“Man's duty is to give to those who are unable to bear (their distresses), not driving them away, nor turning away from the extended hands … to give to those who will pay it back again--has the name of a loan at interest.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 8 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Nālaṭiyār's chapter on liberality says true giving goes to the distressed who cannot repay, and that giving in expectation of a return is only lending at interest.
Valluvar says duty to others expects nothing back, as the world repays nothing to the rain-cloud; the Nālaṭiyār makes the same point by calling a gift that expects repayment a loan at interest.
Sikhism
“One who performs selfless service, without thought of reward, shall attain his Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 286, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani Sahib), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan describes true seva as service done with no desire for any return.
The Kural says true duty toward others looks for nothing in return, as the world gives nothing back to the rain-cloud; Guru Arjan likewise defines real service as service that seeks no reward.
Greek Mythology
Demeter gives Triptolemus a chariot drawn by winged dragons and wheat, with which he travels the inhabited earth sowing grain for all humankind.
— Apollodorus, Library 1.5.2 [11]
In myth, the gift of agriculture is spread freely across the world by the goddess's messenger.
Like rain, the gift of grain is given to everyone, and no one repays Demeter in kind.
Philosophy
“the immortal gods ... help all alike, among them even those who so ill appreciate their bounty. Let us take them for our guides as far as the weakness of our mortal nature permits; let us bestow benefits, not put them out at interest.”
— Seneca, De Beneficiis 1.1 (trans. Aubrey Stewart) [12]
Seneca argues that people should give like the gods, who help everyone, even the ungrateful, rather than treating gifts as loans that earn interest.
Same model: the benefactor imitates a universal, unpaid bounty, as the Kural's rain does.

Real Life Example

Jonas Salk's polio vaccine is released without a patent
12 April 1955 · United States
On 12 April 1955 the polio vaccine developed by Jonas Salk's team at the University of Pittsburgh, with funding from the public donations of the March of Dimes, was announced as safe and effective. Asked in a television interview with Edward R. Murrow who owned the patent, Salk answered that there was no patent and that the vaccine belonged to the people. He declined to patent it or profit from it, and the vaccine was soon used around the world.
— Sources: [13] [14]
One of the twentieth century's most important medical advances was given to the public rather than treated as private property.
The kural says that true duty to others expects nothing in return, as the world gives nothing back to the rain-cloud, and Salk's vaccine reached the world with no return sought for him.
