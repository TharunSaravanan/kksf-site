Kural 11

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

வான்நின்று உலகம் வழங்கி வருதலால் தான்அமிழ்தம் என்றுணரற் பாற்று.
Vaan nindru ulagam vazhangi varudhalaal, Thaan amizhdham endru unarar paatru.

Meaning

The world keeps living because rain falls without fail. So rain deserves to be called amrita (the drink that makes the gods live forever).

Elementary

Imagine a plant on your windowsill. Water it, and it stays green and grows; forget it for a week, and it droops and dies. This kural says the whole world is that plant, and rain is the water. Because rain keeps everything alive, we should treat it like the most precious drink there is.

University

Valluvar's second chapter makes a striking structural choice: immediately after invoking God he turns not to ritual or scripture but to வான் (vaan), the sky and the rain that comes from it. The key term is அமிழ்தம் (amizhdham), the Tamil form of Sanskrit अमृत (amṛta), the deathless nectar of the gods — so rain is not merely useful but the very substance of continued life, which is why G. U. Pope rendered it "the true ambrosial food of all that lives." The verb வழங்கி (vazhangi, "conducting itself, carrying on") frames the world as an ongoing transaction that rain alone keeps in motion. Medieval commentators such as Parimelazhagar read the chapter as establishing the material precondition of அறம் (aram, virtue) itself, and modern secular and ecological readers have seized on exactly this move: Valluvar grounds ethics in a physical dependency rather than in divine command.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Amrit is in the Waters, in the Waters there is healing balm”
— Rigveda 1.23.19 (Griffith translation) [4]
In this Vedic hymn to the Waters, the poet declares that the immortal nectar (amṛta) and every medicine that heals are literally present within water itself.
This is the same identification Valluvar makes — rain is not merely like nectar, it is amrita.
Christianity
“Nevertheless he left not himself without witness, in that he did good, and gave us rain from heaven, and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness.”
— Acts 14:17 (KJV) [5]
Paul, preaching at Lystra, argues that rain and the harvest it produces are themselves sufficient evidence of a good God at work in the world.
Like the Kural, it treats rain as the single most visible thing that keeps human life going and worth honouring.
Islam
“And We have sent down blessed rain from the sky and made grow thereby gardens and grain from the harvest”
— Qur'an 50:9, Qaf (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an calls rain mubārakan — "blessed" — and names it as the direct cause of every garden and grain crop.
The epithet "blessed" for rain parallels the Kural's "amrita": rain is singled out as the uniquely life-giving substance.
Judaism
“Rabbi Abbahu said: The day of rain is greater than the resurrection of the dead.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Ta'anit 7a (Koren–Steinsaltz, William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Talmud's praise of rainfall ranks it above resurrection, reasoning that resurrection benefits only the righteous while rain benefits righteous and wicked alike.
Both texts rank rain alongside immortality itself — the Kural calls it nectar, the Talmud calls it greater than the raising of the dead.
Buddhism
“Even as the great cloud, Kâsyapa, after expanding over the whole universe, pours out the same water and recreates by it all grasses, shrubs, herbs, and trees”
— Saddharma-Pundarika (Lotus Sutra), Chapter 5, "On Plants" (Kern translation, SBE vol. 21) [8]
In the Parable of the Medicinal Herbs, one cloud rains impartially on the whole world and every plant, small or large, takes what it can and grows — the image for how one Dharma nourishes all beings.
It shares the Kural's premise that a single rain is what recreates and sustains every living thing in the world.
Jainism
“At the end of duḥṣamāduḥṣamā in utsarpiṇī, there will be five clouds that will rain for seven days each. Among these the first, named Puṣkara will sow the earth; the second, named Kṣīramegha, will make grain spring up; the third, Ghṛtamegha, will produce oil; the fourth, Amṛtamegha, will make herbs, et cetera.”
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book X (Mahaviracharitra), ch. XIII, 'Utsarpini' (tr. Helen M. Johnson) [9]
In Jain cosmology, when the worst age of the time-cycle ends, a series of great rains, including an 'amrita-cloud', restores the ruined earth and makes life possible again.
Jain tradition, too, imagines world-renewing rain as amrita, the nectar that brings the dead world back to life, which is just what the Kural calls rain.
Sikhism
“O rainbird, the One unto whom you call - everyone longs for that Lord. When He grants His Grace, it rains, and the forests and fields blossom forth in their greenery... O Nanak! The Ambrosial Nectar rains down forever; the Lord gives it to the Gurmukh.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1281, Raag Malar, Vaar Malar, Salok Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the monsoon raag Malar, Guru Amar Das moves from real rain, which turns forests and fields green, to amrit (ambrosial nectar) raining down without end. Rain and amrit are spoken of in the same breath.
Like the Kural, Gurbani connects life-giving rain with amrit, the deathless nectar.
Greek Mythology
In Homer, the gods live on ambrosia and nectar, the food and drink that make them deathless; Calypso sets ambrosia before Hermes, and gods use it to preserve mortal bodies from decay.
— Homer, Odyssey 5.92–94, 199 (ambrosia and nectar of the gods) [11]
Ambrosia is Greek myth's substance of immortality, reserved for the Olympians and occasionally used to keep a human body incorruptible.
The Greek ambrosia and the Kural's amizhdham descend from the same Indo-European root (*n̥-mr̥tos, "undying"), so Valluvar is quite literally calling rain the ambrosia of the world.
Philosophy
“Thales, the founder of this school of philosophy, says the principle is water … getting the notion perhaps from seeing that the nutriment of all things is moist, and that heat itself is generated from the moist and kept alive by it”
— Aristotle, Metaphysics 983b20 (Ross translation) [12]
Aristotle reports the first recorded Greek philosophical thesis: water is the archē, the first principle out of which everything comes and by which everything is nourished.
Both Thales and Valluvar reach for water as the one substance without which the world cannot be or continue.

Real Life Example

Parakramabahu I builds the Parakrama Samudra reservoir
12th century (reign 1153-1186) · Polonnaruwa, Sri Lanka
King Parakramabahu I, under whom the Polonnaruwa kingdom reached its greatest prosperity, built the great reservoir known as the Parakrama Samudra, the Sea of Parakrama. Britannica describes it as the crowning glory of his reign, with a storage area of more than 5,000 acres serving the irrigation of about 18,000 acres. The reservoir, formed by joining older tanks with new ones built in his reign, still holds water today.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A whole kingdom's prosperity was built on capturing and storing the monsoon rain, and the reservoir has continued to sustain farming for more than eight centuries.
The kural calls rain the nectar by which the world keeps living, and Parakramabahu's vast tank shows a state treating every season's rainfall as the treasure on which its life depended.
