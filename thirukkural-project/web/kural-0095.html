Kural 95

Charming Utterance · Iniyavai Kooral · இனியவை கூறல்

பணிவுடையன் இன்சொலன் ஆதல் ஒருவற்கு அணியல்ல மற்றுப் பிற.
Panivudaiyan insolan aadhal oruvarku, Aniyalla matrup pira.

Meaning

Being humble and speaking kindly are a person’s true jewels. Nothing else deserves to be called a jewel.

Elementary

You can put on the nicest shoes and the coolest jacket and still have nobody want to sit with you. But if you're kind and you don't act like you're better than everyone, people want to be near you. This kural says humility and kind words are the jewellery that actually works.

University

அணி (ani) in Tamil poetic usage covers both physical ornament and rhetorical figure, so Valluvar's metaphor carries a faint irony: the true 'figure of speech' is not an embellishment of language but a quality of the speaker. The pairing of பணிவு (panivu, humility/bending) with இன்சொல் is deliberate — sweet speech without humility collapses into condescension, while humility without kind speech remains invisible. The closing அணியல்ல மற்றுப் பிற ('other things are not ornaments') is an absolute exclusion, and Tamil commentators note it as a rejection of the courtly display culture in which jewellery signified rank. The trope of speech-as-the-only-true-ornament is remarkably widespread: Bhartrihari's Sanskrit formulation is so close that some comparatists have argued for shared circulation of the image across the Deccan in the early centuries CE, though no dependency can be demonstrated.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrihari writes that armlets do not adorn a person, nor necklaces bright as the moon, nor bathing, nor perfumes, nor flowers, nor dressed hair — one thing alone adorns a person, and that is cultivated speech; all other ornaments perish, but the ornament of speech is the enduring ornament.
— Bhartrihari, Nīti Śataka, verse 19 [4]
The verse works through a catalogue of conventional finery and dismisses each in turn before naming refined speech as the sole real adornment.
It is the Kural's claim in almost identical terms, including the explicit denial that anything else counts as an ornament.
Christianity
“Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; But let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price.”
— 1 Peter 3:3–4 (King James Version) [5]
1 Peter contrasts visible finery with an inward disposition of meekness, and names the latter the genuinely valuable ornament.
Same metaphor, same exclusion, and the same pairing of humility with the true ornament.
Islam
“And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily, and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace,”
— Qur'an 25:63 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an's portrait of the ideal believer fuses humility of bearing with gentleness of speech, even under provocation.
It names the Kural's exact pair — humility plus kind speech — as the defining marks of the highest human type.
Judaism
Rabbi Yishmael taught that one should be yielding to a superior, pleasant and easy toward the young, and should receive every person with gladness.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:12 [7]
The maxim binds deference upward, gentleness downward and warmth in all directions into a single rule of bearing.
It couples humility with pleasant speech exactly as the Kural does, as the whole of a person's proper adornment.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada repeatedly dismisses outward religious markers — matted hair, animal-skin robes, ritual bathing — as worthless while the interior remains impure, insisting that the only real distinction is an inward one.
— Dhammapada 393–394 (Brāhmaṇa Vagga) [8]
The verses attack the assumption that visible tokens can stand in for the cultivated qualities they are supposed to signify.
It shares the Kural's logic that external adornments are not ornaments at all; only inner quality is.
Jainism
“He alone is really worthy of proud who is careful not to insult other people. A person who merely boasts, has no virtues, cannot command respect.”
— Saman Suttam v. 89 (cf. v. 88) (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [9]
In the Jain virtue of humility (mārdava), real standing comes from never belittling others, not from display or boasting about one's family, beauty or learning (the preceding verse).
Valluvar says humility and kind speech are a person's only true ornament, and the Jain verse likewise grants honour only to the one who neither boasts nor insults.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the Asa di Vaar, after describing the showy but fruitless simmal tree, Guru Nanak names sweetness and humility as the very core of all good qualities.
It is nearly the Kural's own verdict: humility and sweet speech are a person's true ornament, and nothing else deserves the name.
Greek Mythology
“For one man is inferior in comeliness, but the god sets a crown of beauty upon his words, and men look upon him with delight, and he speaks on unfalteringly with sweet modesty, and is conspicuous among the gathered people, and as he goes through the city men gaze upon him as upon a god.”
— Homer, Odyssey 8.169–175 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb / Perseus) [11]
Stung by a young Phaeacian's mockery of his unimpressive appearance, Odysseus replies that the gods distribute gifts unevenly and that gracious, modest speech outshines physical beauty.
This is the Kural's thesis dramatised: speech joined to modesty is the adornment that actually makes a person admired.
Philosophy
“Politeness is to human nature what warmth is to wax.”
— Arthur Schopenhauer, Counsels and Maxims, ch. III 'Our Relation to Others', §36 [12]
Schopenhauer's point is that courtesy softens people who are otherwise rigid and hostile, making them workable — a small expenditure with disproportionate effect.
It backs the Kural's claim that humble, kind speech is the one quality that genuinely does the work other displays only pretend to.

Real Life Example

Gandhi attends the royal reception at Buckingham Palace in his dhoti
5 November 1931 · Buckingham Palace, London, United Kingdom
While in London for the Second Round Table Conference on India's future, Mahatma Gandhi attended King George V's reception for the conference delegates at Buckingham Palace. He went in his customary dhoti, the garment the press called a loincloth, and slippers rather than formal court dress. Queen Mary recorded that he caused great excitement in what she called his odd get-up.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Gandhi treated humility and simplicity, not costly dress, as the proper adornment even at the most formal court in the world.
The kural says humility and kind speech are a person's only real ornament, and Gandhi appeared before a king wearing nothing grander than his everyday dhoti.
