Kural 472

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

ஒல்வ தறிவது அறிந்ததன் கண்தங்கிச் செல்வார்க்குச் செல்லாதது இல்.
Olvadhu arivadhu arindhadhan kan thangich Chelvaarkkuch chellaadhadhu il.

Meaning

Nothing is out of reach for people who know what they can do, learn what the job needs, and keep working steadily at it.

Elementary

If you know what you’re able to do, learn everything you need for it, and then stick with it without giving up, there’s nothing you can’t reach.

University

This couplet turns the caution of 471 into confidence. It has three stages: olvadhu (discerning what is within one’s power), arivadhu (acquiring the knowledge the task needs) and thangi (settling into the work with steady application). The conclusion, chellaadhadhu il (‘there is nothing that will not go’ for such people), is not a claim to unlimited power. It is conditional: success without limit is promised only inside the field that honest self-assessment has marked out. Knowing your limits is exactly what lets you be effective within them. The verse also defends Valluvar’s realism against the charge of defeatism, since measuring your strength is meant to lead to action, not to paralysis.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna teaches that it is better to perform one’s own duty (svadharma), even imperfectly, than another’s duty well. Work suited to one’s own nature does not bring sin.
— Bhagavad Gita 18.47 [4]
The Gita directs each person to the field of action that fits their own nature and capacity, rather than an ambitious role that belongs to someone else.
This resembles the Kural’s olvadhu, knowing what is truly one’s own to accomplish, as the ground on which steady effort succeeds.
Christianity
“And unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability; and straightway took his journey.”
— Matthew 25:15 (KJV) [5]
In the parable of the talents, each servant is entrusted in proportion to his ability. Those who work faithfully with what they have are rewarded.
Capacity is measured first and diligence follows, much like the Kural’s sequence of knowing what one can do and then persisting in it.
Islam
“Do good deeds properly, sincerely and moderately and know that your deeds will not make you enter Paradise, and that the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant even if it were little.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6464 [6]
The Prophet commends deeds done within one’s capacity and kept up steadily, over bursts of effort that cannot be sustained.
This matches the Kural’s pairing of realistic scope (olvadhu) with steady perseverance (thangi) as the way to reach one’s aim.
Judaism
“But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it.”
— Deuteronomy 30:14 (KJV; cf. 30:11–13) [5]
Moses assures Israel that the commandment is neither too hard nor too far off. It lies within their reach, so they can carry it out.
Like the Kural, it frames a task correctly understood as within one’s capacity, and therefore as achievable.
Buddhism
The monk Soṇa, exhausted by excessive striving, thinks of giving up. The Buddha reminds him that a lute plays well only when its strings are neither too taut nor too slack. In the same way, over-aroused persistence leads to restlessness and slack persistence to laziness, so Soṇa should find the right pitch of effort and then pursue his practice. Soṇa does so and attains arahantship.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 6.55, Soṇa Sutta [7]
The lute simile teaches that attainment comes from effort tuned to one’s real capacity and then kept up. Neither strain nor laxity works.
It matches the Kural’s formula of knowing what one can do and then persisting in it. The result is also the same: the goal Soṇa despaired of turns out to be reachable.
Jainism
“A householder is advised that according to his ability and environment he should proceed stage by stage and that he should observe the rules of discipline that are prescribed for each stage.”
— Vilas A. Sangave, 'Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code', section on the eleven pratimas (jainworld.com) [8]
The eleven pratimas, or stages of lay discipline, are climbed like rungs of a ladder: one rises according to one's ability and moves up only after standing firm on the step below.
The Kural promises success to those who know what they can do and keep steadily at it, and the graded Jain path follows the same pattern of honest self-measure and steady progress.
Sikhism
“Make the effort, and you shall live; practicing it, you shall enjoy peace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 522, Raag Gujri, Gujri Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Gujri Ki Vaar Guru Arjan praises udam, steady effort, and practice kept up over time as the way to life and peace.
The Kural says nothing is out of reach for those who know what they can do and keep steadily at it; Guru Arjan likewise promises the fruit of sustained effort.
Greek Mythology
Trapped in the Cyclops’ cave, Odysseus is about to stab the sleeping Polyphemus. He stops when he realises that he and his men could never move the boulder blocking the entrance. Having recognised what is and is not within their power, he devises a plan that fits it, blinding the giant and escaping under the sheep.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.299–305 and 9.375–463 [10]
Odysseus succeeds because he first identifies the limit of his strength and then pursues only a plan that works within it.
It shows the Kural’s point that knowing what is possible is what makes success possible.
Philosophy
Epictetus opens his Handbook by dividing all things into those within our power (judgement, impulse, desire, aversion) and those not within our power (body, property, reputation, office). He holds that someone who attends only to what is within their power cannot be thwarted.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 [11]
For Epictetus, correctly identifying the sphere of one’s real power is the precondition of an unhindered life.
Like the Kural, it promises that nothing can stop those who understand where their power lies and act inside it. The Stoic version turns inward, while Valluvar’s looks outward to practical tasks.

Real Life Example

Thor Heyerdahl's Kon-Tiki raft crosses the Pacific
28 April - 7 August 1947 · Callao, Peru, to Raroia, Tuamotu Islands (French Polynesia)
To test his theory that South Americans could have reached Polynesia, the Norwegian Thor Heyerdahl and five companions built a raft of balsa logs modelled on early South American designs and set out from Callao, Peru. Critics had predicted the raft would break up within weeks, but after about 101 days and some 4,300 miles it reached the reef of Raroia in the Tuamotu Islands.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The voyage showed that such a crossing was physically possible, though scholars did not accept Heyerdahl's wider theory of Polynesian origins.
The kural says nothing is out of reach for those who know what they can do, learn what the task needs and keep steadily at it, and the Kon-Tiki crew prepared carefully and held to their course for over three months.
