Kural 1077

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

ஈர்ங்கை விதிரார் கயவர் கொடிறுடைக்கும் கூன்கையர் அல்லா தவர்க்கு.
Eerngai vidhiraar kayavar kodirudaikkum koonkaiyar allaa thavarkku.

Meaning

After eating, mean people will not even shake the leftover grains off their wet hand for anyone, except for those who would break their jaw with a fist.

Elementary

After lunch, a few crumbs stick to your hand. Shaking them off for the birds costs you nothing. The Kural says base people will not give even that much to someone gentle who asks, but they will hand over anything to a bully who threatens them.

University

The image is precise: eerngai (ஈர்ங்கை, 'the wet hand') just washed after a meal, which the base will not even vidhir (விதிர், 'shake') so that a few grains fall to others. They give only to the kodiru udaikkum koonkaiyar (கொடிறுடைக்கும் கூன்கையர், 'those with bent, clenched hands that break the jaw'). Parimelazhagar and Puliyur Kesikan read koonkai as the fist curled to strike; Kalaignar makes the contrast explicit between the poor, who get nothing, and the thugs, who get what they demand. The couplet joins the chapter's themes of meanness and fear (cf. 1075). Pope: 'From off their moistened hands no clinging grain they shake, / Unless to those with clenched fist their jaws who break.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But charity performed with the expectation of some return, or with a desire for fruitive results, or in a grudging mood, is said to be charity in the mode of passion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.21 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita ranks grudging giving, or giving for some return, as a lower, rajasic kind of charity.
The Kural's base man gives only grudgingly, and only when forced, which the Gita would place well below true giving.
Christianity
“Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks believers to give freely and cheerfully, not reluctantly or because they are compelled.
The Kural's base man gives only 'of necessity', the very opposite of Paul's cheerful giver.
Islam
“they come not to prayer except while they are lazy and that they do not spend except while they are unwilling.”
— Qur'an 9:54 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes hypocrites who give only reluctantly, against their will.
Like the Kural's base, they part with nothing willingly.
Judaism
“A lower level than that is giving him with sadness.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:14 (tr. Eliyahu Touger, via Sefaria) [6]
In Maimonides' ladder of charity, the lowest rung is giving to the poor grudgingly, with a sad face.
The Kural's base man does not reach even this lowest rung unless he is forced.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha contrasts the miser, who does not even value generosity, with the wise person who delights in giving.
The Kural's base man is this miser, who will not part with crumbs unless compelled.
Jainism
“Those mean persons are of the nature of the chisel, which without being struck will not even penetrate a tender leaf, though resting upon it; they will give nothing to the kind-hearted, but will give anything to those who employ force, if they meet them.”
— Naladiyar 36.5, chapter 'Baseness or Envy' (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Naladiyar compares the mean to a chisel that cuts nothing unless it is hammered: they give nothing to gentle people but everything to those who use force.
This is the Kural's couplet in another image: the base give only to those who strike them.
Sikhism
The Sikh langar, the free communal kitchen attached to every gurdwara, serves food to all who come. Britannica notes that everyone sits in a line with no place of higher or lower rank, and that Guru Amar Das required visitors to eat in this casteless langar.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'langar' and 'Sikhism: Sikh practice' [9]
The langar puts vand chhakna (sharing what one has) into practice: food is given freely and equally, without asking who the guest is.
The Kural says the base will not even shake crumbs from their wet hand except for those who would break their jaw; the langar stands for the opposite, food shared freely with everyone without any pressure.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, asks the suitors in his own hall for scraps, the arrogant Antinous refuses him anything and hurls a footstool at him, though the food he withholds is not even his own.
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Antinous gives nothing to the weak man who asks humbly, and is later destroyed by the very man he despised.
Like the Kural's base man, Antinous will not spare a crumb for someone who cannot force him.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes meanness (aneleutheria) as a vice of deficiency in giving and excess in taking, and calls it incurable and more natural to people than prodigality, noting that some mean people refuse to give even from fear that they will be asked for more.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
For Aristotle the mean man's refusal to give is a settled vice, the opposite of the liberal person who gives gladly to the right people.
The Kural's base man shows Aristotle's meanness in its sharpest form, giving nothing unless compelled.

Real Life Example

King John seals Magna Carta
15 June 1215 · Runnymede, England
Faced with a rebellion by his barons and the threat of civil war, King John of England added his seal to Magna Carta on 15 June 1215, granting rights and limits on royal power. Soon afterwards he persuaded the Pope to condemn the charter.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Magna Carta became one of the founding documents of the rule of law, even though the king who granted it did so only under force.
The kural says the base give nothing except to those who would strike them; King John granted his barons their rights only when they took up arms, and tried to take them back at once.
