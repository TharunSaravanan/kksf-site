Kural 1250

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

துன்னாத் துறந்தாரை நெஞ்சத்து உடையேமா இன்னும் இழத்தும் கவின்.
Thunnaath thurandhaarai nenjaththu udaiyaemaa innum izhaththum kavin.

Meaning

If we keep in our heart the one who left us and does not come near, we will lose even more of the beauty we have left.

Elementary

Imagine thinking all day about a friend who went away and never visits. The more you think about it, the more tired and sad you look. So you tell yourself, “If I keep holding on like this, I’ll lose even more of my sparkle.”

University

The closing couplet weighs the cost of constancy: tuṉṉāt tuṟantārai (‘him who, not drawing near, has left us’; tuṉṉu ‘to approach, be joined’, tuṟa ‘to leave, renounce’) neñcattu uṭaiyēmā (‘if we hold in the heart’), iṉṉum iḻattum kaviṉ (‘we shall lose still more of our beauty’). Kaviṉ is beauty or comeliness, linking back to chapter 124 on the wasting of the limbs. Solomon Pappaiah draws out the force of iṉṉum (‘still more’): having already lost her outer beauty, she will now lose her inner beauty too, a reading echoed in Pope’s ‘further loss of dignity’. The line ends the soliloquy on the heroine’s awareness that her loyalty is consuming her, without any resolve to give it up. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna traces how continually dwelling on an object in the mind deepens attachment and leads to ruin.
The Kural’s heroine sees the same pattern in herself: by keeping the absent man in her heart, she is wearing herself away further.
Christianity
“My heart is smitten, and withered like grass; so that I forget to eat my bread.”
— Psalms 102:4 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes grief so deep that his heart withers and he neglects even to eat.
The Kural’s heroine, holding on to the man who left, is likewise withering and losing what beauty she has left.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you.”
— Qur’an 57:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an counsels believers not to be consumed by grief over what has passed out of their reach.
The Kural’s heroine sees that clinging to one who has left her is consuming her, the very despair this verse warns against.
Judaism
“’Stay ye me with dainties, refresh me with apples; For I am love-sick.’”
— Song of Songs 2:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The woman of the Song says her love has made her literally faint and ill.
The Kural’s heroine, too, is being worn down in body by the love she keeps in her heart.
Buddhism
“From craving springs grief, from craving springs fear. For one who is wholly free from craving there is no grief; whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 216 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that holding on to what one craves is the source of grief.
The Kural’s heroine recognises that holding the departed man in her heart is what keeps her grieving and wasting.
Jainism
“When the daughter of the king heard of the ordination of the Jina, laughter and gaiety forsook her, and she was overwhelmed with affliction. Rājīmatī thought: ‘Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him! it is better I should turn nun.’”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28-29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rājīmatī, abandoned on her wedding day when Neminatha renounced the world, loses all her joy, then turns her grief into renunciation.
Like the Kural’s heroine, she is forsaken by the one who ‘renounced’ (tuṟantār), and her gaiety drains away.
Sikhism
“So smash your bracelets along with your bed, O soul-bride, and break your arms, along with the arms of your couch.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 557, Raag Vadhans, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak tells the adorned bride whose Husband does not love her that all her ornaments are worthless and might as well be broken.
The Kural's wife fears that holding in her heart one who has left her will cost her still more of her beauty; Guru Nanak likewise says that adornment and beauty are wasted when the Husband's love is absent.
Greek Mythology
Spurned by Narcissus, Echo wastes away with sleepless care: her body grows thin, her beauty dries up, and at last only her voice and bones remain.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.395-401 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid shows a lover literally consumed by holding on to one who will not return her love.
The Kural’s heroine foresees the same fate: keeping the departed man in her heart will strip away what beauty she has left.
Philosophy
Lucretius warns that love is a wound that festers and grows worse the more it is fed, and advises the lover to stop feeding it with images of the absent beloved before it becomes incurable.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1058-1072 (paraphrase) [11]
Lucretius sees dwelling on the beloved’s image as the very thing that makes love’s sickness worse.
The Kural’s heroine comes to the same realisation: holding him in her heart only deepens her loss.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria's long seclusion after Prince Albert's death
1861-1870s · United Kingdom
Prince Albert died of typhoid fever in 1861 at the age of 42, and Queen Victoria sank into deep mourning. She wore black for the rest of her life and for years rarely appeared in public, spending more than a decade largely in seclusion. She was widely criticised for her withdrawal, her popularity sank, and a strong republican movement developed in Britain.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her case shows how holding on to grief for a lost husband cost even a queen her standing with her people.
The kural warns that keeping in the heart the one who has gone will cost still more of one's beauty and standing, and Victoria's devotion to the absent Albert cost her much of her public esteem.
