Kural 752

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

இல்லாரை எல்லாரும் எள்ளுவர் செல்வரை எல்லாரும் செய்வர் சிறப்பு.
Illaarai ellaarum elluvar selvarai ellaarum seyvar sirappu.

Meaning

Everyone looks down on people who have nothing, and everyone praises people who are rich.

Elementary

Two children come to a party. One wears a torn shirt and the other a shiny new outfit. Some people smile at the second child and ignore the first, even though nothing else is different. Valluvar is telling us this happens, so that we notice it and do not treat people unfairly.

University

The couplet is built on a tight parallelism: illaarai (the have-nots) against selvarai (the wealthy), and ellaarum elluvar (all scorn) against ellaarum seyvar sirappu (all do honour). Mu. Varadarasanar adds in parentheses that the poor are scorned even if they have other virtues and the rich honoured even if they have none, which makes the verse a comment on injustice as well as a fact of life. Parimelazhagar reads it, like 751, as a reason for the ruler to gather wealth: a king without a treasury would suffer the same contempt. Kalaignar’s gloss calls this simply the way of the world (உலக நடப்பு). The sandhi-joined text reads இல்லாரை யெல்லாரு மெள்ளுவர், which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is seen that a poor man, even when he stands near, is accused falsely. Poverty is a state of sinfulness. It behoveth thee not to applaud poverty, therefore.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section VIII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna argues that society treats the poor with suspicion and contempt, so poverty should not be praised.
Arjuna sees the same contempt for the poor that Valluvar describes.
Christianity
“For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment; And ye have respect to him that weareth the gay clothing, and say unto him, Sit thou here in a good place; and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool.”
— James 2:2-3 (KJV) [4]
James rebukes a congregation that gives the best seat to a rich visitor and pushes a poor one to the floor.
James shows the very scene the Kural describes, honour for the rich and scorn for the poor, and calls it sinful partiality.
Islam
“Wealth and children are [but] adornment of the worldly life. But the enduring good deeds are better to your Lord for reward and better for [one’s] hope.”
— Qur’an 18:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an grants that wealth adorns worldly life but says lasting good deeds count for more with God.
The verse agrees that wealth brings worldly honour, as the Kural says, while pointing to a truer measure of worth.
Judaism
“So I observed: Wisdom is better than valor; but The wisdom of the poor is scorned, And their words are not heeded.”
— Ecclesiastes 9:16 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet tells of a poor wise man who saved a city yet was forgotten, and concludes that the poor person’s wisdom is despised.
Kohelet sees the same scorn for the poor as the Kural, even when the poor have real merit.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada calls contentment the greatest wealth, measuring worth by inner qualities rather than possessions.
— Dhammapada 204 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhist teaching places real value in contentment and virtue, not in what a person owns.
This is a counterweight to the Kural’s observation: the world honours the rich, but the Dhamma honours the content.
Jainism
“When (the priests) saw him coming up, emaciated by austerities, in a miserable condition, and with the poorest outfit, they laughed at him, the ruffians.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Brahmin priests at a sacrifice mock and then try to drive off the monk Harikesa because he looks destitute, until his spiritual power is revealed and they beg his forgiveness.
The episode dramatises exactly what the Kural observes, that everyone scorns the person who appears to have nothing, though the Jain text condemns that scorn rather than treating it as a reason to get rich.
Sikhism
“No one respects the poor man. He may make thousands of efforts, but no one pays any attention to him. When the poor man goes to the rich man, and sits right in front of him, the rich man turns his back on him. But when the rich man goes to the poor man, the poor man welcomes him with respect.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1159, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir describes how the world slights the poor and honours the rich, then concludes that the two are brothers and that the truly poor one is he who lacks the Name in his heart.
The Kural says everyone scorns those who have nothing and honours the rich; Kabir paints the very same scene, almost line for line.
Greek Mythology
“An evil shame is the needy man’s companion, shame which both greatly harms and prospers men: shame is with poverty, but confidence with wealth.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod says that poverty brings shame with it and wealth brings confidence and standing.
Hesiod’s shame of poverty and confidence of wealth mirror the Kural’s scorn and honour.
Philosophy
Juvenal’s third satire complains that the hardest thing about unhappy poverty is that it makes people ridiculous, and it describes the poor man in Rome mocked for his torn cloak and patched shoes.
— Juvenal, Satire III, lines 152-153 [11]
The Roman satirist protests that in Rome the poor are laughed at and shut out while the rich are courted.
Juvenal gives the angry version of the Kural’s calm observation that all scorn the poor.

Real Life Example

Twelve-year-old Charles Dickens works in a blacking factory while his father is in debtors' prison
1824 · London, England, United Kingdom
In February 1824 John Dickens was imprisoned for debt in the Marshalsea prison in Southwark, and most of his family joined him there. His twelve-year-old son Charles was sent to work at Warren's Blacking warehouse near Hungerford Stairs, pasting labels on pots of boot blacking. The family was released some months later after an inheritance allowed the debt to be paid, and Dickens later drew on the debtors' prison in novels such as Little Dorrit.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode shows how quickly debt stripped a respectable family of its standing in Victorian society, and Dickens spent much of his writing life exposing how the poor were treated.
The kural observes that everyone looks down on those who have nothing, and the Dickens family's fall into the Marshalsea and a child's labour in a rat-infested warehouse show that contempt in action.
