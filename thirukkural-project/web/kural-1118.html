Kural 1118

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

மாதர் முகம்போல் ஒளிவிட வல்லையேல் காதலை வாழி மதி.
Maadhar mukampoal olivida vallaiyael kaadhalai vaazhi madhi.

Meaning

Long live the moon! If you could shine like this woman's face, you would win my love too.

Elementary

It is like telling the moon, 'You’re nice, Moon, but if you could glow as brightly as my best friend’s happy face, then I’d love you too!'

University

The hero now speaks to the moon directly (an apostrophe, as to the anicham in 1111), blessing it with vaazhi (வாழி, 'may you flourish') and offering a conditional: vallaiyel (வல்லையேல், 'if you are able') to shine (oli vida) like her face, kaadhalai (காதலை, 'you would be one beloved', the verbal noun as predicate) by him. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan: 'you too would earn my love as she does'; Solomon Pappaiah: 'if you shine so that I rejoice, you would win my love'; Kalaignar reads it rather differently, as advice to the moon to shine like her face if it wants to be loved. The playful implication, as Parimelazhagar’s tradition takes it, is that the moon cannot meet the condition. Pope: 'Farewell, O moon! If that thine orb could shine / Bright as her face, thou shouldst be love of mine.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And is it selfish longing then, / That draws our souls on high / Through eyes that have forgot to wink, / As the new moon climbs the sky?”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act II (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
The king defends his love for Shakuntala by comparing his rapt gaze at her to the way all eyes are drawn to the rising new moon.
Both texts set the beloved and the moon side by side as objects of rapt, loving gaze.
Christianity
“Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?”
— Song of Solomon 6:10 (KJV) [4]
The Song praises the beloved as beautiful as the moon and bright as the sun.
Like the Kural, the Song sets the beloved’s radiance on a level with the moon’s.
Islam
“Al-Bara' was asked, “Was the face of the Prophet (as bright) as a sword?” He said, “No, but (as bright) as a moon.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3552 [5]
Asked to describe the Prophet’s face, a companion chooses the moon rather than a sword as the right image of its brightness.
The Kural too measures a face against the moon’s brightness, though it claims the face shines brighter.
Judaism
“And the king loved Esther above all the women, and she obtained grace and favour in his sight more than all the virgins”
— Esther 2:17 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Esther wins the king’s love above every other candidate.
The Kural says the moon could win the lover’s heart only by matching her; in Esther, no rival could match the one the king loved.
Buddhism
“The sun shines by day, the moon shines by night. The warrior shines in armor, the holy man shines in meditation. But the Buddha shines resplendent all day and all night.”
— Dhammapada 387 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada ranks the Buddha’s radiance above that of the sun and moon, which shine only in their own time.
As the Kural claims a face brighter than the moon, the Dhammapada claims a radiance that outshines it, but the radiance is spiritual.
Jainism
“Now this daughter of an excellent king was virtuous and well looking; she possessed all lucky marks of the body, and shone forth like the lightning Saudamani.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana describes the princess Rajimati, betrothed to Neminatha, as radiant like lightning.
Jain scripture too reaches for a light in the sky to describe a woman’s radiance, as the Kural does with the moon.
Sikhism
“Millions of moons twinkle in the sky.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1162, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir lists the vast powers that serve the Lord, among them millions of moons that give light at His door, as the Punjabi commentators explain.
The Kural tells the moon it would be loved only if it could shine like the beloved's face; Kabir's Lord is so radiant that millions of moons are merely His lamps.
Greek Mythology
Selene, the Moon, falls in love with the beautiful shepherd Endymion and visits him as he sleeps in eternal youth on Mount Latmos.
— Apollodorus, Library 1.7.5; Pausanias 5.1.4 (paraphrase) [10]
In Greek myth the moon herself is a lover, drawn by mortal beauty.
The Kural offers the moon a chance to be loved; Greek myth makes the moon the one who loves a mortal’s beauty.
Philosophy
Thomas Aquinas names three conditions of beauty: integrity or perfection, due proportion or harmony, and clarity or brightness.
— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 39, a. 8 (paraphrase) [11]
For Aquinas, radiance is one of the marks by which a thing is beautiful.
The Kural makes brightness the test the moon must pass to be loved, treating clarity as the heart of beauty as Aquinas does.

Real Life Example

Beethoven dedicates the Moonlight Sonata to Giulietta Guicciardi
1801–1802 · Vienna, Austria
In 1801 Ludwig van Beethoven completed his Piano Sonata in C-sharp minor, Op. 27 No. 2, which was published in Vienna in 1802 with a dedication to the young Countess Giulietta Guicciardi, who had studied with him. Beethoven was deeply drawn to her and described her to friends as a lovely, enchanting girl. The name Moonlight Sonata was given only in the 1830s, when the poet Ludwig Rellstab compared its first movement to a boat on Lake Lucerne in moonlight.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sonata became one of the most famous piano pieces ever written, and its best-known name joins the moon to a work first offered to a young woman Beethoven admired.
The kural blesses the moon and tells it that it could win the poet's love only by shining like the beloved's face; in Beethoven's sonata the moon arrived only later as a nickname, while the music itself was given to the woman he admired.
