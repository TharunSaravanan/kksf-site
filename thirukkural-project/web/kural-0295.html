Kural 295

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

மனத்தொடு வாய்மை மொழியின் தவத்தொடு தானஞ்செய் வாரின் தலை.
Manaththodu vaaymai mozhiyin thavaththodu Thaanam seyvaarin thalai.

Meaning

A person whose words are true and match their heart is greater than people who practice both strict self-control and giving to others.

Elementary

Some people do hard things to look holy, like fasting, and some give lots of gifts. This kural says that someone who simply speaks the truth from the heart, every day, is even greater than people who do both of those big things.

University

Here Valluvar ranks truthfulness above the two most prestigious religious practices of his world: thavam (tapas, ascetic austerity, the subject of chapter 27) and dhaanam (charity/gift-giving). The qualifier manaththodu — ‘together with the mind’ — is decisive: only truth that agrees with inner conviction counts, not lip-truth (Kalaignar: ‘udhattalavil indri ulamaara,’ ‘not just from the lips but from the heart’). The couplet belongs to a broad pan-Indian and wider religious critique of ritualism, in which inner integrity outweighs outer observance. Its hierarchy — truth above tapas — is stated almost verbatim in the Mahabharata’s image of truth outweighing a hundred horse-sacrifices.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A hundred horse-sacrifices had once been weighed against Truth, and Truth was found heavier than a hundred horse-sacrifices.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section 74 (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Shakuntala, rejected by King Dushmanta, reminds him that truth outweighs even the greatest Vedic rituals — equal, she says, to studying all the Vedas and bathing at every holy place.
Exactly the Kural’s ranking: truthfulness weighs more than the grandest religious works.
Christianity
“God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.”
— John 4:24 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells the Samaritan woman that true worship is not about the right mountain or temple, but about spirit and truth.
Inner truth, not outward religious performance, is what counts most.
Islam
“Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but [true] righteousness is [in] one who believes in Allāh… Those are the ones who have been true, and it is those who are the righteous.”
— Qur’an 2:177 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says real righteousness is not the direction you face in prayer, but faith, generosity, keeping promises and patience — and it calls such people ‘the ones who have been true.’
Like the Kural, the verse places inner truthfulness above outward ritual form.
Judaism
“Behold, thou desirest truth in the inward parts: and in the hidden part thou shalt make me to know wisdom.”
— Psalm 51:6 (KJV) [4]
In his psalm of repentance David says God wants truth deep inside a person; a few verses later he adds that God does not delight in burnt offerings but in a broken and contrite heart (51:16-17).
Inner truth over sacrifice — the Kural’s ‘truth with the heart’ above austerity and gifts.
Buddhism
“Not by matted hair, nor by lineage, nor by birth does one become a holy man. But he in whom truth and righteousness exist — he is pure, he is a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 393 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha rejects the outward marks of ascetics; the next verse mocks the man in antelope skins who ‘only outwardly’ cleanses himself.
Truth, not ascetic show, makes a person holy — the Kural’s point about austerity.
Jainism
“One may have a tuft or matted hair on the head or a shaven head, remain naked or wear a rag. But if he tells a lie, all this is futile or fruitless.”
— Bhagavatī Ārādhanā 843 (Śivārya), English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [7]
Śivārya says that all the outward marks of asceticism count for nothing if the ascetic is untruthful. In this text, truth outranks the visible practices of tapas.
Valluvar ranks the one who speaks truth from the heart above those who practise both austerity and charity. The Jain verse makes the same ranking from the other side: without truth, austerity is fruitless.
Sikhism
“Pilgrimages, austere discipline, compassion and charity - these, by themselves, bring only an iota of merit.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 4, Japji Sahib, Pauri 21, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Japji Guru Nanak says that the recognised religious works, including austerity and charity, earn only a sesame-seed's worth of honour. What counts is hearing, believing and loving the Name within.
The Kural ranks truthful speech that agrees with the heart above those who practise both austerity and charity. Japji likewise puts austerity and charity below the inner relation with the True One.
Greek Mythology
At Mecone, Prometheus tricked Zeus into choosing bones wrapped in fat rather than meat, and Hesiod explains this deception as the origin of how humans offer sacrifice — the ritual itself is born from a lie.
— Hesiod, Theogony 535-557 (summary) [9]
In Greek myth the central religious ritual, animal sacrifice, is rooted in a trick played on the gods.
A sideways contrast: Greek myth shows ritual without truth at its origin, while the Kural insists truth outranks ritual.
Philosophy
“He who lies is guilty of impiety to the same divinity; for the universal nature is the nature of things that are.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 9.1 (tr. George Long) [10]
Marcus Aurelius argues that because Nature itself is truth, lying is a sin against the divine — honesty is the true piety.
For the Stoic, as for Valluvar, truthfulness is itself the highest religious act.

Real Life Example

Martin Luther refuses to recant at the Diet of Worms
17-18 April 1521 · Worms, Germany (Holy Roman Empire)
Martin Luther, whose Ninety-five Theses of 1517 had condemned the sale of indulgences, was summoned before the Diet of the Holy Roman Empire at Worms under a safe-conduct. On 18 April 1521 he refused to repudiate his writings unless convinced of error by Scripture or by reason, declaring that his conscience was bound by the Word of God. The Edict of Worms then banned his writings and declared him a heretic and outlaw.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Luther's protest began as a challenge to the idea that religious merit could be bought through payments, and at Worms he placed sincere conviction above every outward religious authority.
The kural ranks truth spoken in harmony with one's heart above both austerity and charity, and Luther, who had attacked purchased indulgences, insisted at Worms that he could speak only what his conscience held true.
