Kural 208

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

தீயவை செய்தார் கெடுதல் நிழல்தன்னை வீயாது அடிஉறைந் தற்று.
Theeyavai seydhaar kedudhal nizhaldhannai, Veeyaadhu adiyuraindh thatru.

Meaning

Ruin stays with people who do bad things, just as a person's shadow never leaves them and stays right at their feet.

Elementary

Try running away from your shadow — you can’t! It sticks to your feet wherever you go. This kural says trouble sticks to people who do bad things in the same way. It follows them everywhere.

University

Valluvar sharpens the pursuit image of 207 into a simile of inseparability: the shadow does not merely follow but ‘dwells at the feet’ (அடியுறை) — the phrase also evokes a servant or dependent who lives at one’s feet. A shadow is produced by the person himself, so the image implies that ruin is not an external punishment but a by-product of one’s own action, as inseparable as it is self-generated. The text itself shows a scribal wrinkle: Project Madurai and thirukkural.net read ‘அஇஉறைந்’, an evident typographical corruption; ytamizh.com and kuruvirotti.com give அடிஉறைந் (‘dwelling at the feet’), the reading adopted here. The shadow simile famously also appears in the second verse of the Dhammapada, but applied to good deeds.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“According as one acts, according as one behaves, so does he become. The doer of good becomes good, the doer of evil becomes evil.”
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4.5 [6]
The Upanishad teaches that deeds do not merely earn outcomes but reshape the very being of the doer.
Ruin is inseparable from the evil-doer because he becomes what he does — the inner logic behind Valluvar’s shadow.
Christianity
“Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap.”
— Galatians 6:7 (KJV) [7]
Paul warns that consequences are bound to actions as a harvest is bound to its seed.
Like the shadow, the harvest is produced by the doer himself and cannot be separated from him.
Islam
“And [for] every person We have imposed his fate upon his neck, and We will produce for him on the Day of Resurrection a record which he will encounter spread open.”
— Qur’an 17:13 (Sahih International) [8]
Each person’s deeds and their outcomes are fastened to him like a collar at the neck — impossible to shake off.
Close to the shadow image: consequences are attached to the person’s own body.
Judaism
“He who performs one commandment acquires for himself one advocate, and he who commits one transgression acquires for himself one accuser.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:11 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [9]
Each sin creates a lasting ‘accuser’ that stays with the sinner until judgment.
Each evil act generates a constant companion of ill — a moral shadow.
Buddhism
“If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.”
— Dhammapada 1 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [10]
The Dhammapada opens with a pair of verses: suffering follows evil like the cart-wheel follows the ox, and (verse 2) happiness follows good like a never-departing shadow.
The same simile family — inseparable following at the feet — used for exactly the same point.
Jainism
“As a young calf when let loose among a number of cows naturally seeks out and attaches itself to its own mother, so does the act of a former state of existence seek out and attach itself to him who has performed it.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 11 ('The effect of actions done in a former birth'), st. 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [11]
The Jain Nālaṭiyār pictures past karma as a calf that unerringly finds its own mother in a herd: a deed finds and clings to the one who did it.
Valluvar's shadow that never leaves the evil-doer's feet and the Nālaṭiyār's calf that seeks out its mother are both images of a deed's result that cannot be separated from its doer.
Sikhism
“Dadda: Do not blame anyone else; blame instead your own actions. Whatever I did, for that I have suffered; I do not blame anyone else.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 433, Raag Aasaa (Patti), Pauri 21, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [12]
In the acrostic Patti of Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak teaches that suffering comes from one's own actions and that it is wrong to blame others for it.
The Kural pictures ruin clinging to evildoers like a shadow, something produced by the person himself; Guru Nanak likewise traces a person's suffering back to his own deeds.
Greek Mythology
At the opening of the Odyssey, Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for their misfortunes, when in truth their sorrows beyond fate come from their own reckless folly — citing Aegisthus, who was warned yet did evil and was destroyed.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.32–43 (paraphrase) [13]
Homer’s Zeus insists that much human ruin is self-generated, flowing from one’s own wrongdoing.
Like the shadow, ruin is produced by the person himself, not imposed from outside.
Philosophy
In the Myth of Er, souls are shown receiving, for every wrong they did in life, a penalty ten times over, before choosing their next lives — carrying their characters with them.
— Plato, Republic X, 614b–621d (paraphrase) [14]
Plato portrays the consequences of injustice as following the soul even beyond death.
Next-best: consequences cannot be shed, even at death.

Real Life Example

The forged Hitler Diaries
April 1983 - July 1985 · Hamburg, West Germany (now Germany)
In April 1983 the West German magazine Stern announced that it had obtained Adolf Hitler's secret diaries, for which it had paid 9.3 million marks through its reporter Gerd Heidemann. Within weeks the West German Federal Archives established that the notebooks had been produced after the war; they had been forged by Konrad Kujau, and Heidemann had kept millions of marks of the payments for himself. In 1985 a Hamburg court sentenced both men to more than four years in prison.
— Sources: [15] [16]
A forgery and a fraud that promised fortune and fame brought prison and lasting disgrace on the men who carried them out.
The kural compares ruin to a shadow that never leaves the evildoer's feet, and the forgery and fraud brought both men a disgrace that stayed with them for the rest of their lives.
