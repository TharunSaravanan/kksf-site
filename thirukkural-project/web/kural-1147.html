Kural 1147

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

ஊரவர் கௌவை எருவாக அன்னைசொல் நீராக நீளும்இந் நோய்.
ooravar kavai eruvaaka annaisol neeraaka neeLum-inh noai

Meaning

This love-sickness grows like a crop. The town's gossip is its fertilizer, and my mother's angry words are its water.

Elementary

A farmer puts manure on a plant and waters it, and the plant grows tall. The girl in this verse says the town’s gossip is like manure for her love, and her mother’s scolding is like water. Instead of stopping her love, they make it grow bigger and bigger.

University

Parimelazhagar sets this in a later situation: the marriage is delayed, the heroine is suffering, and the tōḻi, knowing the hero is within earshot, tells her to bear up in view of the town’s talk and her mother’s words; this is her reply. Nōy (நோய்), ‘illness’, is the love-sickness, imagined as a crop (payir) through a partial metaphor (ēkatēca uruvakam): ūravar kauvai, the talk of the town’s women (he notes ūravar here excludes men, by what they do), is eru (எரு), ‘manure’, and aṉṉai col, the mother’s angry words (veñcol), is nīr, ‘water’, so it nīḷum, ‘keeps growing’. His comment is that the very things that should have made it shrink make it spread, and the intended effect is that the hero will marry. The sources print கெளவை for kauvai and the sandhi-joined நீளும்இந்; the reading is otherwise unvaried.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the gopis hear Krishna’s flute at night, they leave at once, and though husbands, fathers and brothers try to stop them, they cannot be held back.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29 (paraphrase) [4]
The Bhagavata shows family opposition doing nothing to check the gopis’ love for Krishna.
The Kural’s heroine finds the same: her family’s scolding only makes her love grow stronger.
Christianity
In the parable of the barren fig tree, the gardener begs for one more year so that he may dig about the tree and dung it, in the hope that it will bear fruit.
— Luke 13:6-9 (KJV) (paraphrase) [5]
Jesus uses the familiar work of manuring a plant to speak about growth given a last chance.
The Kural uses the same farming image of manure making a plant grow, but the ‘plant’ is love and the ‘manure’ is gossip.
Islam
In Nizami’s Layla and Majnun, when talk of the two young lovers spreads, Layla’s family takes her out of school and keeps her shut up at home, and the separation only makes Qays’s love burn more fiercely until he is called Majnun.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (1188) (paraphrase) [6]
Family restriction and public talk, meant to end the love, only deepen it.
The Kural’s heroine says the same: town talk and her mother’s words make her love grow, not die.
Judaism
“Look not upon me, that I am swarthy, That the sun hath tanned me; My mother’s sons were incensed against me, They made me keeper of the vineyards; But mine own vineyard have I not kept.”
— Song of Songs 1:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [7]
The beloved in the Song complains that her own family was angry with her and set her to work, yet she did not keep her own ‘vineyard’, her own heart.
The Kural’s heroine also faces anger at home, and like the Song’s beloved her love grows in spite of it.
Buddhism
“Whoever is overcome by this wretched and sticky craving, his sorrows grow like grass after the rains.”
— Dhammapada 335 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada pictures sorrow growing from craving like grass that has been watered.
The Kural pictures love-sickness growing like a watered crop; the Buddha uses the same farming image for craving and its sorrows.
Jainism
In Hemachandra’s account, the young Jambu’s parents arrange his marriage to eight brides to hold him back from renunciation, but their pleading on the wedding night only strengthens his resolve, and he converts them instead.
— Hemachandra, Parishishtaparvan, life of Jambu (paraphrase) [9]
The family’s efforts to turn Jambu from his chosen path end up confirming it.
The Kural’s heroine also finds that her mother’s opposition only strengthens what it was meant to stop, though her path is love, not renunciation.
Sikhism
“Make your mind the farmer, good deeds the farm, modesty the water, and your body the field.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 595, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak builds a farming metaphor in which the mind, deeds, modesty and body each play a part in raising the crop of the Name.
The Kural pictures love-sickness as a crop with gossip for manure and the mother's harsh words for water; Guru Nanak uses the same farmer's allegory, naming the water and the field that make the spiritual crop grow.
Greek Mythology
In the tale of Cupid and Psyche, Venus, Cupid’s mother, is furious at her son’s love for a mortal girl, scolds him harshly and persecutes Psyche, yet their love endures and ends in marriage.
— Apuleius, Metamorphoses (The Golden Ass) 4-6 (Cupid and Psyche) (paraphrase) [11]
A mother’s anger, meant to break a love, cannot destroy it.
The Kural’s heroine says her mother’s angry words water her love rather than kill it.
Philosophy
Hume observes that opposition and difficulty, instead of weakening a passion, often rouse the spirits and give the passion more force.
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 2.3.4 (paraphrase) [12]
For Hume, obstacles can intensify the feelings they seem to oppose.
The Kural’s heroine describes exactly this: the town’s talk and her mother’s words make her love stronger.

Real Life Example

Sir George More's fury fails to undo John Donne's marriage
1601-1602 · London, England
In December 1601 John Donne secretly married Ann More, the young niece of Lady Egerton. When Donne told her father, Sir George More, some months later, he reacted with fury, sought an annulment, refused a dowry and had Donne dismissed from his post as Sir Thomas Egerton's secretary and briefly imprisoned. The marriage was nevertheless upheld, More was eventually reconciled to his son-in-law, and the couple stayed together, raising a large family.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A parent's anger and the scandal around a secret match did not break the marriage; the couple held together through years of hardship.
The kural says the town's gossip is the manure and the mother's harsh words the water that make love-sickness grow; for Donne and Ann, a parent's fury and the scandal only bound them more firmly to the life they had chosen.
