Kural 73

The Possession of Love · Anbudaimai · அன்புடைமை

அன்போடு இயைந்த வழக்கென்ப ஆருயிர்க்கு என்போடு இயைந்த தொடர்பு.
Anbodu iyaindha vazhakkenba aaruyirkku, Enbodu iyaindha thodarbu.

Meaning

The wise say our precious life is joined to a body of bone and flesh for one reason: so it can live a life of love.

Elementary

Why do you have a body at all — arms, hands, a voice? This kural says: so that you can love people with them. A hug needs arms. Helping needs hands. The body is the tool love uses, and that is what it is for.

University

This is the philosophical hinge of the chapter. The couplet pairs two uses of இயைந்த (iyaindha, "joined with"): the soul's joining with என்பு (bone/body) and the life's joining with அன்பு (love), and asserts that the first exists as the வழக்கு — the settled practice, warrant, or rationale — of the second. ஆருயிர் (aaruyir, "rare/precious life") marks the soul as something hard-won, which sharpens the question of what it is embodied for. Parimelalhagar and later commentators read the verse causally, with some giving it a karmic gloss (embodiment as the fruit of love practised in a former birth) and others, including modern readers like M. Varadarajan, reading it teleologically: bodily existence is *for* loving. Either way Valluvar refuses a view in which the body is a mere prison for the soul — a pointed position in a South Asian intellectual landscape crowded with world-renouncing ascetic schools — and instead makes embodied relationship the point of having been born.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Teaching his wife Maitreyī before renouncing the world, Yājñavalkya explains that a husband is not dear for the husband's sake, nor a wife for the wife's sake, nor wealth or children for their own sakes — all are dear for the sake of the Self.
— Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.4.5 (Yājñavalkya to Maitreyī) [3]
The Upaniṣad grounds every particular love in a single underlying reality, making love rather than possession the thread that runs through all relationships.
Like the Kural, it treats love as the reason our embodied attachments exist at all rather than as an accidental feature of them.
Christianity
“For as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also.”
— Epistle of James 2:26, King James Version [4]
James argues that an inward commitment which never becomes embodied action is not a live thing at all, using the body-and-spirit pair as his analogy.
James uses exactly Valluvar's structure — the soul/body bond as the model for a virtue that must be joined to lived conduct.
Islam
“The example of the believers in their affection, mercy, and compassion for each other is that of a body: when any limb aches, the whole body reacts with sleeplessness and fever.”
— Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6011 (also Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2586), narrated by al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr [5]
The Prophet describes the community of believers as a single organism in which love is the connective tissue that makes one person's pain register in everyone else.
Both texts use the bone-and-body image to say that love is what makes a collection of parts into one living thing.
Judaism
“Commenting on “love your neighbour as yourself” (Leviticus 19:18), Rabbi Akiva declares: “This is a great principle of the Torah.”
— Sifra, Kedoshim 4:12; cf. Jerusalem Talmud, Nedarim 9:4 [6]
Akiva singles out one commandment as the organising principle from which the entire body of Jewish law takes its sense.
Both make love the rationale that the rest of a life — or a legal system — is arranged around, not one item within it.
Buddhism
“Even as a mother protects with her life her child, her only child, so with a boundless heart should one cherish all living beings.”
— Karaṇīya Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipāta 1.8 / Khuddakapāṭha 9, trans. Amaravati Sangha [7]
The Buddha takes the most embodied, most instinctive human love — a mother's — and prescribes it as the model for an unrestricted attitude toward every creature.
Like the Kural, it treats embodied love as the standard and purpose of a living being's existence, not as a private sentiment.
Jainism
“As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to tyrannise over. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to torment.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.5.5.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
The Âcârânga teaches that the righteous person sees every other living being's suffering as their own, and for that reason neither kills nor causes others to kill.
Valluvar says the soul is embodied for the sake of love; the Jain sutra grounds kindness in the soul's recognition of itself in every other embodied being.
Sikhism
“This human body has been given to you. This is your chance to meet the Lord of the Universe.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa (So Purakh section of Rehras), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn recited daily in the Sikh evening prayer, Guru Arjan teaches that human birth is a rare gift whose whole purpose is loving union with God.
Both texts give embodiment a reason: the precious life takes a body in order to live out love.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, an aged couple who unknowingly host the disguised Zeus and Hermes, ask as their reward only that neither should outlive the other; at the end of their lives they are transformed into an oak and a linden growing from one spot, their branches intertwined.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses VIII.611–724 (Baucis and Philemon) [10]
Ovid gives the pair a single fate because their lives had already become a single life; even their transformed bodies remain joined.
It is Valluvar's image of a life and a body inseparably joined by love, rendered as a pair of trees that cannot be separated.
Philosophy
Empedocles taught that the four roots of all things are moved by two opposing cosmic powers, Love (Philotēs) and Strife (Neikos): Love draws the elements together into living, articulated bodies, while Strife pulls them apart again.
— Empedocles of Acragas, On Nature, fragments DK B17, B35 (5th century BCE) [11]
In his physics, love is not a feeling added onto matter but the literal binding force that assembles scattered elements into a living creature.
This is the Kural's claim in cosmological form — the joining of life to flesh and bone is itself the work of love.

Real Life Example

Viktor Frankl writes Man's Search for Meaning
1946 · Vienna, Austria
The Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl was held in four Nazi camps, including Auschwitz, between 1942 and 1945, while his wife, parents and brother died. After liberation he dictated, within nine days, the book later published in English as Man's Search for Meaning. In it he describes a winter march during which he held on to the image of his wife and concluded that love reaches far beyond the physical person of the beloved and finds its deepest meaning in the beloved's inner self.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Frankl's account, written from the extremity of the camps, argues that the meaning of a human life is found in love, even when the body is reduced to bare survival.
The kural claims that the soul's union with the body exists for the sake of love, and Frankl found that, stripped of everything else, love was what gave his embodied life its point.
