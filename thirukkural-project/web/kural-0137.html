Kural 137

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

ஒழுக்கத்தின் எய்துவர் மேன்மை இழுக்கத்தின் எய்துவர் எய்தாப் பழி.
Ozhukkaththin eydhuvar menmai izhukkaththin Eydhuvar eydhaap pazhi.

Meaning

Through good behaviour people become great. Through bad behaviour they earn a shame they never needed to suffer.

Elementary

When you are kind and honest, people look up to you. When you cheat or lie, you get a bad name, and it is a bad name you could easily have avoided.

University

The couplet is built as a strict parallel: ozhukkaththin eydhuvar menmai / izhukkaththin eydhuvar eydhaap pazhi, ‘by conduct they attain eminence; by lapse they attain unattainable blame’. The paradoxical phrase eydhaap pazhi (‘blame that should not be attained’) is read in different ways: as undeserved, as unbearable, or as blame one had no need to incur. Pope's ‘censures undeserved’ takes the first reading, and most modern commentators take the last. Either way, the couplet makes the social stakes of conduct explicit, because menmai (eminence) and pazhi (blame) are verdicts passed by others. Here the chapter moves from inner firmness (136) to public consequence. The same pairing appears in Sanskrit dharmashastra, in the rabbinic ‘crown of a good name’, and in Hesiod's steep path to excellence.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For a man of bad conduct is blamed among people, constantly suffers misfortunes, is afflicted with diseases, and short-lived.”
— Manusmriti 4.157 (tr. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Manu lists the results of bad conduct, and public blame comes first.
This spells out the second half of the Kural: a lapse brings disgrace among people.
Christianity
“The memory of the just is blessed: but the name of the wicked shall rot.”
— Proverbs 10:7 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs sets the lasting honour of the righteous against the rotting reputation of the wicked.
Its two-part structure (honour through goodness, disgrace through wickedness) matches the Kural's parallel lines.
Islam
“Nothing is heavier on the believer's Scale on the Day of Judgment than good character. For indeed Allah, Most High, is angered by the shameless obscene person.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2002 (graded Sahih) [5]
Good character carries the greatest weight, while shameless conduct draws God's displeasure.
Both set honour through good conduct against disgrace through bad conduct.
Judaism
“There are three crowns: the crown of Torah, the crown of priesthood, and the crown of royalty, but the crown of a good name supersedes them all.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:13 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Shimon ranks a good name, earned by conduct, above learning, priesthood and kingship.
Like the Kural, it makes eminence (menmai) the fruit of conduct.
Buddhism
“But the fragrance of the virtuous blows against the wind. Truly the virtuous man pervades all directions with the fragrance of his virtue.”
— Dhammapada 54 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The good name of a virtuous person spreads everywhere, even against the wind, which no flower's scent can do.
This is the Kural's first half: conduct brings a widely recognised eminence.
Jainism
“As a bitch with sore ears is driven away everywhere, thus a bad, insubordinate, and talkative (pupil) is turned out. … Hearing a man thus compared to a dog and a pig, he who desires his own welfare, should adhere to good conduct.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.4–6 (with 1.42) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The lecture on discipline contrasts the disgrace of the ill-behaved, who are driven out everywhere, with the honour of those who follow the conduct of the wise and 'will not incur blame' (1.42).
The Kural pairs greatness gained by conduct with needless disgrace earned by lapse; the Uttarādhyayana draws the same contrast in social terms of expulsion versus honour.
Sikhism
“As are the deeds done, so is the reputation one acquires.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says the name a person earns follows directly from the deeds he does.
The Kural says conduct brings eminence and a lapse brings blame; Guru Nanak ties reputation to deeds in the same direct way.
Greek Mythology
“Badness can be got easily and in shoals: the road to her is smooth, and she lives very near us. But between us and Goodness the gods have placed the sweat of our brows: long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the first”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 287–291 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells his brother that vice is easy to reach, while excellence has to be climbed to by effort.
Both contrast the high standing won by virtue with the low road of vice.
Philosophy
At the close of the Republic, Socrates argues that in the long run the just are honoured by gods and men. The unjust are like runners who start fast and fail at the finish, ending in contempt and disgrace.
— Plato, Republic X, 613b–e [11]
Over time, justice brings honour and injustice brings public disgrace.
This mirrors both halves of the Kural's parallel.

Real Life Example

South African cricket captain Hansie Cronje admits taking bookmakers' money
2000 · South Africa and India
Hansie Cronje, captain of South Africa's national cricket team and one of the country's most admired sportsmen, was charged by Delhi police in April 2000 with fixing one-day matches played in India, which he at first denied. He was sacked as captain, and in testimony before the King Commission of inquiry in June 2000 he admitted taking money from bookmakers and offering teammates money to underperform. In October 2000 the United Cricket Board of South Africa banned him from cricket for life.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Cronje's fall was so striking because his reputation for leadership had been built on years of disciplined play, and it was undone by choices he had no need to make.
The kural contrasts the eminence gained through conduct with the needless disgrace a lapse brings; Cronje rose through the first and fell through the second.
