Kural 204

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

மறந்தும் பிறன்கேடு சூழற்க சூழின் அறஞ்சூழும் சூழ்ந்தவன் கேடு.
Marandhum pirankedu soozharka soozhin, Aransoozhum soozhndhavan kedu.

Meaning

Do not plan someone else's ruin, not even without thinking. If you do, goodness itself will plan the ruin of the one who planned it.

Elementary

It’s like throwing a boomerang at someone — it comes flying back at you. This kural says even thinking up a plan to hurt someone is like that boomerang: goodness itself will turn the plan around so it lands on you.

University

The verb சூழ் (‘to plan, surround, encompass’) is repeated three times, mirroring the plot and counter-plot: the schemer ‘surrounds’ another’s ruin, and Aram (Virtue, personified by Solomon Pappaiah as அறக்கடவுள், the deity of Virtue) ‘surrounds’ the schemer’s ruin in turn. Note the extreme standard set by மறந்தும் (‘even in forgetfulness’): not merely the deed but even a passing intention to harm is forbidden. The textual tradition is worth noting — Project Madurai and thirukkural.net print அறஞ்சூழம், but the standard reading followed by commentators and kuraltheni.com is அறஞ்சூழும் (‘will plot’). The ‘plot that recoils on the plotter’ is a near-universal motif, from Haman’s gallows to Hesiod’s ‘evil planned harms the plotter most’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata’s lac-house episode, Duryodhana sends his agent Purochana to build a flammable house at Varanavata and burn the Pandavas alive; forewarned by Vidura, the Pandavas escape through a tunnel, and Purochana perishes in the very fire prepared for them.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Jatugriha Parva (Ganguli; paraphrase) [5]
The agent of the murderous plot is consumed by his own scheme, while the intended victims survive.
A narrative illustration of the Kural: the plot to ruin others encompasses the plotter.
Christianity
“Whoso diggeth a pit shall fall therein: and he that rolleth a stone, it will return upon him.”
— Proverbs 26:27 (KJV) [6]
The proverb warns that schemes meant to trap others become traps for the schemer.
Direct parallel to the plot rebounding on the plotter.
Islam
“[Due to] arrogance in the land and plotting of evil; but the evil plot does not encompass except its own people.”
— Qur’an 35:43 (Sahih International) [7]
The Qur’an states as a divine law that evil scheming closes in on those who devise it.
Even the verb matches: the Arabic yaḥīqu (‘encompasses’) corresponds to Valluvar’s சூழ் (‘surround, encompass’).
Judaism
“So they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai.”
— Esther 7:10 (KJV) [6]
Haman plotted to destroy Mordecai and the Jews of Persia; his plan was reversed and he died on the gallows he had built for his enemy.
The classic Jewish story of the plot recoiling on the plotter.
Buddhism
“Like fine dust thrown against the wind, evil falls back upon that fool who offends an inoffensive, pure and guiltless man.”
— Dhammapada 125 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
Harm aimed at the innocent blows back into the face of the one who threw it.
Same boomerang structure as the Kural.
Jainism
“As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to kill. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to tyrannise over. As it would be unto thee, so it is with him whom thou intendest to torment… The righteous man who lives up to these sentiments, does therefore neither kill nor cause others to kill (living beings). He should not intentionally cause the same punishment for himself.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.5.5.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The Ācārāṅga tells the would-be harmer that his intended victim is like himself. Jacobi's note explains that the pain one causes others in this life one will suffer in the next.
Valluvar warns that whoever plots another's ruin brings ruin on himself; the Ācārāṅga addresses the person who 'intends' harm and says the punishment comes back to the plotter.
Sikhism
“Do not harbor evil intentions against others in your mind, and you shall not be troubled, O Siblings of Destiny, O friends.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 386, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a hymn in Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan forbids even keeping thoughts of harm toward others in the mind, and says that one who does not will be spared trouble himself.
The Kural forbids plotting another's ruin even absent-mindedly, warning that Virtue will plot the plotter's ruin; Guru Arjan likewise bans evil intent at the level of thought and ties one's own peace to it.
Greek Mythology
“He does mischief to himself who does mischief to another, and evil planned harms the plotter most.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 265–266 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod warns his brother Perses that unjust scheming damages the schemer above all.
Almost a translation of the Kural’s second line.
Philosophy
“He who does wrong does wrong against himself. He who acts unjustly acts unjustly to himself, because he makes himself bad.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 9.4 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius argues that the primary victim of wrongdoing is the wrongdoer, whose character is degraded by the act.
Shares the conclusion that harm aimed at others lands on oneself, though via inner corruption rather than cosmic retribution.

Real Life Example

Titus Oates and the invented Popish Plot
1678-1685 · London, England, United Kingdom
In 1678 Titus Oates, a former Anglican clergyman, swore to a fictitious Jesuit plot to assassinate King Charles II and put his Catholic brother James, Duke of York, on the throne. In the panic that followed, about 35 innocent people were put to death on the strength of the accusations before inconsistencies in his story came to light. In 1684 the Duke of York won 100,000 pounds in damages from Oates for libel, and after James became king in 1685 Oates was convicted of perjury and severely punished, including the pillory and imprisonment.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The man who manufactured a plot to destroy others was eventually ruined by the exposure of his own lies.
The kural warns that whoever plots another's ruin will find virtue itself plotting his ruin in turn, and Oates's invented conspiracy ended in his own conviction and disgrace.
