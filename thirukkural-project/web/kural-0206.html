Kural 206

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

தீப்பால தான்பிறர்கண் செய்யற்க நோய்ப்பால தன்னை அடல்வேண்டா தான்.
Theeppaala thaanpirarkan seyyarka noippaala, Thannai adalvendaa thaan.

Meaning

If you do not want pain and trouble to hurt you, never do bad things to others.

Elementary

If you don’t want anyone to pull your hair, don’t pull anyone else’s hair. This kural says: if you don’t want bad things to happen to you, don’t do bad things to other people.

University

The couplet is built on a pair of parallel compounds — தீப்பால (‘things of the evil kind’) done to others, and நோய்ப்பால (‘things of the suffering kind’) that might afflict oneself — so that the grammar itself enacts reciprocity. Commentators agree the sense is causal: suffering that ‘attacks’ (அடல்) a person is the return of the evil he has done. The argument is prudential and universal — anyone who does not want pain has a reason not to inflict it — which makes this Valluvar’s version of the negative Golden Rule. That rule appears independently in the Mahabharata, in Hillel, in Isocrates, in the Buddha and in the Gospels, one of the most striking convergences in world ethics.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One should never do that to another which one regards as injurious to one’s own self. This, in brief, is the rule of Righteousness.”
— Mahabharata, Anusasana Parva, Section CXIII (Ganguli) [4]
Brihaspati tells Yudhishthira that all dharma can be summed up in this one principle, adding that when one injures another, the injured turns and injures the injurer.
The negative Golden Rule plus the reciprocal consequence — both halves of the Kural.
Christianity
“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets.”
— Matthew 7:12 (KJV) [5]
Jesus states the Golden Rule in positive form as the summary of the Law and the Prophets.
Same reciprocity principle, phrased positively where the Kural is negative.
Islam
“None of you will have faith till he wishes for his (Muslim) brother what he likes for himself.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 13 [6]
The Prophet makes wishing for others what one wishes for oneself a condition of true faith.
Reciprocity principle at the heart of both; the hadith is framed as faith, the Kural as consequence.
Judaism
“That which is hateful to you do not do to another; that is the entire Torah, and the rest is its interpretation.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (Sefaria) [7]
Asked to teach the whole Torah on one foot, Hillel summarizes it as the negative Golden Rule.
Same negative formulation as the Kural: don’t do to others what you don’t want to suffer.
Buddhism
“All tremble at violence; all fear death. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should not kill nor cause another to kill.”
— Dhammapada 129 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha grounds non-harming in empathy: since everyone fears pain as I do, I should not inflict it.
Same self-comparison logic underlying the Kural’s reciprocity.
Jainism
“All beings hate pains; therefore one should not kill them. This is the quintessence of wisdom: not to kill anything. Know this to be the legitimate conclusion from the principle of the reciprocity with regard to non-killing.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.11.9–10, cf. I.11.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga bases ahiṃsā on reciprocity: every being shrinks from pain as one does oneself, and the same lecture tells the monk to treat all creatures 'so as he himself would be treated'.
Valluvar tells whoever does not want suffering to stop inflicting it on others; the Jain reciprocity principle reasons the same way, from one's own hatred of pain to harming no one.
Sikhism
“Fareed, the farmer plants acacia trees, and wishes for grapes. He is spinning wool, but he wishes to wear silk.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Salok Sheikh Farid, Salok 23 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a salok preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid mocks the person who sows thorny deeds and still expects sweet fruit.
The Kural says whoever does not wish to be tormented by suffering should never do evil to others; Farid's farmer shows the same folly of wanting a good harvest from a bad sowing.
Greek Mythology
On his journey to Athens, Theseus defeats a series of brigands by giving each the fate he inflicted on travellers — Sciron is hurled off his own cliff, Procrustes is fitted to his own bed.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 8–11 (paraphrase) [11]
Plutarch notes that Theseus punished the violent by the very violence they had used on others.
Dramatizes the reciprocity the Kural warns of: what you do to others comes back upon you.
Philosophy
“Do not do to others that which angers you when they do it to you.”
— Isocrates, Nicocles (Or. 3) 61 (trans. G. Norlin) [12]
Isocrates, writing in the voice of King Nicocles, gives his subjects this compact rule of conduct.
A classical Greek statement of the negative Golden Rule, nearly identical in form to the Kural.

Real Life Example

The fall of Robespierre
27-28 July 1794 (9-10 Thermidor, Year II) · Paris, France
Maximilien Robespierre was the dominant figure on the Committee of Public Safety during the Reign of Terror of 1793-1794, when many thousands of people were executed as enemies of the Revolution. As opposition to the Terror grew, the National Convention ordered his arrest on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794). The next day he and 21 of his followers were executed by guillotine at the Place de la Revolution, and his fall opened the Thermidorian Reaction against the Terror.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The leader most identified with sending others to the scaffold was himself sent there by the same revolutionary machinery.
The kural says that whoever does not want suffering should never inflict it on others, and Robespierre met the very fate the Terror had dealt out to so many.
