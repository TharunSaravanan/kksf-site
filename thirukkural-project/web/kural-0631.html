Kural 631

Ministry of State · Amaichu · அமைச்சு

கருவியும் காலமும் செய்கையும் செய்யும் அருவினையும் மாண்டது அமைச்சு.
Karuviyum kaalamum seykaiyum seyyum Aruvinaiyum maandadhu amaichchu.

Meaning

A good minister (an adviser to the king) is excellent at planning the tools needed, the right time, the way to do it, and the hard task itself.

Elementary

Think of a team captain planning a school play. A good captain asks: what props do we need, when should we rehearse, how should we do each scene, and what is the hardest part? This kural says a good adviser always thinks about all four of those things.

University

The couplet opens the Amaichiyal (the section on ministers, chapters 64-73) by defining the minister (amaichu, an abstract noun used for the office-holder) through four objects of deliberation: karuvi (instruments or means, which commentators gloss as troops and wealth), kaalam (the fitting time), seykai (the mode or method of execution) and seyyum aruvinai (the difficult undertaking to be carried out). The verb maandadhu (‘excels, is perfected in’) makes the definition a standard of excellence, not merely a job description. Parimelazhagar and later commentators such as Mu. Varadarasanar read the list as the components of good counsel, which lines up closely with Kautilya’s five limbs of mantra (Arthashastra 1.15). The text is stable across ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net and the older-orthography thirukkural-thamizh.org edition, where it appears as ‘karuviyum kaalamum seykaiyum seyyu / maruvinaiyu maanda thamaichchu’. The difference is only in how the words are split; the reading is the same.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Means to carry out works, command of plenty of men and wealth, allotment of time and place, remedies against dangers, and final success are the five constituents of every council deliberation.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.15 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [4]
Kautilya lists what every royal council must weigh: the means, the resources, the right time and place, what could go wrong, and how to finish.
Valluvar’s four items (means, time, method, the hard task) are almost the same checklist.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand?”
— Luke 14:31 (KJV) [5]
Jesus points out that a sensible king sits down and weighs his resources before starting a war.
The Kural asks the minister to weigh means and difficulty in the same way before acting.
Islam
“[Joseph] said, ‘You will plant for seven years consecutively; and what you harvest leave in its spikes, except a little from which you will eat.’”
— Qur’an 12:47 (Sahih International) [6]
Joseph advises the king of Egypt on what to do (plant), when (seven years), and how (store the grain in the ear) to get through a coming famine.
Joseph’s counsel covers the same things the Kural lists: means, timing, method and a hard task.
Judaism
“To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven:”
— Ecclesiastes 3:1 (JPS 1917) [7]
Kohelet teaches that every action has its proper time.
Kaalam, the right time, is one of the four things the Kural’s minister must master.
Buddhism
“And how is a monk one with a sense of time? There is the case where a monk knows the time: ‘This is the time for recitation; this, the time for questioning; this, the time for making an effort [in meditation]; this, the time for seclusion.’”
— Dhammaññu Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 7.64 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
Among seven marks of a worthy person, the Buddha lists knowing the right time for each kind of activity.
Like the Kural, the sutta treats knowing the right time as a skill a capable person must have.
Jainism
“The king of Ujjayinī would be my fight-guest today; yet in a matter to be settled by wit, talk of sword against sword is idle. Therefore, I shall use wit. Verily, wit is a cow of plenty for producing victory.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 10, ch. 11, part 2 ‘Contest between Pradyota and Abhaya’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [9]
When Pradyota besieges Rajagriha, King Shrenika turns to his son and minister Abhayakumara, who judges that this crisis calls for strategy rather than battle and picks his means to suit it.
The Kural defines the minister by skill in choosing the means, the time and the method for a hard task; Abhaya's reply shows a minister weighing the situation and choosing the right instrument.
Sikhism
In the Zafarnamah, his Persian verse letter to Emperor Aurangzeb written in 1706, Guru Gobind Singh set out when force may rightly be used. In its often-quoted verse 22 he says, in effect, that it is lawful to take up the sword only when all other means have failed. The letter judges public policy by moral principle and stresses the importance of means over ends.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (Punjabi University, Patiala, ed. Harbans Singh), 'Zafarnamah', via The Sikh Encyclopedia (paraphrase) [10]
The Zafarnamah is the nearest thing in Sikh sources to a statement of statecraft: it weighs the right means and the right moment before a grave course of action is taken.
The Kural defines the minister as one who thinks through the means, the right time and the method; the Zafarnamah likewise ties a grave decision to exhausting other means and choosing the right time.
Greek Mythology
Kairos, the Greek personification of the opportune moment, was shown by the sculptor Lysippus as a youth with a lock of hair in front and a bald head behind. The point was that the right moment can be grasped as it comes but not once it has passed.
— Posidippus, epigram on the Kairos of Lysippus (Greek Anthology 16.275), summarized via Theoi Project [11]
Greeks made the fleeting right moment into a divine figure that had to be seized at once.
The Kural lists kaalam, knowing the right time, as a core skill of the counsellor.
Philosophy
“…excellence in deliberation, which is rightness with regard to the expedient-rightness in respect both of the end, the manner, and the time.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.9 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle defines good deliberation (euboulia) as getting right what is useful, including the goal, the way it is done, and the timing.
End, manner and time closely match the Kural’s task, method and time.

Real Life Example

Cavour wins France as Piedmont's ally against Austria
1855-1859 · Turin, Paris and Plombieres, France; northern Italy
As prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia, Count Camillo di Cavour brought his small state into the Crimean War alongside Britain and France and then secured a place at the Congress of Paris in 1856, where he raised the Italian question before the great powers. At a secret meeting at Plombieres in July 1858 he and Napoleon III agreed on a war against Austria in the following year. When Austria sent Piedmont an ultimatum in 1859, the Franco-Piedmontese alliance came into force, and the war that followed won Lombardy for Piedmont.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Cavour judged that Piedmont alone could not drive Austria out of Italy, so he spent years acquiring the means (a great-power ally), waiting for the right moment and choosing a method that let Austria appear the aggressor.
The kural defines the excellent minister as one who masters the means, the time, the method and the difficult task itself, and Cavour's patient preparation of all four for the hard task of challenging Austria is a clear case of it.
