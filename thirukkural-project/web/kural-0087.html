Kural 87

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

இனைத்துணைத் தென்பதொன் றில்லை விருந்தின் துணைத்துணை வேள்விப் பயன்.
Inaiththunaith thenbadhondru illai virundhin, Thunaiththunai velvip payan.

Meaning

The good that comes from the holy offering of caring for guests has no fixed size. It is as great as the worth of the guest you serve.

Elementary

If you plant a seed, you can roughly guess how much will grow. But this kural says you can't do that with kindness to guests. How much good comes back depends on who walked through your door — and you can never know that in advance, so you should treat everyone well.

University

Valluvar here names hospitality வேள்வி — sacrifice, the Tamil equivalent of yajña — making explicit what the chapter has implied: feeding a guest is a liturgical act, one of the five great daily sacrifices of the householder, with the guest standing where the fire normally stands. The verse's rhetoric is built on repetition and negation: இனைத்துணைத்து ('of just this much extent') is flatly denied, and replaced by துணைத்துணை ('measure by measure'), a reduplication meaning proportionality without a stated quantity. Parimelalagar defends the claim by quoting Nālaṭiyār 38 — a small gift in worthy hands covers the sky — and concludes that this verse explains why hospitality yields fruit in both this world and the next. The doctrine that merit scales with the recipient's worth is one of the most cross-culturally robust ideas in religious ethics, appearing in nearly identical form in the Pali Abhidhamma, in Jesus's saying about receiving a prophet, and in the Mishnah's list of deeds without measure.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Teaching (and studying) is the sacrifice (offered) to Brahman, the (offerings of water and food called) Tarpana the sacrifice to the manes, the burnt oblation the sacrifice offered to the gods, the Bali offering that offered to the Bhutas, and the hospitable reception of guests the offering to men.”
— Manusmriti 3.70 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East) [4]
Manu's enumeration of the pañca-mahāyajña, the five great daily sacrifices of the householder, formally classifies receiving guests as a sacrifice in its own right — the nṛ-yajña or offering to human beings.
Supplies precisely the category the Kural invokes when it calls hospitality a வேள்வி and speaks of its 'yield.'
Christianity
“He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet's reward; and he that receiveth a righteous man in the name of a righteous man shall receive a righteous man's reward.”
— Matthew 10:41 (King James Version) [5]
Jesus, sending out the twelve, tells them that whoever hosts them will be rewarded according to the standing of the person hosted, down to a cup of cold water given to a little one.
This is the Kural's rule stated almost identically: the host's reward is measured by the measure of the guest.
Islam
“Whoever provides the food for a fasting person to break his fast with, then for him is the same reward as his (the fasting person's), without anything being diminished from the reward of the fasting person.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 807 (graded Sahih), Book on Fasting [6]
The host's return is not a fixed sum but is set by whatever spiritual state the guest happens to be in — the host inherits the guest's own merit.
Exactly the Kural's principle: the measure of the guest is the measure of the benefit.
Judaism
“These are the things that have no measure: The Peah [corner of the field that must be given to the poor], the Bikurim [first-fruits], the appearance-sacrifice, acts of kindness, and the study of the Torah.”
— Mishnah Peah 1:1 [7]
The very first mishnah of Tractate Peah opens the rabbinic law of charity by declaring that certain obligations, hospitality and loving-kindness among them, have no fixed minimum or maximum quantity.
The Kural's opening clause — 'there is nothing of which one can say it is just this much' — is almost a translation of this mishnah.
Buddhism
In the Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta the Buddha enumerates fourteen kinds of personal offering ranked by recipient and states their relative yield: a gift to an animal may be expected to repay a hundredfold, to an immoral ordinary person a thousandfold, to a virtuous ordinary person a hundred-thousandfold, and so on up through stream-enterers, once-returners, arahants and finally a fully enlightened Buddha, whose worth is incalculable.
— Majjhima Nikāya 142, Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta (The Exposition of Offerings) [8]
The sutta is the canonical Buddhist treatment of exactly this question — how the merit of a gift is determined — and answers it entirely in terms of the recipient's spiritual attainment.
It is the Kural's proposition worked out in systematic detail: the measure of the gift's fruit is the measure of the one who receives it.
Jainism
“The effect of giving of the gift–dāna–has distinctions based on the specific manner in which it is given–vidhiviśeṣa, the specific thing given–dravyaviśeṣa, the specific nature of the giver–dātṛviśeṣa, and the specific nature of the recipient–pātraviśeṣa.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.39, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [9]
Umāsvāti's sutra says the merit of a gift varies, depending partly on the worth of the person who receives it. Pūjyapāda's commentary compares this to good soil making a seed yield a rich harvest.
Valluvar says the fruit of the 'sacrifice' of hospitality cannot be fixed in advance but depends on the worth of the guest, which is exactly the Jain teaching of pātraviśeṣa.
Sikhism
“Recognize the Lord's Light within all, and do not consider social class or status; there are no classes or castes in the world hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 349, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak teaches that every person carries the divine Light, so worth is never to be judged by caste or social rank, a principle the langar puts into practice by serving all alike.
Where the Kural says the merit of hospitality is measured by the guest's worth, Gurbani locates every guest's worth in the same divine Light.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon entertain two unremarkable travellers with the last of their food; only when the wine bowl refills itself do they realise they are hosting Jupiter and Mercury, whereupon their reward — their cottage transfigured into a marble temple, their lives extended, their death granted as a single shared moment — is scaled to the rank of the guests they had unknowingly received.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611–724 (Baucis and Philemon) [11]
Ovid's theoxeny turns on the gap between what the hosts thought they were doing and who their guests actually were.
Illustrates the Kural's claim from the inside: the reward could not have been named in advance, because it was set by the measure of the guest.
Philosophy
Aristotle's account of distributive justice holds that what is just in distribution is a proportion, and that the proportion must be fixed by axia — worth or merit — so that equals receive equally and unequals unequally according to their desert.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V.3, 1131a–1131b [12]
The point of the doctrine is that no absolute quantity can be specified in advance; justice consists in the ratio between what is given and the worth of the recipient.
Provides the formal structure of the Kural's claim: not a fixed amount, but a measure indexed to the worth of the person involved.

Real Life Example

Lorenzo de' Medici takes the young Michelangelo into his household
c. 1490-1492 · Florence, Italy
Lorenzo de' Medici, ruler of Florence, opened a school of sculpture in his garden of San Marco, where the teenage Michelangelo attracted his attention. Lorenzo invited the boy to live in the Medici palace, where he was brought up like a member of the family, studied the Medici collection of ancient sculpture under Bertoldo di Giovanni and mixed with Lorenzo's circle of scholars. Michelangelo's early works such as the Battle of the Centaurs date from this stay, which ended with Lorenzo's death in 1492.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Lorenzo's hospitality to a gifted boy yielded a return far beyond anything he could have measured, through Michelangelo's later art.
The kural says the fruit of hospitality cannot be fixed in advance but matches the worth of the guest, and the Medici household's guest turned out to be one of the greatest artists in history.
