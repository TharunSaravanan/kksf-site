Kural 283

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

களவினால் ஆகிய ஆக்கம் அளவிறந்து ஆவது போலக் கெடும்.
Kalavinaal aakiya aakkam alavirandhu, Aavadhu polak kedum.

Meaning

What is gained by stealing may seem to grow beyond all limits, but it will all be lost.

Elementary

It’s like a soap bubble: a stolen treasure puffs up bigger and bigger and looks amazing, and then — pop! — it’s gone, and it can even splash away the things you already had.

University

The couplet turns on the paradox of āvatu pōla, “as if growing”: fraudulent gain has the appearance of prosperity but not its substance. The phrase aḷaviṟantu is read two ways by commentators — as “beyond measure” describing the apparent growth, or (with Mu. Varadarasan and Kalaignar) as the ruin exceeding its natural limit and consuming even legitimately held wealth. Either way, Valluvar denies the fraudster even a prudential victory. The same instability of ill-gotten wealth is a commonplace across Hesiod, Proverbs, Jeremiah and the Qur’an, suggesting a widely shared moral-economic intuition that dishonest gain lacks durable foundations.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Held in bondage by hundreds of desires, and driven by lust and anger, they strive to accumulate wealth by unjust means, all for the gratification of their senses.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.12 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [5]
Krishna describes the demoniac character as heaping up wealth by unlawful means; the surrounding verses trace this path to ruin and lower births.
Both texts expose unjust accumulation as a false prosperity that ends in downfall.
Christianity
“As the partridge sitteth on eggs, and hatcheth them not; so he that getteth riches, and not by right, shall leave them in the midst of his days, and at his end shall be a fool.”
— Jeremiah 17:11 (KJV) [6]
Jeremiah compares wrongly gained wealth to eggs a bird broods over but never hatches: the promise of increase never comes to fruition.
Identical logic to “as if growing, it perishes”: the apparent increase of unjust riches is illusory.
Islam
“Allāh destroys interest and gives increase for charities.”
— Qur’an 2:276 (Sahih International) [7]
The Qur’an contrasts usury, which seems to multiply wealth, with charity, which seems to reduce it; God reverses both appearances.
Same paradox as the Kural: exploitative gain that looks like growth is actually headed for destruction.
Judaism
“Wealth gotten by vanity shall be diminished; But he that gathereth little by little shall increase.”
— Proverbs 13:11 (JPS 1917) [8]
Proverbs contrasts wealth gained through emptiness or dishonesty, which dwindles, with patiently earned wealth, which grows.
Matches the Kural’s claim that fraud-wealth decays while honest wealth endures.
Buddhism
“Just as rust arising from iron eats away the base from which it arises, even so, their own deeds lead transgressors to states of woe.”
— Dhammapada 240 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Dhammapada compares wrongdoing to rust that grows out of iron and then consumes the very metal that produced it.
Mirrors the reading of aḷaviṟantu in which fraud-wealth destroys even the wealth one already had.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
The Uttarādhyayana warns that wealth gained by evil means does not last. Its owners lose it and are left bound by their own passions.
In the Kural, fraudulent wealth seems to swell but perishes and takes more with it. Here wealth acquired by evil deeds is lost and the acquirer is caught in further snares.
Sikhism
“It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In one of the Babar hymns in Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak reflects on the wealth of rulers ruined by invasion. It was piled up through wrongdoing, and it could not save them or go with them.
The Kural says wealth gained by fraud seems to grow but will perish. Guru Nanak describes wealth gained through sin that cannot last and abandons its owner at death.
Greek Mythology
“Wealth should not be seized: god-given wealth is much better; for if a man take great wealth violently and perforce, or if he steal it through his tongue, as often happens when gain deceives men’s sense and dishonour tramples down honour, the gods soon blot him out and make that man’s house low, and wealth attends him only for a little time.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 320-326 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [12]
Hesiod warns his brother Perses that wealth taken by force or by deceitful speech is quickly withdrawn by the gods.
Almost a paraphrase of the Kural: fraudulent wealth stays only a little while and brings the house down with it.
Philosophy
In the Consolation of Philosophy, Lady Philosophy argues that riches are never truly a person’s own: they belong to Fortune, who hands them out and takes them back as she pleases, so no one should rest security or identity on them.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book II [13]
Boethius stresses how unstable external wealth is and how foolish it is to trust it.
Strengthens the Kural’s point: if even honest wealth is unstable, wealth gained by fraud rests on nothing at all.

Real Life Example

The South Sea Bubble bursts
1720 · London, England, United Kingdom
Shares in the South Sea Company rose from about 128 pounds in January 1720 to around 1,000 pounds by June, then collapsed to about 124 pounds by December, ruining thousands of investors and many landed and merchant families. The company had given gifts of its new stock, in effect bribes, to leading politicians and to the king's mistress. Parliament held an inquiry, and the estates of the company's directors were later sold under official authority.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
The bubble became the classic warning of how wealth built on manipulation and corruption can swell rapidly and then destroy far more than it ever produced.
The kural says wealth gained by fraud may seem to grow beyond measure but perishes and takes more with it, which is exactly the arc of the South Sea Company.
