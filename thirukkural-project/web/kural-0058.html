Kural 58

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

பெற்றாற் பெறின்பெறுவர் பெண்டிர் பெருஞ்சிறப்புப் புத்தேளிர் வாழும் உலகு.
PetraaR peRin peRuvar peNdir perunjiRappup, puththeLir vaazhum ulagu.

Meaning

Wives who faithfully honour their husbands will win great praise in the world of the gods.

Elementary

This kural is about a reward that doesn't come in this life. It says that quietly doing right by your family, day after day, with nobody applauding, is the kind of thing that gets remembered in the highest place there is — even if nobody on earth noticed.

University

The couplet is a masterclass in Tamil sound-patterning — petraaR peRin peRuvar peNdir perunjiRappu stacks five p-initial words in a single line — and Valluvar uses the alliterative momentum to carry the chapter from the domestic to the cosmic register. Parimelazhagar supplies the elided verb (vazhipaduthal, 'reverencing'), giving the standard reading: women who honour their husbands are themselves honoured by the puttheLir (devas). But the couplet is genuinely ambiguous in Tamil, and the divergence in the commentaries is instructive: M. Varadarajan reads it as the wife's reward for duty, while M. Karunanidhi reads petraaR as 'a husband of good character whom she obtains', making the couplet about a woman's good fortune in her marriage rather than her merit in it. Either way, the structural function is clear — after four couplets defining the wife's excellence, Valluvar extends its consequence past death, a move he makes sparingly in the Aram book. The comparative material below is unusually rich here, because nearly every tradition surveyed developed an explicit doctrine of otherworldly recompense for the faithful wife, and several of them (Manu, the Talmud, the Pali canon) specify the heavenly destination with the same concreteness Valluvar does.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“She who, controlling her thoughts, words, and deeds, never slights her lord, resides (after death) with her husband (in heaven), and is called a virtuous (wife).”
— Manusmriti 5.165 (trans. G. Bühler, Sacred Books of the East XXV) [4]
Manu promises a specific post-mortem destination — continued union in heaven — as the reward for the wife's fidelity.
The same promise the Kural makes: honour the husband here, obtain glory in the world of the celestials.
Christianity
“Likewise, ye husbands, dwell with them according to knowledge, giving honour unto the wife, as unto the weaker vessel, and as being heirs together of the grace of life; that your prayers be not hindered.”
— 1 Peter 3:7 (King James Version) [5]
1 Peter assigns husband and wife a shared inheritance in the life to come, making the wife a full heir of grace rather than a dependent beneficiary.
Both texts extend the wife's standing from the household into the world beyond it.
Islam
“If a woman prays her five daily prayers, fasts her month, guards her chastity and obeys her husband, it will be said to her: Enter Paradise by whichever of the gates of Paradise you wish.”
— Hadith of the Prophet Muhammad, Musnad Ahmad 1661 and Ibn Hibban 4163; also Mishkat al-Masabih 3254 (graded hasan) [6]
The hadith names four conditions — three of them acts of worship, the fourth marital — and attaches to them an unrestricted entry to Paradise.
Precisely the Kural's structure: fidelity in marriage counted among the things that win 'great glory' in the world of the celestials.
Judaism
“Greater is the promise for the future made by the Holy One, Blessed be He, to women than to men… Rav said to Rabbi Ḥiyya: By what virtue do women merit to receive this reward? Rabbi Ḥiyya answered: They merit this reward for bringing their children to read the Torah in the synagogue, and for sending their husbands to study mishna in the study hall, and for waiting for their husbands until they return from the study hall.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 17a (Koren Noé / William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Gemara asks directly how women earn the World to Come and answers entirely in terms of what they enable for husband and children.
Same claim, same structure: domestic faithfulness converted into an outsized share of heavenly reward.
Buddhism
“Accomplished in sixteen respects, complete with the eight factors, a virtuous laywoman such as she is reborn in the realm of the Agreeable Gods.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 8.49, Paṭhamaidhalokika Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato, SuttaCentral) [8]
The Buddha tells Visākhā that the laywoman who fulfils the four this-worldly and four next-worldly qualities is reborn among the manāpakāyika devas.
The Pali text names the same destination the Kural does — the world where the celestials dwell.
Jainism
“The observance of the five minor vows of the layman, without committing transgressions thereof, bears fruit in the shape of a birth in the heaven-worlds where [the soul] acquires clairvoyance (avadhi jnana), the eight kinds of miraculous powers and a divine body. The low caste Tampala [in respect of the first], Dhanadeva [in respect of the second], then Varisena, Nili and Jayakumar [in respect of the third, fourth and fifth vows respectively] have risen to high, worshipful status.”
— Samantabhadra, Ratnakaraṇḍa Śrāvakācāra vv. 63–64, tr. Champat Rai Jain, 1917, pp. 30–31 [9]
Samantabhadra promises heavenly birth to lay votaries who keep the minor vows, and names the laywoman Nīlī as the exemplar of the chastity vow who rose to honoured status.
Valluvar promises great glory in the gods' world to women who keep faith with their husbands, and the Jain text gives the faithful laywoman Nīlī, along with every vow-keeper, heavenly birth and honour.
Sikhism
“Myriads of Sitas are there, cool and calm in their majestic glory. Their beauty cannot be described.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 8, Japji Sahib, Pauri 37, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In describing the realm of grace (karam khand), the Japji pictures it filled with countless Sitas, the figure remembered in Indian tradition as the model of the faithful wife, dwelling there in serene glory.
Like the Kural's faithful wives who win great glory in the world of the celestials, the Japji places innumerable Sita-like souls in majestic glory in the higher realm.
Greek Mythology
Alcestis volunteers to die in her husband Admetus's place when no one else will; Euripides' play has the house honour her as a blessed spirit and the chorus promise her a hymn among the living, before Heracles wrestles Death and brings her back.
— Euripides, Alcestis (438 BCE), esp. lines 435–475 and 1000–1005 [11]
Greek tragedy's clearest case of a wife's devotion earning her a status beyond ordinary mortality.
Alcestis receives exactly what the Kural promises — great glory beyond the grave for an act of marital fidelity.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates describes souls who live together with self-mastery rather than surrender to appetite: they win a bright and blessed life, and when their time ends they grow their wings together and rise, having begun the same ascent side by side.
— Plato, Phaedrus 256a–e [12]
Plato gives a philosophical rather than devotional version of the promise — shared moral discipline in a bonded pair converts into a shared ascent.
Both texts end a description of earthly fidelity with a claim about what the faithful receive in the world above.

Real Life Example

Clara Schumann carries Robert Schumann's music to the world
1854-1890s · Germany and concert halls across Europe
After Robert Schumann entered an asylum near Bonn in 1854 and died there in 1856, Clara Schumann, widowed at 37 with seven children, supported the family through decades of concert tours across Europe. She made a mission of sharing her husband's music with audiences, and many of his works had their first performances because of her. In her later years she edited his collected works and in 1886 published an instructive edition of his piano music.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Clara Schumann became one of the most celebrated pianists of the nineteenth century while making her husband's music known and preserved.
The kural promises great glory to women who honour the husband they have received, and Clara Schumann, devoted to performing and preserving Robert's music, won lasting fame alongside his.
