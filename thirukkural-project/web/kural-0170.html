Kural 170

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அழுக்கற்று அகன்றாரும் இல்லை அஃதுஇல்லார் பெருக்கத்தில் தீர்ந்தாரும் இல்.
Azhukkatru akandraarum illai aqdhuillaar Perukkaththil theerndhaarum il.

Meaning

No one has ever become great by being jealous, and no one free of jealousy has ever lost the good things they had.

Elementary

Being jealous has never helped anyone become great, not even once. And people who are happy for others never end up losing out because of it. So jealousy has zero upside, and kindness has zero downside.

University

The chapter closes with a balanced pair of universal negatives: அழுக்கற்று அகன்றாரும் இல்லை (none have grown great through envy; அகன்றார், literally “expanded”) and அஃதில்லார் பெருக்கத்தில் தீர்ந்தாரும் இல் (none without it have been cut off from increase). The form is a complete disjunction: envy never helps and its absence never hurts, so envy is irrational even on purely selfish grounds. Read with Kural 169, the couplet resolves the anomaly: apparent exceptions are temporary and the long-run pattern holds. The prudential argument matches Epictetus: if someone gains an advantage by paying a price you did not pay, “don't imagine that he has gained any advantage over you.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Duryodhana, whose envy of the Pandavas drives the whole conflict, dies at Kurukshetra with his brothers and allies destroyed, while the Pandavas, whom he tried to ruin, recover the kingdom.
— Mahabharata (overall narrative; Shalya and Sauptika Parvas), Ganguli translation [3]
The epic's overall arc shows the envious prince losing everything and his envied cousins restored.
Its conclusion matches the Kural's: envy raised no one, and those it targeted did not ultimately fall.
Christianity
“Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine.”
— Luke 15:31 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the prodigal son, the father answers the elder brother, who resents the feast for his returned brother, that he has lost nothing.
The Kural says the unenvious never lose prosperity; the father tells the envious son he never lacked anything to begin with.
Islam
“People envy Qarun's great wealth, saying “Oh, would that we had like what was given to Qārūn.” After the earth swallows him, the same people say, “Oh, how Allāh extends provision to whom He wills of His servants and restricts it!”
— Qur'an 28:79, 82 (Saheeh International) [5]
Those who envied Qarun's riches realize, after his destruction, that envying him would have brought them nothing but ruin.
The story teaches the Kural's lesson: envied prosperity was not real greatness, and envy leads nowhere.
Judaism
“And his brethren envied him; but his father observed the saying.”
— Genesis 37:11 (KJV), with the narrative of Genesis 37-45 [4]
Joseph's brothers envy him and sell him into slavery, yet Joseph rises to rule Egypt and they end up bowing before him.
The envious brothers gained nothing by their envy, and the envied Joseph was not held down, as the Kural says.
Buddhism
“There is the case where a woman or man is not envious. He/she does not envy, begrudge, or brood about others' gains, honor, respect, reverence, salutations, or veneration. ... he/she is influential wherever reborn.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 135 (Cula-kammavibhanga Sutta), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [6]
The Buddha teaches that freedom from envy leads to good rebirth and to influence and standing in future lives.
This is the second half of the Kural: those free from envy never fall from greatness.
Jainism
“Censuring others, praising oneself, concealing good qualities present in others and proclaiming noble qualities absent in oneself, cause the influx (āsrava) of karmas which lead to low-status (nīcagotra).”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.25, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [7]
Jain karma theory holds that belittling others and hiding their merits, the habits of the envious, bind karma that brings low standing in future lives, while the next sutra (6.26) assigns high standing to the reverse disposition.
Valluvar concludes that no one ever grew great through envy, and the Tattvārtha makes it a law of karma that the envious habit of concealing others' virtues leads downward, never up.
Sikhism
“One whose heart is filled with jealousy of others, never comes to any good.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 308, Raag Gauree (Gauri ki Vaar), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Ram Das states flatly that a heart full of jealousy of others never prospers.
The Kural closes by saying no one has ever grown great through envy; Guru Ram Das says the envious never come to any good.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod describes the good Strife, under which a man seeing a rich neighbour works harder to plough, plant, and set his own house in order, and says “This Strife is wholesome for men,” unlike the destructive kind.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 20-24 (trans. Evelyn-White) [9]
Honest emulation, which works to match a neighbour's success rather than resent it, is what makes people prosper.
As the Kural says, prosperity comes to those without envy, not to the envious.
Philosophy
“If another, then, paying fifty cents, takes the lettuce, and you, not paying it, go without them, don't imagine that he has gained any advantage over you. For as he has the lettuce, so you have the fifty cents which you did not give.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 25 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus argues that someone else's gain is never really your loss, so envy has no reason to exist.
Both thinkers argue that envy never helps, and that one who does not envy loses nothing.

Real Life Example

Alfred Russel Wallace, Darwin's greatest champion, is showered with honours
1858-1908 · London, England, United Kingdom
After the joint reading of 1858, Charles Darwin's name came to dominate the theory of natural selection that Alfred Russel Wallace had reached independently. The Natural History Museum notes that Wallace expressed no resentment and was Darwin's greatest supporter, and in 1889 he titled his own book on the theory Darwinism. Wallace rose to the highest ranks of science, receiving the Royal Society's Darwin Medal in 1890 and, in 1908, both its Copley Medal and the Order of Merit.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Wallace's lack of resentment did not cost him: he was honoured throughout his long life as a leading naturalist.
The kural says no one free from envy has ever fallen from prosperity, and Wallace, who never begrudged Darwin his fame, received the highest honours of his day.
