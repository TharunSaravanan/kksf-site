Kural 174

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

இலமென்று வெஃகுதல் செய்யார் புலம்வென்ற புன்மையில் காட்சி யவர்.
Ilamendru vehkuthal seyyaar pulamvendra Punmaiyil kaatchi yavar.

Meaning

People who see clearly and have control over their senses will not long for what belongs to others, even when they think, “We are poor and have nothing.”

Elementary

It is easy not to want your friend’s lunch when you already have a big one. This kural is about someone who is really hungry and still doesn’t take what isn’t theirs, because they’ve trained themselves to be strong inside.

University

This kural removes the most sympathetic excuse for covetousness: need. இலம் என்று (ilam endru, “saying ‘we have nothing’”) captures the self-justifying thought of the poor, and Valluvar insists that even this does not license desire for another’s goods among those who are புலம் வென்ற (pulam venra, “sense-conquered”) and of புன்மையில் காட்சி (punmaiyil kaatchi, “vision free from meanness”). The pairing of sense-mastery with non-covetousness is characteristically Indian (compare the Gita’s sthitaprajna), but the insistence that integrity must survive poverty is universal, as Agur’s prayer in Proverbs attests. Valluvar is not romanticising poverty; he is arguing that virtue that fails under pressure was never virtue.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is able to withdraw his senses from sense objects, as the tortoise draws his limbs within the shell, is to be understood as truly situated in knowledge.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.58 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna describes the steady-minded sage as someone who can pull his senses back from their objects the way a tortoise pulls in its limbs.
The Kural’s pulam venra (sense-conquered) person is exactly this tortoise-like sage, who is not pulled toward others’ goods even in want.
Christianity
“Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound … both to abound and to suffer need.”
— Philippians 4:11-12 (KJV) [4]
Paul says he has learned to be content whether he has plenty or is going without.
Like the Kural’s sage, Paul’s contentment doesn’t collapse when he is poor.
Islam
“[Charity is] for the poor who have been restricted for the cause of Allah … An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint … They do not ask people persistently [or at all].”
— Qur’an 2:273 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises poor people whose self-restraint is so great that outsiders think they are well-off, since they do not press others for what they have.
These are the Kural’s people: genuinely poor, yet with such restraint that they reach for nothing that belongs to others.
Judaism
“Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: Lest I be full, and deny thee … or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain.”
— Proverbs 30:8-9 (KJV) [4]
Agur asks God to spare him from poverty precisely because he knows poverty tempts people to take what isn’t theirs.
Proverbs names the very temptation the Kural addresses (poverty as an excuse to take), though it asks to be spared the test instead of praising those who pass it.
Buddhism
“Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth.”
— Dhammapada 204 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says contentment itself is the greatest wealth, so someone who has it is never truly poor.
This is why the Kural’s sage does not covet even in want: his real wealth is his contentment.
Jainism
“The more you get, the more you want; your desires increase with your means. Though two mâshas would do to supply your want, still you would scarcely think ten millions sufficient.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 8.17, with Jacobi's note summarizing the commentary's story of Kapila (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
In the commentary's frame story, the poor student Kapila set out to earn two pieces of gold; offered any wish by the king, he kept raising his demand until he saw what was happening, renounced on the spot and spoke this verse.
Valluvar says those who have mastered their senses will not covet others' goods even when they think 'we have nothing', and Kapila, a needy man, saw through his own rising craving and gave it up.
Sikhism
“Fareed, if you see someone else's buttered bread, do not envy him for it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Salok Sheikh Farid, Salok 29 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In this salok preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sufi Sheikh Farid tells the person of plain means to be content with simple fare and not to hanker after the richer bread on another's plate.
The Kural says the self-mastered will not covet others' goods even when they think, we are poor; Farid gives the same counsel to the poor man looking at a richer table.
Greek Mythology
“Fools! They know not how much more the half is than the whole, nor what great advantage there is in mallow and asphodel.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 40-41 (tr. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod mocks greedy men who don’t realise that a modest share, even the poor man’s simple food of mallow and asphodel, is worth more than grabbing everything.
Both texts honour the person who can live on little without reaching for more.
Philosophy
“Not even the impudence of my accusers dares to say that I have ever exacted or sought pay of anyone; they have no witness of that. And I have a witness of the truth of what I say; my poverty is a sufficient witness.”
— Plato, Apology, 31b–c (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
At his trial Socrates points to his own poverty as proof that, despite years of teaching, he never took money from anyone, and he had earlier said he lived in utter poverty because of his devotion to the god.
Socrates is the Kural’s person of clear vision who stays poor but does not reach for what is not his.

Real Life Example

Thomas Paine gives up the profits of Common Sense
1776-1783 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
Thomas Paine's pamphlet Common Sense, published in January 1776, sold in enormous numbers and helped turn American opinion toward independence. Paine declined royalties after the first printing so that the pamphlet could be sold cheaply and reprinted freely, and gave his profits to buy mittens for Continental troops. By the end of the Revolution he was penniless and had to ask Congress for reimbursement of his expenses.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Paine, a man of modest means, chose not to enrich himself from the most successful publication of the age.
The kural says the self-mastered do not covet others' wealth even when they think themselves poor, and Paine stayed poor rather than take wealth from his cause.
