Kural 711

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

அவையறிந்து ஆராய்ந்து சொல்லுக சொல்லின் தொகையறிந்த தூய்மை யவர்.
Avaiyarindhu aaraaindhu solluka sollin Thokaiyarindha thooimai yavar.

Meaning

People with pure minds who know words well should first understand the group they are speaking to, and then speak with care.

Elementary

You talk one way to your baby cousin, another way to your friends, and another way to your teacher. A good speaker first looks at who is listening, then picks the right words for them.

University

The chapter opens by making knowledge of the audience (avai, the assembly or council) a precondition of speech: avaiyarindhu (having known the assembly) comes before aaraaindhu (having deliberated) and solluka (let them speak). Parimelazhagar explains that assemblies are of three grades relative to the speaker, those superior, equal, and inferior in learning (mikkaar, oththaar, thaazhndhaar), and the rest of the chapter works through how to behave before each. Sollin thokai (the aggregate of words) is glossed in the commentarial tradition, and in Solomon Pappaiah’s modern paraphrase, as the three kinds of expressive word: direct (senjol), figurative (ilakkanai-chol), and suggestive (kurippu-chol). Thooimai (purity) is a moral term, not a stylistic one: the speaker must not use this command of language to deceive the hearers. The ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net texts print the first word as “அவையறிநது”, an evident typing slip; the sandhi-joined text “அவையறிந் தாராய்ந்து” confirms the reading அவையறிந்து ஆராய்ந்து.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who desireth to obtain a knowledge of the customs of different countries, and also the languages of different nations, and of the usages of different orders of men, knoweth at once all that is high and low.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 33 (Vidura Niti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
Vidura tells King Dhritarashtra that a wise man studies how different peoples and classes speak and behave, and so understands everyone from the highest to the lowest.
Knowing “the usages of different orders of men” is exactly the knowledge of the assembly that the Kural requires before a person speaks.
Christianity
“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man.”
— Colossians 4:6 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells the Colossians that their speech should be gracious and carefully seasoned, so that they can give each person the answer that fits that person.
“How ye ought to answer every man” is the Kural’s point: fit your words to the particular hearer.
Islam
“You should preach to the people according to their mental caliber so that they may not convey wrong things about Allah and His Messenger.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 127 (saying of Ali) [6]
Ali ibn Abi Talib warned teachers to speak to people at the level they can understand, so that they do not misunderstand what they hear and then spread wrong ideas. Bukhari places it under the heading of choosing some people rather than others for certain teachings.
Ali’s rule, speak according to the listener’s capacity, is the Kural’s rule of knowing the assembly before you speak.
Judaism
“A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver.”
— Proverbs 25:11 (KJV) [5]
The proverb praises a word that fits its setting. The Hebrew phrase behind “fitly” suggests a word spoken in the right circumstances.
The Kural’s speaker who first studies the assembly is trying to produce exactly this fitting word.
Buddhism
In the Lotus Sutra the Buddha explains that he teaches through skillful means (upaya), shaping his teaching with many kinds of explanations, parables, and stories to suit the different dispositions and capacities of the beings he addresses.
— Saddharma-pundarika (Lotus Sutra), Chapter 2 ‘Skilfulness’, trans. H. Kern [7]
Mahayana tradition holds that the Buddha adjusted how he taught to the understanding of each audience, while the truth being taught stayed the same.
Skillful means is the Buddhist form of the Kural’s rule: understand the listeners first, then choose your words.
Jainism
“(If the pupil does not understand his short explanation), he should explain at greater length. When the pupil has heard it, he will correctly understand the Truth.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.24 (cf. I.14.23) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga tells the preaching monk to fit his explanation to the hearer: briefly at first, then at greater length if the listener needs it, and never with long-winded explanations of difficult passages (1.14.23).
Like the Kural, it makes judging the audience a precondition of speaking well, so that words are weighed to suit those who hear them.
Sikhism
“Those words are acceptable, which, when spoken, bring honor.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak makes the worth of speech depend on its effect: only words that bring honour are approved.
The Kural asks the clean-minded speaker to weigh his words before speaking; Guru Nanak likewise says speech must be weighed by whether it brings honour.
Greek Mythology
When the Greek army rushes for the ships in Iliad Book 2, Odysseus goes through the camp. To every chieftain he speaks courteously and reasons with him, but to every common soldier he speaks sharply and orders him back to his place.
— Homer, Iliad 2.188-206 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows Odysseus, the master speaker, instantly changing his manner of speech according to the rank of the person in front of him.
Odysseus reads each audience before he speaks, which is the skill this kural asks for.
Philosophy
“Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 1.2 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle defines rhetoric as the skill of seeing what will persuade in each particular case. In the same chapter he says that persuasion depends partly on putting the hearers into the right frame of mind.
“In any given case” captures the Kural’s demand to study this particular assembly before speaking.

Real Life Example

John F. Kennedy addresses the Greater Houston Ministerial Association
12 September 1960 · Rice Hotel, Houston, Texas, United States
During the 1960 presidential campaign, Senator John F. Kennedy spoke to the Greater Houston Ministerial Association, a gathering of clergy, at the Rice Hotel in Houston, to address concerns about his Catholic faith, noting that no Catholic had ever been elected president. He set out his support for religious tolerance and the absolute separation of church and state, said he was not the Catholic candidate for president but the Democratic Party's candidate who happened also to be a Catholic, and asked to be judged on his record of fourteen years in Congress. He then took questions from the ministers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kennedy chose his audience deliberately and spoke directly to the doubts that this particular assembly of clergy held about him.
The kural asks speakers to understand the assembly in front of them before weighing their words, and Kennedy shaped his address precisely to the concerns of the ministers he faced.
