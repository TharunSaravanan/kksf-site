Kural 18

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

சிறப்பொடு பூசனை செல்லாது வானம் வறக்குமேல் வானோர்க்கும் ஈண்டு.
Sirappodu poosanai sellaadhu vaanam, Varakkumel vaanorkkum eendu.

Meaning

If the sky dries up, the festivals and the daily worship offered to the gods here on earth will stop.

Elementary

You cannot have a birthday party if there is no food, no cake and no candles — the party just does not happen. This kural says the same about temple festivals and daily prayers: if the rain stops, there is nothing left to offer, and the celebrating stops too.

University

பூசனை (poosanai), from Sanskrit पूजा, denotes the regular daily offering, while சிறப்பு (sirappu) here carries its technical sense of the annual festival or special honour, so the couplet covers the whole ritual calendar from daily to yearly. வானோர் (vaanor), "those of the sky," is a neat counterpoint to வானம், the sky itself — the sky-dwellers are made hostage to the sky's own generosity. The adverb ஈண்டு ("here," i.e. in this world) localises the collapse: heaven is not harmed, but heaven's honours on earth cease. The argument is quietly subversive of ritual priority, inverting the ordinary Vedic sequence in which sacrifice causes rain, and it is one of the passages most often cited to show Valluvar's materially grounded, non-sectarian ethics.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“An oblation duly thrown into the fire, reaches the sun; from the sun comes rain, from rain food, therefrom the living creatures (derive their subsistence).”
— Manusmṛti 3.76 (Bühler translation, SBE vol. 25) [4]
Manu describes a closed circuit in which ritual offering generates rain, rain generates food, and food generates the creatures who will make the next offering.
The Kural cuts the same circuit at a different point: break the rain link and the offering at the other end becomes impossible.
Christianity
“The meat offering and the drink offering is cut off from the house of the LORD; the priests, the LORD'S ministers, mourn.”
— Joel 1:9 (KJV) [5]
Joel's account of a devastating agricultural catastrophe notes that the Temple's grain and wine offerings simply stop, because there is no grain and no wine to offer.
It is exactly the Kural's claim: when the land dries up, worship itself cannot proceed.
Islam
“And if only the people of the cities had believed and feared Allah, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth; but they denied [the messengers], so We seized them for what they were earning.”
— Qur'an 7:96, Al-A'raf (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an links a community's religious state to whether the heavens open over it with blessing.
It binds worship and rainfall together as the Kural does, but reverses the direction of dependence — here piety conditions rain rather than rain conditioning piety.
Judaism
“At four times of the year the world is judged: On Passover judgment is passed concerning grain; on Shavuot concerning fruits that grow on a tree … and on the festival of Sukkot they are judged concerning water, i.e., the rainfall of the coming year.”
— Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 1:2 (Koren–Steinsaltz, William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Mishnah organises the entire pilgrimage-festival calendar around agricultural verdicts, with the autumn festival of Sukkot devoted to the coming year's rain — the occasion of the Temple's water-libation ceremony.
Both texts make festivals and rainfall a single interlocked system rather than separate spheres.
Buddhism
The Vinaya establishes the vassa, the three-month rains retreat: monks must settle in one place for the monsoon rather than wander, and the year's central communal observances — including the Kathina robe ceremony that follows it — are timed entirely by the rainy season.
— Vinaya Piṭaka, Mahāvagga III (Vassūpanāyikakkhandhaka, the chapter on entering the rains) [8]
The monastic ritual calendar of Theravada Buddhism is literally built around the arrival and departure of the monsoon rains.
Like the Kural it treats the rhythm of religious observance as something set by the rain rather than independent of it.
Jainism
“In that period, in that age the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvîra commenced the Paggusan when a month and twenty nights of the rainy season had elapsed.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Rules for Yatis §1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
Paryushana (here Paggusan), the most important annual Jain festival of fasting, confession, and scripture-reading, is fixed by the rainy season, when monks stay in one place and lay people gather around them.
As the Kural says, the great festivals and regular worship depend on the rains, and the chief Jain festival is literally timed by the monsoon.
Sikhism
At Hardwar, while pilgrims threw water east as offerings to their ancestors, Guru Nanak threw water west. He said he was watering his fields in Punjab, and asked how their water could reach ancestors so much farther away. He then taught that the true sacrifice of the age was giving food to those who remember God's Name.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter IV, pp. 50-52 [10]
In Macauliffe's account of Guru Nanak's life, the Guru turns a water-offering ritual into a lesson. Water is for living fields, and real worship is feeding people and remembering the Name, not offerings to gods or the dead.
The Kural says worship of the celestials stops when rain fails. Sikhism agrees that water sustains life but rejects such ritual worship and puts feeding the hungry in its place.
Greek Mythology
“So she would have destroyed the whole race of men with cruel famine and have robbed them who dwell on Olympus of their glorious right of gifts and sacrifices, had not Zeus perceived and marked this in his heart.”
— Homeric Hymn 2, To Demeter, lines 310–313 (Evelyn-White translation) [11]
Demeter's withdrawal of the earth's fertility threatens not only humanity but the Olympians themselves, who would lose the offerings that only a fed humanity can make.
This is the Kural's exact argument: when the earth stops yielding, the gods are the ones deprived of their worship.
Philosophy
Cicero's Stoic speaker explains that whenever something brought great benefit to humankind, the ancients took it to come from divine goodness and gave the thing itself the name of a god — so that grain came to be called Ceres and wine Liber.
— Cicero, De Natura Deorum 2.60 [12]
The argument makes the names and cults of certain gods derivative of the material goods they supply.
It runs parallel to the Kural's inversion: the honours paid to the celestials depend on the material bounty that makes those honours possible.

Real Life Example

Megadroughts contribute to the decline of Angkor
14th-15th centuries · Angkor, Cambodia
Tree-ring records show that Angkor, the capital of the Khmer Empire and its great temple complex, suffered decades-long droughts in the mid-14th and early 15th centuries, separated by periods of intense monsoon flooding. Researchers conclude that these climate extremes strained the city's water system and, together with other factors, contributed to Angkor's decline as a capital.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Even one of the greatest temple cities ever built depended on reliable monsoon rain, and its long decline followed the failure of that rain.
The kural warns that if the sky dries up, the festivals and daily worship of a land cannot go on, and Angkor's temple capital weakened when its monsoon failed.
