Kural 590

Detectives · Otraadal · ஒற்றாடல்

சிறப்பறிய ஒற்றின்கண் செய்யற்க செய்யின் புறப்படுத்தான் ஆகும் மறை.
Sirapariya otrinkan seyyarka seyyin Purappaduththaan aagum marai.

Meaning

A king should not reward a spy where others can see. If he does, he gives away his own secret.

Elementary

Imagine a secret helper who quietly tells the teacher when someone is being picked on. If the teacher gave that helper a big prize in front of the whole class, everyone would know who the helper was, and the secret would be ruined. This kural says to thank secret helpers quietly.

University

சிறப்பு அறிய means ‘so that the honour is known [to others]’; ஒற்றின்கண் செய்யற்க, ‘do not do it to the spy’; செய்யின் புறப்படுத்தான் ஆகும் மறை, ‘if you do, you become the one who brought the secret out into the open (புறப்படுத்தல்)’. The irony is that after all the care in 585-589 to keep spies hidden, the king himself becomes the leak by rewarding them publicly. Pappaiah reads the couplet as ‘reward the spy, but reward him secretly’, which fits Sun Tzu’s and Kautilya’s view that spies must be generously paid. Mu. Varadarasanar reads மறை as both the secret information and the spy’s identity. The text agrees across sources; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi form வொற்றின்கட் செய்யற்க செய்யிற் / புறப்படுத்தா னாகு மறை.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That king whose secret plans other people, (though) assembled (for the purpose), do not discover, (will) enjoy the whole earth, though he be poor in treasure.”
— Manusmriti 7.148 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
Manu puts keeping counsel secret above wealth as a source of royal power.
The Kural’s warning about leaking the secret through public honours protects exactly this kind of secrecy.
Christianity
“But when thou doest alms, let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth: That thine alms may be in secret: and thy Father which seeth in secret himself shall reward thee openly.”
— Matthew 6:3-4 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that good deeds and gifts should be given quietly, without public display.
Giving in secret instead of in public view is the Kural’s advice on how to reward a spy.
Islam
“If you disclose your charitable expenditures, they are good; but if you conceal them and give them to the poor, it is better for you, and He will remove from you some of your misdeeds [thereby].”
— Quran 2:271 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran allows open giving but calls concealed giving better.
Its preference for concealed giving matches the Kural’s preference for a spy’s reward given out of sight.
Judaism
“A talebearer revealeth secrets: but he that is of a faithful spirit concealeth the matter.”
— Proverbs 11:13 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs contrasts the gossip who exposes secrets with the trustworthy person who keeps them.
A king who honours his spy publicly acts like the ‘talebearer’ who reveals secrets, and the Kural wants him to be the faithful one who keeps them.
Buddhism
In the standard description of right speech, the disciple does not repeat elsewhere what he has heard in one place in order to divide people, nor repeat here what he has heard there.
— Majjhima Nikaya 41, Sāleyyaka Sutta, on right speech (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist ethics teaches restraint in passing on what one has heard, to avoid causing harm and division.
The Kural also asks the king not to let slip, through a public gesture, what should stay unspoken.
Jainism
“the divulging from jealousy or other motives of the secret intention of another person as divined by watching his gestures or facial expression”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.26, sākāra-mantrabheda, Digambara interpretation (Cāmuṇḍarāya's Cāritrasāra) as given in R. Williams, Jaina Yoga (1963), quoted in the wisdomlib.org Jainism glossary [8]
Among the faults that break the layperson's vow of truthfulness (Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.26) is sākāra-mantrabheda; the Digambara interpretation reproduced here defines it as exposing a secret read from outward signs, and Siddhasena's commentary links a related fault to 'divulging affairs of state'.
The Kural warns that a king who honours a spy in public betrays the secret through an outward sign; Jain ethics names the leaking of hidden matters through visible signs as a breach of the truth vow.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself good, goodness shall not draw near him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan teaches that goodness put on display and claimed as one's own loses its worth.
The Kural warns that a king who honours his spy publicly gives away the secret the spy's work depends on; Guru Arjan offers only a distant echo, that worth announced in public is undone by the announcement.
Greek Mythology
King Midas’s barber, the only one who knew his master had donkey’s ears, could not bear to keep the secret. He whispered it into a hole in the ground and covered it, but reeds grew there and whispered the secret to the wind for everyone to hear.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.180-193 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth shows that a secret, once let out in any form, finds its way to everyone.
The Kural warns the king not to be the one who lets it out, by a gesture as small as a public reward.
Philosophy
“Hence it is that which none in the whole army are more intimate relations to be maintained than with spies. None should be more liberally rewarded. In no other business should greater secrecy be preserved.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 13.14 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu joins generous rewards for spies with the strictest secrecy in handling them.
Reward richly but in secret is exactly the balance this Kural asks for.

Real Life Example

Double agent Garbo receives his MBE in secret
December 1944 · London, United Kingdom
Juan Pujol Garcia, codenamed Garbo, was a Spanish double agent run by MI5 who fed the Germans false reports from an imaginary network of agents, helping convince them that the main Allied invasion would come at the Pas de Calais rather than Normandy. In July 1944 the Germans awarded him the Iron Cross, still believing he worked for them. In December 1944 the British awarded him the MBE, presented by MI5's Director General, but in secret to avoid exposing him, and his identity as Garbo was not publicly uncovered until 1984.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Britain honoured one of its most valuable agents while making sure that no one outside a tiny circle knew it, protecting both the man and the deception.
The kural warns a ruler not to honour a spy where others can see, lest he himself reveal the secret, and Garbo's private MBE followed that counsel to the letter.
