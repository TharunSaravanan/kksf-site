Kural 1296

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

தனியே இருந்து நினைத்தக்கால் என்னைத் தினிய இருந்ததென் நெஞ்சு.
Thaniye irundhu ninaiththakkaal ennaith thiniya irundhadhen nenju.

Meaning

Whenever I sat alone and thought of him, my own heart seemed ready to eat me alive.

Elementary

Have you ever been alone and kept thinking about something sad until you felt worse and worse? Your thoughts can feel like they are gnawing at you. The wife says that when she was alone, her own heart chewed at her like that.

University

Taṉiyē iruntu niṉaittakkāl means ‘when I sat alone and thought’, and tiṉiya iruntatu ‘it was there to eat (me)’: the heart, instead of comforting her, devoured her. Commentators differ on what she was thinking of: Mu. Varadarasanar says ‘his faults’ (avaruṭaiya tavaṟukaḷ), which fits the chapter’s quarrel; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar say ‘the separation from her lover’; Puliyur Kesikan adds that the heart, ‘not being a companion to me’, tormented her. Pope renders ‘My heart consumes me when I ponder lone, / And all my lover’s cruelty bemoan.’ The past tense (iruntatu) recalls the time of separation now that he is back. The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For the mind is restless, turbulent, obstinate and very strong, O Krsna, and to subdue it is, it seems to me, more difficult than controlling the wind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.34 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Arjuna admits that his mind is turbulent and nearly impossible to calm.
The wife finds her own heart just as turbulent, turning against her when she is alone.
Christianity
“My soul is exceeding sorrowful unto death: tarry ye here, and watch.”
— Mark 14:34 (KJV) [4]
In Gethsemane Jesus speaks of a sorrow so heavy it seems to crush him.
The wife too feels an inner sorrow strong enough to consume her when she is alone.
Islam
“He said, ‘I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know.’”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
Yaqub, grieving alone for his lost son Yusuf, keeps his sorrow to himself and to God.
Like Yaqub, the wife carries a grief that works on her in solitude, though she has only her own heart to speak to.
Judaism
“I sleep, but my heart waketh;”
— Song of Songs 5:2 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman in the Song says that even when she lies down, her heart stays awake, listening for her beloved.
Like the wife alone at night, her body rests but her heart will not let her be.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares painful feeling to being struck by an arrow; the untaught person, by brooding and lamenting over it, is struck by a second arrow of mental pain, while the wise person feels the first and not the second.
— Samyutta Nikaya 36.6, Sallatha Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The sutta shows how the mind adds its own suffering on top of what has happened.
The wife’s solitary brooding is that second arrow: her own heart adds to the pain of separation.
Jainism
Asked how he manages the wild horse that runs about, Gautama tells Kesi that the horse is the mind, unruly and dreadful, and that he keeps it in check by the discipline of the Law.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 23 (Kesi and Gautama) (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the dialogue between Kesi and Gautama, the untrained mind is a wild horse that runs where it will.
Left alone, the wife’s heart runs wild like that horse and turns on its rider.
Sikhism
“The beautiful song-bird sings, perched on the mango tree; but how can I endure the pain in the depths of my being?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1108, Raag Tukhaari (Barah Maha), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha (song of the twelve months), Guru Nanak's soul-bride sits alone in spring and finds the beauty around her only deepens her inner pain.
Like the Kural's speaker whose own heart seems to devour her when she sits alone thinking of her husband, Guru Nanak's bride is consumed from within by longing.
Greek Mythology
Ariadne, waking alone on the shore of Naxos after Theseus has sailed away, wanders the empty beach recalling him and is consumed by grief and memory.
— Ovid, Heroides 10 (Ariadne to Theseus) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Ariadne shows a woman tormented by her own thoughts once she is left by herself.
Like Ariadne, the wife alone finds her own memories devouring her.
Philosophy
Boethius begins the Consolation of Philosophy alone in prison, writing laments with the Muses of poetry, who only feed his grief until Lady Philosophy drives them away.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book I, prose 1 (paraphrase) [11]
Boethius shows how a mind left to itself in sorrow can nourish its own pain.
The wife’s heart, like Boethius’s Muses, fed her grief when she sat alone.

Real Life Example

Mary Shelley begins her Journal of Sorrow
2 October 1822 · Italy
In the summer of 1822 the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley and his friend Edward Williams drowned when their boat sank in a storm while sailing to Lerici in Italy. Left a widow at twenty-four, Mary Shelley began a new journal on 2 October 1822, later known as her Journal of Sorrow, addressing the blank page as the confidant to whom she could pour out her innermost thoughts. Her only regular companions at this time were her young son, Percy Florence, and the journal itself.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Alone with her memories, Mary Shelley turned her grief inward onto the page, where it could be expressed but not escaped.
The kural’s wife says that whenever she sat alone and thought of her husband, her own heart seemed ready to devour her, which matches Mary Shelley’s solitary, grieving journal.
