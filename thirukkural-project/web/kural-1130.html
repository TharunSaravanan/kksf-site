Kural 1130

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

உவந்துறைவர் உள்ளத்துள் என்றும் இகந்துறைவர் ஏதிலர் என்னும்இவ் வூர்.
Uvandhuraivar ullaththul endrum igandhuraivar aedhilar ennumiv voor.

Meaning

He always lives happily in my heart, yet this town says he lives far away and does not love me.

Elementary

Sometimes people only see what is on the outside. Someone might think two friends have drifted apart because they live in different places, but the friends know they still care about each other every day. The girl in this poem says her love is always in her heart, even if the town thinks he is gone.

University

The chapter closes by contrasting uvandhu uraivar (உவந்துறைவர், 'he dwells rejoicing') ullaththul endrum ('in my heart always') with the town's verdict that he igandhu uraivar (இகந்துறைவர், 'lives having gone away') and is aedhilar, unloving. The repetition of uraivar and the refrain line shared with 1129 sharpen the irony: physical distance is visible to the town, but inner presence is visible only to her. Mu. Varadarasanar, Pulavar Kesikan, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar agree; Pope: 'Rejoicing in my very soul he ever lies; / "Her love estranged is gone far off!" the village cries.' Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“You are never actually separated from Me, for I am the Soul of all creation.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.47.29 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, vedabase.io) [3]
Through his messenger Uddhava, Krishna tells the grieving gopis of Vraja that although he has gone to Mathura he is never truly apart from them.
Just as the Kural's heroine insists that her absent lover still dwells within her, Krishna assures the gopis that he dwells within them though others see only his absence.
Christianity
“Judge not according to the appearance, but judge righteous judgment.”
— John 7:24 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns his critics not to judge by what things look like on the surface.
The town in the Kural judges the absent lover by appearances; the heroine knows the truth within.
Islam
“O you who have believed, avoid much [negative] assumption. Indeed, some assumption is sin.”
— Qur'an 49:12 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an forbids jumping to suspicious conclusions about others and backbiting.
The Kural's town assumes the worst of an absent lover; the Qur'an names such assumption a sin.
Judaism
When Samuel is impressed by the appearance of Jesse's eldest son, God tells him that people look on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart.
— 1 Samuel 16:7 (paraphrase) [6]
The story teaches that the truth of a person lies in the heart, not in what is outwardly visible.
The town sees only the lover's absence; the heroine, like God in the story, sees what is in the heart.
Buddhism
“O Atula! Indeed, this is an ancient practice, not one only of today: they blame those who remain silent, they blame those who speak much, they blame those who speak in moderation. There is none in the world who is not blamed.”
— Dhammapada 227 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada observes that people will find fault with anyone, whatever they do.
The town finds fault with the heroine's lover no matter what; the Buddha says such blame is as old as the world.
Jainism
“In the resting-places there once, in a night, the single wanderers asked him (who he was, and why he was there); as he did not answer, they treated him badly; but he persevered in his meditations, free from resentment.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.8.2.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
The Acaranga records how people who did not understand Mahavira's silent practice misjudged and mistreated him.
The Kural's town also misreads what it cannot see, judging the lover by outward signs.
Sikhism
“Why do you go looking for Him in the forest? Although he is unattached, he dwells everywhere. He is always with you as your companion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 684, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur tells seekers who think the Lord is far away and must be sought in the wilderness that He already dwells with them, within.
The town in the Kural says the lover lives far away, while the heroine knows he dwells rejoicing in her heart; Gurbani answers those who think the Beloved distant with the same certainty that He is always near.
Greek Mythology
After her husband Protesilaus dies at Troy, Laodamia keeps a likeness of him in her room; a servant who sees her embracing it assumes she has a secret lover and reports her to her father.
— Hyginus, Fabulae 104 (paraphrase) [10]
Her family misreads her devotion to her absent husband as betrayal.
Like the Kural's town, onlookers misjudge a love they cannot see from outside.
Philosophy
In the allegory of the cave, prisoners who see only shadows on a wall take them for the whole of reality.
— Plato, Republic VII, 514a-517a (paraphrase) [11]
Plato's image shows how people who see only surfaces mistake them for the truth.
The Kural's town sees only the lover's absence and takes it for lack of love, while the heroine knows the reality within.

Real Life Example

George IV is buried wearing Maria Fitzherbert's portrait
1830 · England
George IV and Maria Fitzherbert, whom he had secretly and invalidly married in 1785, finally parted in 1811, after which she lived privately on an allowance. Yet when George IV died in 1830 he was wearing a locket with her miniature portrait around his neck, and at his own request he was buried with it at his breast.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The burial revealed that a bond the public had long thought finished had stayed with the king to the end.
The kural's heroine says the town calls her lover distant and unloving though he dwells joyfully in her heart; the world believed George IV had long left Maria behind, but her portrait stayed over his heart until his death.
