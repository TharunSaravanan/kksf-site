Kural 934

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

சிறுமை பலசெய்து சீரழிக்கும் சூதின் வறுமை தருவதொன்று இல்.
Sirumai palaseydhu seerazhikkum soodhin varumai tharuvadhondru il.

Meaning

Nothing makes a person as poor as gambling. It brings many troubles and destroys a person's good name.

Elementary

Some bad habits make you a little poorer. Gambling is the champion of them all: it takes your money, gets you into trouble, and makes people stop trusting you.

University

Sirumai pala seydhu (சிறுமை பல செய்து) means 'causing many miseries' (Mu. Varadarasanar: துன்பம் பலவற்றையும்), though sirumai literally means 'smallness, meanness' and can also suggest degradation. Seer (சீர்) here is renown or good standing, glossed as புகழ் by all four commentators. The superlative construction soodhin ... ondru il ('there is nothing like gambling') makes gambling the paramount cause of varumai (வறுமை, poverty). Textual note: both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print the verb as சீரழக்கும், but ytamizh's own transliteration ('seerazhikkum') and every gloss (கெடுக்கும், அழிக்கும்) support the standard சீரழிக்கும், so சீரழக்கும் looks like a shared typographical slip; this reading should be confirmed against a printed edition. Kesikan's gloss adds that gambling does this 'to those who love it' (தன்னை விரும்பியவருக்கு).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The gambler's wife is left forlorn and wretched: the mother mourns the son who wanders homeless. In constant fear, in debt, and seeking riches, he goes by night unto the home of others.”
— Rig Veda 10.34.10, trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith [3]
The Gambler's Hymn describes the gambler sinking into debt, homelessness and disgrace, dragging his family down with him.
It is a vivid picture of the poverty and lost good name the Kural attributes to gambling.
Christianity
“And when he had spent all, there arose a mighty famine in that land; and he began to be in want.”
— Luke 15:14 (KJV) [4]
Having wasted everything, the prodigal son falls into real poverty.
This is the want that the Kural says gambling, above everything else, brings.
Islam
“He who played Nardashir (a game similar to backgammon) is like one who dyed his hand with the flesh and blood of swine.”
— Sahih Muslim 2260 (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet compares playing this game of chance to defiling one's hand with something forbidden.
The hadith matches the Kural's sense that gambling degrades a person's standing.
Judaism
“And these on the following list are the ones who are disqualified by the Sages from bearing witness due to their unseemly behavior...: One who plays with dice [bekubbiyya] for money...”
— Mishnah Sanhedrin 3:3 as given in Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 24b (Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
The Mishnah strips the habitual dice-player of the right to testify, a public loss of standing.
The gambler's lost credibility is the Kural's seer azhikkum, the ruin of good name.
Buddhism
“(iii) loss of wealth, (iv) his word is not relied upon in a court of law, (v) he is despised by his friends and associates...”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), trans. Narada Thera [7]
Among the six evils of gambling, the Buddha names lost wealth, lost credibility and lost respect.
This is the Kural's double damage of poverty and ruined reputation.
Jainism
“After having enjoyed pleasant seats, beds, carriages, riches, and pleasures, after having squandered his wealth which he had so much trouble in gaining, and after having committed many sins, he will, under the burden of his Karman, and believing only in the visible world, be grieved in the hour of death like the ram at the arrival of a guest.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.8–9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana describes the pleasure-seeker who squanders hard-earned wealth and piles up sins, only to face grief and the weight of his karma at death.
Valluvar says nothing impoverishes like gambling, which brings many miseries and destroys good standing; the Jain verse likewise shows indulgence squandering hard-won wealth and ending in misery.
Sikhism
“The position of the losing gambler is similar to the deer caught in a trap.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 36, Pauri 3 (English translation as published on BaniDB/SikhiToTheMax and searchgurbani.com) [9]
Bhai Gurdas compares the gambler who has lost his money (vit haar jooaaree) to a deer caught in a snare, unable to get free and facing ruin.
The Kural says nothing brings poverty and misery like gambling, and Bhai Gurdas’s gambler stripped of his wealth and trapped like a deer is that misery made visible.
Greek Mythology
After the boy Patroclus killed a playmate in a quarrel over dice, his father Menoetius had to bring him away from Opoeis to live as an exile in the house of Peleus.
— Homer, Iliad 23 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
A game of dice cost the young Patroclus his home and standing.
It shows gambling causing misery and loss of place, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“For it is for gain that both of them ply their craft and endure the disgrace of it...”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says the gambler and the thief accept public disgrace for the sake of profit.
The disgrace Aristotle names is the Kural's ruined reputation.

Real Life Example

Beau Brummell flees England to escape his gambling debts
16 May 1816 · London, England, and Calais and Caen, France
George Bryan Brummell, the leader of London fashion and friend of the Prince Regent, inherited a fortune of about 30,000 pounds in 1799, but gambling and extravagance exhausted it, and on 16 May 1816 he fled to Calais to escape his creditors. He spent the rest of his life in France, hopelessly in debt, was imprisoned for debt in Caen in 1835, and after two strokes was given shelter in the charitable asylum of Bon Sauveur in Caen. He died there in 1840, a pauper.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The most admired man in Regency society ended his life poor, dishonoured and forgotten abroad.
The kural says nothing brings poverty like gambling, which causes many miseries and destroys a person's good name, and gambling took both Brummell's fortune and his place in society.
