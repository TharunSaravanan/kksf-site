Kural 605

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

நெடுநீர் மறவி மடிதுயில் நான்கும் கெடுநீரார் காமக் கலன்.
Neduneer maravi madidhuyil naangum Keduneeraar kaamak kalan.

Meaning

Putting things off, forgetting, laziness and too much sleep are four things people headed for ruin love. They are like a pleasure boat that carries them to disaster.

Elementary

Imagine four friends who always say: ‘Do it later’, ‘Oops, I forgot’, ‘I’m too tired’, and ‘Just five more minutes of sleep.’ They feel comfy, like a boat ride, but the boat is floating toward a waterfall. Good habits are how you steer the boat back.

University

This is the chapter’s best-known couplet and its most careful analysis. It lists four linked dispositions: நெடுநீர், literally ‘long-drawn nature’, i.e. procrastination (Varadarasanar: காலம் நீட்டித்தல்); மறவி, forgetfulness or heedlessness (compare ch. 54, பொச்சாவாமை); மடி, sloth; and துயில், excessive sleep. It calls them the காமக் கலன் of the கெடுநீரார், ‘those whose nature is to perish’. Parimelazhagar and most modern commentators take கலன் as a vessel or ship (மரக்கலம்; Pappaiah: சிறு படகு), giving Pope’s ‘pleasure-boat … to ruin’s shore’. Because கலன் can also mean ‘ornament’, some readers take the line as ‘the ornaments the doomed love to wear’, but the vessel reading is standard. The rhyme நெடுநீர் / கெடுநீரார் binds cause to result. Sivayogi Sivakumar’s gloss of நெடுநீர் as ‘long weeping’ is idiosyncratic and not followed by the classical commentators. The text is identical in both sources apart from sandhi (மடிதுயி னான்குங்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The six faults should be avoided by a person who wisheth to attain prosperity, viz., sleep, drowsiness, fear, anger, indolence and procrastination.”
— Mahābhārata, Udyoga Parva 33 (Vidura Nīti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Vidura advises King Dhritarashtra that anyone who wants prosperity must give up six faults, which include sleep, indolence and procrastination.
Three of Vidura’s six (sleep, indolence, procrastination) are three of Valluvar’s four, and both are warnings addressed to rulers about ruin.
Christianity
“And he cometh, and findeth them sleeping, and saith unto Peter, Simon, sleepest thou? couldest not thou watch one hour? Watch ye and pray, lest ye enter into temptation.”
— Mark 14:37-38 (KJV) [4]
In Gethsemane, the disciples fall asleep at the critical hour, and Jesus warns that sleep and slackness leave them open to failure.
Sleep at the decisive moment, one of the Kural’s four, is shown here as the beginning of the disciples’ collapse that night.
Islam
“Satan puts three knots at the back of the head of any of you if he is asleep. On every knot he reads and exhales the following words, ‘The night is long, so stay asleep.’ … otherwise one gets up lazy and with a mischievous heart.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1142 [5]
The Prophet describes the pull of sleep as Satan whispering ‘the night is long’, which can only be broken by remembering God, washing and prayer.
‘The night is long, so stay asleep’ is the voice of procrastination and sleep together, which is the attraction of the Kural’s pleasure boat.
Judaism
“Rabbi Dosa ben Harkinas said: morning sleep, midday wine, children’s talk and sitting in the assemblies of the ignorant put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:10 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Dosa gives a list of four comfortable habits, starting with sleeping late, that together drive a person out of the world.
Like Valluvar, the sage gives a list of four pleasant indulgences that carry a person to ruin, and oversleeping comes first.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada describes the sluggish, gluttonous person who spends his time sleeping and rolling about like a fattened hog, and says such a fool is reborn again and again.
— Dhammapada 325 [7]
The verse pictures laziness and oversleeping as keeping a person trapped in the cycle of repeated birth.
Sleep and sloth carrying the fool around the round of rebirth is the Buddhist form of the Kural’s boat bearing the doomed to ruin.
Jainism
“'If he does not get (victory over his will) early, he will get it afterwards;' such reasoning presupposes the eternity of human life. But such a man despairs when his life draws to its close, and the dissolution of his body approaches.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.9; cf. 4.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The text attacks procrastination: putting off effort assumes life lasts for ever. The same lecture (4.6) urges, 'Though others sleep, be thou awake!'
Two of the Kural's four vices on the 'pleasure boat' to ruin, procrastination and oversleeping, are exactly what Uttaradhyayana 4 condemns.
Sikhism
“Wake up, O mind! Wake up! Why are you sleeping unaware?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 726, Raag Tilang, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur calls the heedless (gaafal) mind out of its sleep.
The Kural names procrastination, forgetfulness, laziness and oversleeping as the pleasure boat of those bound for ruin; Guru Tegh Bahadur's cry against heedless sleep addresses two of the four, forgetfulness and sleep, directly.
Greek Mythology
In Odyssey Book 10, Odysseus has sailed for nine days within sight of Ithaca with the bag of winds from Aeolus. He falls asleep from exhaustion, his crew open the bag, and the storm drives the ship all the way back.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.28-55 (trans. S. Butler) [10]
A single sleep at the wrong time, together with the crew’s careless curiosity, wrecks a homecoming that was almost complete.
Sleep literally steers the ship to disaster, a close picture of the Kural’s ‘vessel’ of the doomed.
Philosophy
In On the Shortness of Life, Seneca warns that the greatest obstacle to living is expectancy, which hangs on tomorrow and loses today, and that people postpone life while it runs past them.
— Seneca, De Brevitate Vitae 9 (trans. J. W. Basore) [11]
Seneca argues that habitual postponement wastes the only time we really have, the present.
Seneca’s diagnosis of postponement explains the first and most dangerous passenger in the Kural’s boat, நெடுநீர்.

Real Life Example

The Swiss watch industry is overtaken by the quartz watch
1969-early 1980s · Switzerland
Although quartz timekeeping had been developed in Switzerland, Swiss watchmakers were slow to adopt it, while Seiko of Japan launched the Quartz Astron, the first quartz wristwatch on the market, on 25 December 1969. Cheaper and more accurate quartz watches from Asia then took over the market, and by the late 1970s the two largest Swiss watch groups were close to insolvency. Employment in the Swiss watch industry fell from almost 90,000 at the end of the 1960s to about 30,000 by the mid-1980s, before the Swatch, launched in 1983, helped lead a recovery.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A world-leading industry that delayed acting on a technology it had helped create lost most of its jobs within about fifteen years.
The kural names delay and heedlessness among the pleasant vessels that carry people to ruin, and the Swiss watchmakers' slowness to act on quartz carried a proud industry close to collapse.
