Kural 194

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

நயன்சாரா நன்மையின் நீக்கும் பயன்சாராப் பண்பில்சொல் பல்லா ரகத்து.
Nayansaaraa nanmaiyin neekkum payansaaraap, Panbilsol pallaa rakaththu.

Meaning

Useless, rude words spoken in front of many people pull a person away from goodness and take away the good in them.

Elementary

Think of a garden. Every time you throw rubbish into it, the flowers have less room to grow. Rude, useless talk in front of others is like throwing rubbish into your own heart’s garden. Bit by bit, it pushes the good things out.

University

The couplet is built on two paired negatives, பயன்சாரா (“not joined to benefit”) and நயன்சாரா (“not joined to nayan”, i.e., virtue or propriety), with பண்பில் (panpil, “uncultured, graceless”) in between. The claim is about moral formation, not reputation: such speech நன்மையின் நீக்கும் (“removes one from goodness”), gradually alienating the speaker from his own better character. Mu. Varadarasan reads the result as ‘not joining with aram and being removed from good’, while Kalaignar stresses that it also destroys the listeners’ pleasure. The setting பல்லாரகத்து (“among many”) again marks public speech as the place where habits of character are formed. The same point appears in Paul’s warning that ‘vain babblings’ ‘increase unto more ungodliness’ and in the Buddha’s listing of idle chatter among unrighteous conduct.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Words that do not cause distress, are truthful, inoffensive, and beneficial, as well as regular recitation of the Vedic scriptures—these are declared as austerity of speech.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna defines disciplined speech as words that are both pleasing (priya) and beneficial (hita), among other qualities.
The Gita’s priya-hita pairing mirrors the Kural’s nayan-payan pairing. Speech lacking both falls short of dharma, and the Kural adds that it removes one from goodness.
Christianity
“But shun profane and vain babblings: for they will increase unto more ungodliness.”
— 2 Timothy 2:16 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns Timothy that empty, irreverent chatter has a snowballing corrupting effect.
Both texts say vain speech actively carries the speaker away from goodness.
Islam
“And of the people is he who buys the amusement of speech to mislead [others] from the way of Allah without knowledge and who takes it in ridicule.”
— Qur’an 31:6 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an condemns lahw al-ḥadīth, idle or diverting talk, as a means of leading people away from God’s path.
Both texts say frivolous speech pulls people away from the good path, whether the speaker’s own or other people’s.
Judaism
“Merriment and frivolity accustom one to sexual licentiousness.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:13 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Akiva warns that light-headed, jesting talk slowly accustoms a person to serious transgression.
Both texts see frivolous words as a slope that carries a person away from virtue.
Buddhism
“He is a gossip: as one who tells that which is unseasonable … and he speaks out of season speech not worth recording, which is unreasoned, indefinite, and unconnected with good.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 41, Saleyyaka Sutta (trans. Ñanamoli Thera) [7]
The Buddha lists idle chatter (samphappalāpa) as one of four kinds of ‘verbal conduct not in accordance with the Dhamma, unrighteous conduct’.
In both texts, speech ‘unconnected with good’ is a form of unrighteousness in its own right, not merely bad manners.
Jainism
“He should not tell anything sinful or meaningless or hurtful, neither for his own sake nor for anybody else's, nor without such a motive.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana puts meaningless speech in the same forbidden class as sinful and hurtful speech, whatever the speaker's motive.
Valluvar says useless, graceless words cut a person off from virtue, and the Jain rule likewise treats meaningless speech as a moral fault on a level with sinful and hurtful words.
Sikhism
“By mere talk, people do not earn passage to Heaven. Salvation comes only from the practice of Truth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh (Vaar Maajh, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Vaar Maajh, Guru Nanak insists that talking gets no one to the goal; only truth lived in action does.
The Kural says useless, graceless words cut a person off from virtue and goodness; Guru Nanak likewise separates mere talk from the lived truth that alone carries a person forward.
Greek Mythology
“So long as he was silent, they never suspected that he was not a pigeon like themselves. But one day he was unwise enough to start chattering.”
— Aesop, ‘The Jackdaw and the Pigeons’ (trans. V. S. Vernon Jones, 1912) [10]
Aesop’s jackdaw disguises itself among the pigeons, gives itself away by chattering, and is driven out by both pigeons and jackdaws.
Chattering cuts the jackdaw off from both flocks, as the Kural says graceless talk cuts a person off from goodness.
Philosophy
“The buffoon, on the other hand, is the slave of his sense of humour, and spares neither himself nor others if he can raise a laugh, and says things none of which a man of refinement would say.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.8 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle describes the vice of excess in conversation: the buffoon’s tasteless talk departs from the mean of ready wit.
Aristotle’s buffoon speaks what ‘a man of refinement’ would not, which is the Kural’s பண்பில்சொல் (graceless words). Both writers call such speech a vice.

Real Life Example

Lysenko's report to the August 1948 session of the Soviet agricultural academy
31 July - 7 August 1948 · Moscow, Soviet Union (now Russia)
At a session of the Lenin All-Union Academy of Agricultural Sciences approved by Joseph Stalin, its president Trofim Lysenko delivered a report denouncing Mendelian genetics and its supporters, and announced that the Communist Party's Central Committee had approved it. After the session, classical genetics was officially condemned, and many scientists working in it were dismissed or moved to other fields. Teaching and research in standard genetics stayed effectively banned in the Soviet Union into the 1960s, and Lysenko is now remembered as a symbol of science corrupted by politics.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rhetoric with no scientific substance, delivered before a large assembly with state backing, set back a whole field of science and harmed many careers.
The kural says that graceless words with no benefit, spoken before many, cut a person off from goodness, and Lysenko's speeches put his name to the persecution of honest scientists.
