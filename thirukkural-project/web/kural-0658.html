Kural 658

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

கடிந்த கடிந்தொரார் செய்தார்க்கு அவைதாம் முடிந்தாலும் பீழை தரும்.
Kadindha kadindhoraar seydhaarkku avaidhaam Mudindhaalum peezhai tharum.

Meaning

People who do the things the wise have told them not to do will suffer for it, even if those things work out.

Elementary

If you sneak a cookie from the jar after being told not to, you might get the cookie, but then you feel worried and guilty, and you might get caught later. Even when a wrong plan ‘works’, it ends up making you unhappy.

University

The first line plays on the verb kaṭi- (‘forbid, reject’): kaṭinta, ‘things forbidden (by the great)’, and kaṭintu orār, ‘those who do not reject them and turn away’. Solomon Pappaiah specifies that such people act ‘thinking to gather wealth’, keeping the chapter’s focus on means of gain. The concessive muṭintālum, ‘even if they are accomplished’, is the point: success does not clean a forbidden act, which still brings pīḻai (suffering, affliction). Parimelazhagar’s tradition takes the suffering to come in this life as well as the next, through loss of reputation and later retribution. All sources agree on the text; thirukkural-thamizh.org divides it as செய்தார்க் கவைதா / முடிந்தாலும்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Yama teaches Nachiketas that the good (śreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) are two different things; the wise choose the good, while the foolish choose the pleasant and fall short of the true goal.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2 [4]
The Upanishad separates what gives immediate satisfaction from what is truly good, warning that success in the pleasant can be failure in the real aim.
The Kural’s forbidden deed that ‘succeeds’ is a clear case of preyas won and śreyas lost.
Christianity
“Bread of deceit is sweet to a man; but afterwards his mouth shall be filled with gravel.”
— Proverbs 20:17 (KJV) [5]
The proverb says what is gained by fraud tastes sweet at first but turns bitter and painful.
This is the Kural’s point exactly: the wrong deed succeeds, and then brings grief.
Islam
“So when they forgot that by which they had been reminded, We opened to them the doors of every [good] thing until, when they rejoiced in that which they were given, We seized them suddenly, and they were [then] in despair.”
— Quran 6:44 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran describes people who ignored warnings and prospered for a time, until their prosperity ended suddenly in ruin.
As in the Kural, success after ignoring what was forbidden only leads to a harder fall.
Judaism
Proverbs says that treasures gained by a lying tongue are a vapour driven to and fro, and that those who seek them are seeking death.
— Proverbs 21:6 (JPS 1917) [7]
Riches obtained by falsehood are pictured as a breath that vanishes and as a snare for the one who pursues them.
The Kural too says a forbidden deed that gains its object still ends in suffering.
Buddhism
“So long as an evil deed has not ripened, the fool thinks it as sweet as honey. But when the evil deed ripens, the fool comes to grief.”
— Dhammapada 69 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada warns that the first sweetness of a wrong act hides the pain it will later bring.
This is the same pattern as the Kural: the forbidden deed may succeed at first, but suffering follows.
Jainism
“As the burglar caught in the breach of the wall perishes by the work the sinner himself had executed, thus people in this life and the next cannot escape the effect of their own actions.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.3, with Jacobi's note citing Devendra (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The commentator Devendra explains the image: a burglar who had dug his way through a wall is crushed when the wall above the breach comes down, killed by the very work he had completed.
Valluvar says forbidden deeds bring suffering even when they succeed; the burglar's finished breach, which becomes his death, is a vivid picture of exactly that.
Sikhism
“Why do you do such evil deeds, that you shall have to suffer so?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa ki Vaar, Pauri 21) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
This pauri of Asa ki Vaar warns that a person must reap the fruit of what they do, so wrong deeds only lead to their own suffering.
The Kural says deeds the wise forbid bring suffering even when they succeed; the pauri likewise insists that wrong deeds return as suffering to their doer.
Greek Mythology
King Midas asks Dionysus that everything he touches turn to gold; the wish is granted, but his food and drink turn to metal, and he begs to be freed from the gift.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 [11]
Midas gets exactly what his greed asked for, and that success becomes his torment.
Midas is the Kural’s warning in myth form: a wrongly sought end, once achieved, brings suffering.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates argues that the tyrant, who seems to have succeeded completely through injustice, is in truth the most wretched of men, enslaved to his desires and living in constant fear.
— Plato, Republic IX (576b-580c) [12]
Against Thrasymachus and Glaucon, Plato argues that injustice harms the one who does it, however well it seems to go.
The Kural states the same idea briefly: wrongful deeds, even when they succeed, bring suffering to the doer.

Real Life Example

The Recruit shares-for-favours scandal brings down Japan's prime minister
1988-1989 · Tokyo, Japan
In the mid-1980s the Recruit company offered cheap, unlisted shares in its real estate subsidiary Recruit Cosmos to nearly 200 politicians, officials and businessmen, who profited when the shares began trading. When the deals came to light, several cabinet ministers resigned, including Finance Minister Kiichi Miyazawa in late 1988, and Recruit's founder Hiromasa Ezoe was indicted in 1989. Prime Minister Noboru Takeshita, who admitted that he and his aides had received stocks, donations and loans from Recruit, announced his resignation on 25 April 1989 and left office in June.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The share deals did exactly what they were meant to do, enriching those who received them, yet the scheme ended in resignations, indictments and public disgrace.
The kural says that for those who do what the wise forbid, such deeds bring suffering even when they succeed, and the profitable Recruit share deals became the ruin of many who took part in them.
