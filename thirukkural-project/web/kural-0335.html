Kural 335

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

நாச்செற்று விக்குள்மேல் வாராமுன் நல்வினை மேற்சென்று செய்யப் படும்.
Naachchetru vikkul mel vaaraamun nalvinai Mersendru seyyap padum.

Meaning

Before your tongue stops working and the death rattle (the last breathing sound of a dying person) rises in your throat, hurry to do good deeds.

Elementary

If you want to say sorry or help someone, don't wait for ‘someday’, because someday might not come. This kural says do your good deeds now, while you still can talk and act.

University

Valluvar gives the chapter's abstract point a stark physical image: “naa chetru” (the tongue locked or stilled) and “vikkul mel vaaraa mun” (before the hiccup, the death rattle, rises), which are the classical signs of the last moments of life. The imperative “mersendru” (going forward, pressing ahead) carries the sense of taking the initiative and not waiting for circumstances. The image of a silenced tongue also implies that at the end one cannot even speak one's intentions, let alone carry them out. The kural thus moves from recognising impermanence to acting urgently, an ethic of timeliness found in Rabbi Eliezer's ‘repent one day before your death’, the Quran's warning against wishing for a reprieve at death, and Marcus Aurelius' ‘while it is in thy power, be good’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who relinquish the body while remembering Me at the moment of death will come to Me. There is certainly no doubt about this.”
— Bhagavad Gita 8.5 [3]
Krishna teaches that a person's state of mind at death shapes what follows, which implies preparing throughout life for that moment.
Both texts treat the moment of death as decisive and urge preparation well before it arrives.
Christianity
“I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work.”
— John 9:4 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says good works must be done while there is still daylight, because night is coming when no one can work.
The Kural's silenced tongue is Jesus' ‘night’: the point after which good deeds can no longer be done.
Islam
“And spend [in the way of Allah] from what We have provided you before death approaches one of you and he says, "My Lord, if only You would delay me for a brief term so I would give charity and be among the righteous.”
— Quran 63:10 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran urges believers to give now, before death comes and they beg in vain for a little more time to do good.
The same urgency: act before the final moment, when the chance to do good is gone.
Judaism
“Rabbi Eliezer said: ... And repent one day before your death.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:10 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Since no one knows the day of their death, the Talmud explains, this means repenting every day.
Both texts tell people to act before death while hiding its date, so the only safe time is now.
Buddhism
“For him who is assailed by death there is no protection by kinsmen. None there are to save him — no sons, nor father, nor relatives. Realizing this fact, let the wise man, restrained by morality, hasten to clear the path leading to Nibbana.”
— Dhammapada 288-289 [7]
The Buddha tells the wise to hurry along the moral path, since nothing can protect a person once death arrives.
‘Hasten’ is the Kural's ‘mersendru’: the certainty of death is a reason to act on the good without delay.
Jainism
“He who can call Death his friend, or who can escape him, or who knows that he will not die, might perhaps decide: this shall be done tomorrow.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.27 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The young men refuse to postpone the religious life, since no one can count on escaping death until tomorrow.
The Kural urges doing good before the tongue fails and the death rattle comes; this verse likewise forbids putting off dharma to an uncertain tomorrow.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, that which you have to do tomorrow - do it today instead; and that which you have to do now - do it immediately! Later on, you will not be able to do anything, when death hangs over your head.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir, Salok 138 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir urges the seeker to stop postponing what matters, because once death arrives the chance to act is gone.
The Kural says to hurry to do good before the tongue is stilled and the death rattle comes; Kabir gives the same urgent counsel not to wait until death is upon us.
Greek Mythology
“Say not a word in death's favour; I would rather be a paid servant in a poor man's house and be above ground than king of kings among the dead.”
— Homer, Odyssey 11 (Butler translation) [10]
In the underworld the great hero Achilles tells Odysseus that even the humblest living life is better than glory among the dead, where nothing more can be done.
Achilles' regret shows why the Kural insists on acting now: once life ends, no deed remains possible.
Philosophy
“Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.17 (George Long translation) [11]
Marcus Aurelius reminds himself that death is always near, so the time to be good is now.
‘While it is in thy power’ matches the Kural's ‘before the tongue is stilled’: goodness must be done while one still can.

Real Life Example

Thomas Guy builds Guy's Hospital in the last years of his life
1721-1724 · Southwark, London, England
Thomas Guy, a London bookseller and governor of St Thomas' Hospital who had grown very rich by selling his South Sea Company shares in 1720, decided in 1721, at about the age of 76, to build a new hospital for patients who could not be helped elsewhere. Construction began in 1722, and the building was roofed before he died on 27 December 1724. His will left most of his remaining fortune, over 200,000 pounds, to complete and maintain the hospital, which opened soon after his death.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Guy did not postpone his great charity to a distant future but began it in old age and pushed it forward while he still could. Guy's Hospital remains one of London's major teaching hospitals.
Valluvar urges people to hurry and do good before the tongue is stilled at death, and Guy raced to build his hospital in the final years of his life.
