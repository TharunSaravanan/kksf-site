Kural 637

Ministry of State · Amaichu · அமைச்சு

செயற்கை அறிந்தக் கடைத்தும் உலகத்து இயற்கை அறிந்து செயல்.
Seyarkai arindhak kadaiththum ulakaththu Iyarkai arindhu seyal.

Meaning

Even when you know from books how to do something, act only after you understand how the world really works.

Elementary

You can read a book about riding a bicycle, but you also need to notice the real road, the bumps and the traffic. This kural says that even after learning the rules, you must look at the real world and act in a way that fits it.

University

The couplet sets seyarkai, the ‘made’ or acquired knowledge of methods found in treatises, against ulakaththu iyarkai, the natural ways of the world. The minister should act (seyal) only after knowing the second as well as the first. Mu. Varadarasanar’s gloss makes seyarkai explicitly nool arivu (book knowledge). Solomon Pappaiah extends it to methods learned from foreign books and applies iyarkai to the character of one’s own land and people. Commentators in Parimelazhagar’s tradition often recall the grammarians’ rule that ulakam, ‘world’, can mean the worthy or experienced people whose conduct sets the norm. There is a small textual variant. ytamizh.com and thirukkural-thamizh.org read arindhak kadaiththum (with the doubled k), while thirukkural.net prints arindha kadaiththum. The meaning does not change.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Even a man of knowledge acts according to his own nature, for everyone follows the nature he has acquired from the three modes. What can repression accomplish?”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.33 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
Krishna observes that even the learned must reckon with the nature of beings, which cannot simply be overridden.
Like the Kural, it warns that knowledge must take account of how things actually are (iyarkai).
Christianity
“To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:22 (KJV) [5]
Paul describes adapting his approach to the character and situation of each audience.
This is the Kural’s advice to fit one’s method to the ways of the people one is dealing with.
Islam
“And We did not send any messenger except [speaking] in the language of his people to state clearly for them, and Allah sends astray [thereby] whom He wills and guides whom He wills.”
— Qur’an 14:4 (Sahih International) [6]
God sends each messenger in his own people’s language so that the message fits those who hear it.
Even revealed truth is delivered in a form suited to the world it enters, as the Kural advises.
Judaism
“A person should never deviate from the local custom, as Moses ascended to heaven on high and did not eat bread while he was there, whereas the ministering angels descended down to this world, as guests visiting Abraham, and they ate bread.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 86b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [7]
Rabbi Tanhum bar Hanilai teaches that one should follow the customs of the place one is in, as even Moses and the angels did.
This is the Kural’s rule to act in keeping with ulakaththu iyarkai, the ways of the world around you.
Buddhism
In the Aranavibhanga Sutta, the Buddha says that one should not insist on one’s own dialect or override ordinary usage. The same bowl is called by different names in different regions, and a wise person uses whatever word is current there instead of insisting on his own.
— Aranavibhanga Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 139 [8]
The Buddha warns against rigidly imposing one standard where local usage differs.
This is the Kural’s point that correct theory must bend to the real ways of the world.
Jainism
“You, although knowing nīti and well versed in the seventy stories, were caught by her like a parrot by a cat.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 10, ch. 11, part 2 ‘Contest between Pradyota and Abhaya’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [9]
Pradyota taunts Abhayakumara, who was captured when a courtesan posing as a pious Jain laywoman drugged him: all his learning in statecraft (niti) did not protect him from a trick of the world.
The Kural warns that knowing methods from books is not enough without knowing how the world actually behaves; Abhaya's capture is a cautionary case of learned policy failing against real-world deceit.
Sikhism
“You may read and read loads of books; you may read and study vast multitudes of books. … O Nanak, only one thing is of any account: everything else is useless babbling and idle talk in ego.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 467, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a famous salok of Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak says that mountains of book learning count for little unless they lead to what truly matters.
The Kural tells the minister to know how the world actually works as well as what the books say; Guru Nanak likewise refuses to treat book learning as enough by itself.
Greek Mythology
Shipwrecked on the Phaeacian shore, Odysseus meets the princess Nausicaa. He decides not to clasp her knees as a formal suppliant might, in case it offends her, and speaks gently from a distance instead.
— Homer, Odyssey 6 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Odysseus knows the formal rules of supplication but adjusts them to the real situation in front of him.
This is the Kural’s advice: know the rules, then act according to how the world actually is.
Philosophy
“Nor is practical wisdom concerned with universals only-it must also recognize the particulars; for it is practical, and practice is concerned with particulars. This is why some who do not know, and especially those who have experience, are more practical than others who know…”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.7 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle argues that general knowledge is not enough for action; one must also grasp particular circumstances, which experience provides.
This is the Kural’s contrast between book methods (seyarkai) and knowing the world (iyarkai).

Real Life Example

The Tanganyika groundnut scheme fails
1947-1951 · Kongwa and other sites, Tanganyika (now Tanzania)
In 1947 Britain's Labour government launched a plan, run by the Overseas Food Corporation, to grow groundnuts on cleared land in Tanganyika to ease a post-war shortage of fats. It was commissioned after a survey completed in only seven weeks, with no pilot scheme, and it failed to allow for the area's soil and rainfall; at Kongwa there was enough rain for a groundnut crop only once between 1947 and 1951. The scheme was abandoned on 9 January 1951, with about 36.5 million pounds written off.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The planners relied on a plan drawn up on paper and on heavy machinery, while ignoring local conditions and the knowledge of local farmers, and the project collapsed.
The kural warns that even someone who knows from books how a thing should be done must act only after learning how the world actually works, and the groundnut scheme failed for want of exactly that knowledge.
