Kural 365

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

அற்றவர் என்பார் அவாஅற்றார் மற்றையார் அற்றாக அற்றது இலர்.
Atravar enbaar avaaatraar matraiyaar Atraaga atradhu ilar.

Meaning

Only people who have cut off desire truly deserve to be called people who have given up the world. Others, whatever they have given up, are not fully free like them.

Elementary

Throwing your candy away doesn’t really count if you keep thinking about candy all day. Valluvar says the real “giver-upper” is the person who stopped wanting, not just the person who stopped having.

University

The couplet plays on atravar (“those who are cut off”, the renunciants) and avaa atraar (“those cut off from desire”), and ends by saying that others are not free in that full sense (atraaga atradhu ilar). Valluvar draws a sharp line between outward renunciation (robes, poverty, leaving home, the themes of the earlier chapter Thuravu) and inward renunciation. This extends his critique of the false ascetic in Chapter 28 (Koodaavozhukkam, “Imposture”). For Valluvar, freedom is measured by what one no longer wants, not by what one no longer owns.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A person who has given up all desires for sense gratification, who lives free from desires, who has given up all sense of proprietorship and is devoid of false ego – he alone can attain real peace.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.71 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna says that only the one who has given up desires inwardly, not just outwardly, reaches real peace.
The Gita’s “he alone” matches the Kural’s claim that only the desire-free are truly renunciants.
Christianity
“And they that are Christ’s have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts.”
— Galatians 5:24 (KJV) [4]
Paul defines true belonging to Christ as having put to death the desires themselves, not just outward behavior.
Both locate true renunciation in the death of inner desire.
Islam
“Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a traveler.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6416 [5]
The Prophet tells Ibn ‘Umar to hold the world loosely, like a traveler passing through who doesn’t cling to anything.
Both describe detachment as an inner stance toward the world. The hadith does not spell out the Kural’s contrast between inner and outer renunciation.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit) [6]
Ben Zoma teaches that real strength is mastering one’s own desires (the yetzer), not conquering others.
Both redefine an honored title (the ‘mighty’, the ‘renunciant’) so that it belongs to whoever has mastered inner desire.
Buddhism
“There is one who, turning away from desire (for household life) takes to the life of the forest (i.e., of a monk). But after being freed from the household, he runs back to it. Behold that man! Though freed, he runs back to that very bondage!”
— Dhammapada 344 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes a man who outwardly renounces home life but, with his craving still intact, runs straight back to it.
This is exactly the Kural’s point: outward renunciation without cutting off craving is not true freedom.
Jainism
“If an ordained monk, through carelessness, does not strictly keep the great vows, if he does not restrain himself, but desires pleasure, then his fetters will not be completely cut off.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The ascetic in the Twentieth Lecture warns that taking orders is not enough. A monk who keeps the outward form but still desires pleasure remains bound, and the next verses call him 'empty like a clenched fist'.
Valluvar says only those who have cut off desire are truly renunciants, and that others are not fully 'cut free'. The Jain verse says the same: a monk who still desires pleasure does not have his fetters completely cut off.
Sikhism
“When you abandon hope and desire, then you become a true Sannyaasi.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 356, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak redefines the renunciant: the true sannyasi is not one who wears the robe but one who has given up hope and desire.
The Kural says only those who have cut off desire deserve to be called true renunciants, whatever else others have given up; Guru Nanak says exactly this.
Greek Mythology
After washing away his golden touch, King Midas comes to hate riches and goes to live in the woods and fields as a worshipper of Pan. But his foolish mind stays unchanged, and he soon earns a pair of ass’s ears for judging Pan’s music above Apollo’s.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-193 (paraphrase) [10]
Midas outwardly gives up wealth, but he has not been inwardly transformed.
He loosely illustrates the Kural’s warning that outward renunciation is not the same as true inner freedom.
Philosophy
“Whoever, then, would be free, let him wish nothing, let him decline nothing, which depends on others else he must necessarily be a slave.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 14 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus defines freedom as wanting nothing that depends on things outside oneself. Anyone who still wants such things remains a slave.
This is a direct parallel. Only the one who has cut off desire is truly free, and everyone else is still bound.

Real Life Example

Queen Christina abdicates but seeks other crowns
1654-1668 · Uppsala, Sweden, and Rome, Italy
Queen Christina of Sweden abdicated on 6 June 1654, the day her cousin Charles X Gustav was crowned, and left Sweden at once; she had secretly become a Roman Catholic and settled in Rome. Within a few years she was negotiating with Cardinal Mazarin of France in a scheme to make herself queen of Naples, which collapsed in 1657. About ten years later, with the support of Pope Clement IX, she tried without success to be chosen for the throne of Poland.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Christina gave up a throne in outward form, yet her later schemes show that the wish to reign had not left her.
The kural says only those who have cut off desire are truly renunciants, and that others, whatever they have given up, are not free in the same way, which is precisely the contrast between Christina's abdication and her later pursuit of crowns.
