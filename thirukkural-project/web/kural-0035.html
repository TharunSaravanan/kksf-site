Kural 35

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

அழுக்காறு அவாவெகுளி இன்னாச்சொல் நான்கும் இழுக்கா இயன்றது அறம்.
Azhukkaaru avaavekuli innaachchol naankum, Izhukkaa iyandradhu aram.

Meaning

True goodness means living while keeping away from four things: jealousy, greed, anger and harsh words.

Elementary

Four things spoil a good day: wanting what your friend has, wanting more and more for yourself, losing your temper, and saying something mean. This kural says being good mostly means steering around those four.

University

Valluvar here gives virtue a negative definition — a via negativa built from four named faults, each of which will later receive its own chapter or sub-treatment in the Kural. அழுக்காறு (envy at another's prosperity), அவா (craving running out through the senses), வெகுளி (the anger that erupts when craving is blocked) and இன்னாச்சொல் (the harsh word that anger produces) are arranged by Parimelazhagar as a causal chain rather than a list, so the four are really one process caught at four stages. The verbal form இழுக்கா ('not slipping') is a negative participle drawn from the vocabulary of footing and balance, so the image is of walking a path without stumbling. Note the textual crux: some manuscripts and a persistent popular tradition read ஈயாமை (miserliness) in place of நான்கும், but the reading with நான்கும் is the one carried by the major commentarial tradition and by the standard printed editions used here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 (Bhaktivedanta, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna names kāma, krodha and lobha as the three entrances to ruin, and makes their abandonment the precondition of any genuine spiritual progress.
Three of Valluvar's four faults appear here as a named set to be renounced, defining right conduct by exclusion just as the Kural does.
Christianity
“Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice:”
— Ephesians 4:31 (KJV) [4]
Paul lists the interior dispositions and their verbal outlets together, treating the malice behind the words and the words themselves as one package to be discarded.
It matches the Kural's structure of defining virtue as steady conduct cleared of a short, named list of faults.
Islam
In a hadith recorded by Muslim, the Prophet forbids a cluster of interpersonal faults in a single breath — envying one another, driving up prices against one another, hating one another, turning away from one another, undercutting one another's sales — commands believers to live as brothers, and then strikes his own chest three times saying that piety (taqwa) is here.
— Sahih Muslim 2564a, narrated by Abu Hurayra [5]
The list works exactly like a definition by exclusion, and the gesture toward the chest relocates piety from the listed outward behaviours to the heart that produces them.
It gives virtue the same shape the Kural does: a named set of interpersonal faults avoided, with the real seat of the matter in the heart.
Judaism
“Rabbi Elazar Ha-kappar said: envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 (trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
A compact rabbinic maxim naming three appetites that destroy a person's standing in this world and the next.
It is the same rhetorical move as the Kural — a short enumerated list of faults whose avoidance constitutes a whole ethic.
Buddhism
In the Saleyyaka Sutta the Buddha itemises the ten courses of unwholesome action for the brahmins of Sala, and among them are harsh speech — words that are rough, bitter and abusive to others — covetousness for what belongs to another, and a mind of ill will; conduct in accordance with the Dhamma is then defined simply as the abstention from each of these.
— Majjhima Nikaya 41, Saleyyaka Sutta (the ten courses of unwholesome and wholesome action) [7]
The sutta's whole method is to define right conduct as the negation of an enumerated list of bodily, verbal and mental faults.
Three of the Kural's four faults appear in that list, and the definitional method — virtue as what remains when the named faults are excluded — is identical.
Jainism
“Anger destroys love, pride destroys modesty, deceit destroys friendship; greed is destructive of everything.” … “One ought to put an end to anger through calmness, pride by modesty, deceit by straight-forwardness and greed by contentment.”
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.38-39 (numbering varies by edition), as Saman Suttam vv. 135-136, Samyamasutra (tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; text via jainworld.com) [8]
Jain ethics names four passions (kashaya), anger, pride, deceit and greed, as the fundamental stains that bind karma to the soul. The Dasavaikalika says what each one destroys and which virtue overcomes it.
The Kural defines virtue as conduct that steadily avoids four named faults. Jainism likewise defines purity through a fixed set of four passions, two of which (anger and greed) are the same as the Kural's.
Sikhism
“O anger, you are the root of conflict; compassion never rises up in you.” … “O greed, you cling to even the great, assaulting them with countless waves.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sehskriti, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Salok Sehskriti Guru Arjan addresses lust, anger, greed, attachment and ego one by one as personified enemies, the vices Sikh teaching calls the five thieves.
Two of Valluvar's four faults, anger and greed, appear here as named forces that destroy compassion and right conduct.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid's Metamorphoses Athena, angered by the jealous Aglauros, sends her to the house of Invidia (Envy) — a black, rotting dwelling where the goddess sits gnawing vipers, never smiling, wasting away at the sight of others' good fortune. Invidia breathes her poison into Aglauros' breast, and the girl is consumed from within until she hardens into black stone on the threshold she had jealously guarded.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.760-832 (Invidia and Aglauros) [10]
Envy is portrayed as a self-destroying corrosion that literally petrifies the person who hosts it, rather than as an injury done to its object.
It gives vivid mythic form to the first item in the Kural's list of four, and to the idea that these faults ruin the person who harbours them.
Philosophy
“But not every action nor every passion admits of a mean; for some have names that already imply badness, e.g. spite, shamelessness, envy, and in the case of actions adultery, theft, murder… It is not possible, then, ever to be right with regard to them; one must always be wrong.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6 (1107a), trans. W. D. Ross [11]
Aristotle carves out an exception to his own doctrine of the mean: certain named passions and acts are bad in themselves, admitting no correct amount or occasion.
Like the Kural's four, these are faults to be excluded outright rather than moderated, and virtue is what proceeds without slipping into them.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela wears the Springbok jersey at the Rugby World Cup final
24 June 1995 · Ellis Park, Johannesburg, South Africa
Nelson Mandela, imprisoned from 1962 until his release in 1990 and elected South Africa's president in 1994, attended the 1995 Rugby World Cup final between South Africa and New Zealand. Before the match he walked into the Springbok changing room wearing a Springbok jersey with captain Francois Pienaar's number six, and after South Africa's victory he presented the trophy to Pienaar on the field while wearing it. The Nelson Mandela Foundation describes the moment as an enduring symbol of the democratic government's approach to reconciliation and nation-building.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Mandela embraced a sporting symbol cherished by many white South Africans, choosing reconciliation over resentment after decades of imprisonment under apartheid.
The kural defines virtue as conduct that steadily keeps clear of envy, greed, anger and harsh words, and Mandela's public gesture of goodwill toward former opponents shows anger and bitterness set aside.
