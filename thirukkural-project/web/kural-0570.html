Kural 570

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

கல்லார்ப் பிணிக்கும் கடுங்கோல் அதுவல்லது இல்லை நிலக்குப் பொறை.
Kallaarp pinikkum kadungoal adhuvalladhu Illai nilakkup porai.

Meaning

A cruel ruler who keeps ignorant, unlearned people as his helpers is the heaviest weight the earth has to carry. There is none heavier.

Elementary

Picture a bully who gathers other kids who don’t know right from wrong to help him push everyone around. That gang makes the whole playground miserable. The kural says a cruel ruler with foolish helpers is the heaviest weight the world has to carry.

University

The closing couplet names the social mechanism of tyranny: கடுங்கோல் (the harsh sceptre) பிணிக்கும் (binds, attaches) கல்லார் (the unlearned) to itself, meaning it draws in and relies on men without learning or moral training to carry out its will. The traditional commentary, followed by Mu. Varadarasanar, Kalaignar, and Pope (‘Tyrants with fools their counsels share’), reads it this way; Pappaiah’s gloss instead stresses a regime that punishes so as to terrify, propped up by those who have not studied the books of justice. பொறை means a load or burden, and the idea of the earth (நிலம்) groaning under wicked rulers is widespread in Indian thought. The couplet ties back to chapter 41 (கல்லாமை, lack of learning) and closes the chapter by showing that terror is never exercised alone. thirukkural-thamizh.org prints the sandhi-joined ‘கடுங்கோ லதுவல்ல தில்லை’, identical in wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Bhagavata Purana tells how Mother Earth, overburdened by vast armies of demonic rulers posing as kings, took the form of a cow and went weeping to Brahma, who promised that the Lord would descend as Krishna to lift the burden.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.1.17–23 [3]
The myth pictures tyrannical kings and their forces as a literal weight the earth can no longer bear.
The Kural’s ‘நிலக்குப் பொறை’ (burden on the earth) is the same image, applied to a cruel regime and its unlearned agents.
Christianity
“Let them alone: they be blind leaders of the blind. And if the blind lead the blind, both shall fall into the ditch.”
— Matthew 15:14 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that leaders without true understanding, followed by others equally unseeing, bring ruin on all together.
A harsh ruler bound to the unlearned is a case of the blind leading the blind, with everyone falling.
Islam
“So he bluffed his people, and they obeyed him. Indeed, they were [themselves] a people defiantly disobedient [of Allāh].”
— Quran 43:54 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran describes Pharaoh deceiving his people into following him, and holds them responsible too.
A tyrant who binds the undiscerning to his service is the pairing the Kural calls the earth’s heaviest burden.
Judaism
“For three things the earth is disquieted, and for four which it cannot bear: For a servant when he reigneth; and a fool when he is filled with meat.”
— Proverbs 30:21–22 (KJV) [4]
Agur lists things the earth itself ‘cannot bear’, including the unfit man who seizes rule and the fool who prospers.
The earth that cannot bear a fool in power is the Kural’s ‘no greater burden on the earth’.
Buddhism
The Mangala Sutta names ‘not associating with fools, associating with the wise’ as the very first of the highest blessings.
— Mangala Sutta (Sutta Nipata 2.4) [6]
The Buddha places the choice of companions at the foundation of a good life.
The Kural’s tyrant does the opposite, binding the unlearned to himself, and so becomes a curse rather than a blessing.
Jainism
“When reprimanded a wise man should not be angry, but he should be of a forbearing mood; he should not associate, laugh, and play with mean men.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Uttaradhyayana tells the disciplined person to avoid keeping company with base people.
The Kural's tyrant binds the unlearned to himself as his helpers; the Jain verse warns against exactly that kind of association.
Sikhism
“Deer, falcons and government officials are known to be trained and clever. When the trap is set, they trap their own kind; hereafter they will find no place of rest.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1288, Raag Malaar (Malaar ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak compares the officials of an oppressive state to decoy deer and hunting falcons, trained creatures used to snare their own kind.
The Kural says the heaviest burden on the earth is a harsh sceptre that binds unlearned men to itself as its helpers; Guru Nanak shows how such rule works through trained servants who prey on their own people.
Greek Mythology
In a fragment of the lost epic Cypria, Zeus pities the Earth, weighed down by the countless and increasingly lawless race of men, and plans the Trojan War to lighten her burden.
— Cypria, fragment 1 (preserved in a scholium on Iliad 1.5) [9]
Greek myth, like Indian myth, imagines the Earth as literally burdened by human wickedness.
The Kural’s ‘பொறை’ on the earth has a direct Greek counterpart in Earth’s plea to Zeus.
Philosophy
Plato describes how the tyrant must rid himself of the brave, the high-minded and the wise, and is left to live among the worthless, hiring bodyguards from the worst elements, as a doctor purges the best and keeps the worst.
— Plato, Republic 8.567b–d [10]
For Plato, tyranny by its nature drives out good and learned men and depends on the base and ignorant.
This is the Kural’s ‘கல்லார்ப் பிணிக்கும் கடுங்கோல்’ analysed from the inside.

Real Life Example

Ivan the Terrible's oprichnina
1565-1572 · Muscovy (Russia)
In 1565 Tsar Ivan IV set aside a large part of his realm as the oprichnina, a territory under his direct personal control, and formed a personal force, the oprichniki, which grew to several thousand men. Through them he carried out years of terror against boyars, towns and ordinary people, including the devastation of Novgorod in 1570. When the oprichniki failed to defend Moscow against a Crimean Tatar attack in 1571, Ivan abolished the oprichnina in 1572.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
A ruler who governed by terror bound to himself a body of men whose only qualification was loyalty to his will, and they inflicted lasting damage on the country while failing it in a real emergency.
The kural calls a harsh sceptre that gathers unworthy helpers the heaviest burden the earth can bear, and the oprichnina shows how such a ruler and his chosen enforcers together weighed upon an entire land.
