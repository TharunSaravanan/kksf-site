Kural 530

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

உழைப்பிரிந்து காரணத்தின் வந்தானை வேந்தன் இழைத்திருந்து எண்ணிக் கொளல்.
Uzhaippirindhu kaaranaththin vandhaanai vendhan Izhaiththirundhu ennik kolal.

Meaning

When a relative who left the king for no reason comes back wanting something, the king should give what they need, but wait and judge them carefully before taking them back.

Elementary

Imagine a teammate who quit for no reason and now wants to rejoin because he needs something. The captain can help him, but should watch how he behaves for a while before trusting him fully again. Welcome back, but with open eyes.

University

Parimelazhagar takes the bare உழைப் பிரிந்து (‘having left one’s side’) to imply that there was no reason for leaving, and காரணத்தின் வந்தானை as ‘one who came back because of some purpose’. He explains இழைத்திருந்து as ‘having done or supplied that purpose’: if the king does not meet the motive that brought the man back, he may leave again and join the enemy. He explains எண்ணிக் கொளல் as ‘accept him after examination’, because the man left without love and came back out of interest. Taken together with 529, Parimelazhagar sees two cases: the kinsman whom the king’s own fault drove away should be allowed to return freely, while the one who left without cause should be taken back only after scrutiny. Varadarasan similarly says the king should grant the help sought and then examine before re-admitting him, while Pappaiah reads இழைத்திருந்து as ‘waiting patiently’. Karunanidhi instead describes the man as one who left on a pretext and returned for a genuine reason.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Ravana’s brother Vibhishana deserts Lanka and comes to Rama asking for refuge, Rama first holds a council: Sugriva and others suspect a spy, and Hanuman argues from Vibhishana’s conduct and motive that he is sincere. Only after weighing their views does Rama accept him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda, sargas 17-19 [3]
Rama hears Vibhishana’s reasons, has him assessed and then takes him in, even promising him Lanka’s throne.
Rama both meets the man’s purpose and examines him before accepting, which is the Kural’s இழைத்திருந்து எண்ணிக் கொளல்.
Christianity
“Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry.”
— 2 Timothy 4:11; cf. Acts 15:37-39 (KJV) [4]
Paul had refused to take John Mark on his second journey because Mark had earlier left the mission (Acts 15:37-39); years later, having seen him proven, Paul asks for him again.
Someone who once left is taken back only after a period of testing, as the Kural advises.
Islam
“He said, ‘No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful.’”
— Qur’an 12:92 (Sahih International); cf. 12:58-90 [5]
Yusuf forgives the brothers who betrayed him, but only after testing them over several visits, including the planted cup, until he sees they have changed.
Taking back estranged kin after examining them is the Kural’s method.
Judaism
When the brothers who sold Joseph arrive in Egypt needing grain, Joseph does not reveal himself at once. He tests them, holding Simeon and demanding that Benjamin be brought, and reveals himself and welcomes them only after Judah offers himself in Benjamin’s place.
— Genesis 42-45 [6]
The kin come back out of need, and Joseph examines them before restoring the bond.
They come ‘for a reason’, their need is met with grain, and they are examined before being accepted, which is exactly what the Kural prescribes.
Buddhism
The monastic code requires someone who formerly followed another sect and now asks to join the Sangha to live under a four-month probation (titthiya-parivāsa) so that his conduct and sincerity can be observed before he receives full ordination.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Mahavagga I.38 [7]
The Sangha admits newcomers from rival groups only after a period of examination.
Accept the one who comes, but only after weighing him, as the Kural advises.
Jainism
Jain monastic discipline includes upasthapana, or re-initiation: a monk who has seriously lapsed, or who comes back after leaving, is not simply restored but must undergo expiation and be ordained again.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.22 (kinds of expiation, including upasthapana) (various translations) [8]
The Tattvartha Sutra lists re-initiation among the kinds of expiation (prayascitta) by which the community deals with a monk who has broken with it.
The Kural advises the king to meet the returning kinsman's need but weigh him before taking him back; Jain discipline likewise readmits the returner only after a deliberate process.
Sikhism
“But one who feels good only when good is done for him, and feels bad when things go badly - do not call him a lover. He trades only for his own account.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad distinguishes true love from a bond kept by calculation, in which a person stays only while things go his way.
The Kural tells the ruler to meet the need of the kinsman who left without cause and came back for his own purpose, and then to weigh him carefully; Guru Angad names exactly that kind of attachment: love that trades on its own account.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus returns after twenty years, Penelope does not take him back at once. She tests him with the secret of their bed, carved from a living olive tree, and embraces him only when he answers correctly.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 23 [10]
The one who has come back is carefully examined before being fully accepted.
This is the Kural’s எண்ணிக் கொளல, accepting only after deliberation.
Philosophy
In Cicero’s dialogue On Friendship, Laelius advises that we should judge people before we give them our affection rather than love first and judge afterwards, and should test a character before trusting it.
— Cicero, De Amicitia (Laelius on Friendship) 85 [11]
Cicero makes careful assessment come before full attachment.
This is the Kural’s rule for the returning kinsman: examine first, then embrace.

Real Life Example

Athens takes back Alcibiades
407-406 BCE · Athens, Greece
In 415 BCE the Athenian general Alcibiades, recalled from the expedition to Sicily to face charges of sacrilege, fled to Sparta, Athens's enemy, and advised the Spartans; he later went over to the Persian governor Tissaphernes. After leading the Athenian fleet to victories in the Hellespont, he returned to Athens in triumph in 407 BCE, the old charges were dropped, and he was given supreme command above all other generals. When a subordinate he had left in charge of the fleet lost a battle to the Spartans at Notium in 406 BCE, Alcibiades was not re-elected general and withdrew to Thrace.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Athens restored a former defector to the highest command almost at once, without lasting trust on either side, and the arrangement collapsed within about a year.
The kural advises a ruler to meet the needs of a kinsman who left and came back for his own purposes, but to weigh him carefully before taking him back, and Athens's haste with Alcibiades shows what happens when that weighing is skipped.
