Kural 482

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

பருவத்தோடு ஒட்ட ஒழுகல் திருவினைத் தீராமை ஆர்க்குங் கயிறு.
Paruvaththotu Otta Ozhukal Thiruvinaith Theeraamai Aarkkung Kayiru.

Meaning

Acting at the right time is like a rope that ties good fortune down so it cannot slip away from you.

Elementary

Farmers plant when the rains come, not whenever they like. If you do things at the right time, good luck stays with you as if tied by a rope. If you ignore the time, it wanders off.

University

Paruvam means season or ripe time, and otta ozhukal means conducting oneself so as to cling to it. The image in the second line is striking: thiru (Fortune, often personified as the goddess Lakshmi or Tirumakal) is fickle by nature, and timely action is the kayiru (rope) that aarkkum (binds) her so that she does not theeraamai, that is, depart. Parimelazhagar glosses paruvam as the time suited to the undertaking. He reads the couplet as a claim that wealth lasts only when action keeps pace with circumstance. Renaissance Europe has a close analogue in Machiavelli’s Fortuna, who favours the man whose conduct matches the times. The Tamil text is identical in both sources consulted.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let the king undertake his march in the fine month Margasirsha, or towards the months of Phalguna and Kaitra, according to the (condition of his) army.”
— Manusmṛti 7.182 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu’s rules for kings give seasonal windows for campaigns, chosen to suit harvests and the readiness of the army.
This is paruvam in concrete form: royal success depends on moving with the season, not against it.
Christianity
“And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not.”
— Galatians 6:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul uses the farmer’s image of harvest in its proper season to urge steady good work that will bear fruit at the right time.
Like the Kural, Paul ties reward to season, although his focus is on endurance until the season comes.
Islam
The Prophet said that there are two blessings in which many people are cheated: health and free time. People let both pass without using them, and later regret it.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6412 (Book of Ar-Riqaq) [5]
The hadith treats the right time to act as a gift that is easily wasted, not something always available.
The Kural adds that using the season well is exactly what keeps good fortune bound to you.
Judaism
“He [also] used to say: If I am not for myself, who is for me? But if I am for my own self [only], what am I? And if not now, when?”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 1:14 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Hillel’s three questions end by insisting that the present moment is the time to act, since postponement has no natural end.
‘If not now, when?’ is the moral core of keeping in step with the season.
Buddhism
“The idler who does not exert himself when he should, who though young and strong is full of sloth, with a mind full of vain thoughts — such an indolent man does not find the path to wisdom.”
— Dhammapada 280 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada condemns the one who fails to make an effort at the time for effort (uṭṭhāna-kāla).
Missing the season for effort loses the prize, which is the Kural’s point about Fortune slipping loose.
Jainism
“At the right time a monk should sally forth, and he should return at the right time; avoiding to do anything out of time, he should do what is appropriate for each period of the day.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The opening lecture on discipline makes timeliness a rule of the monk's whole day: each act has its proper hour and nothing is to be done out of season.
The kural says acting in step with the season binds Fortune; this verse shares the principle of doing each thing at its fitting time, though for a Jain monk the reward is discipline, not wealth.
Sikhism
“This human body has been given to you. This is your chance to meet the Lord of the Universe.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa (So Purakh / Rehras), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn recited daily in Rehras, Guru Arjan tells the soul that the human birth is its ripe moment, the opportunity not to be let slip.
The Kural says acting in step with the season is the rope that holds Fortune fast; Guru Arjan calls human life the one season in which the highest prize can be won.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod’s farmer’s calendar tells Perses to start the harvest when the Pleiades rise and to plough when they set. It fixes each task to a sign in the sky and warns that the man who ploughs late will reap little.
— Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 383–617 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
For Hesiod, prosperity comes to the one who keeps the gods’ seasons, and ruin to the one who acts out of time.
This is paruvaththotu otta ozhukal, living in step with the season, as the path to lasting wealth.
Philosophy
In The Prince, chapter 25, Machiavelli argues that Fortune governs about half of human affairs. He says a prince prospers when his way of acting fits the times and falls when the times change and he does not.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. XXV (tr. W. K. Marriott) [11]
Machiavelli treats Fortune as a force that can be managed by matching one’s conduct to the quality of the times.
His Fortuna and Valluvar’s Thiru are the same fickle goddess, and both are held by timely conduct.

Real Life Example

Vasco da Gama crosses the Indian Ocean with and against the monsoon
1498-1499 · Malindi, Kenya, and Calicut (Kozhikode), India
Guided by a pilot engaged at Malindi on the East African coast, Vasco da Gama's ships left on 24 April 1498 and, carried by the monsoon winds, reached the Malabar coast near Calicut on 18 May, a crossing of about 24 days. On the way home he left Calicut in late 1498 before the monsoon winds had turned in his favour, and the return crossing of the Arabian Sea took nearly three months, during which many of his crew died of scurvy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The same ocean took less than four weeks to cross in the right season and nearly three months in the wrong one, at a terrible cost in lives.
The kural says acting in step with the season is the rope that binds Fortune, and da Gama's two crossings show fortune held by timing on the way out and lost by ignoring it on the way back.
