Kural 992

Courtesy · Panpudaimai · பண்புடைமை

அன்புடைமை ஆன்ற குடிப்பிறத்தல் இவ்விரண்டும் பண்புடைமை என்னும் வழக்கு.
Anputaimai aandra kutippiraththal ivvirantum panputaimai ennum vazhakku.

Meaning

Love for others and being born into a good family: these two together make up the way called courtesy.

Elementary

Think of a kind heart as the seed and a good home as the garden it grows in. When a child has both a loving heart and a family that teaches good ways, gentle manners bloom. Valluvar says courtesy needs both.

University

The second couplet names the two roots of panbudaimai: anbudaimai (அன்புடைமை, 'possession of love', the title of chapter 8) and aandra kudip-piraththal (ஆன்ற குடிப்பிறத்தல், 'being born in an eminent, accomplished family'). Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar take aandra kudi as a high or noble family, while Solomon Pappaiah reinterprets it as a family that 'lives in harmony with the world' (உலகத்தோடு ஒத்து வாழும் குடும்பம்), softening the hereditary emphasis; Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as living a birth worth reflecting on. Pope's 'Benevolence and high born dignity, / These two are beaten paths of courtesy' preserves vazhakku (வழக்கு) as a 'beaten path' or established usage. The kural links this chapter back to Kudimai (ch. 96, 'Nobility of family'), which places good family upbringing at the base of character; modern readers often stress that the second element is about formation and tradition rather than caste rank. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The unsuccessful yogī, after many, many years of enjoyment on the planets of the pious living entities, is born into a family of righteous people, or into a family of rich aristocracy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.41 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna assures Arjuna that one whose spiritual effort was cut short is reborn into a righteous or noble household, where good formation can resume.
The Gita, like the Kural, treats birth into a worthy family as a real support for refined character.
Christianity
“Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil;”
— 1 Corinthians 13:4–5 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as patient and kind and as the thing that keeps a person from behaving unseemly, that is, discourteously.
This joins the Kural's first root, love, directly to courteous behaviour, though it omits the role of family.
Islam
“You see that the people are of different natures. Those who were the best in the pre-lslamic period, are also the best in Islam if they comprehend religious knowledge.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3493, 3494 (Book of Virtues and Merits) [5]
The Prophet compares people to mines of differing quality, saying the noble-natured before Islam remain the best within it when they gain understanding.
Like the Kural, it treats inherited good nature as a foundation that, joined with right understanding, yields excellence of character.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that the people of Israel are known by three family traits, being merciful, being bashful or modest, and performing acts of kindness, and that whoever has these traits is fit to join the people.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 79a [6]
The rabbis define belonging to the people by inherited moral traits of compassion, modesty and kindness rather than by bloodline alone.
It fuses the Kural's two roots, a kindly heart and the character of a worthy lineage, into one definition of a refined people.
Buddhism
In the Vasala Sutta the Buddha tells the brahmin Aggika Bharadvaja that one does not become an outcast or a brahmin by birth, but by one's deeds.
— Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7 [7]
The Buddha rejects birth as the measure of worth and locates nobility entirely in conduct.
This stands in tension with the Kural's second root, offering a contrast: for the Buddha, kindness and conduct alone make the noble person.
Jainism
“By one's actions one becomes a Brâhmana, or a Kshattriya, or a Vaisya, or a Sûdra.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The monk Gayaghosha tells a sacrificing priest that a person's station is made by deeds, not by birth.
Jainism, like Buddhism, would ground courtesy in conduct rather than lineage, which qualifies the Kural's appeal to good birth.
Sikhism
“Recognize the Lord's Light within all, and do not consider social class or status; there are no classes or castes in the world hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 349, Raag Asa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak tells his hearers to see God's light in everyone and not to ask about anyone's jaati (caste or birth status). Sikh teaching consistently refuses to treat lineage as a source of worth.
The Kural names loving kindness and birth in a worthy family as the two roots of courtesy; Guru Nanak affirms the first, in recognising God's light in all, but rejects the second, refusing to weigh anyone by birth.
Greek Mythology
When Telemachus and Pisistratus arrive at Sparta, Menelaus welcomes them before learning their names and remarks that their bearing shows they come of a line of sceptred kings, since base men could not have such sons.
— Homer, Odyssey 4.60–64 [10]
In Homer, gracious and noble bearing is read as the visible mark of good birth.
It reflects the Kural's assumption that a worthy family shows itself in refined manners.
Philosophy
Aristotle distinguishes being well-born (eugenes), the excellence of one's stock, from being noble in character (gennaios), noting that the well-born often degenerate and fail to live up to their ancestry.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.15 [11]
Aristotle grants that good birth is a real advantage but insists it must be matched by personal excellence.
This mirrors the Kural's pairing: lineage alone is insufficient unless joined to a kindly character.

Real Life Example

Princess Diana shakes hands with an AIDS patient
April 1987 · Middlesex Hospital, London, United Kingdom
In April 1987 Diana, Princess of Wales, opened Britain's first hospital ward dedicated to patients with HIV/AIDS, at the Middlesex Hospital in London. In front of the press she shook the hand of a patient without wearing gloves, at a time when many people wrongly believed the virus could be passed on by touch. The photograph circulated around the world and is credited with helping to challenge the stigma attached to the disease.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A member of the royal family used her position to show simple warmth to people who were being shunned.
The kural says courtesy is made of loving kindness joined to birth in a worthy family, and Diana, born into an aristocratic family and married into the royal one, joined her rank to visible compassion.
