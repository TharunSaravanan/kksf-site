Kural 889

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

எட்பக வன்ன சிறுமைத்தே ஆயினும் உட்பகை உள்ளதாங் கேடு.
Etpaka vanna sirumaiththe aayinum utpakai ulladhaang ketu.

Meaning

Even if hostility inside a group is as small as half a sesame seed, ruin is hiding inside it.

Elementary

A sesame seed is one of the tiniest seeds there is, and half a seed is smaller still. The poet says even a grudge that small, kept hidden inside a group, can grow until it breaks everything apart.

University

The measure of smallness is etpakavu (எட்பகவு, the split half of a sesame seed), among the tiniest units in Tamil idiom; anna (அன்ன) means 'like'. However sirumaiththu (சிறுமைத்து, slight) the utpakai is, ketu (கேடு, ruin) is ulladhu (உள்ளது, present) within it. Mu. Varadarasanar says that even at that size it carries ruin able to destroy a whole family, and Solomon Pappaiah reads it as destruction already present inside the faction. Sivayogi Sivakumar instead reads 'ulladhu' as 'all that exists', giving 'ruin to everything', a reading the word order allows. The thirukkural.net rendering ('even a dissent as small as a seed') and Pope ('slight as shred of sesame seed') agree with the dominant reading. The text is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Maya Sabha, Duryodhana mistakes a crystal floor for water and falls into a pool he took for solid floor, and Bhima and others laugh at him; the humiliation feeds the envy behind the dice game and the war.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Duryodhana's account of the palace) [3]
A small moment of mockery within the extended family helps set off the Kurukshetra catastrophe.
As the Kural warns, a slight internal grievance can hold ruin within it.
Christianity
“Even so the tongue is a little member, and boasteth great things. Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth!”
— James 3:5 (KJV) [4]
James warns that a tiny spark, like a careless word, can set a whole forest ablaze.
Both texts insist that a small seed of discord carries vast destructive power.
Islam
The Prophet taught that Satan has despaired of being worshipped by the believers in Arabia, but has not given up on sowing dissension among them.
— Sahih Muslim 2812 [5]
The hadith presents internal discord as the opening through which harm still enters a community.
Like the Kural, it treats even small internal division as dangerous.
Judaism
The Talmud says Jerusalem was destroyed because of Kamtza and Bar Kamtza: a host's servant invited the wrong man to a feast, the host publicly threw out his enemy, the sages present stayed silent, and the humiliated guest went to Rome and informed against the Jews.
— Babylonian Talmud, Gittin 55b-56a [6]
A trivial mix-up between two similar names, combined with an existing grudge, sets off national catastrophe.
This is the Kural's sesame-seed enmity: tiny in origin, ruinous in effect.
Buddhism
“Think not lightly of evil, saying, ‘It will not come to me.’ Drop by drop is the water pot filled. Likewise, the fool, gathering it little by little, fills himself with evil.”
— Dhammapada 121 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada warns that small wrongs, ignored, accumulate into great harm.
The Kural similarly refuses to treat small enmity as harmless.
Jainism
“One should not be complacent with a small debt, slight wound, spark of fire and slight passion, because what is small (today) may become bigger (later).”
— Saman Suttam 134 (English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [8]
This well-known Jain maxim sets a small passion beside a small debt, wound and spark: none of them may be trusted to stay small.
The Kural says that enmity within, even as slight as half a sesame seed, holds ruin inside it, and the Jain maxim makes the same point that a trifling inner fault must never be dismissed.
Sikhism
“Even if the poisonous snake is fed large amounts of milk, it will still yield only poison.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 171, Raag Gauree, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das observes that a poisonous nature stays poisonous however much it is nourished, so that the harm remains hidden within.
The Kural holds that even enmity as small as half a sesame seed contains ruin, and Gurbani likewise says that poison held inside is never made harmless.
Greek Mythology
Eris, the goddess of Strife, throws a golden apple inscribed for the fairest among the goddesses at a wedding feast; the quarrel between Hera, Athena and Aphrodite leads to the judgement of Paris and the Trojan War.
— Apollodorus, Epitome 3.2 (trans. J. G. Frazer) [10]
A single apple becomes the seed of the most famous war in Greek myth.
As the Kural says, strife no bigger than a seed can hold enormous ruin.
Philosophy
“In revolutions the occasions may be trifling, but great interests are at stake. Even trifles are most important when they concern the rulers.”
— Aristotle, Politics 5.4 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle cites how the constitution of Syracuse was overturned by a love-quarrel between two young men in the government.
Like the Kural, he warns that the smallest internal dispute among those in power can bring down the state.

Real Life Example

A single deputy's objection breaks up the Polish Sejm
1652 · Warsaw, Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth
In 1652 a single deputy, Wladyslaw Sicinski, objected to extending the session of the Sejm, and his lone protest ended it, the first time the principle known as the liberum veto was used to break up a parliament. Under the practice, any one member could defeat a measure or dissolve a session and nullify its acts, and it was later used so often that business was frequently paralysed and foreign powers bribed deputies to use it. The veto was abolished only in the Constitution of 3 May 1791, shortly before the Commonwealth was partitioned out of existence in 1795.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A rule that let the smallest dissent paralyse the whole state became one of the main weaknesses of the Commonwealth.
The kural says that even internal enmity as small as a split sesame seed holds ruin within it, and the liberum veto let a single objection undo a whole parliament.
