Kural 1132

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

நோனா உடம்பும் உயிரும் மடலேறும் நாணினை நீக்கி நிறுத்து.
Nonaa udambum uyirum madalaerum naaninai neekki niruththu.

Meaning

My body and my life cannot bear this any longer. They have put shame aside and are ready to ride the madal (the palm-stem horse of a refused lover).

Elementary

Have you ever been so upset that you cried in front of everyone, even though you usually would feel shy? The speaker here says his whole body and heart are hurting so much that his shyness has simply been set aside.

University

Nōṉā (நோனா) is the negative participle of nōl, 'to endure': the body and the life-breath (uyir) that 'cannot bear' the separation are made the subjects of the verb, as if they act on their own. Nāṇiṉai nīkki niṟuttu (நாணினை நீக்கி நிறுத்து), 'having removed and set aside shame', is the key phrase of the chapter: nāṇ, the sense of reserve that Tamil ethics praises (cf. chapter 102, Nāṇuṭaimai), is not destroyed but deliberately put to one side. Commentators in Parimelazhagar's line take this as the hero telling the confidante that his resolve is already made; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses 'my body and life have resolved to ride the madal, having set shame aside', while Pappaiah softens it to 'are thinking of riding'. Pope: 'My body and my soul, that can no more endure, / Will lay reserve aside, and mount the horse of palm.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Krishna plays his flute on the autumn night, the cowherd women of Vraja drop whatever they are doing, leave husbands and households, and run to him in the forest, heedless of what anyone will say.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29 (Rasa-panchadhyayi) (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana presents the gopis' abandonment of social propriety as the highest form of devotion.
Like the Kural's lover, the gopis set aside shame and reserve because love has become unbearable.
Christianity
A woman known in the town as a sinner enters a Pharisee's house where Jesus is dining, weeps over his feet, wipes them with her hair, and anoints them, ignoring the disapproval of everyone present.
— Luke 7:36-50 (KJV) (paraphrase) [4]
Jesus defends her, saying her many sins are forgiven because she loved much.
Like the Kural's speaker, she lets love overrule every rule of decorum in front of onlookers.
Islam
Rumi opens his Masnavi by saying that only one whose garment has been torn by a mighty love is cleansed of greed and every fault.
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi I.22 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.A. Nicholson) [5]
For Rumi, being 'torn' by love is a purifying loss of self-protection.
The Kural's lover is similarly 'torn open': his reserve has been stripped away by love too strong to bear.
Judaism
The Talmud tells that Rachel, daughter of the wealthy Ben Kalba Savua, chose the poor shepherd Akiva against her father's wishes, and her father vowed she would receive nothing from his property.
— Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 62b-63a (paraphrase; William Davidson Edition, Sefaria) [6]
Rachel gave up wealth, status and family approval for the man she loved and believed in.
Like the Kural's speaker, she set aside what society expected of her because her love would not let her do otherwise.
Buddhism
Nanda, the Buddha's half-brother, ordained on the day of his wedding, tells the monks he is discontented and wants to return to lay life because he keeps remembering his beautiful fiancée's parting words.
— Udana 3.2 (Nanda Sutta) (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha then leads him to a higher understanding, and Nanda eventually becomes an arahant.
Nanda's longing, like the Kural lover's, is strong enough to make him ready to throw off the role he is expected to keep.
Jainism
“When Râgîmatî perceived that Rathanêmi's strength of will was broken, and temptation had got the better of him, she did not lose her presence of mind and defended her Self on that occasion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Rathanemi, seeing Rajimati in a cave, loses his composure and proposes to her; she keeps hers.
Rathanemi is in the Kural lover's state, with restraint overpowered, and the Jain text sets beside him a figure who refuses to let go of hers.
Sikhism
“The three qualities are shattered, doubt and fear run away, and the clay pot of public opinion is broken.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 986, Raag Maalee Gauraa, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das describes the devotee absorbed in the Lord's praise, for whom fear and the fragile 'pot' of worry about what people think are smashed.
The Kural's body and soul set shame aside to mount the madal; in Gurbani the lover of God likewise breaks the clay pot of public opinion (lok-laaj).
Greek Mythology
Sappho describes how, at the sight of the beloved, her tongue breaks, a thin fire runs under her skin, her eyes see nothing, her ears roar, and she trembles until she seems close to death.
— Sappho, Fragment 31 (paraphrase) [10]
Her famous poem lists the physical collapse that love brings about in the body.
The Kural likewise says it is the body and the life-breath that can no longer bear love and so throw off restraint.
Philosophy
Hume argues that reason alone can never oppose a passion in directing the will, and that reason is and ought only to be the slave of the passions.
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 2.3.3 (paraphrase) [11]
For Hume, feeling, not reason, ultimately moves us to act.
The Kural's lover is a living example: his considered sense of shame has been simply overruled by passion.

Real Life Example

Longfellow persists after Fanny Appleton refuses him
1837-1843 · Boston and Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States
Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, a young widower, fell in love with Frances (Fanny) Appleton after travelling with her family in Switzerland. He was crushed when she rejected his marriage proposal in 1837, but he did not give up. She accepted him in the spring of 1843, and they married in July 1843; her father gave the couple Craigie House as a wedding gift.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A respected professor and poet kept up a suit that had already been refused, accepting the embarrassment of pressing on for six years.
The kural's lover says that body and life can no longer bear the pain and so shame must be set aside; Longfellow likewise set aside the pride of a rejected suitor and kept pursuing the woman he loved.
