Kural 634

Ministry of State · Amaichu · அமைச்சு

தெரிதலும் தேர்ந்து செயலும் ஒருதலையாச் சொல்லலும் வல்லது அமைச்சு.
Theridhalum thaerndhu seyalum orudhalaiyaach Sollalum valladhu amaichchu.

Meaning

A good minister can study a problem carefully, act only after choosing wisely, and give a firm, clear answer.

Elementary

A good referee watches the play closely, thinks for a moment, and then makes a clear call. He does not say “maybe”. This kural says a good adviser looks carefully, chooses wisely, and then speaks clearly.

University

Three abilities are named. Theridhal is examining or understanding the matter. Thaerndhu seyal is acting after deliberate selection of means. Orudhalaiyaach sollal is speaking decisively (orudhalai, ‘one-sided’, i.e. unambiguous). Commentators differ on the last. Varadarasanar and Kesikan take it as boldness and clarity, while Solomon Pappaiah stresses asserting only what is beneficial. The Parimelazhagar tradition ties it to giving the king a settled opinion rather than a list of options, much as Kautilya’s teacher Pishuna asks for counsellors ‘capable of giving decisive opinion’. ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net read valladhu amaichu, and thirukkural-thamizh.org prints the older form ‘valla thamaichchu’. The two are the same words divided differently.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Hence he shall consult such persons as are believed to be capable of giving decisive opinion regarding those works about which he seeks far advice.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.15 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [4]
In the Arthashastra’s debate on counsel, the teacher Pishuna argues that a king should consult men who can give firm, decisive advice rather than half-hearted opinions.
This is orudhalaiyaach sollal, the decisive word, which the Kural requires of a minister.
Christianity
“But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil.”
— Matthew 5:37 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that speech should be plain and definite, without evasive hedging or extra oaths.
The Kural likewise praises the minister who can say one clear thing.
Islam
“So pardon them and ask forgiveness for them and consult them in the matter. And when you have decided, then rely upon Allah.”
— Qur’an 3:159 (Sahih International) [6]
The Prophet is told to consult his companions and, once a decision is made, to carry it out with trust in God.
This follows the Kural’s order: think it through, decide, then act firmly.
Judaism
“Every purpose is established by counsel; and with good advice carry on war.”
— Proverbs 20:18 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs teaches that plans succeed when built on deliberation, and that even war must be waged by wise guidance.
This is the Kural’s thaerndhu seyal: acting only after deliberate choice.
Buddhism
In the Kalama Sutta, the Buddha tells the Kalamas not to accept teachings on report, tradition or a teacher’s authority alone. They should examine them, and once they know for themselves what is skilful and beneficial, adopt and practise it.
— Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 [8]
The Buddha lays out a method of careful examination followed by committed action.
It follows the Kural’s sequence of theridhal (examining) and then thaerndhu seyal (acting on what one has chosen).
Jainism
“He, being wise, smelled the food-bag and pronounced this decision, ‘There is here a serpent that poisons by its glance, that originated from the combination of substances. If he had opened the bag, he would have been consumed, certainly. So turn it loose in the forest with your face averted.’”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 10, ch. 11, part 2 ‘Contest between Pradyota and Abhaya’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [9]
Held captive at Pradyota's court, Abhayakumara is asked about a messenger's food-bag; he examines it, gives a clear diagnosis and a definite instruction, and the danger is shown to be real.
The Kural says a minister must study a matter, act after careful choice and state a firm conclusion; Abhaya examines the bag and gives a single, decisive verdict.
Sikhism
“Wisdom leads us to serve our Lord and Master; through wisdom, honor is obtained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a salok of Saarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak praises akal, practical wisdom and discernment, as what guides right action and earns honour.
The Kural wants the minister to understand a matter, act after careful choice and state his conclusion firmly; Guru Nanak likewise makes discerning wisdom the guide of right action.
Greek Mythology
Trapped in the Cyclops’ cave, Odysseus first wants to stab the sleeping Polyphemus. He stops himself when he realizes that his men could never move the huge stone from the doorway. He works out a slower plan (the wine, the blinding stake and the escape under the rams) and carries it through.
— Homer, Odyssey 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Odysseus checks his impulse, thinks the situation through, and then acts on a well-chosen plan.
This shows the Kural’s ideal of examining a situation and acting only after careful choice.
Philosophy
In the Discourse on Method, Descartes’ second provisional maxim is to be as firm and resolute in action as he can. Once he has settled on a course, even a doubtful one, he follows it steadily, like travellers lost in a forest who should walk straight in one direction rather than wander in circles.
— Descartes, Discourse on Method, Part III [12]
Descartes argues that after reasonable deliberation, being decisive is itself a practical virtue.
This matches the Kural’s thaerndhu seyal and its firm, one-directional conclusion.

Real Life Example

The Beveridge Report sets out a plan against the five giant evils
24 November 1942 · London, United Kingdom
In June 1941 William Beveridge was asked to head a committee of inquiry surveying Britain's patchwork social insurance schemes. His report, Social Insurance and Allied Services, was presented to Parliament on 24 November 1942 and proposed a comprehensive system of social insurance to attack five great evils: want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness. More than 600,000 copies of the report and its summaries were sold by February 1944, and after the war Parliament passed the National Insurance Act of 1946 and established the National Health Service in 1948.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Beveridge's report was valued because it combined a thorough survey of existing provision with a single, clearly argued plan rather than a list of loose options.
The kural names three abilities of a counsellor, examining a matter fully, choosing the course with care and stating a firm, clear conclusion, and the Beveridge Report is a model of all three.
