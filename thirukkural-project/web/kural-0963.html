Kural 963

Honour · Maanam · மானம்

பெருக்கத்து வேண்டும் பணிதல் சிறிய சுருக்கத்து வேண்டும் உயர்வு.
Perukkaththu vendum panidhal siriya Surukkaththu vendum uyarvu.

Meaning

When you have plenty, be modest and gentle. When you have very little, hold your head high.

Elementary

When you win a game, it is good to be kind and not show off. When you lose, it is good to stand tall and not beg or sulk. This kural says to bow a little in good times and stand straight in hard times.

University

A perfectly balanced antithesis: perukkam (பெருக்கம், increase, prosperity) is set against surukkam (சுருக்கம், contraction, want), and panidhal (பணிதல், bending, humility) against uyarvu (உயர்வு, height, unbending dignity). Siriya (சிறிய, 'small') intensifies the second half: in a narrow, reduced state. Mu. Varadarasanar reads uyarvu as 'height that does not bow' (பணியாத உயர்வு), and Solomon Pappaiah as not stooping to others in poverty. Maanam here is not pride but a discipline that runs against circumstance: humble when one could afford arrogance, upright when one is tempted to grovel. Pope's version on ytamizh.com: 'Bow down thy soul, with increase blest, in happy hour; / Lift up thy heart, when stript of all by fortune's power.' Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not disturbed in mind even amidst the threefold miseries or elated when there is happiness, and who is free from attachment, fear and anger, is called a sage of steady mind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada; vedabase.io) [3]
Krishna describes the steady sage, who is neither shaken by misery nor puffed up by happiness.
The Kural asks for the same steadiness in fortune and misfortune, expressed as humility and dignity.
Christianity
“I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need.”
— Philippians 4:12 (KJV) [4]
Paul says he has learned how to live rightly both in plenty and in need.
The Kural gives the rule for each state: humility in plenty, dignity in need.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you. And Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful -”
— Quran 57:23 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran teaches neither despair in loss nor pride in gain.
This matches both halves of the Kural: no arrogance in prosperity, no collapse in poverty.
Judaism
Moses warns Israel that when they have eaten their fill, built fine houses, and seen their flocks, silver and gold multiply, their hearts may be lifted up and they may forget the LORD who brought them out of Egypt.
— Deuteronomy 8:12-14 [6]
The Torah warns that prosperity breeds arrogance and forgetfulness.
This supports the first half of the Kural, humility in prosperity, but not the second.
Buddhism
“The good renounce (attachment for) everything. The virtuous do not prattle with a yearning for pleasures. The wise show no elation or depression when touched by happiness or sorrow.”
— Dhammapada 83 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The wise are neither elated by good fortune nor cast down by bad.
The Kural turns this evenness into a rule of conduct: humble in plenty, upright in want.
Jainism
“Among all those who live in this fertile and wide world, those who give not alms to others, amongst the rich, though exceeding rich, are poor indeed; while those who go not to beg alms of the rich, though they have become exceedingly poor, are indeed illustrious.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 30 (மானம், 'Honour'), stanza 6 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar says a rich man who does not give is truly poor, while a poor man who will not beg from the rich is truly great.
The second half matches the Kural's dignity in want closely; the first half links right conduct in plenty to generosity rather than humility.
Sikhism
“That man, who in the midst of pain, does not feel pain, who is not affected by pleasure, affection or fear, and who looks alike upon gold and dust;”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 633, Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the person who stays the same in pain and pleasure and sees gold and dust alike.
The Kural asks for humility in prosperity and an upright head in want; Guru Tegh Bahadur asks that neither fortune nor misfortune change who one is.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus tells how the rich king Croesus asked Solon who was the happiest of men, expecting to be named; Solon refused to call any man happy before his end. Later, defeated and on the pyre, Croesus called out Solon's name and was spared by Cyrus.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.30-33 and 1.86-87 [10]
Croesus' pride in prosperity is humbled, and he learns wisdom in defeat.
It shows the cost of lacking the humility in prosperity that the Kural requires.
Philosophy
In Book 2 of the Consolation, Lady Philosophy reminds Boethius that Fortune turns her wheel by nature, raising the low and casting down the high, so no one should be puffed up by her gifts or crushed by their loss.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2 [11]
Since fortune always turns, neither rising nor falling should change who a person is.
The Kural draws the practical rule: bow when high, stand tall when low.

Real Life Example

Albert Einstein declines the presidency of Israel
November 1952 · Princeton, New Jersey, United States
After Chaim Weizmann, Israel's first president, died on 9 November 1952, Ambassador Abba Eban wrote to Albert Einstein on behalf of Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion to ask whether he would accept the presidency if the Knesset elected him. Einstein declined within days, replying that he was deeply moved by the offer but lacked the natural aptitude and experience to deal with people and carry out official duties. Yitzhak Ben-Zvi was elected president instead.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
At the height of his world fame, Einstein turned down a head of state's office with a modest account of his own limits.
The kural asks for humility when fortune swells, and Einstein answered the greatest honour offered to him by declaring himself unfit for it.
