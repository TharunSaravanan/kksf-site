Kural 611

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

அருமை உடைத்தென்று அசாவாமை வேண்டும் பெருமை முயற்சி தரும்.
Arumai udaiththendru asaavaamai vendum Perumai muyarchi tharum.

Meaning

Never give up by telling yourself a task is too hard. Effort itself gives you the strength and greatness to get it done.

Elementary

Imagine a big hill you have to climb to get home. If you stand at the bottom saying 'it’s too steep,' you never move. But every step you take makes your legs a little stronger, until the hill doesn’t look so big any more. Trying is what makes you able.

University

The chapter title ஆள்வினை (literally ‘man-work’, human exertion) is glossed by the Thirukkural-Thamizh edition as இடைவிடாத முயற்சி, ‘unceasing effort’, and this opening couplet names the first enemy of effort: அசாவு, the despondency that sets in when a task is judged அருமை (difficult). Parimelazhagar takes பெருமை not as fame but as the ‘greatness’ or capacity needed to accomplish the deed, so the claim is causal: capacity is produced by striving rather than being a precondition for it; Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah (ytamizh.com) both read it this way, while Kalaignar renders பெருமை as ‘great strength’. Placed immediately after ஊக்கமுடைமை (ch. 60, energy) and மடியின்மை (ch. 61, absence of sloth), the chapter moves from inner zeal to its outward expression in sustained work, culminating in the famous claim of kural 620 that effort can turn back fate itself. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural-thamizh.org, the latter printing the older sandhi-joined form (அருமை யுடைத்தென் றசாவாமை).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“You should thus perform your prescribed Vedic duties, since action is superior to inaction. By ceasing activity, even your bodily maintenance will not be possible.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.8 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna tells the hesitant Arjuna that withdrawing from action is never the higher path; even keeping the body alive requires work.
Like the Kural, the Gita refuses to let the difficulty of a task become an excuse for not acting.
Christianity
“And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not.”
— Galatians 6:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul urges the Galatians not to grow tired of doing good, promising a harvest to those who do not give up.
‘Faint not’ is almost a translation of the Kural’s அசாவாமை, not sinking into weariness before the work is done.
Islam
“Indeed, with hardship [will be] ease.”
— Qur’an 94:6 (Sahih International) [5]
The short Meccan sura consoles the Prophet in difficulty by twice promising that ease comes together with hardship, then tells him to keep labouring (94:7).
Both texts tell the believer that a task’s hardness is not a reason to despair of it.
Judaism
“It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you at liberty to neglect it”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:16 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon teaches that the vastness of a task does not release a person from the obligation to work at it.
Tarfon and Valluvar both deny that ‘it is too great for me’ is a valid reason to hold back.
Buddhism
“You yourselves must strive; the Buddhas only point the way.”
— Dhammapada 276 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha tells his followers that no teacher can do the work of liberation for them; the effort must be their own.
Like the Kural, the verse locates the power to accomplish in one’s own striving, not in circumstances.
Jainism
“One cannot quickly arrive at discernment; therefore one should exert one's self, abstain from pleasures, understand the world, be impartial like a sage, and guard one's self: (thus) never be careless.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The sutra admits the goal is hard and slow to reach, but takes that as a reason to exert oneself, not to give up.
As the Kural says never to lose heart by calling a task too hard, because effort brings the strength to finish it, the Jain text answers difficulty with steady self-exertion.
Sikhism
“So tell me - how can the fort of doubt and the moat of Maya be overcome? … Worshipping the Perfect Guru, this awesome force is subdued.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 522, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, pauri 15), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a pauri of Gujri ki Vaar, Guru Arjan describes the five passions as an army no hero can withstand, asks how such a fortress could ever be breached, and answers that it can be overcome.
The Kural says never lose heart because a task looks too hard; Guru Arjan names the hardest of tasks and refuses to call it impossible.
Greek Mythology
“But between us and Goodness the gods have placed the sweat of our brows: long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the first; but when a man has reached the top, then is she easy to reach, though before that she was hard.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 286-292 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells his idle brother Perses that excellence lies up a steep road that becomes easy only once one has climbed it.
Hesiod’s ‘hard at first, easy at the top’ is the Kural’s point that effort creates the capacity the task seemed to demand.
Philosophy
Seneca remarks to Lucilius that it is not because things are difficult that we do not dare; it is because we do not dare that they are difficult.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 104.26 [11]
The Stoic argues that the difficulty we attribute to a task is largely produced by our own timidity toward it.
This is almost exactly Valluvar’s warning against calling a thing ‘too hard’ and sinking into despondency.

Real Life Example

Dashrath Manjhi cuts a road through a hill by hand
c. 1960-1982 · Gehlaur, Gaya district, Bihar, India
Dashrath Manjhi, a poor labourer from the village of Gehlaur near Gaya, lived on one side of a rocky hill that forced villagers into a long detour to reach the nearest town and its services. After the death of his wife, he set out to cut a passage through the hill himself, working for about twenty-two years with a hammer and chisel. The finished cut, commonly described as about 360 feet long and 30 feet wide, greatly shortened the journey, and the Bihar government later honoured him as the Mountain Man.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A task that looked impossible for one man with hand tools was completed because he kept working at it year after year, alongside his ordinary labour.
The kural says never to lose heart by calling a task too hard, because effort itself gives the strength to accomplish it, and Manjhi's road was cut by a man who simply refused to treat the hill as too big.
