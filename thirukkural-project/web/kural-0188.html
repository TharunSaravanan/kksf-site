Kural 188

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

துன்னியார் குற்றமும் தூற்றும் மரபினார் என்னைகொல் ஏதிலார் மாட்டு.
Thunniyaar kutramum thootrum marabinaar, Ennaikol ethilaar maattu.

Meaning

If people make a habit of telling everyone the faults of those closest to them, what will they not do to strangers?

Elementary

If someone spreads secrets about their own best friend, imagine what they’d say about you! This kural says: watch how people talk about their closest friends — that’s how they’ll treat everyone else.

University

The couplet argues a fortiori from intimacy. Tuṉṉiyār (“those who are close”) are the people whom affection, obligation, and self-interest should all protect; if the slanderer’s marapu (settled disposition, habit) leads him to tūṟṟu (scatter abroad, as chaff is winnowed) even their faults, then no restraint can be expected toward ētilār, those who are unconnected to him. The rhetorical question ennaikol (“what indeed?”) leaves the outcome to the listener’s imagination. Valluvar is making a practical point about trust: how a person speaks of his intimates is the best predictor of how he will treat everyone else. The verse also implies that backbiting is not an occasional lapse but a trait, marapu, which, once formed, spares no one.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As the honest are always pained at speaking ill of others, so do the wicked always rejoice in doing the same thing.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Sambhava Parva §74 (K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Shakuntala says this after King Dushyanta, her own husband, publicly reviles her as a lewd woman and denies their son.
The king who slanders his own wife and child shows the Kural’s point: one who defames his intimates will spare no one.
Christianity
“Thou sittest and speakest against thy brother; thou slanderest thine own mother’s son.”
— Psalm 50:20 (KJV) [4]
God rebukes the wicked man who slanders even his own brother.
The same outrage as the Kural: slander aimed at the closest of kin.
Islam
“whoever screened a Muslim, Allah will screen him on the Day of Resurrection.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2442 [5]
The Prophet teaches that a Muslim is a brother to be protected, and that covering a brother’s faults earns God’s covering of one’s own.
Islam sets the opposite ideal: those close to you are owed concealment of their faults, not exposure.
Judaism
Miriam and Aaron spoke against their own brother Moses; Miriam was struck with tzara’at and shut outside the camp for seven days.
— Numbers 12:1–15 (paraphrase) [6]
The Torah’s example of lashon hara is aimed at a sibling, and later tradition treats it as the model case of the sin.
Even a prophetess who spoke against her own brother was punished, showing how serious it is to disparage intimates.
Buddhism
“For a liar who has violated the one law (of truthfulness) who holds in scorn the hereafter, there is no evil that he cannot do.”
— Dhammapada 176 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada warns that once a person abandons truthful speech, no evil is beyond him.
Both argue from one proven vice to unlimited danger: if he does this, what won’t he do?
Jainism
“If he is frequently angry; if he perseveres in his wrath; if he spurns friendly advice; if he is proud of his learning; if he finds fault with others; if he is angry even with friends; if he speaks evil even of a good friend behind his back; ... then he is called ill-behaved.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.7–9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Among the fourteen faults that mark an ill-behaved monk who cannot reach nirvāṇa, the Uttarādhyayana lists speaking evil even of a good friend behind his back.
Valluvar asks what a man who spreads the faults even of those closest to him would not do to strangers, and the Jain text singles out exactly this, slandering even a good friend behind his back, as a sign of bad character.
Sikhism
“He slanders others, and pollutes himself with his own filth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 88, Sri Raag (Sri Raag ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das teaches that the habit of slandering others dirties the slanderer's own mind.
The Kural asks what a person who habitually broadcasts even his intimates' faults will not do to strangers; Guru Amar Das shows how deeply such a habit corrupts the one who has it.
Greek Mythology
“paying this penalty, they say, for the shameful weakness he displayed in failing to keep a bridle on his lips, when admitted by gods, though he was but mortal, to share the honours of their feasts like one of them.”
— Euripides, Orestes 4–10 (E. P. Coleridge) [10]
Tantalus was welcomed to the gods’ own table and then betrayed their confidences with an unbridled tongue.
Tantalus fits the Kural’s type: one who exposes even those who took him into their inner circle.
Philosophy
“When he is sitting with others, he loves to criticise one who has just left the circle; nay, if he has found an occasion, he will not abstain from abusing his own relations.”
— Theophrastus, Characters 28, ‘The Evil-Speaker’ (Jebb) [11]
Theophrastus sketches the Evil-Speaker, who attacks whoever has just left the room and does not spare his own family.
Theophrastus draws the Kural’s portrait exactly: a backbiter who spares not even his kin.

Real Life Example

Truman Capote publishes La Cote Basque 1965
November 1975 · New York City, United States
In November 1975 Esquire magazine published La Cote Basque 1965, a chapter from Truman Capote's unfinished novel Answered Prayers. Capote's wealthy society friends, whom he called his swans, recognised themselves as thinly disguised characters whose private secrets, confided to him over years of friendship, were exposed. Most of them cut him off, and Babe Paley, his closest friend among them, never spoke to him again.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14] [15]
Capote treated the confidences of his closest friends as material to broadcast, and in doing so lost nearly all of them.
The kural asks what a person who broadcasts the faults even of his intimates will not do to strangers, and Capote's friends, seeing their own secrets in print, drew exactly that conclusion.
