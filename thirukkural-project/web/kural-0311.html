Kural 311

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

சிறப்பீனும் செல்வம் பெறினும் பிறர்க்குஇன்னா செய்யாமை மாசற்றார் கோள்.
Sirappeenum selvam perinum pirarkku innaa Seyyaamai maasatraar kol.

Meaning

Even if hurting someone would bring them great riches and fame, people with pure minds keep firmly to their rule of never causing pain to others.

Elementary

Imagine someone offers you a huge treasure chest, but only if you push another kid down to get it. A truly good person says “no thanks” every time. Valluvar says the pure-hearted never hurt anyone, not even for the biggest prize in the world.

University

The chapter opens by testing the principle of non-harm against its strongest temptation: sirappu eenum selvam, wealth that “gives birth to” distinction and social standing. The word kol (settled principle, fixed resolve) matters: non-harm is presented not as a mood or a calculation but as a standing commitment of the maasatraar, “those without stain,” whose minds are clear of the anger and greed that make harm seem attractive. Placed in the turavaraviyal (the section on the renunciant’s virtues), the couplet implies that the refusal to profit from another’s suffering is a defining mark of spiritual maturity, not merely of good manners. The same structure appears in Cicero’s Stoic claim that profiting by a neighbour’s loss is more contrary to nature than death or poverty, and in Plato’s thought-experiment of the Ring of Gyges, which asks whether anyone would stay just if injustice were profitable and safe. Valluvar’s answer is that the stainless would.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him avoid (the acquisition of) wealth and (the gratification of his) desires, if they are opposed to the sacred law, and even lawful acts which may cause pain in the future or are offensive to men.”
— Manusmriti 4.176 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu tells the householder to give up wealth and pleasures whenever getting them would go against dharma, and even to avoid legal acts that would cause pain or offend others.
Like the Kural, Manu ranks not-causing-pain above getting rich: wealth gained against righteousness or at others’ cost is to be refused.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that no amount of worldly gain is worth the loss of one’s inner life.
Both texts set the greatest possible riches against moral integrity and find the riches not worth it. Valluvar names harming others as the price the pure refuse to pay.
Islam
“O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally, but rather trade by mutual consent.”
— Qur’an 4:29 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The Qur’an forbids enriching yourself at another person’s expense by unjust means; wealth must change hands only by fair, willing exchange.
Both forbid seeking wealth through another’s loss or pain. The Kural adds that the pure refuse such gain even when it would bring prestige.
Judaism
“Better is a little with righteousness than great revenues without right.”
— Proverbs 16:8 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that a small, honest income is worth more than a fortune gained unjustly.
Same weighing as the Kural: great revenue on one side, righteousness on the other, and righteousness wins.
Buddhism
“He is indeed virtuous, wise, and righteous who neither for his own sake nor for the sake of another (does any wrong), who does not crave for sons, wealth, or kingdom, and does not desire success by unjust means.”
— Dhammapada 84 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha describes the truly wise person as one who will do no wrong for any reason, and who does not seek wealth or success by unjust means.
Nearly the Kural’s own claim: the pure person will not do wrong even when wealth or success is the reward.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana warns that wealth gained by harmful deeds does not last and leaves its owner bound by his own passions and hatred.
Valluvar's stainless people refuse to cause pain even for wealth that would bring them distinction. The Jain verse explains why: wealth gained by evil deeds is lost, and the hatred behind it remains.
Sikhism
Macauliffe records that at Saiyidpur (Eminabad) Guru Nanak lodged with Lalo, a low-caste carpenter, and declined the great feast of Malik Bhago, the local steward. When Malik Bhago demanded why, the Guru took Lalo's coarse bread in one hand and Bhago's rich bread in the other and squeezed them: it is said milk came from Lalo's bread and blood from Bhago's, because Bhago's wealth had been gained by bribery and oppression.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter IV, pp. 43-44 (paraphrase) [8]
The story, from the Janamsakhi tradition as Macauliffe retells it, sets honest poverty above lavish wealth that was squeezed out of other people's suffering. Guru Nanak refuses the rich man's hospitality outright.
The Kural says the stainless will not cause pain even for wealth that brings great prestige; Guru Nanak turned away from just such prestigious wealth because it had been gained by hurting others.
Greek Mythology
In Plato’s retelling of the myth, the shepherd Gyges finds a gold ring that makes him invisible. Freed from all consequences, he seduces the queen, kills the king, and takes the throne. Glaucon uses the tale to ask whether anyone would stay just if injustice were profitable and could never be discovered.
— Plato, Republic II, 359c–360d (the Ring of Gyges) [9]
The Ring of Gyges tests whether people avoid harming others only because they fear getting caught.
The Kural gives the answer Socrates goes on to defend: the truly pure refuse to harm others for gain even when the gain is huge and the harm would go unpunished.
Philosophy
“Well then, for a man to take something from his neighbour and to profit by his neighbour's loss is more contrary to Nature than is death or poverty or pain or anything else that can affect either our person or our property.”
— Cicero, De Officiis III.21 (trans. Walter Miller, Loeb 1913) [10]
Cicero argues that gaining at another’s expense is worse, and more against human nature, than suffering any personal loss.
Cicero and Valluvar both say the good person would rather stay poor than get rich by hurting someone else.

Real Life Example

John Woolman refuses to write wills that bequeath enslaved people
1740s · Colonial New Jersey (British North America)
As a young clerk in the early 1740s, the Quaker John Woolman was asked to write a bill of sale for an enslaved woman; he did so against his conscience but told his employer he believed slavery was inconsistent with Christianity. Afterwards, working as a tailor and scrivener who depended on the goodwill of his customers, he refused to draw up wills in which enslaved people would be passed on to heirs, telling one respected neighbour that he could not write his will without breaking his own peace. He went on to spend much of his life persuading Quakers to free those they held in slavery, and his Journal was published in 1775.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Woolman chose to lose business and risk offending respected customers rather than earn fees from documents that would keep human beings in bondage.
The kural says the stainless hold firmly to the rule of never causing pain, even when harming others would bring wealth and standing, and Woolman turned away paid work rather than lend his pen to slavery.
