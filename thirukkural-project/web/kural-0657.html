Kural 657

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

பழிமலைந்து எய்திய ஆக்கத்தின் சான்றோர் கழிநல் குரவே தலை.
Pazhimalaindhu eydhiya aakkaththin saandror Kazhinal kuravae thalai.

Meaning

For good people, being very poor is better than getting rich in a shameful way.

Elementary

Would you rather have a small lunch you paid for honestly, or a big feast you got by cheating? The Kural says the small honest lunch is worth far more, because you can enjoy it with a clear heart.

University

Paḻi malaintu, ‘having taken on / worn blame’ (malai- as in wearing a garland), describes wealth won by accepting disgrace, and ākkattiṉ is the comparative ablative, ‘than such wealth’. Kaḻi nalkuravu is ‘extreme, excessive poverty’ (kaḻi = beyond measure). Parimelazhagar takes it as the poverty of the cāṉṟōr, and Mu. Varadarasanar adds that they accept it because they keep purity of action (viṉait-tūymai). Talai means ‘head, foremost’, so such poverty ranks first. The couplet restates 656 as a general value judgement and prepares for the fate of tainted wealth in 658-660. All three sources agree on the text; thirukkural-thamizh.org shows the sandhi forms மலைந் தெய்திய வாக்கத்திற்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna describes people of demonic nature who, bound by hundreds of desires and given to lust and anger, strive to pile up wealth by unjust means for their own enjoyment.
— Bhagavad Gita 16.12 [4]
The Gita counts gathering wealth through injustice (anyāyena artha-sañcaya) among the marks of a nature bound for ruin.
The Kural’s tainted wealth is exactly the Gita’s wealth amassed by unjust means, which the wise would never choose over poverty.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [5]
Jesus asks what any gain could be worth if it costs the self.
The Kural asks the same question in social terms: no fortune is worth one’s honour, so honest poverty is better.
Islam
“Say, ‘Not equal are the evil and the good, although the abundance of evil might impress you.’ So fear Allah, O you of understanding, that you may be successful.”
— Quran 5:100 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran warns believers not to be dazzled by the abundance of what is corrupt.
Like the Kural, it ranks a little that is good above a great deal gained through wrong.
Judaism
The Psalmist declares that the little a righteous man has is better than the great wealth of many wicked.
— Psalm 37:16 (JPS 1917) [7]
Psalm 37 comforts the upright, who may see the wicked prosper, with the promise that honest little is worth more.
This is almost the same comparison as the Kural: righteous poverty over wicked plenty.
Buddhism
“Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth. A trustworthy person is the best kinsman, Nibbana the highest bliss.”
— Dhammapada 204 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada redefines wealth as contentment, not as possessions.
The poverty of the good that the Kural praises is rich in the contentment and trustworthiness this verse values.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira warns that wealth won by evil deeds does not last, and that its owners end up caught in the snares of their own passions.
The kural rates the poverty of the good above wealth won through disgrace; the Jain verse explains why such wealth is no prize, since it is lost and leaves its owner bound.
Sikhism
At Saiyidpur (Eminabad), Macauliffe relates, Guru Nanak stayed with Lalo, a poor carpenter, and declined the feast of the rich steward Malik Bhago. Called to explain, he squeezed Lalo's coarse bread and Bhago's rich bread: milk flowed from the first and blood from the second, showing honest labour against wealth won by bribery and oppression.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion, vol. 1 (1909), Life of Guru Nanak, ch. IV, pp. 43-44 [10]
The story contrasts the pure food of honest work (kirat karni) with the tainted riches of exploitation, and the Guru openly chose the poor man's bread.
The Kural says the deepest poverty of good people is better than wealth gained through disgrace; the Guru's preference for Lalo's simple bread over Bhago's feast makes the same choice.
Greek Mythology
The poor, pious old couple Baucis and Philemon alone welcome Zeus and Hermes in disguise after their rich neighbours turn the gods away; the neighbours’ homes are flooded, and the couple’s hut becomes a temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 [11]
Ovid sets humble goodness against prosperous impiety, and the gods reward the poor couple.
Like the Kural, the myth shows the poverty of the good as worth more than the wealth of the unworthy.
Philosophy
Epictetus says that if he can gain wealth while keeping himself modest, faithful and high-minded, he will; but if he must lose his own goods so that others may gain things that are not good, it is unjust and foolish to ask it.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 24 [12]
For the Stoic, integrity is a real good and money is not, so no trade of one for the other makes sense.
This is the Kural’s reasoning: better to stay poor than to exchange one’s good name for money.

Real Life Example

Vera Caslavska accepts years of hardship rather than betray her stance
1968-1989 · Prague, Czechoslovakia
The Czech gymnast Vera Caslavska, winner of 22 gold medals at Olympic, world and European level, signed the Two Thousand Words manifesto for democratic reform in June 1968 and, after the Soviet-led invasion that August, went into hiding before winning four golds at the Mexico City Olympics. Because of her political convictions she fell out of favour with the Communist authorities, was refused employment and for two decades was barred from travelling, at times working as a cleaner. After the fall of communism in 1989 she became president of the Czechoslovak Olympic Committee and an adviser to President Vaclav Havel.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Caslavska could have had the comfortable life of a national sporting hero by bending to the authorities, but she accepted poverty and obscurity instead.
The kural says the deepest poverty of good people is better than wealth gained by taking on disgrace, and Caslavska's two decades of hardship rather than giving up her stance is a modern example.
