Kural 1274

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

முகைமொக்குள் உள்ளது நாற்றம்போல் பேதை நகைமொக்குள் உள்ளதொன் றுண்டு.
Mukaimokkul ulladhu naatrampol pedhai nakaimokkul ulladhon rundu.

Meaning

Just as a sweet smell is hidden inside a flower bud that has not opened, something is hidden inside this innocent girl's small, shy smile.

Elementary

A flower bud doesn’t smell much yet, but the sweet smell is already inside, waiting. The poet says his beloved’s small smile is like that bud: it holds a feeling she hasn’t said out loud yet.

University

The couplet pairs two ‘buds’: mukai-mokkuḷ, the closed bud of a flower that holds its nāṟṟam (fragrance), and nakai-mokkuḷ, the bud of a smile, the half-formed smile of the pētai (innocent young woman), which holds ‘something’. Varadarasanar and Kesikan gloss that something as a kuṟippu, a hidden intent; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar make it explicit as her thoughts of her lover filling the smile. As in 1272-1273, the speaker is traditionally the husband addressing the confidante about signs he can read in the heroine. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; Pope’s rendering on ytamizh (‘budding smile of this dear damsel’s eyes’) adds the eyes, which are not in the Tamil.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Seeing Shakuntala, Dushyanta describes her lower lip as having the colour of a young leaf, her arms as tender shoots, and youth, alluring as a flower, as bound up in her limbs.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 1 (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa describes a young woman’s beauty as a flower that has not yet fully opened.
The Kural uses the same budding-flower image for a girl whose feeling is still folded within her smile.
Christianity
“The flowers appear on the earth; the time of the singing of birds is come, and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land; The fig tree putteth forth her green figs, and the vines with the tender grape give a good smell. Arise, my love, my fair one, and come away.”
— Song of Solomon 2:12-13 (KJV) [4]
The Song pictures love as a spring of budding vines already giving off their fragrance.
As in the Kural, fragrance from unopened growth stands for love that is about to blossom.
Islam
A hadith teaches that smiling in the face of one’s brother is an act of charity.
— Jami at-Tirmidhi 1956 (paraphrase) [5]
The Prophet treats a smile as something that carries real meaning and value to the one who receives it.
The Kural also treats a smile as carrying a message, though a lover’s rather than a charitable one.
Judaism
“Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out. Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his precious fruits.”
— Song of Songs 4:16 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The bride asks the winds to release the fragrance held within her garden for her beloved.
In both poems a fragrance held within waits to be released to the beloved.
Buddhism
“Like a beautiful flower full of color and also fragrant, even so, fruitful are the fair words of one who practices them.”
— Dhammapada 52 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada distinguishes a flower that only looks beautiful from one that also carries fragrance.
The Kural also prizes the fragrance inside the flower, the hidden meaning behind the visible smile.
Jainism
“Meekly and politely they approach him with their manifold arts to win his heart; and talking sweetly in confidential conversation they make him do (what they like).”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga warns monks that sweet manners and pleasant talk may carry a hidden purpose.
The Kural also reads a hidden meaning in a gentle outward sign, but with delight rather than the Jain monk’s caution.
Sikhism
“Like the fragrance which remains in the flower, and like the reflection in the mirror,”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 684, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur goes on to say that, like fragrance in a flower, the Lord dwells deep within, so one should search for Him in one's own heart.
The Kural says something lies hidden in the young woman's budding smile as fragrance lies hidden in an unopened bud; Guru Tegh Bahadur uses the same image of hidden fragrance for the Lord hidden in the heart.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that Aphrodite’s portion among gods and men includes the whispers of maidens and their smiles, along with sweet delight, love and gentleness.
— Hesiod, Theogony 203-206 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
In Hesiod a maiden’s smile belongs to Aphrodite’s domain and carries love’s meaning.
The Kural likewise finds love folded inside a girl’s smile.
Philosophy
Aristotle distinguishes what exists only potentially from what exists actually, as the oak is present in the acorn before it grows.
— Aristotle, Metaphysics IX (Theta) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, things can contain what they will become before it is visible.
The bud that already holds its scent, and the smile that already holds a feeling, are poetic cases of the potential within the actual.

Real Life Example

Paul Laurence Dunbar writes We Wear the Mask
1890s · United States
The African American poet Paul Laurence Dunbar, whose early collections Majors and Minors (1895) and Lyrics of Lowly Life (1896) won the praise of the critic William Dean Howells, wrote the poem We Wear the Mask. The poem describes people who smile and sing in public while hiding pain and grief behind that cheerful face. It asks the world not to look too closely, while the speaker cries out privately to God.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem became one of the best-known statements in American literature of feeling hidden behind a smile.
The kural says something lies hidden inside a young woman's budding smile, as fragrance lies inside a closed bud; Dunbar's poem is about what lies concealed behind a smile.
