Kural 549

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

குடிபுறங் காத்தோம்பிக் குற்றம் கடிதல் வடுவன்று வேந்தன் தொழில்.
Kutipurang Kaaththompik Kutram Katidhal Vatuvandru Vendhan Thozhil.

Meaning

Protecting the people from outside harm, caring for them, and punishing crime is not a fault in a king. It is his proper job.

Elementary

A lifeguard who blows her whistle at kids running by the pool isn’t being mean. It is her job, keeping everyone safe. A fair ruler who stops people from doing wrong is doing the same kind of job.

University

Parimelazhagar separates two kinds of protection: புறம் காத்து, guarding the people from others (enemies, robbers, grasping officials), and ஓம்பி, protecting them from himself (for example, by not over-taxing). Only then comes குற்றம் கடிதல், removing crime by fitting punishment. வடு அன்று (‘not a scar or blemish’) answers an implied objection that punishment, as a form of harm, conflicts with the ethic of non-injury taught in the Aram section (e.g. Ch. 32–33); வேந்தன் தொழில் (‘the king’s work’) makes it a duty of office. Varadarasan follows Parimelazhagar closely. Pappaiah reads குற்றம் as the people’s grievances (குறைகள்), which shifts the emphasis from punishment to redress. Kalaignar adds that the ruler should punish whoever the offender is, without fearing reproach. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads புறம் as ‘beyond the borders’. The couplet prepares for the harsher 550. The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A king who punishes those who do not deserve it, and punishes not those who deserve it, brings great infamy on himself and (after death) sinks into hell.”
— Manusmriti 8.128 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu says that failing to punish the guilty disgraces a king as much as punishing the innocent.
The blemish lies in not punishing, which is the reverse side of the Kural’s ‘punishment is no blemish’.
Christianity
“For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil. … for he beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil.”
— Romans 13:3–4 (KJV) [4]
Paul teaches that the ruler’s sword against evildoers is part of his God-given service, not a wrong.
Punishing wrongdoing is the ruler’s proper work, not a stain, as in the Kural.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due and when you judge between people to judge with justice.”
— Qur’an 4:58 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an treats authority as a trust and just judgment as a command from God.
Judging and correcting wrongdoing is a sacred duty of those in authority, not a fault.
Judaism
“pray for the welfare of the government, for were it not for the fear it inspires, every man would swallow his neighbor alive.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:2 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Rabbi Hanina teaches that the fear government inspires is what stops people from devouring one another.
The ruler’s power to restrain wrongdoers is a blessing to pray for, not a blemish.
Buddhism
“It is my desire that there should be uniformity in law and uniformity in sentencing. I even go this far, to grant a three-day stay for those in prison who have been tried and sentenced to death.”
— Ashoka, Pillar Edict IV (trans. S. Dhammika) [7]
Ashoka, a Buddhist king, kept courts and punishments but insisted on uniform law and granted a reprieve for appeals.
Even a king devoted to non-harm accepts punishment as part of his work, as the Kural says, while tempering it.
Jainism
“A king will be the punisher of those transgressing boundaries. Seated on a very high throne, consecrated first, having at hand the fourfold army, he should have unbroken commands.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 1, ch. 2, part 8 ‘Coronation as king’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [8]
When the earlier laws of reproach fail and people start doing wrong, Rishabha explains that a consecrated king is needed to punish transgressors, and he is then made the first king.
The Kural says that protecting the people and curbing crime by punishment is a king's proper work, not a blemish. In Jain sacred history the office of king was created for exactly that purpose.
Sikhism
Guru Hargobind was both pir, spiritual leader, and mir, chieftain of his people, responsible for protecting them against tyranny with force of arms.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Sikhism: Guru Hargobind” by W. H. McLeod (paraphrase) [9]
From the sixth Guru onward, armed protection of the community became a recognised duty of Sikh leadership.
The Kural says guarding the people and curbing wrongdoing is no blemish on a king but his proper work; Sikh tradition likewise treats protecting the people against tyranny as a duty, not a stain on spiritual authority.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Eumenides, Athena founds the court of the Areopagus and urges the Athenians not to banish all fear from their city, since no mortal who fears nothing remains just; the court is to be a guardian of the land, awake while others sleep.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides 681–706 [10]
The goddess of wisdom establishes lawful punishment as the city’s protection.
Justice that restrains crime is presented as a gift to the city, not a stain, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“But he who desires to inflict rational punishment does not retaliate for a past wrong which cannot be undone; he has regard to the future, and is desirous that the man who is punished, and he who sees him punished, may be deterred from doing wrong again.”
— Plato, Protagoras 324a–b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Protagoras argues that rational punishment is not revenge but a way of protecting the future by deterring wrongdoing.
Punishment understood as protection is the Kural’s குற்றம் கடிதல் as the king’s duty.

Real Life Example

La Reynie becomes Paris's first lieutenant general of police
1667 · Paris, France
By an edict of March 1667, Louis XIV created the office of lieutenant of police for Paris, bringing together in one office policing duties that had been divided among many officials, and appointed Gabriel Nicolas de La Reynie, who held it until 1697. The office was responsible for public safety, fires and floods, street cleaning, the food supply and public order. Under La Reynie, streets were lit at night by thousands of lanterns and refuse was collected at fixed hours.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The crown treated keeping the capital safe, clean and orderly as a regular duty of government rather than an occasional show of force.
The kural says guarding the people from harm, caring for them and curbing crime is no blemish on a king but his proper work, and the new Paris police office joined protection, public care and law enforcement in one royal duty.
