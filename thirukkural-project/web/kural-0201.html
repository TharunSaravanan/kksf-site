Kural 201

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

தீவினையார் அஞ்சார் விழுமியார் அஞ்சுவர் தீவினை என்னும் செருக்கு.
Theevinaiyaar anjaar vizhumiyaar anjuvar, Theevinai ennum serukku.

Meaning

Bad people are not afraid of the pride that comes from doing wrong. Truly good people are afraid of it.

Elementary

Imagine two kids near a hot stove. One has touched it so many times he doesn’t even flinch anymore. The other is careful and keeps her hands away. This kural says good people are like the careful kid — they are scared of doing bad things, while people who do bad things all the time have stopped being scared at all.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a paradox: fear, usually a mark of weakness, is here the mark of excellence (விழுமியார், “the eminent”). The object of dread is not punishment but “the pride called evil-doing” (தீவினை என்னும் செருக்கு) — Parimelazhagar reads செருக்கு as the intoxication or swagger that evil deeds produce, which dulls conscience the more one indulges it. The couplet thus describes moral desensitization: habitual wrongdoers lose the very faculty of dread that protects the noble. This anticipates what Buddhist ethics calls hiri-ottappa (moral shame and moral dread) and Jewish ethics calls yirat cheit (fear of sin) — fear reconceived as a moral sense rather than cowardice.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Janārdana, although these men, overtaken by greed, see no fault in killing one’s family or quarreling with friends, why should we, with knowledge of the sin, engage in these acts?”
— Bhagavad Gita 1.37–38 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [4]
On the battlefield Arjuna contrasts the Kauravas, whose greed has blinded them to sin, with himself and his brothers, who can still see the wrongness of what they are about to do and recoil from it.
Exactly Valluvar’s split: the wrongdoers feel no dread, while those with moral sight fear the sin.
Christianity
“The fear of the LORD is to hate evil: pride, and arrogancy, and the evil way, and the froward mouth, do I hate.”
— Proverbs 8:13 (KJV) [5]
Wisdom speaks in Proverbs, defining true reverence as a hatred of evil — and naming pride and arrogance first among the things hated.
Links holy fear, the avoidance of evil, and the rejection of pride — the same triad as the Kural’s “dread of the pride of evil.”
Islam
“Only those fear Allah, from among His servants, who have knowledge.”
— Qur’an 35:28 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an ties genuine fear of God (khashyah) to knowledge: the more one truly understands, the more one is moved to reverent caution about wrongdoing.
Like the Kural, it makes fear a sign of the elevated, not the base — those with understanding fear; the heedless do not.
Judaism
“Anyone whose fear of sin precedes his wisdom, his wisdom is enduring, but anyone whose wisdom precedes his fear of sin, his wisdom is not enduring.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:9 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Rabbi Hanina ben Dosa makes yirat cheit — fear of sin — the foundation on which lasting wisdom must stand.
Both texts treat fear of wrongdoing as the defining trait of the truly excellent person.
Buddhism
“Monks, these two bright qualities guard the world. Which two? Conscience & concern.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.9, Lokapala Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha names hiri (conscience/moral shame) and ottappa (concern/moral dread of the results of wrong action) as the ‘guardians of the world’ that hold society together.
Ottappa is almost a technical name for Valluvar’s theme: the dread of evil deeds that the noble keep and the corrupt have lost.
Jainism
“Papabhiru : Apprehensive of sin.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of thirty-five qualities of the ideal householder, item 3, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, Jaina Ethics (jainworld.com) [9]
Among the thirty-five qualities Hemacandra's Yogaśāstra lists for the ideal Jain householder is being afraid of sin; Sangave notes that one who keeps these rules becomes, among other things, 'afraid of sinning.'
Valluvar makes dread of evil the mark of the noble, and the Jain ideal householder is defined in the same way, by his apprehension of sin.
Sikhism
“You people are unconscious; you should be afraid of sin.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 220, Raag Gauree, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a hymn in Raag Gauree, Guru Tegh Bahadur tells the heedless that what they lack is a healthy dread of wrongdoing.
The Kural says those steeped in evil feel no fear of it while the truly noble do; Guru Tegh Bahadur likewise treats fearlessness about sin as a sign of being spiritually asleep.
Greek Mythology
In Hesiod’s account of the degenerate Iron Age, the goddesses Aidos (Shame) and Nemesis (Righteous Indignation) wrap themselves in white robes and abandon humankind for Olympus, leaving mortals with bitter sorrows and no defense against evil.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 197–201 (paraphrase) [11]
For Hesiod, a healthy society depends on Aidos and Nemesis — an inner shame at wrongdoing and a dread of the retribution it provokes. When people stop feeling them, moral collapse follows.
Mirrors the Kural’s point that losing the dread of evil is itself the sign of the wicked; the noble keep Aidos.
Philosophy
“This sign, which is a kind of voice, first began to come to me when I was a child; it always forbids but never commands me to do anything which I am going to do.”
— Plato, Apology 31d (Jowett) [12]
Socrates describes his daimonion, an inner warning that checks him before wrong or unwise action — an early philosophical image of conscience as a restraining dread.
Valluvar’s noble person is one whose inner alarm still sounds; Socrates is the classic example of such a person.

Real Life Example

Andrei Sakharov turns against nuclear testing
1957-1963; Nobel Peace Prize 1975 · Moscow, Soviet Union (now Russia)
Andrei Sakharov, a physicist who had played a leading part in building the Soviet hydrogen bomb, became concerned in 1957 about the biological hazards of radioactive fallout from nuclear tests and began campaigning to halt or limit testing, which brought him into conflict with Nikita Khrushchev in 1961. He later said he had helped promote the 1963 treaty banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in outer space and under water. After a 1968 essay of his was published abroad he was removed from weapons work, and in 1975 he was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, which the Soviet authorities did not allow him to travel to Oslo to receive.
— Sources: [13] [14]
One of the people best placed to understand the harm his work could do was the one who grew most alarmed by it, at real cost to his own career and safety.
The kural says that the noble dread evil deeds while hardened wrongdoers feel no fear, and Sakharov's growing dread of the harm his weapons could cause marked him out among those around him.
