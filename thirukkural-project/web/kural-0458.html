Kural 458

Avoiding Mean Associations · Sitrinanjeraamai · சிற்றினஞ்சேராமை

மனநலம் நன்குடைய ராயினும் சான்றோர்க்கு இனநலம் ஏமாப் புடைத்து.
Mananalam nankudaiya raayinum saanrorkku Inanalam emaap pudaiththu.

Meaning

Even people of good character who already have a very good mind find that good friends make them safer and stronger.

Elementary

Even very good and wise people still need good friends. Good company works like a protective wall that keeps their goodness safe.

University

The concessive ஆயினும் (‘even though’) blocks the obvious objection: surely the already-good (சான்றோர்) do not need company? Valluvar replies that for them too, இனநலம் is ஏமாப்பு, security or fortification. The word suggests protection against the slow wearing-down of virtue by circumstance. Virtue, on this view, is not a fixed possession but a condition to be kept up, and company is part of the upkeep. The couplet guards against the moral self-sufficiency that 454 already undercut.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Arjuna, the greatest archer of his age and a man of dharma, collapses into confusion on the battlefield and is restored only by Krishna, who rides beside him as charioteer and friend.
— Bhagavad Gita, Chapters 1–2 [4]
Even the most capable and righteous hero needs a steadying companion at the decisive moment.
This is the Kural’s point that good company is a fortress even for the good.
Christianity
“And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together.”
— Hebrews 10:24–25 (KJV) [5]
Believers, including the mature, are to keep gathering so they can spur each other to goodness.
In both texts even the virtuous need a good community to support their goodness.
Islam
“And keep yourself patient [by being] with those who call upon their Lord in the morning and the evening, seeking His countenance.”
— Qur’an 18:28 (Sahih International) [6]
Even the Prophet, the best-minded of men, is told to stay in the company of sincere worshippers and not follow the heedless.
This is a direct parallel to ‘even for the wise, good company is a fortress’.
Judaism
Honi the Circle-Maker, a holy man, sleeps for seventy years. When he wakes, no one recognises or respects him, and he prays for death. The Talmud concludes: ‘either companionship or death’.
— Babylonian Talmud, Ta’anit 23a [7]
Even a saint cannot flourish without a fitting circle.
This makes the Kural’s point in its strongest form: even the virtuous depend on good company.
Buddhism
“Should a seeker not find a companion who is better or equal, let him resolutely pursue a solitary course; there is no fellowship with the fool.”
— Dhammapada 61 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The committed seeker should look for good or better companions, and prefer solitude to a foolish one.
This shares the Kural’s high valuation of good company and its rejection of bad. It differs in allowing solitude as an alternative.
Jainism
“A Sramana engaged in austerities, who longs for righteousness, should eat the proper quantity of allowed food, should select a companion of right understanding, and should live in a place suited to seclusion.” … “If he does not meet with a clever companion who surpasses or equals him in virtue, he should live by himself, abstaining from sins and not devoted to pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.4–5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Even the ascetic already devoted to austerity and righteousness is told to choose a companion of right understanding, ideally one equal or superior in virtue. Jainism also allows him to live alone rather than accept a lesser companion.
The kural says that even for the already virtuous, good company is a source of security. The Jain text likewise has even the advanced ascetic seek a worthy companion, though it qualifies the need more than Valluvar does.
Sikhism
“In the Company of the Holy, the mind becomes stable.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 271, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan teaches that the Saadh Sangat steadies the mind so that it stops wandering.
The Kural says even the already-virtuous gain security and strength from good company; Guru Arjan presents holy company as the steadying support of the mind.
Greek Mythology
When Diomedes volunteers for the night raid on the Trojan camp, he asks for a companion, saying that when two go together one sees before the other what is best. He chooses Odysseus.
— Homer, Iliad 10.222–247 [11]
Even the bravest hero knows that a good companion multiplies judgement and safety.
This fits the Kural’s ‘fortress’ of good company, even for the excellent.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that even the supremely happy good man needs friends, since no one would choose to have all goods on condition of being alone. Man is a political creature whose nature is to live with others.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.9 [12]
Self-sufficiency does not remove the need for good company, even at the height of virtue.
This is the same claim as the Kural: even the sanror are fortified by good company.

Real Life Example

Marcel Grossmann helps Einstein with the mathematics of general relativity
1912-1913 · Zurich, Switzerland
As students at the Zurich Polytechnic, Albert Einstein often missed lectures and relied on the careful notes of his friend Marcel Grossmann to prepare for exams. In 1912, when Einstein returned to Zurich with the physical ideas for a new theory of gravity but not the mathematics to express it, he turned to Grossmann, by then a professor of geometry, who guided him through the relevant mathematical literature. In 1913 they published a joint paper outlining the theory, with Einstein writing the physics and Grossmann the mathematical part.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Even one of the most original minds in history needed a trusted friend at crucial moments to make progress.
The kural says that even for the wise, who already have goodness of mind, good company is a source of security and strength, and Grossmann was that support for Einstein.
