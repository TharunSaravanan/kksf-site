Kural 842

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

அறிவிலான் நெஞ்சுவந்து ஈதல் பிறிதியாதும் இல்லை பெறுவான் தவம்.
Arivilaan nenjuvandhu eedhal piridhiyaadhum illai peruvaan thavam.

Meaning

When a fool happily gives something away, it is only because of the good luck the receiver earned from past good deeds.

Elementary

Suppose a kid who never shares suddenly hands you his best sandwich with a big smile. You didn’t earn it from his good sense; you were just lucky that day. The poet says a fool’s gift is like that: it comes from the receiver’s good luck, not the giver’s wisdom.

University

The kural is ironic: a person without understanding (arivilaan, அறிவிலான்) does not give wisely, so if he gives nenjuvandhu (நெஞ்சுவந்து, with a gladdened heart), the explanation must lie outside him, in the thavam (தவம், austerity, accumulated merit) of the recipient (peruvaan). Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah gloss thavam as nalvinai (good karma), and the old English rendering speaks of ‘the receiver’s merit (in a former birth)’, reflecting the karmic reading of the classical commentators. Kalaignar softens it to ‘the receiver’s good fortune’, while Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as the receiver’s own spiritual quality drawing the gift. The underlying point is that the fool’s generosity is unreliable and unearned, an accident of providence rather than a virtue. No textual variants appear between ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And charity performed at an impure place, at an improper time, to unworthy persons, or without proper attention and respect is said to be in the mode of ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.22 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
The Gita classifies giving by the understanding behind it, and gift without discernment belongs to tamas, the mode of ignorance.
A fool’s gift has no wisdom behind it; any good it does is not the giver’s doing.
Christianity
“And it shall be, that thou shalt drink of the brook; and I have commanded the ravens to feed thee there.”
— 1 Kings 17:4 (KJV) [4]
God feeds Elijah through ravens, creatures that give without any understanding of what they do.
The gift reaches the receiver because of who he is before God, not because of the giver’s wisdom.
Islam
“And there is no creature on earth but that upon Allah is its provision, and He knows its place of dwelling and place of storage.”
— Qur’an 11:6 (Sahih International) [5]
Provision (rizq) is assigned by God; whoever hands it over is only the channel.
As in the kural, what the receiver gets is due to what was allotted to him, not the giver’s sense.
Judaism
In the Book of Esther, the fickle King Ahasuerus, who earlier approved Haman’s decree, is moved after a sleepless night to heap honor on Mordecai and later grants Esther’s plea.
— Esther 6–8 (Tanakh) [6]
Jewish tradition reads the turn of events as hidden providence working through an unreliable ruler.
The king’s generosity came not from his wisdom but from the merit and destiny of those who received it.
Buddhism
In the Dakkhinavibhanga Sutta the Buddha describes four kinds of purification of an offering, including one purified by the recipient but not by the giver: the giver lacks virtue, yet the gift bears fruit because the recipient is virtuous.
— Majjhima Nikaya 142, Dakkhinavibhanga Sutta [7]
The worth of a gift can come entirely from the person who receives it.
Very close to Valluvar: a gift from an unworthy giver yields good because of the receiver’s merit.
Jainism
The Tattvārtha Sūtra teaches that the merit of a gift is not fixed. It varies with four things: the manner of giving, the thing given, the giver, and the worthiness of the recipient (pātra).
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.39 (Digambara numbering; 7.34 in the Śvetāmbara recension), Umāsvāti; cf. tr. Vijay K. Jain, wisdomlib.org [8]
In Jain teaching on charity (dāna), a gift's fruit depends partly on who receives it, which is why a worthy recipient such as a restrained monk adds to the merit of the offering.
The Kural ironically credits a fool's glad gift to the recipient's own merit, and Jain dāna theory likewise counts the recipient's worthiness as one of the things that decide what a gift is worth.
Sikhism
“He Himself is the Creator, and He Himself is the Enjoyer; He Himself gives, and causes others to give.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 554, Raag Bihaagraa, Bihaagre Ki Vaar (pauri 15), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a pauri of Bihaagre Ki Vaar, Guru Ram Das says that God is the real Giver and is the one who moves others to give.
The Kural explains a fool's glad giving by the receiver's merit, not the giver's virtue; Gurbani likewise traces any gift beyond the human giver, to God who causes others to give.
Greek Mythology
In Aristophanes’ comedy Plutus, the god of wealth is blind, which is why riches fall to people at random rather than to the deserving.
— Aristophanes, Plutus [10]
Wealth flows without discernment; who gets it is not decided by anyone’s wisdom.
The fool’s gift is similarly blind; its landing on someone reflects their luck.
Philosophy
Aristotle says the generous person gives to the right people, the right amounts and at the right time, whereas the prodigal gives without discrimination, often to those who should not receive.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 [11]
Real generosity needs practical wisdom; giving without it is not a virtue.
Valluvar likewise refuses to credit the fool’s gift as virtue; any benefit is the receiver’s good fortune.

Real Life Example

The Boston Red Sox sell Babe Ruth to the New York Yankees
26 December 1919 (announced January 1920) · Boston and New York, United States
In late December 1919 Boston Red Sox owner Harry Frazee, who was short of money, agreed to sell the contract of his star player Babe Ruth to the New York Yankees owners Jacob Ruppert and Tillinghast Huston. The Yankees paid 25,000 dollars in cash and three promissory notes, about 100,000 dollars in all, and the sale was announced in January 1920. Ruth went on to his greatest years with the Yankees.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sale is remembered as one of the most lopsided deals in sports history, transforming the Yankees and stripping Boston of its best player.
The kural says ironically that when a fool gives something away gladly, the cause lies in the good fortune of the receiver, and the Yankees owed their great prize to Frazee's willingness to part with it rather than to any skill of their own.
