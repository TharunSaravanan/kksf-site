Kural 413

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

செவியுணவின் கேள்வி உடையார் அவியுணவின் ஆன்றாரோடு ஒப்பர் நிலத்து.
Seviyunavin kelvi udaiyaar aviyunavin Aandraarodu oppar nilaththu.

Meaning

People who have gained knowledge by listening, which is food for the ears, are equal on earth to the gods, who are fed by offerings in sacred fires.

Elementary

In old times people offered food to the gods through a sacred fire. Valluvar says people who ‘eat’ wise words through their ears are like gods living on earth.

University

A carefully balanced pair of compounds: செவியுணவு (ear-food) is set against அவியுணவு (oblation-food, havis), so that ‘those who have hearing’ are matched with ‘the exalted ones who eat oblations’ — the devas. Parimelazhagar glosses ஆன்றார் as the celestials; நிலத்து (‘on earth’) marks the hearers as terrestrial counterparts of the gods. The logic continues the food metaphor of 412: just as the gods are sustained by subtle offerings rather than gross food, the learned are sustained by subtle instruction. The kural thus elevates the listener’s status, not merely the value of what is heard.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna says that even one who merely listens to this teaching with faith and without envy is freed from sin and attains the auspicious worlds of the righteous.
— Bhagavad Gita 18.71 [4]
The Gita closes by promising elevated status — the heavenly realms of the virtuous — not only to the one who studies but to the one who simply hears it faithfully.
Both texts raise the attentive hearer to a quasi-divine rank.
Christianity
“But blessed are your eyes, for they see: and your ears, for they hear. For verily I say unto you, That many prophets and righteous men have desired to see those things which ye see, and to hear those things which ye hear…”
— Matthew 13:16–17 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells his disciples that their hearing places them in a privileged position beyond that of prophets and the righteous of old.
Hearing confers exalted status — the Kural likens it to divinity, Jesus to a blessedness surpassing prophets.
Islam
The Prophet said that no people gather in a house of God reciting and studying the Book among themselves but that tranquillity descends on them, mercy covers them, the angels surround them, and God mentions them among those who are with Him.
— Sahih Muslim 2699 [6]
Those who gather to hear and study revelation are placed in the company of angels and in God’s own presence.
Like the Kural’s hearers ‘equal to the gods’, these listeners are drawn into the celestial company while still on earth.
Judaism
When Israel at Sinai said “we will do and we will hear” (naʿaseh ve-nishma), the Talmud relates that a heavenly voice asked, “Who revealed to My children this secret that the ministering angels use?” — and angels crowned each Israelite.
— Exodus 24:7; Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 88a [7]
By committing themselves to hearing, the people adopted the manner of angels and were honoured as such.
An almost exact parallel: devoted hearers are made equal to celestial beings on earth.
Buddhism
The Mangala Sutta lists among the ‘highest blessings’ much learning (bahusacca — literally ‘much hearing’) and hearing the Dhamma at the proper time (kālena dhammassavanaṃ); the discourse is spoken in reply to a deva, and its close says those who fulfil these blessings are everywhere victorious and go everywhere in safety.
— Sutta Nipāta 2.4 (Mangala Sutta) [8]
Hearing is counted among the supreme blessings discussed between the Buddha and the gods.
The Kural exalts the hearer to divine rank; the Mangala Sutta, addressed to gods, counts hearing among the highest blessings.
Jainism
“As Sakra the thousand-eyed, the wielder of the thunderbolt, the fortress-destroyer, the king of gods, (has no equal), neither has a very learned monk.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.23 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Uttaradhyayana's lecture on the very learned (bahussuya, literally 'one who has heard much') ranks such a monk as without equal, like Indra among the gods.
Valluvar says those rich in learning gained by hearing are equal on earth to the gods. The Jain text matches the 'much-heard' monk with the king of the gods.
Sikhism
“Listening-Shiva, Brahma and Indra.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Japji Sahib (pauri 9), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the ninth pauri of Japji Sahib Guru Nanak says that by listening, ordinary people rise to the standing of the highest beings, here named as Shiva, Brahma and Indra.
The Kural says those rich in hearing equal the gods on earth; Japji likewise says listening raises a person to the rank of the gods.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod describes the Muses singing on Olympus and delighting the great mind of Zeus with their song — the gods, who feast on nectar and ambrosia, also feast on what they hear.
— Hesiod, Theogony 36–52 [11]
In Greek myth, listening to song is itself one of the divine pleasures on Olympus.
Both imagine the gods as consumers of a subtle ‘food’; the Kural makes human listeners share that divine diet.
Philosophy
Pythagoras’ school admitted novices as akousmatikoi, ‘listeners’, who spent years in silence hearing the master’s sayings; the tradition held Pythagoras himself to be a divine or semi-divine figure whose hearers participated in his wisdom.
— Iamblichus, On the Pythagorean Life 72, 81–82 [12]
Listening was the defining activity of the first rank of Pythagorean disciples, and the community treated the teaching as quasi-divine.
The Pythagorean ‘hearers’ are an institutional version of the Kural’s honoured listeners, drawing near to the divine through the ear.

Real Life Example

UNESCO recognises the oral tradition of Vedic chanting
2008 · India
The Vedas, the oldest sacred texts of Hinduism, are classed as shruti, a Sanskrit word meaning what is heard, and were preserved for many centuries through memorisation and recitation before being written down. Their hymns and formulae were chanted at sacred rituals, including sacrifices, and reciters are trained from childhood in exact techniques of pitch accent, pronunciation and word combination so that the sound of every word stays unaltered. In 2008 UNESCO inscribed the Tradition of Vedic Chanting on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The recognition honours one of the world's oldest bodies of knowledge, handed down for thousands of years almost entirely by ear and regarded by its tradition as sacred.
The kural matches those who feed on the food of the ear with the gods who receive sacrificial oblations, and the Vedic tradition, learned by hearing and chanted at those very sacrifices, treats what is received through the ear as holy.
