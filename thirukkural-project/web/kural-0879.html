Kural 879

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

இளைதாக முள்மரம் கொல்க களையுநர் கைகொல்லும் காழ்த்த இடத்து.
Ilaidhaaka mulmaram kolka kalaiyunar kaikollum kaazhththa itaththu.

Meaning

Cut down a thorny tree while it is still young. Once it grows hard, it will hurt the hand of the person who tries to cut it.

Elementary

A tiny thorny weed in the garden can be pulled out with two fingers. If you leave it for a year, it grows big, tough thorns that scratch your hands when you finally try to pull it. Problems are the same: deal with them while they are small.

University

The couplet is a pure agricultural image applied to enmity, as all the commentators make explicit: iḷaitāka (‘while young’) muḷmaram (முள்மரம், ‘thorn tree’) kolka (‘destroy’); otherwise, kāḻtta iṭattu (‘when it has hardened’, from kāḻ, the hard heartwood) it kai kollum (‘destroys the hand’) of the kaḷaiyunar (களையுநர், ‘those who weed it out’). Mu. Varadarasanar keeps the literal image; Kalaignar draws the lesson that enmity must be put down before it matures; Sivayogi Sivakumar adds the explicit note that enmity must not be allowed to grow. Solomon Pappaiah reads the thorn tree as one who intends to destroy us. The verb kol- (‘kill’) appears in both lines, giving a pointed reversal: kill it, or it kills your hand. The text matches in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At Duryodhana’s birth, ill omens appeared, and Vidura and the Brahmins advised Dhritarashtra to abandon the child for the sake of the family; out of affection he refused, and the conflict that followed destroyed the Kuru line.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva (Sambhava Parva) [3]
The epic presents a danger recognised early and left to grow until it consumed everyone.
It is the Kural’s thorn tree left uncut, wounding the very hands that later tried to remove it.
Christianity
“Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death.”
— James 1:15 (KJV) [4]
James traces how a small inner impulse grows step by step into something deadly.
Both texts warn that what is small and manageable now becomes lethal once full grown.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not follow the footsteps of Satan.”
— Qur’an 24:21 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns against the first small steps of wrongdoing, which lead on to greater harm.
Like the Kural, it urges stopping a danger at its first stage.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that the evil inclination is at first like a spider’s thread, but in the end it becomes like the thick ropes of a wagon.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 52a [6]
What begins as something easily broken becomes, if left alone, something that cannot be broken.
This is the same image of early weakness and later strength as the Kural’s young and hardened thorn tree.
Buddhism
“Just as a tree, though cut down, sprouts up again if its roots remain uncut and firm, even so, until the craving that lies dormant is rooted out, suffering springs up again and again.”
— Dhammapada 338 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha uses a tree image to teach that a danger must be removed at the root or it returns.
The Kural also uses a tree to teach thorough, timely removal of what will harm you.
Jainism
“‘O Gautama, in the innermost heart there grows a plant which brings forth poisonous fruit; how have you torn it out?’ “I have thoroughly clipped that plant, and torn it out altogether with its roots; thus I have got rid of the poisonous fruit.” … “Love of existence is that dreadful plant which brings forth dreadful fruit; having regularly torn it out, I live pleasantly.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23.45–46, 48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In the dialogue between Keśi and Gautama, craving for existence is pictured as a poisonous plant growing in the heart, which the sage must pull out by the roots.
The Kural tells us to cut down the thorn tree while it is young, before it can wound the hand, and Gautama likewise speaks of uprooting a harmful growth completely before it bears its poisonous fruit.
Sikhism
“Fareed, the farmer plants acacia trees, and wishes for grapes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 23), Bhagat Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Farid uses the image of a farmer who sows thorny acacia yet hopes to harvest grapes, to show that what one lets take root decides what one later reaps.
The Kural uses a thorn tree to teach that enmity must be cut while young before it hardens; Farid's thorny acacia makes the related point that what is allowed to grow determines the harvest.
Greek Mythology
Before Paris was born, Hecuba dreamed she gave birth to a firebrand that set Troy ablaze; the seer warned of the danger, but the child survived, grew up, and his abduction of Helen brought about the city’s destruction.
— Apollodorus, Library 3.12.5 [10]
The myth shows a foretold danger not dealt with in time, which grows into the ruin of all.
It parallels the Kural’s warning that what is left to mature will destroy those who later try to handle it.
Philosophy
Machiavelli compares political troubles to a wasting fever, which at first is easy to cure but hard to detect, and later easy to detect but hard to cure; the Romans, he says, dealt with troubles as soon as they foresaw them.
— Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 3 [11]
A wise ruler acts on dangers early, while they are still small.
This is the Kural’s thorn tree turned into a theory of statecraft.

Real Life Example

Germany remilitarizes the Rhineland
7 March 1936 · Rhineland, Germany
On 7 March 1936 Hitler repudiated the Locarno agreements and ordered German troops into the Rhineland, which had been demilitarized under the post-war settlement. Britain and France condemned the move but did not intervene; after consulting the British, the French cabinet decided not to risk a confrontation. Germany then fortified the Rhine frontier, and the Imperial War Museum notes that the success encouraged Hitler to pursue an even more aggressive foreign policy.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Historians widely see the failure to act in 1936, while German forces were still weak, as a missed chance to stop a danger that later cost far more to remove.
The kural warns to cut down the thorn tree while it is young, and the Rhineland crisis is the classic case of a threat left to harden.
