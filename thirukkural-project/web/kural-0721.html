Kural 721

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

வகையறிந்து வல்லவை வாய்சோரார் சொல்லின் தொகையறிந்த தூய்மை யவர்.
Vakaiyarindhu vallavai vaaisoraar sollin Thokaiyarindha thooimai yavar.

Meaning

People with pure minds who know words well and understand their listeners will not stumble when they speak before a powerful group.

Elementary

Imagine you know every line of the school play, and you have peeked out to see who is in the audience. Because you know your words and you know who is listening, your voice stays steady. Knowing your words and knowing your listeners keeps you from getting tongue-tied.

University

This couplet opens Avaiyañcāmai (“not fearing the assembly”). It pairs with the chapter before it, Avaiyaṟital (knowing the assembly), inside the Amaicciyal, the section on ministers. Parimelazhagar reads vakai aṟintu (“knowing the kind”) as first working out what sort of assembly one is in. Vallavai is the assembly of the able or powerful, and vāy sōrār means “their mouths will not slip,” that is, they will not falter or make mistakes out of fear. Sollin tokai aṟinda tūymaiyavar are “the pure who know the tokai (range or classification) of words,” which commentators connect with a grammarian’s knowledge of word-classes and how to use them. So the chapter’s first answer to stage fright is skill combined with integrity. The sandhi-joined text reads “சொல்லின் றொகையறிந்த,” where றொ is simply ன் fused with தொ, and the words are unchanged.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking truthfully and beneficially and in avoiding speech that offends. One should also recite the Vedas regularly.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna describes a discipline of speech: words should be true and helpful, should not wound, and should rest on steady study of scripture.
The Gita joins pure speech to learning in the same way the Kural praises “the pure who know the range of words.”
Christianity
“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man.”
— Colossians 4:6 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks believers to speak graciously and with some bite, so that they can give each person a fitting answer.
Knowing how to answer “every man” is close to the Kural’s knowing what kind of assembly one faces.
Islam
“My Lord, expand for me my breast [with assurance] … And untie the knot from my tongue / That they may understand my speech.”
— Qur’an 20:25, 27-28 (Saheeh International) [5]
Before facing Pharaoh’s court, Moses asks God to give him confidence and to free his tongue so that his words will be understood.
This is a prayer for exactly what the Kural describes: not stumbling in speech before a powerful gathering.
Judaism
“A wise man does not speak before one who is greater than he in wisdom, And does not break into his fellow’s speech; And is not hasty to answer; He asks what is relevant, and he answers to the point”
— Pirkei Avot 5:7 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah lists the traits of a wise person in discussion: he respects rank, does not interrupt, does not rush, and speaks to the point.
Like the Kural, it says that well-ordered speech in learned company depends on knowing the setting and knowing how words work.
Buddhism
“The Realized One knows the right time to speak so as to explain what he knows to be true, correct, and beneficial, but which is disliked by others.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 58, Abhayarājakumāra Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha sorts speech by whether it is true, whether it helps, and whether it is welcome. He speaks only what is true and beneficial, and he chooses the right moment.
This amounts to knowing the kinds of words and the kind of occasion, which the Kural says keeps a speaker from faltering.
Jainism
“A monk should be modest though he be of a fearless mind; he should expound the Syâdvâda; he should use the two (permitted) kinds of speech, living among virtuous men, impartial and wise.”
— Sutrakritanga I.14.22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga's lecture on the Nirgrantha tells the teaching monk to pair a fearless mind with modesty, to speak in the qualified, many-sided manner of syadvada, and to use only the permitted kinds of speech.
The Kural's pure-minded speaker who knows the full range of words and does not stumble before a powerful assembly matches this monk, who is fearless yet modest and weighs every word he speaks among others.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else - says Nanak, listen, mind: call him spiritually wise.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 16), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur makes fearlessness, neither inspiring fear nor yielding to it, the mark of the truly wise (giani).
The Kural says those who know the full range of words will not falter before a powerful gathering; Guru Tegh Bahadur says true wisdom is shown precisely by not being afraid of anyone.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says the Muses pour sweet dew on the tongue of a king they favour. His words then flow gently, he speaks surely in the assembly, and he quickly settles even a great quarrel.
— Hesiod, Theogony 81-93 [10]
In Hesiod’s picture, a ruler who speaks well in public has been given the gift of the Muses, and the people look to him when he gives judgement.
Speaking steadily and without error before an assembly is the ideal the Kural describes.
Philosophy
Socrates argues that a true orator must know the different kinds of souls and the kinds of speech that suit each one, and must be able to see, in the moment, what sort of listener is in front of him.
— Plato, Phaedrus 271c-272a [11]
For Plato, the art of speaking rests on classifying both listeners and speeches and matching one to the other.
This is very close to vakai aṟintu, knowing the kind of assembly, joined with knowing the classes of words.

Real Life Example

Frederick Douglass delivers What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?
5 July 1852 · Corinthian Hall, Rochester, New York, United States
At the invitation of the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society, Frederick Douglass addressed an audience of five to six hundred people, each paying a small admission fee, at Corinthian Hall on 5 July 1852. He praised the signers of the Declaration of Independence as great men and then set out how the freedoms celebrated on Independence Day were denied to enslaved Americans. The address became the most celebrated of his speeches.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Douglass understood exactly the audience before him, people proud of the nation's founding, and built his argument on their own reverence for its ideals.
The kural says that those who know the full range of words and first take the measure of the assembly will not falter before it, and Douglass's speech is a model of reading an audience and speaking without faltering.
