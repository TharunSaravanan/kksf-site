Kural 847

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

அருமறை சோரும் அறிவிலான் செய்யும் பெருமிறை தானே தனக்கு.
Arumarai sorum arivilaan seyyum perumirai thaane thanakku.

Meaning

A fool who does not keep good advice, or lets an important secret slip, does great harm to themselves.

Elementary

If your coach gives you a secret play and you shout it across the field, or you just forget it because you weren’t listening, your own team loses. The poet says the fool who lets good advice slip away hurts himself most.

University

The crux is arumarai sorum (அருமறை சோரும்): arumarai is ‘a rare/precious marai’, where marai may mean a secret, a hidden counsel, or a sacred teaching, and sorum means ‘lets slip, lets go loose’. Mu. Varadarasanar follows the traditional reading: the fool fails to keep a precious secret in his mind and blurts it out. Pappaiah and Kalaignar instead read it as failing to accept or follow precious advice, Puliyur Kesigan as studying the rare scriptures yet growing slack and missing their true sense, and Sivayogi Sivakumar as abandoning rare insights. All converge on the second line: perumirai (பெருமிறை, great harm or affliction) is done by the fool thaane thanakku, ‘himself to himself’, echoing 843. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Udyoga Parva, Duryodhana repeatedly rejects the counsel of Vidura, Bhishma, Drona and finally Krishna himself to make peace with the Pandavas, and the war that follows destroys him and his house.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Ganguli) [3]
The Mahabharata presents Duryodhana as the archetype of the ruler who throws away wise advice.
Letting precious counsel slip brought on him a ruin no enemy could have engineered alone.
Christianity
“They would none of my counsel: they despised all my reproof. Therefore shall they eat of the fruit of their own way, and be filled with their own devices.”
— Proverbs 1:30–31 (KJV) [4]
Wisdom warns that those who refuse counsel will reap the results of their own choices.
The harm falls on the fool himself, just as the kural says.
Islam
“And they will say, "If only we had been listening or reasoning, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze.”
— Qur’an 67:10 (Sahih International) [5]
The damned confess that their ruin came from failing to listen and reflect.
Their great harm was self-made by letting the warning slip.
Judaism
King Rehoboam rejects the counsel of the elders who served his father Solomon to lighten the people’s burden and follows his young companions instead; the ten northern tribes break away from him.
— 1 Kings 12:1–19 (Tanakh) [6]
The narrative shows a ruler losing most of his kingdom by discarding wise advice.
Rehoboam’s loss was the perumirai he did to himself by letting good counsel go.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure.”
— Dhammapada 76 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares a wise adviser to a guide to buried treasure.
Valluvar’s fool lets that precious ‘hidden’ counsel slip, losing the treasure.
Jainism
“Wise, fearless monks consider even a rough instruction as a benefit, but the fools hate it, though it produces patience and purity of mind.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on discipline contrasts the wise pupil who values a teacher's correction with the fool who rejects the very counsel that would help him.
On the reading of the Kural in which the fool lets precious counsel slip and so harms himself, the Uttarādhyayana gives the same picture: the fool hates the instruction that would have made him patient and pure.
Sikhism
“Take the Guru’s advice, you ignorant fool; without devotion, even the clever have drowned.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 288, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib (ashtapadi 19, stanza 6), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan tells the ignorant to take the Guru's counsel, warning that even clever people sink without it.
The Kural says the fool who lets precious counsel slip does great harm to himself; Guru Arjan urges the fool to hold fast to the Guru's advice for the same reason.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus warns his son Icarus to fly a middle course, neither too low nor too near the sun; Icarus ignores the advice, the wax melts and he falls into the sea.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 [10]
Clear instructions are given and thrown away in a moment of thrill.
Icarus’ fall is harm done by himself to himself through neglected counsel.
Philosophy
“If any man is able to convince me and show me that I do not think or act right, I will gladly change; for I seek the truth by which no man was ever injured. But he is injured who abides in his error and ignorance.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.21 (Long) [11]
Marcus welcomes correction and notes that the person who refuses it harms himself.
The one who rejects counsel and stays in error is injured by his own hand, as the kural says.

Real Life Example

Russian armies send uncoded radio orders before Tannenberg
August 1914 · East Prussia (present-day northern Poland)
In August 1914 two Russian armies invaded East Prussia but transmitted many of their orders over open radio without encoding them. German forces intercepted the messages, learning the location and direction of march of the Russian Second Army and that the First Army was not coming to its aid. Using this knowledge, the German Eighth Army encircled and almost destroyed the Russian Second Army at the Battle of Tannenberg at the end of August.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Tannenberg became a classic lesson in the cost of failing to guard vital information, as the Russians handed their enemy the secret that decided the battle.
The kural says the fool who lets a precious secret slip does great harm to himself, and the Russian commands broadcast their own plans to the enemy who then defeated them.
