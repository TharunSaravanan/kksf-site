Kural 718

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

உணர்வ துடையார்முன் சொல்லல் வளர்வதன் பாத்தியுள் நீர்சொரிந் தற்று.
Unarva thudaiyaarmun sollal valarvadhan Paaththiyul neersorindh thatru.

Meaning

Speaking to people who can understand is like watering a garden bed where plants are already growing.

Elementary

Watering a plant that is already sprouting makes it shoot up fast. Watering an empty patch of dirt does nothing. Telling good ideas to someone who wants to learn is like watering the sprout.

University

This kural is the positive counterpart of the chapter’s closing image (720). Unarvadhu udaiyaar are people who have understanding, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah stress that they understand for themselves (thaame unargindra), even without being told. Speaking to them is like watering (neer sorindhatru) a paaththi, a small irrigated garden bed, in which the crop is valarvadhu, already growing of its own accord. The image is agricultural and exact: the water does not create growth but speeds up growth that is already under way, so the speaker’s words bear fruit at once. Parimelazhagar takes this as the assembly of equals in learning, and so completes the scheme begun in 714. The text agrees across sources; the sandhi text reads “உணர்வ துடையார்முற் சொல்லல்” and “பாத்தியு ணீர்சொரிந் தற்று”.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Mundaka Upanishad teaches that when a student has approached the teacher properly, with a calm mind and controlled senses, the wise teacher should fully impart to him the knowledge of Brahman, by which the imperishable is known.
— Mundaka Upanishad 1.2.13 [4]
Teaching is to be poured out fully on the prepared and receptive student.
The prepared student is the Kural’s growing crop, and the teacher’s words are the water that makes it flourish.
Christianity
“But he that received seed into the good ground is he that heareth the word, and understandeth it; which also beareth fruit, and bringeth forth, some an hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.”
— Matthew 13:23 (KJV) [5]
In the parable of the sower, the word bears fruit only in the hearer who understands, the good soil.
The same agricultural logic as the Kural: words bear fruit in understanding hearers.
Islam
“The example of guidance and knowledge with which Allah has sent me is like abundant rain falling on the earth, some of which was fertile soil that absorbed rain water and brought forth vegetation and grass in abundance.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 79 (narrated by Abu Musa) [6]
The Prophet compares his teaching to rain. Fertile ground absorbs it and flourishes, while barren ground gains nothing.
The receptive soil that turns rain into growth is the Kural’s bed of growing crops.
Judaism
The Mishnah rules that the most profound teachings, such as the Work of the Chariot, may be expounded only to a student who is wise and understands on his own.
— Mishnah Chagigah 2:1 (Sefaria, William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz edition) [7]
Rabbinic tradition keeps its deepest instruction for hearers who already have the understanding to take it further for themselves.
“Understands on his own” is almost exactly the Kural’s unarvadhu udaiyaar, those whose understanding already grows by itself.
Buddhism
In the parable of the plants, one great cloud rains equally on all the herbs, shrubs and trees of the earth. Each takes up the water according to its own nature and size and grows accordingly, and in the same way beings receive the Buddha’s single teaching according to their capacity.
— Saddharma-pundarika (Lotus Sutra), Chapter 5 ‘On Plants’, trans. H. Kern [8]
The Lotus Sutra pictures teaching as rain whose effect depends on how far each plant has grown.
The Kural’s water poured on growing crops is the Lotus Sutra’s rain on plants that are ready to take it in.
Jainism
“(The teacher) takes delight in instructing a clever (pupil), just as the rider (in managing) a well-broken horse; but he tires to instruct a foolish (pupil), just as the rider (tires to manage) an unbroken horse.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Uttaradhyayana says that teaching depends on the receiver: instruction given to a capable hearer is a pleasure and bears fruit, while instruction given to a dull one wears out the teacher.
This matches the Kural's point that speaking to those who already understand is like watering plants that are already growing.
Sikhism
“The Word is the tree; the garden of the heart is the farm; tend it, and irrigate it with the Lord's Love.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 354, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak pictures the Guru's word as a tree planted in the garden of a receptive heart and watered with love.
The Kural likens speaking to people who understand to watering a bed where plants are already growing; Guru Nanak likewise pictures the word flourishing in a watered, prepared heart.
Greek Mythology
Athena, disguised as Mentor, counsels the young Telemachus. Because he is ready to listen, her advice quickly takes root: he calls an assembly, sets sail to seek news of his father, and grows into a man fit to stand beside Odysseus.
— Homer, Odyssey Books 1-3 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
The goddess’s words fall on a mind already beginning to mature, and they produce rapid growth.
Telemachus is the growing crop, and Athena’s counsel is the water that makes it flourish.
Philosophy
Socrates says the true teacher, like a sensible farmer, chooses a fitting soul and plants in it words joined with knowledge. Such words can defend themselves and bear seed that grows again in other minds.
— Plato, Phaedrus 276b-277a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Plato contrasts this real cultivation of receptive minds with scattering words carelessly, as in writing or in pleasure-gardens that come to nothing.
Plato’s farmer sowing in a fit soul is the Kural’s watering of the growing bed.

Real Life Example

Einstein translates and extends Satyendra Nath Bose's paper
1924 · Dacca (now Dhaka, Bangladesh) and Berlin, Germany
In 1924 Satyendra Nath Bose, a physicist at Dacca University, wrote a short paper deriving Planck's radiation law without relying on classical physics; he sent it to Philosophical Magazine but heard nothing back. He then sent it to Albert Einstein, who received it in June 1924, recognised that it solved a problem he had struggled with, translated it into German himself and had it published in Zeitschrift fur Physik in August 1924. Einstein quickly extended Bose's method to material particles, leading to what became known as Bose-Einstein statistics and the prediction of Bose-Einstein condensation.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Bose's ideas, ignored by one journal, bore fruit almost at once when they reached a mind ready to understand and develop them.
The kural likens speaking to people of understanding to watering a garden bed where the plants are already growing, and Bose's work flourished the moment it reached Einstein.
