Kural 272

Inconsistent Conduct · Koodaavozhukkam · கூடாவொழுக்கம்

வானுயர் தோற்றம் எவன்செய்யும் தன்னெஞ்சம் தான்அறி குற்றப் படின்.
Vaanuyar thotram evanseyyum thannenjam Thaanari kutrap padin.

Meaning

What good is looking as holy as the sky is high, if your own heart knows the wrong you have done?

Elementary

Suppose you wear a shiny superhero costume, but inside you know you just took your friend’s toy without asking. The costume doesn’t make you a hero. What you know about yourself matters more than how you look.

University

வானுயர் தோற்றம் (“a sky-high appearance”) refers to the imposing garb and bearing of the ascetic, and Valluvar poses a rhetorical question: of what use is it? The criterion he sets is not public exposure but self-knowledge — தன்னெஞ்சம் தான் அறி குற்றம், the fault the heart itself is aware of. Valluvar thus locates moral judgement in conscience rather than reputation, a remarkably interior standard for an ancient ethical text. The kural parallels the Gita’s notion of mithyācāra (the pretender whose mind dwells on what his outward restraint pretends to renounce), and Stoic ideas of conscience as the one witness who cannot be deceived.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who restrains the senses and organs of action, but whose mind dwells on sense objects, certainly deludes himself and is called a pretender.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.6 [3]
Krishna says that someone who outwardly acts restrained but inwardly keeps craving is a mithyācāra — a fake.
Exactly the Kural’s case: impressive outer discipline is worthless when the heart knowingly clings to what it pretends to have given up.
Christianity
“For if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things.”
— 1 John 3:20 (KJV) [4]
John says that when our own heart accuses us, God — who knows even more than our heart — sees the whole truth.
Like the Kural, the decisive verdict comes from the heart’s own knowledge of its guilt, not from outward appearance.
Islam
“Rather, man, against himself, will be a witness, Even if he presents his excuses.”
— Qur’an 75:14–15 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says each person is a witness against himself and knows the truth about his own deeds, whatever excuses he offers.
Matches the Kural’s ‘fault the heart itself knows’: no outward show can override inner self-knowledge.
Judaism
“Forasmuch as this people draw near me with their mouth, and with their lips do honour me, but have removed their heart far from me…”
— Isaiah 29:13 (KJV) [4]
God complains through Isaiah that the people honour him outwardly with words while their hearts are elsewhere.
Same gap the Kural exposes — splendid outward devotion, empty or guilty heart — and the same verdict that the outward form is worthless.
Buddhism
“The evil-doer suffers here and hereafter; he suffers in both the worlds. The thought, ‘Evil have I done,’ torments him…”
— Dhammapada 17 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada says the wrongdoer is tormented by his own awareness of what he has done.
Supports the Kural’s focus on the self-known fault as what really counts, whatever the outward show.
Jainism
“One who for a long time wears a shaven crown and mortifies himself, but who is careless with regard to the vows, and neglects penance and self-control, will not be a winner in the battle (of life).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.41 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Mahavira's tradition warns that a monk may keep the outward marks of asceticism for years while inwardly neglecting the vows that make it real.
Valluvar asks what use a sky-high appearance of holiness is when the heart knows its own faults; this verse makes the same point about a shaven head worn over a careless inner life.
Sikhism
“Mentally, we are impure and black, but outwardly, we appear white.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 85, Vaar Sri Raag (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok of Vaar Sri Raag Guru Nanak speaks in the first person for all of us, admitting that our minds are impure and black while we look white and clean outside.
The Kural asks what use a sky-high holy appearance is when one's own heart knows its faults; Guru Nanak's confession is exactly that inner knowledge of the gap between how we look and what we are.
Greek Mythology
After killing his mother, Orestes is hounded by the Furies (Erinyes) — spirits of guilt and vengeance whom he cannot escape even at Apollo’s shrine, until he is tried before Athena’s court.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides [9]
Orestes’ protected status at a holy place cannot silence the pursuit of his guilt.
Like the Kural, sacred surroundings and status do not neutralise a known wrong; guilt follows the doer.
Philosophy
“If your deeds are honourable, let everybody know them; if base, what matters it that no one knows them, as long as you yourself know them? How wretched you are if you despise such a witness!”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 43.5 (tr. Gummere) [10]
Seneca tells Lucilius that hiding a shameful act is pointless, because you yourself are always a witness to it.
Almost Valluvar’s exact point: reputation is irrelevant when the self knows its own fault.

Real Life Example

Rousseau, author of a famous book on education, gives up his own children
1740s-1762 · Paris, France
By his own account, Jean-Jacques Rousseau and his partner Therese Levasseur had five children, and each was left at the Paris foundling hospital shortly after birth. In 1762 Rousseau published Emile, or On Education, which became one of the most influential works ever written on bringing up children. His abandonment of his children was later used against him by Voltaire, and in his Confessions Rousseau acknowledged and tried to justify what he had done.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The gap between Rousseau's celebrated public teaching and his private conduct became one of the best-known examples of a moral authority undercut by what he knew of himself.
The kural asks what use a sky-high appearance of virtue is when the heart knows its own faults, and Rousseau's own Confessions record the fault behind his lofty reputation.
