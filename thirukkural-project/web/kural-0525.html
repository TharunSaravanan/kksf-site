Kural 525

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

கொடுத்தலும் இன்சொலும் ஆற்றின் அடுக்கிய சுற்றத்தால் சுற்றப் படும்.
Kotuththalum insolum aatrin atukkiya Sutraththaal sutrap patum.

Meaning

A person who gives freely and speaks kindly will be surrounded by relatives, circle after circle of them.

Elementary

If you share your crayons and also say nice things to people, everyone wants to be near you. Then their brothers and sisters come too, and soon there’s a big happy circle around you. Sharing plus kind words is like a magnet for family.

University

Parimelazhagar notes that the verb ஆற்றின் (‘if one is able to accomplish’) stresses how hard it is to practise both virtues in due measure. He explains அடுக்கிய சுற்றம் as kin ‘stacked’ in succession: the kin of one’s kin, and then their kin, bound in a widening chain. He explicitly identifies the two means with the Sanskrit statecraft terms dāna (gift) and sāma (conciliation), the first two of the four upāyas, so this kural brings the Arthaśāstra toolkit into the domestic setting of kinship. Kurals 525-528, he says, set out the ‘means of embracing kin’. இன்சொல் (‘sweet speech’) links back to Chapter 10, இனியவை கூறல், and it anticipates the Buddhist saṅgahavatthu of giving and kind speech.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Straw (for a seat), ground (for sitting upon), water (to wash the feet and face), and, fourthly sweet words,--these are never wanting in the houses of the good.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 36 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura says that the houses of the good always have simple gifts for guests together with kind words.
Giving together with sweet speech is the Kural’s formula for drawing people close.
Christianity
“Be kindly affectioned one to another with brotherly love; in honour preferring one another; ... Distributing to the necessity of saints; given to hospitality.”
— Romans 12:10, 13 (KJV) [4]
Paul joins brotherly affection and courtesy with material generosity toward the community.
Warmth together with giving is how the community holds together, much as the Kural says.
Islam
“and to parents do good and to relatives, orphans, and the needy. And speak to people good [words] and establish prayer and give zakah.”
— Qur’an 2:83 (Sahih International) [5]
The covenant verse joins material kindness to relatives with the command to speak good words.
This is the Kural’s pair of giving and kind speech in one sentence, with relatives named.
Judaism
“Anyone who gives a peruta to a poor person receives six blessings, and whoever consoles him with words of comfort and encouragement receives eleven blessings.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 9b (William Davidson Edition) [6]
Rabbi Yitzhak ranks kind, comforting words above even the coin that is given.
The Kural too joins the gift to the sweet word and treats both as essential.
Buddhism
“There are these four grounds for the bonds of fellowship. Which four? Generosity, kind words, beneficial help, consistency.”
— Sangaha Sutta, AN 4.32 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha lists the saṅgahavatthu, the qualities that hold a family or community together, and the first two are giving and kind speech.
This is almost the Kural’s own formula: generosity and kind words bind kin around you.
Jainism
“if he does not share with others; if he is always unkind: then he is called ill-behaved.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira closes his list of the faults of the ill-behaved monk with two failures: not sharing and unkindness.
The Kural names generosity and kind speech as what draws circle after circle of kin; the Jain text names their opposites, not sharing and unkindness, as the marks of the man who has failed.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak names sweetness and humility as the very core of every good quality.
The Kural says kin gather in circle after circle round one who gives freely and speaks kindly; Guru Nanak places that sweetness at the heart of virtue.
Greek Mythology
Before the war with the Titans, Zeus frees the Hundred-Handers, who are children of Earth like the gods themselves, gives them nectar and ambrosia and speaks to them respectfully to ask for their help. Won over, they fight on his side and secure his victory.
— Hesiod, Theogony 617-663 [10]
Zeus wins over powerful relatives by combining gifts with courteous words.
Giving together with kind speech gathers kin as allies, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
Seneca insists that the manner of giving matters as much as the gift: a benefit given readily, with a kind face and gracious words, binds the receiver, while one given grudgingly or with harsh words loses its value.
— Seneca, De Beneficiis, Books I-II [11]
For Seneca, generosity and courteous speech together are what make a gift create lasting goodwill.
The Kural too requires both the gift and the sweet word.

Real Life Example

Moshoeshoe gathers scattered peoples into the Basotho nation
1820s-1830s · Thaba Bosiu, Lesotho
During the wars and migrations that convulsed Southern Africa in the early nineteenth century, the chief Moshoeshoe led his people to the nearly impregnable mountain stronghold of Thaba Bosiu. His following grew as other African peoples were drawn by the protection he could provide, and he had a reputation for generous treatment of allies and enemies alike. He united these groups of refugees into the Sotho (Basotho) nation, and he is regarded today as the father of Lesotho.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Moshoeshoe built a nation not from a single bloodline but by drawing ever more peoples into his circle through protection and generous dealing.
The kural says one who can both give freely and deal kindly will be surrounded by kin, circle upon circle, and Moshoeshoe's generosity turned scattered refugees into one people.
