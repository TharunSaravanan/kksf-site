Kural 180

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

இறலீனும் எண்ணாது வெஃகின் விறல்ஈனும் வேண்டாமை என்னுஞ் செருக்கு.
Iraleenum ennaadhu vehkin viraleenum Vendaamai ennum serukku.

Meaning

Wanting others’ things without thinking about the result leads to ruin. The great strength of not wanting them leads to victory.

Elementary

Grabbing for something that isn’t yours without thinking is like reaching into a fire: you get burned. But being strong enough to say “I don’t need that” is like winning a race. This kural says not wanting is a kind of superpower.

University

The chapter closes with a perfectly balanced antithesis built on a rhyme: இறல் ஈனும் (iral eenum, ‘gives birth to destruction’) against விறல் ஈனும் (viral eenum, ‘gives birth to victory’). The verb ஈன் (een, ‘to bear, as offspring’) makes both outcomes the natural children of their causes. எண்ணாது (ennaadhu, ‘without reflecting’) identifies thoughtlessness about consequences as the root of covetousness, while வேண்டாமை என்னும் செருக்கு (vendaamai ennum serukku) calls non-desire a kind of செருக்கு (serukku, ‘pride, exaltation, greatness’). Valluvar reclaims a word usually negative and makes it the dignity of the person who needs nothing from others. The idea that restraint is itself a victory appears in Epictetus, in the Qur’anic promise of success to those saved from their own avarice, and in the Buddhist ‘craving-freed’ who vanquishes all suffering.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell-lust, anger, and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna names greed (lobha) as one of three gates to self-destruction and says every sensible person should abandon it.
Matches the destructive half of the Kural (iral eenum): unreflective greed gives birth to ruin.
Christianity
“But godliness with contentment is great gain.”
— 1 Timothy 6:6 (KJV) [4]
Paul says the real profit in life is godliness combined with contentment.
The positive half of the Kural: not wanting is itself a gain and a victory. The surrounding verses (6:9-10) supply the destructive half.
Islam
“…and find not any want in their breasts of what the emigrants were given but give [them] preference over themselves, even though they are in privation. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul - it is those who will be the successful.”
— Qur’an 59:9 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises the people of Madinah who felt no craving for what others received, and says those protected from their own greed (shuhh) are the successful ones.
Close to the Kural’s viral eenum: freedom from inner craving is what yields success and victory.
Judaism
“He that hasteth to be rich hath an evil eye, and considereth not that poverty shall come upon him.”
— Proverbs 28:22 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says someone in a greedy rush to get rich doesn’t stop to consider that it will bring him to ruin.
The Kural’s ennaadhu (without thinking) is Proverbs’ ‘considereth not’; both say thoughtless greed leads to ruin.
Buddhism
“The gift of Dhamma excels all gifts; the taste of the Dhamma excels all tastes; the delight in Dhamma excels all delights. The Craving-Freed vanquishes all suffering.”
— Dhammapada 354 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says the person free of craving conquers all suffering.
Victory through non-desire: the Buddhist version of the Kural’s viral eenum vendaamai.
Jainism
“Though a man should conquer thousands and thousands of valiant (foes), greater will be his victory if he conquers nobody but himself.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.34 (cf. 9.36) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
Asked by Indra to subdue rival princes, King Nami replies that victory over one's own self, including the greed he goes on to name, is greater than any outward conquest.
Valluvar ends by saying that the proud strength of not wanting gives birth to victory, and Nami's teaching names that very self-mastery the greatest victory.
Sikhism
“Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir, Salok 155 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok in the Guru Granth Sahib, Bhagat Kabir sets greed against forgiveness: one leads to spiritual death, the other is where God dwells.
The Kural's closing antithesis says coveting gives birth to destruction while the strength of not wanting gives birth to victory; Kabir draws the same contrast between greed and death on one side and the divine presence on the other.
Greek Mythology
“…as often happens when gain deceives men’s sense and dishonour tramples down honour, the gods soon blot him out and make that man’s house low, and wealth attends him only for a little time.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 320-326 (tr. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod warns that a man who seizes wealth when greed clouds his judgment is soon destroyed by the gods, and the wealth doesn’t last.
The Kural’s iral eenum ennaadhu vehkin: coveting without thought brings destruction.
Philosophy
“…bring to your mind both points of time: that in which you will enjoy the pleasure, and that in which you will repent and reproach yourself after you have enjoyed it; and set before you, in opposition to these, how you will be glad and applaud yourself if you abstain … how much better it is to be conscious of having gained so great a victory.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 34 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus advises weighing what comes after giving in to a desire against the joy of holding back, and calls holding back a great victory.
Both halves of the Kural: think of the consequences (the opposite of ennaadhu), and see that abstaining is a victory (viral).

Real Life Example

The Athenian expedition to Sicily ends in disaster
415-413 BCE · Syracuse, Sicily
In 415 BCE Athens, answering an appeal from Segesta and hoping to extend its power over Sicily, sent a great fleet and army against Syracuse, over the objections of the general Nicias. Syracuse, reinforced by Sparta and Corinth, held out, and in 413 BCE the Athenians lost the decisive naval battle in the harbour of Syracuse. The retreating army surrendered, the generals Nicias and Demosthenes were executed, and Thucydides records that at least 7,000 prisoners were taken.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Thucydides presents the expedition as an overreach by a city that wanted more than it needed, and its failure was a turning point toward Athens' defeat in the Peloponnesian War.
The kural says coveting without weighing the consequences gives birth to destruction, and Athens' reach for Sicily ended in the loss of its fleet and army.
