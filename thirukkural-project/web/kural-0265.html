Kural 265

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

வேண்டிய வேண்டியாங் கெய்தலால் செய்தவம் ஈண்டு முயலப் படும்.
Vendiya vendiyaangu eydhalaal seydhavam Eendu muyalap padum.

Meaning

Strict self-control lets a person get whatever they want, just as they want it. So it should be worked at with effort, here and now.

Elementary

If you practise the piano every day, one day you can play any song you want, just the way you want. Valluvar says tapas is like that for your whole life — so start practising now, not later.

University

The verse gives the motivational logic of tapas: it is effective, delivering desired ends ‘as desired’ (வேண்டியாங்கு). ஈண்டு (‘here’) is read by Parimelazhagar as ‘in this birth’, urging urgency, while Mu. Varadarasan reads it as ‘even in the householder’s state’, democratizing tapas beyond the forest. The claim sits in tension with the Gita’s ideal of desireless action and with Valluvar’s own later chapter on the destruction of desire (அவா அறுத்தல்); commentators resolve this by noting that for the truly austere the highest ‘desire’ is liberation itself. The couplet thus captures tapas at its most instrumental while quietly pointing beyond instrumentality.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The child Dhruva, rejected by his father, performs severe tapas in the forest of Madhuvana; Vishnu appears and grants him a kingdom and ultimately the eternal pole-star position.
— Bhagavata Purana 4.8-9 [3]
The Bhagavata Purana’s Dhruva story is the paradigm of tapas yielding exactly what the practitioner sought, and more.
Embodies ‘obtaining what one desires, as one desires’ through tapas.
Christianity
“But thou, when thou fastest, anoint thine head, and wash thy face; That thou appear not unto men to fast, but unto thy Father which is in secret: and thy Father, which seeth in secret, shall reward thee openly.”
— Matthew 6:17-18 (KJV) [4]
Jesus assures that sincere, hidden fasting will be rewarded by God.
Both texts affirm that real austerity produces real results; Jesus stresses secrecy.
Islam
“Allah said, ‘All the deeds of Adam’s sons (people) are for them, except fasting which is for Me, and I will give the reward for it.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1904 (Hadith Qudsi) [5]
Fasting is singled out as the act whose reward God personally bestows, beyond ordinary measure.
Affirms the unique efficacy of ascetic discipline that motivates the Kural’s call to pursue it.
Judaism
“Then said he unto me, Fear not, Daniel: for from the first day that thou didst set thine heart to understand, and to chasten thyself before thy God, thy words were heard, and I am come for thy words.”
— Daniel 10:12 (KJV) [4]
After three weeks of fasting and mourning, Daniel is told his self-affliction secured an answer from heaven.
Self-chastening directly obtains what was sought — the logic of this Kural.
Buddhism
In his first sermon the Buddha rejects both indulgence in sense pleasures and devotion to self-mortification as extremes, teaching the Middle Way after his own years of extreme austerity failed to bring awakening.
— Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11) [6]
Buddhism explicitly denies that harsh bodily austerity reliably delivers the highest goal.
An instructive contrast: Buddhism shares the goal-orientation but disputes that severe tapas is the means.
Jainism
“As a large tank, when its supply of water has been stopped, gradually dries up by the consumption of the water and by evaporation, so the Karman of a monk, which he acquired in millions of births, is annihilated by austerities, if there is no influx of bad Karman.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.5-6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Jain doctrine holds that tapas, once new karmic inflow is stopped, burns away accumulated karma (nirjarā), and so is the decisive means to liberation.
Valluvar urges penance now because it obtains what one desires; the Jain text also presents tapas as supremely effective, but its goal is the shedding of karma rather than the fulfilment of wishes.
Sikhism
“In the remembrance of God, hopes are fulfilled.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 263, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Sukhmani Guru Arjan lists the fruits of simran, meditative remembrance of God, and among them says that a person's hopes are fulfilled.
Valluvar urges penance now because it gives whatever one desires; Guru Arjan makes the same promise for remembering God.
Greek Mythology
“But between us and Goodness the gods have placed the sweat of our brows; long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the first.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 289-291 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod teaches that excellence and prosperity are gained only through hard toil, which becomes easy once the summit is reached.
Shares the Kural’s conviction that strenuous discipline is the reliable road to what one seeks.
Philosophy
“For of all things good and fair, the gods give nothing to man without toil and effort.”
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.28 (trans. E. C. Marchant) [10]
In Prodicus’ fable of Heracles at the crossroads, Virtue tells the young hero that every good thing is earned by labour.
Same principle as the Kural: disciplined effort is the price and the guarantee of every desired good.

Real Life Example

Charles Goodyear discovers vulcanization after years of hardship
1839 (patent 1844) · United States
Charles Goodyear spent years experimenting to make rubber stable in heat and cold, during which his family lived in poverty and he was jailed for debt. In 1839 he found that rubber mixed with sulfur and heated became strong and stable, the process later called vulcanization, and he patented it in 1844. Others made fortunes from the process, and Goodyear himself died in debt.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Goodyear's discovery transformed industry, and his story is often told as an example of single-minded persistence through hardship.
The kural urges people to take up disciplined effort now because it delivers what one seeks, and Goodyear's years of privation did deliver the exact result he had been pursuing.
