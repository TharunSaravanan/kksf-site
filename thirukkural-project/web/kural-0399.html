Kural 399

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

தாமின் புறுவது உலகின் புறக்கண்டு காமுறுவர் கற்றறிந் தார்.
Thaaminburuvadhu ulaginburak kandu Kaamuruvar katrarindhaar.

Meaning

When learned people see that the learning that brings them joy also brings joy to the whole world, they love learning even more.

Elementary

When you discover a great story and tell your friends, they love it too, and that makes you want to find even more stories. Learning works like that. Sharing it makes everyone happy, so you want to learn more.

University

The kural describes a self-reinforcing cycle of joy. The learned find pleasure (இன்புறுவது) in their own knowledge, then see that the same knowledge brings pleasure to the world (உலகு இன்புற). That discovery makes them desire (காமுறுவர்) learning all the more. Valluvar's use of kaamam, a word more often tied to love and desire, is striking: learning becomes a passion. The kural suggests that the highest motive for study is neither prestige nor profit but shared delight, which makes knowledge a social good. The idea is echoed in Seneca's statement that he learns only in order to teach, and in the Mishnah and hadith traditions that praise those who learn in order to share.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Gita describes the liberated sages as sarva-bhūta-hite ratāḥ, those who delight in the welfare of all beings, and says such people attain liberation.
— Bhagavad Gita 5.25 [3]
Krishna portrays the truly wise as people whose own inner fulfilment overflows into joyful work for the good of all living beings.
Like the Kural, it links the joy of the wise with the joy of the world. The Gita's focus is spiritual realization more than learning as such.
Christianity
“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”
— Matthew 5:16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his disciples not to hide the light they have received, but to let it shine for the benefit of all.
Like the Kural, it treats what one has received as something to share with the world. The Gospel does not describe the learner's growing desire for learning.
Islam
“The best among you (Muslims) are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5027 [5]
The Prophet joins learning and teaching into a single excellence. The highest rank goes to those who both acquire knowledge and pass it on.
Like the Kural, it makes learning and delighting others with it inseparable parts of one noble activity.
Judaism
“He who learns in order to teach, it is granted to him to study and to teach.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:5 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Ishmael teaches that someone who studies with the aim of sharing knowledge is rewarded with further opportunities to learn and to teach.
A good match for the Kural's cycle: sharing learning leads to more learning.
Buddhism
“The gift of Dhamma excels all gifts; the taste of the Dhamma excels all tastes; the delight in Dhamma excels all delights.”
— Dhammapada 354 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha calls the sharing of true teaching the greatest gift and delight in the Dhamma the greatest delight.
Like the Kural, it joins personal delight in wisdom with giving wisdom to others.
Jainism
“Therefore, seeker after the highest truth, study the sacred lore, in order to cause yourself and others to attain perfection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The lecture on 'the very learned' ends by saying that learning is pursued for one's own good and for the good of others alike. What perfects the learner should also benefit the world.
Valluvar says the learned love learning all the more when they see that it delights the world as well as themselves. The Jain verse likewise ties one's own study to the good it brings to others.
Sikhism
“Contemplate and reflect upon knowledge, and you will become a benefactor to others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 356, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that knowledge truly reflected upon makes a person a benefactor of others (paropkari).
The Kural says the learned love learning more when they see it delight the whole world; Guru Nanak likewise sees the proof of real learning in the good it does for others.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod sings of the Muses, whose songs gladden the great mind of Zeus. When a person loved by the Muses sings, people weighed down with grief forget their sorrows.
— Hesiod, Theogony 36–52, 96–103 [10]
In Greek myth, the gifts of the Muses (poetry, song and learning) delight both gods and humans and relieve the world's sorrow.
Like the Kural, it shows learning and art spreading joy outward to the whole world.
Philosophy
Seneca tells Lucilius that he is glad to learn so that he may teach. No knowledge, however excellent, would please him if he had to keep it to himself, and he would refuse wisdom itself if it came on condition of never sharing it.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 6.4 [11]
For Seneca, the joy of learning cannot be separated from the joy of sharing it. Knowledge kept to oneself loses its pleasure.
An excellent match: the learner's delight grows through the delight of others, which is the Kural's exact cycle.

Real Life Example

Rabindranath Tagore founds Visva-Bharati
1921 · Santiniketan, West Bengal, India
Rabindranath Tagore, who had won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913, started an experimental school at Santiniketan in 1901. In 1921 he expanded it into Visva-Bharati, a world university whose stated aim was a meeting of the cultures of East and West. The institution became a central university of India in 1951, and Santiniketan was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Tagore turned his own delight in literature, music and art into an institution meant to share that delight with students from across the world.
The kural says the learned love learning all the more when they see it delight the world, and Tagore built a university to spread to the world the learning that delighted him.
