Kural 925

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

கையறி யாமை உடைத்தே பொருள்கொடுத்து மெய்யறி யாமை கொளல்.
Kaiyari yaamai udaiththe porulkoduththu meyyari yaamai kolal.

Meaning

Paying money just to buy a state where you no longer know yourself shows that you do not understand what you are doing.

Elementary

Imagine spending all your pocket money on a ticket for a ride that makes you so dizzy you can't remember your own name, and then trips you over. You'd be paying to lose yourself. The Kural says buying liquor is that kind of bad deal.

University

The couplet balances two forms of ignorance: kaiyariyaamai (கையறியாமை) and meyyariyaamai (மெய்யறியாமை). The second clearly means not knowing one's own body or self, the stupor that porul koduththu (பொருள்கொடுத்து, 'by giving wealth') one kolal (கொளல், 'takes on'). The first is read in two ways. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah take it as not knowing what to do, a failure of practical sense. Puliyur Kesikan (thirukkural.net) reads it as having one's past karma as its cause, so only an ill-fated person would make such a bargain. Kalaignar calls it folly beyond words. The Kural's irony is economic: wealth, the subject of Porutpaal, is spent to buy unawareness. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“From anger, delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost, one falls down again into the material pool.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.63 (Prabhupada trans.) [3]
Krishna traces how a clouded mind loses memory and then understanding.
The drinker in the Kural pays for the same sequence of delusion, lost self-awareness and downfall.
Christianity
“Be not among winebibbers; among riotous eaters of flesh: For the drunkard and the glutton shall come to poverty: and drowsiness shall clothe a man with rags.”
— Proverbs 23:20-21 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that heavy drinking leads to stupor and poverty.
The bad bargain is the same: money goes out, and stupor and ruin come back.
Islam
“They ask you about wine and gambling. Say, ‘In them is great sin and [yet, some] benefit for people. But their sin is greater than their benefit.’”
— Qur'an 2:219 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an weighs wine's small gain against its far greater harm.
Like the Kural, it judges drinking a losing bargain.
Judaism
“Rabbi Dosa ben Harkinas said: morning sleep, midday wine, children's talk and sitting in the assemblies of the ignorant put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:10 (Kulp trans., Sefaria) [6]
Wine in the middle of the day is listed among the habits that waste a person's life.
As in the Kural, drinking is something a person chooses that takes him out of his own life and purpose.
Buddhism
The fifth lay precept is to refrain from surā-meraya-majja, intoxicants that are a ground for pamāda, heedlessness.
— Khuddakapatha 2 (Dasa Sikkhapada / five precepts) [7]
Buddhist ethics defines intoxicants by what they do: they produce heedlessness.
Pamāda is the Kural's meyyariyaamai, not knowing oneself.
Jainism
“Wine deludes the mind and a deluded person tends to forget piety; the person who forgets piety commits hiṃsā without hesitation.”
— Amṛtacandra, Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya v. 62 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2012), as quoted in the explanatory note to Ratnakaraṇḍaka-śrāvakācāra v. 66 on wisdomlib.org [8]
Amṛtacandra explains that wine clouds the mind, a clouded mind forgets dharma, and a person who has forgotten dharma harms living beings without a second thought.
Valluvar mocks the man who pays good money to buy a state in which he no longer knows himself; Amṛtacandra traces what that self-forgetting leads to, from the clouded mind to forgotten dharma and then to violence.
Sikhism
“One person brings a full bottle, and another fills his cup. Drinking the wine, his intelligence departs, and madness enters his mind; he cannot distinguish between his own and others”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 554, Raag Bihaagraa, Bihaagre ki Vaar, Salok, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das describes the drinking party itself: one brings the bottle, another pours, and the drink takes away the drinker’s mind until he no longer knows his own from what is someone else’s. It also makes him forget his Lord.
The Kural mocks the one who pays to buy a state where he no longer knows himself, and Guru Amar Das describes that same self-loss in detail.
Greek Mythology
Circe gives Odysseus' men a mixture with Pramnian wine laced with drugs; they forget their homeland and are turned into swine.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 10 [10]
The men eagerly take a drink that robs them of memory and of their human selves.
They take a drink and receive self-forgetting in return, the Kural's meyyariyaamai.
Philosophy
“penalties are doubled in the case of drunkenness; for the moving principle is in the man himself, since he had the power of not getting drunk and his getting drunk was the cause of his ignorance.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.5 (W. D. Ross trans.) [11]
Aristotle holds that ignorance a person brings on himself through drink does not excuse him; it makes him more blameworthy.
This is the Kural's argument exactly: whoever pays for his own ignorance chose his folly.

Real Life Example

Hogarth's Gin Lane and the Gin Act end London's Gin Craze
1751 · St Giles, London, England
In the first half of the eighteenth century cheap, unlicensed gin became so widely sold in London that by 1723 Londoners were estimated to drink on average a pint a week, and people addicted to it spent their wages on drink and sold or pawned their possessions to buy more. In 1751 William Hogarth published Gin Lane, set in St Giles, where a sign over a gin cellar advertised that a customer could be drunk for a penny and dead drunk for twopence, and where the pawnbroker, distiller and undertaker thrive while the poor decay. The print supported the Gin Act of 1751, which restricted who could sell gin and raised its cost, after which gin drinking declined.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Gin Craze is a famous case of a whole city paying away its earnings and goods to buy oblivion.
The kural mocks those who pay good money to buy a state in which they no longer know themselves, and the gin sellers of St Giles literally priced unconsciousness at a penny or twopence.
