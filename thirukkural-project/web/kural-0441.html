Kural 441

Seeking the Aid of Great Men · Periyaaraith Thunaikkodal · பெரியாரைத் துணைக்கோடல்

அறனறிந்து மூத்த அறிவுடையார் கேண்மை திறனறிந்து தேர்ந்து கொளல்.
Aranarindhu mootha arivudaiyaar kenmai Thiranarindhu therndhu kolal.

Meaning

Think carefully and find the right way to win the friendship of older, wise people who know dharma (the right way to live).

Elementary

Some people know what is right and have learned a lot from long experience. This kural says: find people like that, think carefully about who they really are, and then work to make them your friends and helpers.

University

The chapter opens by fixing three qualifications for the ‘great’ (periyar): they know aram (virtue/duty), they are ‘mootha’ (ripened, whether in age or in understanding), and they possess arivu (knowledge). The verb structure is significant: ‘thiran arindhu’ (knowing the method) and ‘therndhu’ (having examined) both precede ‘kolal’ (taking). Friendship with the wise is not an accident of proximity but a deliberate, tested acquisition — a theme Valluvar develops later in the chapters on friendship (79–83). Parimelazhagar notes that this counsel is addressed first to the king, for whom advisers are an instrument of rule, but it generalises readily to anyone: one’s judgment is only as good as the judgment of those one keeps close.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized souls can impart knowledge unto you because they have seen the truth.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 [4]
Krishna tells Arjuna that real knowledge comes by deliberately approaching those who have already realized it, and by serving and questioning them with humility.
Both texts say wisdom is gained by consciously seeking out and cultivating those who are already wise — and both specify a method (‘thiran arindhu’ / humble inquiry and service).
Christianity
“He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed.”
— Proverbs 13:20 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs teaches that we absorb the character of those we walk with: stay close to wise people and you grow wise; keep company with fools and you share their ruin.
Same premise as the Kural — deliberately choosing wise companions is how one gains wisdom and safety.
Islam
“The example of a good companion (who sits with you) in comparison with a bad one, is like that of the musk seller and the blacksmith’s bellows (or furnace); from the first you would either buy musk or enjoy its good smell while the bellows would either burn your clothes or your house, or you get a bad nasty smell thereof.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2101 (narrated by Abu Musa) [6]
The Prophet compares a good companion to a perfume seller — you benefit just by being near him — and a bad companion to a blacksmith’s furnace, which harms you even if you never touch it.
Implies the same discernment the Kural demands: choose one’s companions carefully, because their quality rubs off.
Judaism
“Appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion and judge all men with the scale weighted in his favor.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:6 [7]
Joshua ben Perahiah tells each person to actively make someone their teacher (asei lekha rav) and to acquire (k’neh) a companion — both are things you must go out and get.
Like the Kural’s ‘therndhu kolal’ (choose and take), the Mishnah treats a wise mentor as something one must deliberately acquire.
Buddhism
“Not to associate with the foolish, but to associate with the wise; and to honor those who are worthy of honor — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Mangala Sutta (Khp 5 / Sn 2.4), tr. Narada Thera [8]
Among the Buddha’s list of the highest blessings, the very first is to avoid fools, keep company with the wise, and honor those who deserve honor.
Matches the Kural’s instruction to seek and honor the virtuous and wise as companions.
Jainism
“This is the road to it: to serve the Gurus and the old (teachers), to avoid throughout foolish people, to apply oneself earnestly to study, and to ponder zealously on the meaning of the Sûtras.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira's discourse on the causes of carelessness names service of the gurus and the elders as the first step on the road to liberation, paired with avoiding fools.
Valluvar asks the king to weigh carefully and then win the friendship of those mature in wisdom and virtue. The Jain text likewise puts deliberate attachment to the old and learned at the head of the right path.
Sikhism
“How is the Society of the Saints to be known? There, the Name of the One Lord is chanted.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 72, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak gives a test for recognising true company (satsangat): it is the gathering where the One Name is praised.
The Kural tells one to learn the right way, weigh carefully, and then win the friendship of the wise; Guru Nanak likewise gives a criterion for discerning which company is truly worth joining.
Greek Mythology
In Odyssey Book 2, the goddess Athena takes the form and voice of Mentor, an old and trusted friend of Odysseus, to guide the young Telemachus as he sets out to act like a man and a prince.
— Homer, Odyssey 2 [11]
Telemachus succeeds because he accepts guidance from an elder of proven loyalty and wisdom — so famous an image that ‘mentor’ became the word for a wise guide.
Shows the Kural’s ideal: a young ruler-to-be strengthened by an aged, virtuous and knowing counselor.
Philosophy
“Associate with those who will make a better man of you. Welcome those whom you yourself can improve. The process is mutual; for men learn while they teach.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 7 (tr. Gummere) [12]
Seneca advises Lucilius to be selective about company and to seek those whose influence raises one’s own character.
Same deliberate selection of improving companions that the Kural commends.

Real Life Example

Liu Bei wins Zhuge Liang as his adviser
c. 207 · Longzhong, near Xiangyang, Hubei, China
Liu Bei, at the time a minor warlord without a secure base, sought out the scholar Zhuge Liang, who was living in retirement at Longzhong, and persuaded him to enter his service as an adviser. Zhuge Liang helped him organise a large army, urged an alliance with Sun Quan that led to victory over Cao Cao at the Red Cliffs in 208, and helped him found the Shu-Han state in 221. On his deathbed Liu Bei urged his son to rely on Zhuge Liang's advice.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Liu Bei's rise from a wandering commander to an emperor rested heavily on the wise counsellor he had deliberately sought and kept.
The kural advises learning the right way to win, and then securing, the friendship of the wise and mature, and Liu Bei's pursuit of Zhuge Liang became the classic Chinese example of doing so.
