Kural 389

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

செவிகைப்பச் சொற்பொறுக்கும் பண்புடை வேந்தன் கவிகைக்கீழ்த் தங்கும் உலகு.
Sevikaippach sorporukkum panbudai vendhan Kavigaikkeezhth thangum ulagu.

Meaning

The whole world will live safely under the royal umbrella (the sign of his rule) of a king with the good character to put up with words that are bitter to hear.

Elementary

Good medicine often tastes bad, but it makes you better. A wise king listens even when someone tells him an unpleasant truth. Because of that, everyone feels safe living under his rule.

University

Sevi kaippa — ‘so that the ear tastes bitterness’ — is a vivid synaesthetic image for criticism that is unpleasant to hear. The commentators specify that these are the admonitions of wise elders and ministers, whose duty (developed in the later chapter on ‘Seeking the Help of the Great’) is to rebuke the king when needed. The kavigai (royal parasol) is the symbol of sovereign protection, so the kural’s claim is that tolerance of criticism is not a weakness but the condition under which the whole world will rest securely under the ruler’s shade. Vidura’s warning in the Mahabharata that speakers and hearers of unpleasant but healing words are rare, and Marcus Aurelius’ readiness to be corrected, express the same insight.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“They are abundant, O king, that can always speak agreeable words. The speaker, however, is rare, as also the hearer, of words that are disagreeable but medicinal.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva 37 (Vidura Niti), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Vidura tells King Dhritarashtra that flatterers are everywhere, but people who speak or accept bitter-but-healing truth are rare.
‘Disagreeable but medicinal’ words are exactly the Kural’s ear-bittering speech that a great king must bear.
Christianity
“Reprove not a scorner, lest he hate thee: rebuke a wise man, and he will love thee.”
— Proverbs 9:8 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says a fool hates correction, but a wise person loves the one who corrects him.
The wise ruler of the Kural is the ‘wise man’ who welcomes rebuke rather than resenting it.
Islam
“The best fighting (jihad) in the path of Allah is (to speak) a word of justice to an oppressive ruler.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4344 [5]
The Prophet said one of the greatest acts of striving is to tell a truth to an unjust ruler’s face.
Presents the other side of the Kural’s relationship: speaking hard truth to power is a sacred duty, which the good king must bear.
Judaism
The prophet Nathan confronts King David with the parable of the poor man’s lamb and declares “Thou art the man”; David, rather than punishing him, answers “I have sinned against the LORD.
— 2 Samuel 12:1-13 (KJV) [4]
When the prophet Nathan boldly accused King David of a terrible wrong, David accepted the rebuke instead of getting angry.
A king who bears words bitter to the ear — the exact virtue this kural praises.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure.”
— Dhammapada 76 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says someone who points out your faults is as valuable as a guide to buried treasure.
Reproof as treasure — the reason the Kural’s king should bear bitter words.
Jainism
“A man who returns, as it were, to the vomit, is not praised; but you want to confiscate the property left by the Brâhmaṇa.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.38, 14.53 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Queen Kamalavati sharply rebukes King Ishukara for planning to seize an heirless Brahmin's estate. The king takes this bitter criticism to heart: he renounces his kingdom, and the narrator reports that 'the king and the queen … they all reached perfection.'
Valluvar promises that the world will shelter under the umbrella of the king who can bear words bitter to the ear. Ishukara is a Jain example of such a king, who accepts a stinging reproof and is raised by it.
Sikhism
“The kings are tigers, and their officials are dogs; they go out and awaken the sleeping people to harass them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1288, Raag Malaar (Vaar Malaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Vaar Malaar Guru Nanak speaks a famously bitter truth about the rulers of his day, comparing kings to tigers and their officials to dogs that prey on ordinary people.
The Kural praises the king gracious enough to bear words that are bitter to his ears; Guru Nanak's verse is an example of the bitter, unwelcome truth that a good ruler ought to be able to hear.
Greek Mythology
Nestor tells Agamemnon that because Zeus has given him the sceptre and the right to judge, it is his duty above all to speak and also to listen, and to act on another man’s good counsel — urging him to repair his quarrel with Achilles.
— Homer, Iliad 9.96-113 (tr. A. T. Murray) [9]
The wise old Nestor reminds King Agamemnon that a king must listen to advice, even advice he does not want to hear.
The duty of a sceptred king to hear hard counsel is the Kural’s central point.
Philosophy
“If any man is able to convince me and show me that I do not think or act right, I will gladly change. For I seek the truth by which no man was ever injured.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.21 (tr. George Long) [10]
The Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius writes that he will gladly change if anyone shows him he is wrong.
A ruling emperor embracing correction — the Kural’s king who bears bitter words.

Real Life Example

Frederick Douglass confronts Abraham Lincoln about Black soldiers
10 August 1863 · The White House, Washington, D.C., United States
The abolitionist Frederick Douglass, who had been recruiting Black soldiers for the Union Army, went to the White House on 10 August 1863 and told President Lincoln he could no longer recruit because Black soldiers received less pay than white soldiers, had no chance of promotion and were not protected when captured. Lincoln received him, listened seriously and explained his reasons, and Douglass later recalled that the president was serious and even troubled by what he had said. Although not all his demands were met, Douglass came away with lasting respect for Lincoln's openness.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
A president in the middle of a civil war gave his full attention to blunt criticism from a man born into slavery, and the meeting began a relationship of mutual respect.
The kural praises a ruler gracious enough to bear words that are bitter to hear, and Lincoln received Douglass's sharp complaints with patience and seriousness.
