Kural 1206

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

மற்றியான் என்னுளேன் மன்னோ அவரொடியான் உற்றநாள் உள்ள உளேன்.
Matriyaan ennulen manno avarodiyaan utranaal ulla ulen.

Meaning

What else keeps me alive? I live only by remembering the happy days I spent with him.

Elementary

When someone you love is far away, you might look at photos of happy times together to feel better. The lady in this poem says remembering their happy days is what keeps her going, like food that keeps her strong.

University

The couplet makes memory the very means of survival. Avaroṭu yāṉ uṟṟa nāḷ are ‘the days when I was joined with him’; uḷḷa, ‘by recalling’, I live (uḷēṉ). The first line’s maṟṟu yāṉ eṉṉ uḷēṉ, ‘otherwise, by what do I live?’, carries the particles maṉ and ō, which the commentators treat as expletives adding pathos. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss: ‘It is by thinking of the days I was with him that I am alive; by what else?’ Pope renders: ‘How live I yet? I live to ponder o’er / The days of bliss with him that are no more.’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Held captive in Lanka, Sita endures her grief by fixing her mind on Rama, and when Hanuman finds her, she says she will keep her life only a little longer in the hope of seeing him again.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (Sita’s laments and her words to Hanuman) (paraphrase) [3]
Sita’s life in captivity is sustained by remembrance of and hope for her husband.
Sita is the classic Indian image of the Kural’s wife who lives only by thinking of her absent husband.
Christianity
“I thank my God upon every remembrance of you.”
— Philippians 1:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells his far-off friends that each time he remembers them he gives thanks.
Remembrance of loved ones sustains Paul in prison as it sustains the Kural’s wife in separation.
Islam
Verses attributed to Majnun say that he passes by the dwellings of Layla and kisses this wall and that wall, not because he loves the houses, but for love of the one who once lived in them.
— Attributed to Qays ibn al-Mulawwah (Majnun Layla), Diwan (paraphrase) [5]
Majnun keeps his love alive by clinging to every trace of the absent Layla.
Like the Kural’s wife living on memories of days together, Majnun lives on the traces of his beloved’s past presence.
Judaism
“I remember the days of old; I meditate on all Thy doing; I muse on the work of Thy hands.”
— Psalm 143:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
In distress, the psalmist sustains himself by recalling earlier days of God’s nearness.
Like the Kural’s wife, the psalmist survives a time of absence by remembering days of closeness.
Buddhism
A man whose only son has died goes daily to the cemetery crying ‘Where are you, my only child?’, and the Buddha tells him that sorrow, lamentation and despair are born from those who are dear.
— Majjhima Nikaya 87, Piyajatika Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Piyajatika Sutta shows memory of a lost loved one becoming the man’s whole life, and diagnoses it as the source of grief.
The Kural’s wife lives on memories; the Buddha warns that such dependence on the dear is what makes grief.
Jainism
In the Jain Ramayana, Sita, carried off to Lanka, refuses Ravana and resolves not to take food until she receives news of Rama, holding fast to him in thought.
— Vimalasuri, Paumacariya (Jain Ramayana), Sita in Lanka (paraphrase) [8]
The Jain retelling keeps Sita’s steadfast inner attachment to her husband as the strength that sustains her in captivity.
Sita lives only by her bond with the absent Rama, as the Kural’s wife lives only by memory of their days together.
Sikhism
“She weeps, thinking of her Husband Lord, and she cherishes His virtues; her Husband Lord does not die, and does not leave.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 583, Raag Vadhans, Alaahanee, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Alaahanee (songs of mourning) in Raag Vadhans, Guru Amar Das describes the soul-bride who keeps her Husband Lord alive in her heart by remembering His virtues.
The Kural's wife says she stays alive only by remembering the days she spent with him; the soul-bride in Gurbani likewise lives by remembering her Beloved's virtues, with tears.
Greek Mythology
Penelope, hearing the bard sing of the Greeks’ return from Troy, asks him to stop, because she always remembers with longing so dear a head, her husband, whose fame is spread throughout Greece.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.337-344 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Penelope’s twenty years of waiting are sustained by constant remembrance of Odysseus.
Penelope lives, as the Kural’s wife does, by never ceasing to remember the husband who is away.
Philosophy
Laelius, mourning his friend Scipio, says that Scipio still lives for him, that the memory of their friendship and shared life sustains him, and that of all fortune’s gifts nothing compares with that friendship.
— Cicero, On Friendship (Laelius de Amicitia) 27.102-104 (paraphrase) [11]
Cicero’s Laelius finds in remembered companionship the strength to go on.
Laelius lives on memories of days with Scipio as the Kural’s wife lives on memories of days with her husband.

Real Life Example

Li Qingzhao's Afterword remembers her life with Zhao Mingcheng
c. 1132–1134 · Song dynasty China
The poet Li Qingzhao married Zhao Mingcheng in 1101, and together they built a great collection of inscriptions, books and art, living for about ten years in Qingzhou. Zhao fell ill on the way to a new post and died in 1129. Li completed his Record of Metal and Stone and wrote an afterword recalling their life, including their game of guessing where a passage appeared in a book, when she laughed so hard that her tea spilled.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Widowed, uprooted by war and having lost much of the collection, Li held on to her married life through memory and set it down in one of China's most moving prose pieces.
The kural's speaker says she lives only by remembering the days she spent with her husband, and Li Qingzhao's Afterword is that remembering put into writing.
