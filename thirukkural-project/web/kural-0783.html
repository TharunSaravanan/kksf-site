Kural 783

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

நவில்தொறும் நூல்நயம் போலும் பயில்தொறும் பண்புடை யாளர் தொடர்பு.
Navildhorum noolnayam polum payildhorum panpudai yaalar thodarpu.

Meaning

Friendship with good people is like a great book. The more often you go back to it, the more you enjoy it.

Elementary

Do you have a favourite book you have read many times? Each time, you notice something new and like it even more. Being friends with a kind, good person is like that book: every time you spend time together, the friendship gets sweeter.

University

The couplet sets two verbs of repetition side by side: navil-thoRum (நவில்தொறும், ‘each time it is studied or recited’) and payil-thoRum (பயில்தொறும், ‘each time one associates’). Nūl-nayam (நூல்நயம்) is the excellence or beauty of a learned text, which reveals new depths with every reading; paṇbudaiyāḷar (பண்புடையாளர்) are people of refined character, and thodarpu (தொடர்பு) is their companionship. Parimelazhagar’s point is that good company, unlike pleasures that tire with use, grows sweeter with each meeting, just as a good book does. The comparison fits the Kural’s own high regard for study (chapters 40-42). ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net give the same text; பண்புடை யாளர் is a metrical split of பண்புடையாளர்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The thoughts of My pure devotees dwell in Me, their lives are fully devoted to My service, and they derive great satisfaction and bliss from always enlightening one another and conversing about Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 10.9 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna describes devotees who find lasting joy in teaching one another and talking together about the divine.
As in the Kural, the company of the good is a kind of shared study that keeps giving more delight.
Christianity
“Ointment and perfume rejoice the heart: so doth the sweetness of a man’s friend by hearty counsel.”
— Proverbs 27:9 (KJV) [4]
The proverb compares a friend’s sincere counsel to perfume that gladdens the heart.
Both texts say the company of a good friend keeps bringing fresh sweetness.
Islam
“The example of a good pious companion and an evil one is that of a person carrying musk and another blowing a pair of bellows. The one who is carrying musk will either give you some perfume as a present, or you will buy some from him, or you will get a good smell from him”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5534 [5]
The Prophet compares a good companion to a musk-seller: you always come away with something good, even if only the fragrance.
Like the Kural’s good book, every meeting with a good companion leaves you richer.
Judaism
When Honi the Circle-Drawer woke after seventy years, the scholars in the study hall did not believe who he was and gave him no respect, so he prayed for death; Rava explains the episode by the folk saying that it is either friendship or death.
— Babylonian Talmud, Taanit 23a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
For the rabbis, the companionship of fellow learners was so necessary that a scholar without it was better off dead.
The Talmud and the Kural both picture the best friendship as the company of the learned, which feeds the mind as study does.
Buddhism
“Do not associate with evil companions; do not seek the fellowship of the vile. Associate with the good friends; seek the fellowship of noble men.”
— Dhammapada 78 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada tells the reader to choose the company of the noble and avoid the vile.
Its ‘noble men’ are the Kural’s paṇbudaiyāḷar, whose company is worth seeking out.
Jainism
“The enjoyment of the friendship of men of acute understanding is desirable as the joys of heaven.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 24 'Improper Friendship', stanza 3 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar compares the company of discerning friends to heavenly happiness, and in the next sentence calls the company of the unlearned a hell.
The Kural says the friendship of people of good character gets sweeter each time, like a fine book; the Naladiyar likewise counts the company of the wise among life's highest delights and ties it to learning.
Sikhism
“Kabir! The sandalwood tree is good, even though it is surrounded by weeds. Those who dwell near the sandalwood tree, become just like the sandalwood tree.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1365, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 11) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir uses the sandalwood tree to show how the company of the good gradually passes on its fragrance to those who keep near it.
The Kural says the company of people of fine character gives more delight the longer one keeps it, like a great book; Kabir says those who stay close to the good slowly take on their fragrance.
Greek Mythology
When Agamemnon’s envoys reach Achilles’ tent, they find him playing the lyre and singing the deeds of heroes, with Patroclus sitting opposite in silence, waiting for him to finish.
— Homer, Iliad 9.185-191 [10]
Homer shows two friends whose quiet, everyday companionship is pleasure enough.
This is the long, ever-deepening companionship of two noble friends that the Kural compares to rereading a good book.
Philosophy
“Perfect friendship is the friendship of men who are good, and alike in virtue; for these wish well alike to each other qua good, and they are good themselves.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle ranks above all other kinds the friendship between good people who love each other for their character.
Aristotle’s perfect friends are the Kural’s people of fine character, whose bond keeps growing because it rests on lasting virtue.

Real Life Example

The Inklings read their work aloud in Oxford
1933-1949 · Oxford, England, United Kingdom
From about 1933, C. S. Lewis, J. R. R. Tolkien and a circle of Oxford friends met weekly, usually on Thursday evenings in Lewis's rooms at Magdalen College, to read aloud and criticise one another's writing in progress. The group heard and discussed early drafts of Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings and Lewis's The Great Divorce, and its members also gathered at the Eagle and Child pub. The Thursday meetings continued until the end of the 1940s.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Out of these repeated meetings came both lasting friendships and some of the most widely read books of the twentieth century.
Valluvar says friendship with people of fine character is like a great book that gives more delight each time one returns to it, and for the Inklings friendship and the rereading of books were the same weekly pleasure.
