Kural 735

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

பல்குழுவும் பாழ்செய்யும் உட்பகையும் வேந்தலைக்கும் கொல்குறும்பும் இல்லது நாடு.
Palkuzhuvum paazhseyyum utpakaiyum vendhalaikkum kolkurumpum illadhu naadu.

Meaning

A true country has no groups fighting each other, no enemies inside who bring ruin, and no killer gangs that trouble the king.

Elementary

A sports team can have the best players, but if they split into little groups that fight each other, and some even help the other side, they will lose. A country is the same: it needs its people to pull together.

University

Where kural 734 dealt with dangers from outside, this one turns to dangers from within. Palkuzhu is ‘many groups’, which Parimelazhagar reads as factions that quarrel among themselves, paazhseyyum utpakai is ‘internal enmity that works ruin’, and vendhalaikkum kolkurumpu is ‘murderous petty chiefs (or bands) that harass the king’; kurumpu can mean a small hill-chief or a gang of marauders. Solomon Pappaiah modernises the list as caste, religious and political factions, disloyal party members, and local strongmen. The theme is developed fully in chapter 89 (Utpakai, Internal Enmity). The ytamizh text prints the last word as ‘இல்லத’, which appears to be a typographical slip for the standard ‘இல்லது’ found in the sandhi text and other editions.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Protect thyself, O king, from all fears that arise from within. Fears, however, that arise from outside are of little consequence. The first kind of fear, O king, may cut thy roots in a single day.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section 107 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Bhishma tells Yudhishthira that quarrels and disunion inside a kingdom are far more dangerous than foreign enemies.
Bhishma and Valluvar agree that internal enmity is the danger that can destroy a land from its roots.
Christianity
“Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house divided against itself shall not stand.”
— Matthew 12:25 (KJV) [4]
Answering critics, Jesus points out that any kingdom torn by internal division will fall.
‘Brought to desolation’ is close to the Kural’s paazhseyyum, ‘working ruin’: division inside a land destroys it.
Islam
“And obey Allah and His Messenger, and do not dispute and [thus] lose courage and [then] your strength would depart; and be patient. Indeed, Allah is with the patient.”
— Qur’an 8:46 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns the believers that quarrelling among themselves will drain away their courage and strength.
The strength that ‘departs’ through dispute is what the Kural’s land keeps by being free of factions.
Judaism
The Talmud asks why the Second Temple was destroyed when the people of that time studied Torah, kept the commandments and did acts of kindness. It answers that there was baseless hatred among them, and teaches that baseless hatred weighs as much as idolatry, forbidden relations and bloodshed together.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 9b [6]
The rabbis locate the fall of Jerusalem in hatred among Jews themselves, not only in the Roman armies.
Yoma’s teaching is a historical example of the Kural’s ‘internal enmity that works ruin’.
Buddhism
“What have you heard, Ananda: do the Vajjis assemble and disperse peacefully and attend to their affairs in concord?” ... “So long, Ananda, as this is the case, the growth of the Vajjis is to be expected, not their decline.”
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahaparinibbana Sutta 1.4 (trans. Sister Vajira and Francis Story) [7]
When King Ajatasattu plans to attack the Vajjis, the Buddha says they cannot be defeated as long as they meet often and act in harmony.
The Buddha’s point is the Kural’s: a people without factions cannot easily be destroyed.
Jainism
“Punishing thieves and robbers, cut-purses and burglars, you should establish public safety; thus you will be a Kshattriya.” Nami answered: “Men frequently apply punishment wrongly: the innocent are put in prison, and the perpetrator of the crime is set at liberty.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.28 and 9.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Indra, testing the renouncing King Nami, urges him to secure public safety by punishing criminals, and Nami replies that royal punishment often goes astray.
The Kural wants a land free of internal enemies and murderous gangs; the Jain dialogue accepts that ideal of public safety, voiced by Indra, while doubting that a king's punishments can achieve it justly.
Sikhism
“Now, the Merciful Lord has issued His Command. Let no one chase after and attack anyone else. Let all abide in peace, under this Benevolent Rule.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 74, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan proclaims the halemi raj, a gentle rule under God's command in which no one harasses or preys on another and everyone lives at ease.
The Kural's true country is free of quarrelling factions, ruinous internal enemies and gangs that trouble the realm; Guru Arjan's benevolent rule is a land where no one attacks anyone else.
Greek Mythology
At the end of the Oresteia, Athena calls on the Furies, now called the Kindly Ones, to bless Athens, and the chorus prays that civil strife (stasis) may never roar in the city and that citizens may never shed each other’s blood in revenge.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides 976-987 [10]
The trilogy ends by turning the spirits of vengeance into guardians against civil war.
The prayer against stasis is the Greek form of the Kural’s wish for a land free of faction and murderous internal enemies.
Philosophy
“For indeed any city, however small, is in fact divided into two, one the city of the poor, the other of the rich; these are at war with one another; and in either there are many smaller divisions.”
— Plato, Republic IV, 422e-423a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates says most cities are not truly one city but many hostile factions, which is why they are weak.
Plato’s ‘many cities’ within one city is exactly the Kural’s palkuzhu, the many factions a real land must be free of.

Real Life Example

The Gunpowder Plot is discovered
4 to 5 November 1605 · Palace of Westminster, London, England
A group of conspirators led by Robert Catesby planned to blow up the House of Lords during the State Opening of Parliament, killing King James I, his family, his chief ministers and the members present. An anonymous letter warned Lord Monteagle to stay away from Parliament, and he passed it to the government. On the night of 4 November 1605 a search of the cellars beneath the House of Lords found Guy Fawkes with 36 barrels of gunpowder, and the plot collapsed.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The plot showed how a small band of internal enemies could threaten to destroy a kingdom's entire leadership in a single stroke.
The kural says a true country is free of internal enemies who work ruin and of murderous bands that trouble the ruler, and the Gunpowder Plot is one of history's best-known examples of exactly such a danger.
