Kural 1112

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

மலர்காணின் மையாத்தி நெஞ்சே இவள்கண் பலர்காணும் பூவொக்கும் என்று.
Malarkaanin maiyaaththi nenjae ivalkan palarkaanum poovokkum endru.

Meaning

My heart, whenever you see flowers you get confused and think her eyes are like them. But flowers can be seen by anyone, and her eyes are not like that.

Elementary

Suppose your best friend has a special smile. Whenever you see a smiley sticker, you think, 'That looks like my friend!' and get excited. Then you laugh at yourself: a sticker anyone can buy is nothing like the real smile.

University

The hero addresses his own nenju (நெஞ்சு, 'heart'), a common device in Tamil love poetry, and scolds it for its confusion (மையாத்தல், maiyaaththal, 'to be bewildered'). The flowers in question are taken by the commentators as kuvalai (குவளை, blue water-lily), the standard comparison for dark eyes; Puliyur Kesikan names the kuvalai explicitly. The sting is in palar kaanum (பலர் காணும், 'seen by many'): common flowers that every passer-by may look at cannot resemble eyes that he alone beholds, which Solomon Pappaiah brings out ('the eyes of my wife whom I alone see'); Kalaignar instead reads it as 'her eyes are a flower that many marvel at', a minority interpretation. Parimelazhagar’s tradition takes the verse as the hero rejecting the conventional comparison as unworthy. Pope: 'You deemed, as you saw the flowers, her eyes were as flowers, my soul, / That many may see.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The lotus over your ear is so near your eye, and so like it, that I was confused.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
Dushyanta, asked to blow pollen from Shakuntala’s eye, excuses his slowness by saying the lotus she wears is so like her eye that he could not tell them apart.
Both lovers are bewildered by flowers that resemble the beloved’s eyes, though Kalidasa lets the likeness stand while the Kural denies it.
Christianity
“The flowers appear on the earth; the time of the singing of birds is come, and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land;”
— Song of Solomon 2:12 (KJV) [4]
The beloved calls his love to come out into a spring world full of flowers.
For the lover in both poems the flowering world keeps turning his thoughts to the beloved.
Islam
The Qur’an tells believing men to lower their gaze and guard their modesty, saying this is purer for them.
— Qur’an 24:30 (paraphrase; cf. Sahih International) [5]
Islamic ethics asks the eye not to wander after beauty, because the look itself can mislead the heart.
The Kural’s lover finds his heart misled by every flower that reminds him of her eyes; Islam would counsel restraint of the gaze itself.
Judaism
“My beloved is mine, and I am his, That feedeth among the lilies.”
— Song of Songs 2:16 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The maiden of the Song pictures her beloved among the lilies, so that the flowers and the lover belong to one picture.
As in the Kural, the beloved and the flowers are bound together in the lover’s mind.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that perception can be distorted (vipallasa): the untrained mind sees the permanent in the impermanent and the beautiful in what is not, and so is led astray.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.49, Vipallasa Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhism treats the mind’s quick leap from a sight to a pleasant fantasy as a distortion to be recognised.
The Kural’s lover catches his own heart making a false likeness between common flowers and her eyes, a small case of the distortion the Buddha describes.
Jainism
“If you fall in love with every woman you see, you will be without hold like the Hatha-plant, driven before the wind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The nun Rajimati warns the monk Rathanemi that a heart that runs after every attractive sight has no anchor.
The Kural’s lover also finds his heart swept away by every flower that reminds him of her eyes; Jainism sees that unsteadiness as the danger.
Sikhism
“I can see no other as great as You; O Lord of Wisdom, You are pleasing to my mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 305, Raag Gauri, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gauri, Guru Ram Das tells the Lord that nothing else he sees can stand beside Him, and that He alone delights his mind.
The Kural's lover tells his heart that common flowers, which anyone may look at, are no match for his beloved's eyes; Guru Ram Das also refuses to set anything beside the One he loves.
Greek Mythology
After Apollo’s beloved Hyacinthus is killed by a discus, the god makes a flower spring from his blood and marks its petals with his grief, so that the flower forever recalls the youth.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.162-219 (paraphrase) [10]
In Ovid’s telling the flower becomes a living reminder of the one Apollo loved.
In both poems a flower calls up the beloved in the lover’s mind, though the Kural’s lover insists no flower is truly like her.
Philosophy
David Hume argues that beauty is not a quality in things themselves but exists in the mind that contemplates them, so that each mind may perceive a different beauty.
— David Hume, 'Of the Standard of Taste' (1757) (paraphrase) [11]
For Hume, judgments of beauty depend on the sentiment of the observer.
The Kural’s lover shows this at work: the flowers others admire seem to him nothing beside the eyes his own mind treasures.

Real Life Example

Shakespeare's Sonnet 130 rejects the usual comparisons
1609 (published) · London, England
Shakespeare's sonnets were first printed in 1609 in a quarto published by Thomas Thorpe. Sonnet 130 begins by saying that the poet's mistress's eyes are nothing like the sun, and goes on to deny that her lips are coral red or her cheeks like roses. In its closing couplet the poet says his love is as rare as any woman who has been praised with false comparisons.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The sonnet is famous for poking fun at the stock flattery of love poetry while still declaring real love.
The kural scolds the lover's heart for imagining that common flowers anyone can see resemble his beloved's eyes, and Sonnet 130 likewise refuses the stock comparisons for a mistress's eyes, insisting that she is beyond such ordinary likenesses.
