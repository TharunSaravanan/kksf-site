Kural 794

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

குடிப்பிறந்து தன்கண் பழிநாணு வானைக் கொடுத்தும் கொளல்வேண்டும் நட்பு.
Kudippirandhu thankan pazhinaanu vaanaik koduththum kolalvendum natpu.

Meaning

Make friends with someone from a good family who feels ashamed of doing wrong, even if it costs you something.

Elementary

Some friends are like a good umbrella: worth saving your pocket money for. A friend who comes from a caring home and feels bad if he does something wrong is that kind of friend, so it is worth giving up something to have him.

University

Kudippirandhu (குடிப்பிறந்து) means ‘born into a good family’, the kudi of chapter 96 (Kudimai), and pazhi naanuvaan (பழிநாணுவான்) is ‘one who feels shame at blame’, that is, someone whose sense of honour keeps him from disgraceful acts. Koduththum (கொடுத்தும், ‘even by giving’) carries the force of ‘even at a price’. Parimelazhagar takes it literally as giving wealth to secure such a friend, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah likewise say ‘even by paying a price’, while Kalaignar softens it to ‘by any means’. After three couplets of warning, the chapter turns here to the positive: some friendships are worth paying for. The ytamizh and thirukkural.net texts agree; the sandhi-joined edition reads தன்கட் and கொடுத்துங் … வேண்டு, orthographic variants only.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man’s descent may be discerned by his conduct, his country by his pronunciation of language, his friendship by his warmth and glow”
— Chanakya Niti 3.2 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya says that good birth shows itself in conduct, and true friendship in warmth.
Both link good family to visible good conduct; the Kural adds that such a person is worth acquiring as a friend.
Christianity
“A faithful friend is a strong defence: and he that hath found him, hath found a treasure. Nothing can be compared to a faithful friend, and no weight of gold and silver is able to countervail the goodness of his fidelity.”
— Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) 6:14-15, Douay-Rheims [5]
Ben Sira says a loyal friend is worth more than any amount of gold or silver. (Sirach is deuterocanonical, accepted by Catholic and Orthodox but not Protestant Christians.)
Ben Sira and Valluvar both say a good friend is worth any price.
Islam
“O you who have believed, fear Allah and be with those who are true.”
— Qur’an 9:119 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse tells believers to keep company with the truthful.
It shares the Kural’s instruction to actively seek out people of integrity as companions.
Judaism
“Joshua ben Perahiah used to say: appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion and judge all men with the scale weighted in his favor.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:6 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
The Hebrew verb here, k’neh, means ‘acquire’ or ‘buy’, and the rabbinic tradition (for example Avot de-Rabbi Natan) reads it as telling a person to obtain a good companion even at personal cost.
This is the closest Jewish parallel to koduththum: a true companion is worth buying.
Buddhism
“Monks, a friend endowed with seven qualities is worth associating with. Which seven? He gives what is hard to give. He does what is hard to do. He endures what is hard to endure. He reveals his secrets to you. He keeps your secrets. When misfortunes strike, he doesn’t abandon you. When you’re down & out, he doesn’t look down on you.”
— Mitta Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 7.35 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha lists seven qualities that make a person worth cultivating as a friend.
The sutta and the Kural agree that a friend of proven character is worth going out of your way to keep.
Jainism
“Therefore, though the friendship of those who, like the channel which fructifies the rice-field, be ever so far away, we must nevertheless go to obtain it.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 8 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar says the friendship of truly helpful people is worth going to great lengths for, however far away they are.
The Kural says the friendship of a well-born person who fears blame is worth securing even at a price, and the Naladiyar likewise counts the right friend worth whatever effort it takes to win them.
Sikhism
“The sight of them banishes my evil-mindedness; they are my only true friends. I have searched the whole world; O servant Nanak! How rare are such persons!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 520, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan says the true friend is the one whose very presence drives out one's evil-mindedness, and such friends are so rare that he had to search the whole world for them.
The Kural says the friendship of a person with a living sense of shame at wrongdoing is worth winning even at a price; Guru Arjan agrees that such a friend, who turns one away from wrong, is rare and worth any search.
Greek Mythology
Pylades, a prince of Phocis raised alongside Orestes, stays with him through exile, madness and danger; in Euripides’ Iphigenia among the Taurians each insists on dying so that the other may live.
— Euripides, Iphigenia among the Taurians; Aeschylus, Libation Bearers [11]
Pylades was the Greek byword for the high-born, honourable friend who would pay any price for his companion.
He is the kind of well-born, honour-bound friend the Kural says is worth securing at any cost.
Philosophy
“But it is natural that such friendships should be infrequent; for such men are rare.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle says friendship between good people is the best kind but is scarce, because good people themselves are scarce.
If such friends are rare, it follows, as the Kural says, that they are worth a high price.

Real Life Example

Catherine the Great buys Diderot's library and leaves it with him
1765 · Paris, France, and St Petersburg, Russia
In 1765 Catherine the Great learned that the French philosopher Denis Diderot, editor of the Encyclopedie, was about to sell his library to provide a dowry for his daughter. She bought the collection for 15,000 livres on condition that the books remain with Diderot for the rest of his life, and paid him a yearly sum as librarian of his own books. Diderot afterwards recruited scholars and artists on her behalf and, in 1773, travelled to St Petersburg, where he met the empress for long conversations.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Catherine was willing to pay generously to win the friendship and goodwill of one of the most respected thinkers in Europe.
The kural advises securing, even at a price, the friendship of a worthy person who shrinks from disgrace, and Catherine quite literally paid to gain Diderot's friendship.
