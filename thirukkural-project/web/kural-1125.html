Kural 1125

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

உள்ளுவன் மன்யான் மறப்பின் மறப்பறியேன் ஒள்ளமர்க் கண்ணாள் குணம்.
Ulluvan manyaan marappin marappariyaen ollamark kannaal gunam.

Meaning

If I ever forgot the good qualities of the girl with bright, fighting eyes, I could remember them. But I never forget them at all.

Elementary

You only need to remember something if you have forgotten it. The poet says he never needs to remember the girl he loves, because she is never out of his mind for a moment.

University

The paradox turns on ulluvan (உள்ளுவன், 'I would recall') against marappu ariyaen (மறப்பறியேன், 'I do not know forgetting'): recollection presupposes forgetting, and since he never forgets her, he never has to 'remember' her. The particle man (மன்) marks the conditional as unrealised. Ol-amar-kannaal (ஒள்ளமர்க் கண்ணாள்) is read two ways: Mu. Varadarasanar and Pope take amar as 'battle', hence 'bright, warring eyes' (Pope: 'whose eyes gleam like the warrior's dart'), while Pulavar Kesikan reads ollivaai amarndha, 'bright and calmly set'. Pope's note, following the commentators, says the hero speaks to the heroine's maid (tozhi), who had asked whether he still remembered her. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Under the sage Durvasa's curse, King Dushyanta forgets Shakuntala entirely, until he sees the ring he gave her and his memory and love flood back.
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Acts IV-VI (paraphrase; cf. tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [3]
Kalidasa's drama turns on forgetting and remembering a beloved.
The Kural's lover insists he can never forget; Kalidasa shows the anguish when a lover does forget and must remember again.
Christianity
“Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee.”
— Isaiah 49:15 (KJV) [4]
God assures his people that his love is more unforgetting than even a mother's.
Both texts define deepest love as the impossibility of forgetting the one loved.
Islam
“I did not feel jealous of any of the wives of the Prophet (ﷺ) as much as I did of Khadija though I did not see her, but the Prophet (ﷺ) used to mention her very often”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3818 [5]
Aisha recalls that years after Khadija's death the Prophet kept speaking of her and honouring her friends.
Like the Kural's lover, the Prophet never forgot his beloved's qualities; she was always on his lips.
Judaism
The exiles by the rivers of Babylon swear that if they ever forget Jerusalem, their right hand should lose its skill and their tongue cleave to the roof of the mouth.
— Psalm 137:5-6 (paraphrase) [6]
The psalm makes forgetting the beloved city unthinkable, something they would rather be maimed than do.
The Kural's lover too declares forgetting impossible; the psalm's beloved is a city instead of a person.
Buddhism
The aged brahmin Pingiya tells his teacher that he is never apart from the Buddha even for a moment: he sees him with his mind as with his eye, day and night, and spends the nights paying him homage.
— Sutta Nipata 5.18, Pingiya's verses at the close of the Parayana Vagga (paraphrase) [7]
His devotion is so constant that the Buddha is always present in his recollection.
The Kural's lover never has to remember his beloved because he never forgets; Pingiya describes the same unbroken recollection, directed to his teacher.
Jainism
“A Sramana, engaged in penance, should not allow himself to watch the shape, beauty, coquetry, laughter, prattle, gestures, and glances of women, nor retain a recollection of them in his mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain monastic discipline forbids even keeping the memory of a woman's charms.
The Kural's lover cannot stop recalling the qualities of his beloved; the Jain ascetic is told to let precisely such recollection go.
Sikhism
“They do not forget, even for an instant, the Support of the breath of life. They live by constantly meditating on Him, O Nanak.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 80, Sri Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes devotees who never let the Beloved slip from mind, so that unbroken remembrance (simran) becomes the very thing they live by.
The Kural's lover never forgets his beloved's qualities and so never needs to recall them; Gurbani's devotees likewise never forget, even for an instant.
Greek Mythology
Kept for years on Calypso's island and offered immortality, Odysseus still sits on the shore longing for Penelope and home, and tells the goddess that he wants to go back to her.
— Homer, Odyssey 5.149-224 (paraphrase) [10]
Odysseus's longing for his wife does not fade over long separation or in the face of a goddess's love.
Like the Kural's lover, Odysseus cannot forget the qualities of the one he loves.
Philosophy
Montaigne writes that after the death of his friend Étienne de La Boétie he had grown so used to being one of two that he now felt only half himself, and that he had never stopped missing him.
— Michel de Montaigne, Essays I.28, 'Of Friendship' (paraphrase) [11]
The essay on friendship describes a union of two souls so complete that the friend's absence is felt constantly.
The Kural's lover never forgets his beloved; Montaigne describes a friend who is never out of his mind years after his death.

Real Life Example

Su Shi dreams of his late wife Wang Fu
1075 · Song dynasty China
In 1075 the Song dynasty poet and official Su Shi wrote a lyric to the tune Jiang Chengzi after dreaming of his first wife, Wang Fu, who had died ten years earlier at about twenty-seven. The poem says that for ten years the living and the dead have known nothing of each other, and that although he does not try to think of her, he cannot forget her. In the dream he returns home and sees her, and he wakes in grief.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem is one of the most famous elegies in Chinese literature, admired for expressing lasting love in very plain words.
The kural's lover says he would remember his beloved's qualities if he ever forgot them, but he never forgets; Su Shi's poem turns on the same paradox, saying he does not set out to remember his wife because he has never been able to forget her.
