Kural 388

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

முறைசெய்து காப்பாற்றும் மன்னவன் மக்கட்கு இறையென்று வைக்கப் படும்.
Muraiseydhu kaappaatrum mannavan makkatku Iraiyendru vaikkap padum.

Meaning

A king who rules fairly and protects his people will be seen by them as a god.

Elementary

When a lifeguard keeps everyone safe and is always fair, the swimmers look up to them like a hero. This kural says a ruler who is fair and protects everyone is looked up to by the people almost like God.

University

The word irai carries a deliberate double meaning — both ‘lord/king’ and ‘God’ — and the chapter title Iraimaatchi itself plays on it. Crucially, Valluvar makes divinity conditional: the king is ‘placed’ (vaikkappadum) as god by the people only because he does murai (justice, due order) and protects them. This inverts the claim of inherent divine kingship found in texts like the Laws of Manu, grounding sacral status in performance rather than birth. Aristotle’s remark that a person of pre-eminent virtue ‘may truly be deemed a God among men’ and Psalm 82’s address to judges as ‘gods’ who must defend the poor show the same conditional logic.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Even an infant king must not be despised, (from an idea) that he is a (mere) mortal; for he is a great deity in human form.”
— Manusmriti 7.8 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
The Laws of Manu say that the king, even a child king, is a god in human form and must be respected.
Shares the Kural’s king-as-god image, but Manu makes divinity inherent, while the Kural makes it earned by justice and protection.
Christianity
“For he is the minister of God to thee for good.”
— Romans 13:4 (KJV) [4]
Paul teaches that a ruler who punishes wrong and protects the good is acting as God’s servant.
The ruler who does justice stands in God’s place for the people, close to the Kural’s claim.
Islam
The Prophet listed a just ruler first among the seven whom God will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His (Sahih al-Bukhari 660).
— Sahih al-Bukhari 660 (paraphrased) [5]
Islam teaches that a just ruler will receive God’s special protection on Judgment Day.
A just, protecting ruler holds a uniquely exalted status — though Islam strictly denies any divinity to humans.
Judaism
“I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.”
— Psalm 82:6 (with 82:3), (KJV) [4]
In Psalm 82, God calls the rulers and judges ‘gods’, but only after demanding that they ‘defend the poor and fatherless’ — and warns that the unjust among them will die like men.
Rulers are called ‘gods’ precisely in their role of doing justice and protecting the weak, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
In the Agganna Sutta, the first king is called Mahasammata, ‘the Great Elect’, chosen by the people to judge and punish wrongdoers in return for a share of rice (DN 27).
— Digha Nikaya 27 (Agganna Sutta) [6]
Buddhist texts say the first king was simply chosen by the people to keep order, not sent by the gods.
Like the Kural, it grounds kingship in doing justice for the people — but it deliberately denies the king divine status.
Jainism
“Here (on earth) you are the highest man, Reverend sir, and hereafter you will be the highest; exempt from all blemishes you will reach Perfection, a higher state than which there is none in this world.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.58 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Sakra, king of the gods, throws off his disguise and worships King Nami, who has renounced his throne and mastered his passions. In Jainism even the gods bow to the perfected soul, and no ruler is divine by office.
Valluvar says the just protector-king will be held by his people as a god. Jainism also ranks a royal figure above the gods, but only Nami, who has conquered himself, earns that honour, and a reigning king does not.
Sikhism
“That king sits upon the throne, who is worthy of that throne.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1088, Raag Maaroo, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak teaches that a throne belongs only to the one who is worthy of it; Sikh thought reserves the title of True King (sacha patshah) for God alone.
The Kural says a king who rules justly and protects his people is regarded as divine; Guru Nanak likewise makes the right to rule depend on worthiness, though he does not grant any king divine status.
Greek Mythology
“And when he passes through a gathering, they greet him as a god with gentle reverence, and he is conspicuous amongst the assembled.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 91-92 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod says a king who judges fairly is greeted by the people like a god.
Almost exactly the Kural: the just king is ‘placed as god’ by his people.
Philosophy
“Such an one may truly be deemed a God among men.”
— Aristotle, Politics 3.13, 1284a (tr. Jowett) [10]
Aristotle says a person whose virtue far surpasses everyone else’s would be like a god among humans and should rule.
Divine regard grounded in pre-eminent virtue — the same conditional deification as the Kural.

Real Life Example

The Roman Senate deifies Augustus
17 September 14 CE · Rome, Italy
Augustus, the first Roman emperor, died on 19 August 14 CE, and on 17 September the Senate enrolled him among the gods of the Roman state with the title divus, the deified one. Ancient accounts credit the honour to his achievements, including restoring peace after decades of civil war and maintaining law and order. A temple to the Divine Augustus was built in Rome, and the deification set a precedent for later emperors.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Augustus had resisted being named a god while alive, but after his death the state honoured the ruler who had brought order and security as divine. His case shows how rulers who were seen to protect their people could be raised to sacred status.
The kural says the king who rules justly and protects his people will be regarded as a god, and Rome formally deified Augustus for bringing peace and order.
