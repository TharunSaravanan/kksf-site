Kural 1030

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

இடுக்கண்கால் கொன்றிட வீழும் அடுத்தூன்றும் நல்லாள் இலாத குடி.
Idukkankaal kondrida veezhum aduththoondrum nallaal ilaadha kudi.

Meaning

A family with no good person to hold it up will fall, like a tree, when trouble cuts at its roots.

Elementary

A young tree needs a stick tied beside it so the wind doesn't knock it over. Valluvar says a family is like that tree: when hard times blow, it needs a strong, caring person standing beside it, or it will fall.

University

The image is of a tree: idukkan (இடுக்கண், ‘misfortune’) strikes at its kaal (கால், ‘foot, base’) and kondrida (கொன்றிட, ‘cutting, killing’) brings it down, unless there is a nallaal (நல்லாள், ‘good person’) who aduththu oondrum (அடுத்து ஊன்றும், ‘stands close and props it up’). Varadarasanar and Pappaiah make the axe explicit (துன்பமாகிய கோடாரி, ‘the axe of misfortune’), and Kesikan names the tree; Pappaiah reads nallaal as ‘good children’. The couplet closes the chapter by turning 1021's vow into a warning about its absence. Pope gives ‘When trouble the foundation saps the house must fall, / If no strong hand be nigh to prop the tottering wall.’ The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“With the destruction of the dynasty, the eternal family tradition is vanquished, and thus the rest of the family becomes involved in irreligion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 1.39 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Arjuna fears that once a family's leading men fall, the whole family collapses.
This is the Kural's warning: a family without its supporters falls.
Christianity
Jesus says that a house built on sand falls when the rains, floods and winds beat on it, and great is its fall.
— Matthew 7:26-27 (KJV) [4]
A house without a solid foundation cannot survive the storm.
Like the Kural, it shows a household with nothing to hold it up collapsing under misfortune.
Islam
“A faithful believer to a faithful believer is like the bricks of a wall, enforcing each other.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 481 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet, lacing his fingers together, describes believers as bricks that hold each other up.
Like the Kural, it says a community stands only when its members prop one another up.
Judaism
Kohelet observes that two are better than one, for if one falls the other lifts him up, but woe to the one who falls alone with no one to raise him.
— Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (Tanakh) [6]
The sage warns that someone with no companion to help has nothing to save him when he falls.
The Kural's family with no one to prop it up suffers this same fall.
Buddhism
“By effort and heedfulness, discipline and self-mastery, let the wise one make for himself an island which no flood can overwhelm.”
— Dhammapada 25 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha urges building, through effort, a refuge that disaster cannot sweep away.
It answers the Kural's warning from the other side: steady effort makes a refuge that misfortune cannot overturn.
Jainism
“My father would have spent all he possessed, for my sake; but he could not rid me of my pains, hence I say that I am without protection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Anathi explains that even a devoted family could not protect him from suffering, which led him to renounce the world.
Jainism treats the family's support as finally unable to hold up the soul, so it gives an honest contrast to the Kural's faith in the family's prop.
Sikhism
“When You stand over our heads, O Lord and Master, how can we suffer in pain?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 749, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that no pain can overwhelm those who have the Lord standing over them as protector.
The Kural warns that a family with no good person to prop it up falls like a tree when misfortune strikes; Guru Arjan gives the other side, that those with a protector standing over them cannot be brought down by pain.
Greek Mythology
When Achilles kills Hector, Troy's great defender, the city's lament is as though all Ilion were already burning, and with its prop gone Troy soon falls.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 22 and 24 [10]
Hector was the one man holding up his city and family; his death leaves them defenceless.
It is the Kural's image exactly: a house with no one to prop it falls when misfortune strikes.
Philosophy
“Or how can prosperity be guarded and preserved without friends? The greater it is, the more exposed is it to risk. And in poverty and in other misfortunes men think friends are the only refuge.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.1, 1155a (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Opening his account of friendship, Aristotle says that without others to stand by it no fortune can be kept safe, and that in misfortune they are the only support.
Like the Kural's family with no one to prop it up, Aristotle's prosperity left without friends is exposed to fall when misfortune strikes.

Real Life Example

The Medici are driven from Florence after Lorenzo's death
1492-1494 · Florence, Italy
Lorenzo de' Medici, who had steered Florence and his family's fortunes for over two decades, died in April 1492. His son Piero faced a French invasion under Charles VIII in 1494, made concessions to the French king that outraged the city, and was forced to flee as the Medici palace was sacked; he never saw Florence again.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Without a capable leader to hold it up, a dominant family lost its power within two years when crisis came.
The kural warns that a family with no able person to prop it up falls like a tree when misfortune strikes its root, as the Medici house did in 1494.
