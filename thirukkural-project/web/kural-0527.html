Kural 527

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

காக்கை கரவா கரைந்துண்ணும் ஆக்கமும் அன்னநீ ரார்க்கே உள.
Kaakkai karavaa karaindhunnum aakkamum Annanee raarkke ula.

Meaning

A crow does not hide its food. It calls its family with a caw and eats with them. Good fortune comes only to people who are like that.

Elementary

When a crow finds food, it doesn’t sneak off with it. It says ‘Caw! Caw!’ so its whole family comes to share. People who share like the crow end up with the most good things in life, because everyone sticks with them.

University

This is the best-known couplet of the chapter. Parimelazhagar explains that when crows find food they do not hide it (கரவா) but caw (கரைந்து) to summon their flock and eat together, and that the ‘prosperities’ (ஆக்கமும்) that come through kin belong only to those who have such a nature (அன்ன நீரார்). He lists those prosperities as freedom from enemies and great wealth. He reads the உம் in ஆக்கமும் as an ‘inclusive’ particle: such people gain not only virtue (அறம்) and pleasure (இன்பம்) but material wealth (பொருள்) as well. He defines the crow-like nature as arranging things so that kin enjoy everything one enjoys oneself. Some modern readers (e.g. Karunanidhi) take ஆக்கம் more broadly as ‘elevation’. The crow’s calling of its kin is proverbial in Tamil culture and is echoed in later didactic literature.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He feeds no trusty friend, no man to love him. All guilt is he who eats with no partaker.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.6 (trans. R. T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Vedic hymn condemns the man who eats his food alone and shares it with no one.
The crow that calls others to its food is the opposite of the man who ‘eats with no partaker’.
Christianity
“And all that believed were together, and had all things common; And sold their possessions and goods, and parted them to all men, as every man had need.”
— Acts 2:44-45 (KJV) [4]
The first Jerusalem community pooled what it had and gave to each as they needed.
Nothing is hidden and everything is shared, as with the Kural’s crow.
Islam
“They love those who emigrated to them and find not any want in their breasts of what the emigrants were given but give [them] preference over themselves, even though they are in privation. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul - it is those who will be the successful.”
— Qur’an 59:9 (Sahih International) [5]
The Ansar of Medina shared their homes and goods with the migrants, and the Qur’an promises success to those saved from stinginess.
Open-handed sharing leads to success, which echoes the Kural’s ‘prosperity belongs to such people’.
Judaism
Isaiah describes the fast God desires as sharing one’s bread with the hungry, bringing the homeless poor into one’s house, clothing the naked, and not hiding oneself from one’s own flesh, that is, one’s kin.
— Isaiah 58:7-8 [6]
The prophet treats open sharing with the needy, and never turning away from one’s own kin, as true piety, and promises that light and healing will follow.
‘Hide not thyself from thine own flesh’ is very close to the Kural’s கரவா, not hiding one’s food from one’s kin.
Buddhism
“If beings knew, as I know, the results of giving & sharing, they would not eat without having given, nor would the stain of selfishness overcome their minds.”
— Itivuttaka 26 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha says that anyone who understood the fruit of sharing would never eat without first sharing.
This is the crow’s rule: never eat without first calling others to share.
Jainism
At the naming feast of the infant Mahavira, his parents sit down in the dining hall together with their friends, relations, kinsmen, agnates, cognates and followers, and they partook, ate, tasted, and interchanged (bits) of a large collation of food, drink, spices, and sweetmeats.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira § 104 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Kalpa Sutra describes the royal family not keeping its good fortune to itself but calling all its kin to eat and share at one table.
Valluvar's crow caws to call its kin and eats with them; the Jain royal house likewise calls its whole kindred to share its food in celebration.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
This salok is a scriptural root of kirat karni and vand chhakna: earn by honest work and share what you earn.
The Kural praises the crow that does not hide its food but calls its kin to eat with it; Guru Nanak likewise makes sharing what one has the mark of the right path.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod advises the farmer to invite his friend to his feast, and above all whoever lives near him, because neighbours come running when trouble strikes while more distant kinsmen are slow to arrive.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 342-345 [10]
Hesiod treats sharing one’s table with those around one as a practical way of building a circle that will help in need.
Like the crow, one calls others to share and so builds up support, though Hesiod ranks neighbours above distant kin.
Philosophy
“For this cause it would seem that we ought to summon our friends readily to share our good fortunes (for the beneficent character is a noble one),”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.11 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle advises calling friends in readily to share one’s good fortune.
Calling others in to share one’s good fortune is exactly what the Kural’s crow does.

Real Life Example

Fan Zhongyan founds the Fan clan charitable estate
1049-1050 · Suzhou, China
Fan Zhongyan, a Song-dynasty scholar-official from Suzhou who had been orphaned of his father in infancy, used his own funds late in his career to buy farmland near his home town and set it up as a charitable estate for his clan. Rents from the land supplied grain to members of the Fan lineage and helped them meet the costs of weddings, funerals and schooling. The estate continued for centuries and became a model for later lineage trusts in China.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Rather than keep the fruits of his success for his own household, Fan arranged for the whole clan to share in them, and his example shaped Chinese kinship institutions for generations.
The kural praises the crow that does not hide its food but calls its kin to share it, and Fan Zhongyan's estate turned his own prosperity into a meal for the whole clan.
