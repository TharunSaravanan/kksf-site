Kural 327

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

தன்னுயிர் நீப்பினும் செய்யற்க தான்பிறிது இன்னுயிர் நீக்கும் வினை.
Thannuyir neeppinum seyyarka thaanpiridhu Innuyir neekkum vinai.

Meaning

Even if it costs you your own life, do not do anything that takes away the precious life of another being.

Elementary

Suppose a bully says, “Hurt that puppy or I’ll hurt you.” Valluvar says: don’t hurt the puppy, even if you get hurt yourself. The puppy’s life is just as sweet to it as yours is to you.

University

This is one of the most uncompromising couplets in the Kural: one must not perform “the act that removes another’s sweet life” (piṟitu iṉṉuyir nīkkum viṉai) even if it means losing one’s own. The adjective iṉ (sweet) applied to the other’s life insists on its equal preciousness. Varadarasan explicitly reads it as forbidding killing even to save oneself — a stance close to Jain absolutism and more radical than most legal traditions, which permit self-defence. The Talmud reaches a similar conclusion for the case of being forced to kill an innocent person under threat, reasoning that one cannot know one’s own blood is “redder” than another’s.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Shibi, asked by a hawk to surrender a dove that had taken refuge with him, offered his own flesh — finally his whole body — rather than let the dove be killed.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (story of Shibi, the hawk and the dove) [4]
In the Mahabharata’s tale, the gods Indra and Agni test Shibi, who chooses self-sacrifice over allowing a life to be taken.
Enacts the Kural precisely: give up your own life rather than cause another being’s death.
Christianity
“But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.”
— Matthew 5:39 (KJV) [5]
In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus teaches non-retaliation, accepting harm rather than inflicting it.
Like the Kural, it asks one to bear harm to oneself rather than harm another.
Islam
“If you should raise your hand toward me to kill me - I shall not raise my hand toward you to kill you. Indeed, I fear Allāh, Lord of the worlds.”
— Qur’an 5:28 (Saheeh International) [6]
Abel (Habil), threatened with murder by his brother, refuses to kill even to defend himself.
Nearly the Kural in narrative form: better to be killed than to kill.
Judaism
“It is preferable that he should kill you and you should not kill. What did you think, that your blood is redder and more precious than his? Perhaps that man’s blood is redder.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 25b (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [7]
Rava rules that a man ordered to kill another on pain of death must let himself be killed rather than murder.
Exactly the Kural’s teaching: even to save your own life, do not take another’s.
Buddhism
The Buddha says that even if bandits were to saw a monk limb from limb with a two-handled saw, one who let his mind turn to hatred would not be following his teaching.
— Majjhima Nikaya 21, Kakacupama Sutta [8]
In the Simile of the Saw the Buddha demands non-hatred and non-retaliation even under mortal attack.
Like the Kural, it forbids answering even a threat to one’s own life with harm to another.
Jainism
The monk Dharmaruci was given a bitter, poisonous gourd dish as alms by the Brahmin woman Nāgaśrī. Told by his teacher to discard it, he saw ants die after tasting a single drop, and rather than let more creatures perish he ate the whole dish himself and died.
— Jñātādharmakathāṅga, ch. 16 (Draupadī's former birth as Nāgaśrī); retold in 'Dharmaruchi Muni', Jain stories, jainworld.com [9]
This celebrated canonical story presents a monk who gave up his own life rather than cause the death of even tiny creatures.
Valluvar says one must not take another's precious life even at the cost of one's own; Dharmaruci literally chose his own death over killing ants.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahala 9, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In his saloks Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the spiritually wise person as one who neither threatens others nor is cowed by threats. He himself chose execution in 1675 rather than yield, accepting death rather than inflicting it.
The Kural forbids taking another's life even at the cost of one's own; Guru Tegh Bahadur's ideal of never terrorising others, and his own martyrdom, come close to that self-sacrificing non-violence.
Greek Mythology
Oedipus kills Laius at a crossroads after being struck by him on the road, yet this killing — done in what he saw as self-defence — brings pollution and plague on Thebes and ruin on himself.
— Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 800–813 [11]
In Sophocles, a killing provoked by a threat to oneself still carries full bloodguilt.
Supports the Kural’s point that saving or defending oneself does not make killing innocent.
Philosophy
Asked by Polus whether he would rather suffer injustice or do it, Socrates answers that he would want neither, but if forced to choose he would rather suffer it than do it.
— Plato, Gorgias 469b–c [12]
The exchange arises in a discussion of tyrants who kill whom they please; Socrates holds that doing wrong harms the soul more than suffering it.
Like the Kural, it prefers suffering harm — even death — to inflicting it.

Real Life Example

Franz Jagerstatter refuses to fight and is executed
9 August 1943 · Austria and Germany
Franz Jagerstatter, an Austrian Catholic farmer who was married with three children, refused to serve as a soldier in Hitler's army because he believed the war was unjust and incompatible with his faith. He offered to serve as a medic instead, but this was refused. He was convicted of undermining military morale and executed on 9 August 1943, and the Catholic Church beatified him as a martyr in 2007.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Jagerstatter chose to die rather than take part in killing he believed was wrong.
The kural says do not take another's precious life even if it costs your own, and Jagerstatter gave his life rather than fight in an unjust war.
