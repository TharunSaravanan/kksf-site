Kural 96

Charming Utterance · Iniyavai Kooral · இனியவை கூறல்

அல்லவை தேய அறம்பெருகும் நல்லவை நாடி இனிய சொலின்.
Allavai theya arampperugum nallavai, Naadi iniya solin.

Meaning

If a person thinks about what is truly good and then says it kindly, wrongs will fade away and goodness will grow.

Elementary

It's like weeding a garden. Every time you plant something good — a kind, helpful word — there's a little less room for weeds. Do it often enough and the weeds shrink while the flowers spread. This kural says kind, useful words work on the world like that.

University

The verb நாடி (naadi, 'having sought out, investigated') is the hinge of the couplet and is easy to miss. Valluvar is not recommending spontaneous niceness but a deliberate search for what will actually benefit the hearer, delivered pleasantly. The two clauses form a causal pair with a striking agricultural logic: அல்லவை தேய ('evils wearing thin, eroding') and அறம் பெருகும் ('virtue swelling, increasing') — a single practice simultaneously depletes one and augments the other. This is one of the few places where Valluvar assigns speech a world-altering rather than merely personal effect, anticipating the argument of chapters 19 (on not slandering) and 20 (on avoiding useless words). The claim that gentle words can actually reduce the quantity of evil in the world finds its most dramatic mythic expression in Aeschylus and its clearest scriptural statement in Qur'an 41:34.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Patanjali prescribes that by deliberately cultivating friendliness toward the happy, compassion toward the suffering, gladness toward the virtuous and equanimity toward the wicked, the mind becomes clear and undisturbed.
— Patanjali, Yoga Sūtras 1.33 [4]
The sutra treats chosen benevolent attitudes as a technique: adopting them dissolves the mental afflictions that generate harmful action.
It shares the Kural's mechanism — deliberately sought-out goodwill erodes what is bad and increases what is good.
Christianity
“Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.”
— Ephesians 4:29 (King James Version) [5]
Paul asks for speech selected specifically for its capacity to build up the hearer — the negative rule and the positive purpose stated together.
'Good to the use of edifying' is the Kural's நல்லவை நாடி — seeking out what is genuinely good to say.
Islam
“And not equal are the good deed and the bad deed. Repel [evil] by that which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend.”
— Qur'an 41:34 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an prescribes a better response as the active means of dissolving hostility, converting an enemy rather than defeating him.
It is the Kural's claim that evil wears away and good grows precisely through the better, gentler word.
Judaism
The Talmud describes the person who studies Torah, is honest in business and speaks pleasantly with people: of such a one others say, 'happy is the father who taught him Torah — see how fine his ways are.' The one whose dealings and speech are unpleasant produces the opposite response, and thereby desecrates the divine Name.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 86a [7]
Pleasant speech is treated as having public theological consequence: it makes holiness attractive, while harshness discredits it.
It matches the Kural's claim that pleasant, well-chosen speech visibly increases virtue in the world around the speaker.
Buddhism
“He speaks words that are soothing to the ear, that are affectionate, that go to the heart, that are polite, appealing and pleasing to people at large.”
— Cunda Kammāraputta Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya 10.176 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha's positive definition of right speech to Cunda the silversmith, given as the direct replacement for abusive speech rather than merely its absence.
It is the Kural's programme exactly: abandoning harsh speech in favour of deliberately pleasing speech is what causes evil to decline and good to grow.
Jainism
“Every beneficial advice given by a group-fellow though unpalatable to the mind at first, proves wholesome in the end, like a medicine which is better in taste becomes agreeable in effect.”
— Saman Suttam v. 94 (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [9]
Jain teaching on truthful speech puts benefit (pathya) to the hearer first, and accepts that benefit sometimes comes in words that are hard to hear.
Valluvar asks the speaker to seek out what is genuinely beneficial before speaking pleasantly, and the Jain verse shares that stress on benefit, though it tolerates bitterness where the Kural insists on sweetness.
Sikhism
“Fareed, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger. Your body shall not suffer from any disease, and you shall obtain everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381-1382, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 78) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid counsels returning good for evil without harbouring anger, promising wholeness and every good thing as the result.
As the Kural says good words sought out and kindly spoken make evil wane and virtue grow, Farid holds that answering evil with good overcomes it.
Greek Mythology
“I love the eyes of Persuasion, who guided my tongue and mouth against the fierce refusal of these deities.”
— Aeschylus, Eumenides 970 (cf. 885) (trans. H. W. Smyth, rev. Bannon and Nagy) [11]
At the close of the Oresteia, Athena wins over the vengeful Erinyes not by force but by Peitho (Persuasion); they are transformed into the Eumenides, the Kindly Ones, and the cycle of blood-vengeance ends.
It is the Kural's claim enacted on a civic scale: gentle words literally cause evil to wear away and blessing to increase.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates distinguishes the mere knack of flattery from a genuine art of rhetoric, which he defines as psychagōgia — a leading of souls — and argues that it is legitimate only when the speaker knows the truth and knows what will genuinely benefit the particular soul in front of him.
— Plato, Phaedrus 261a–274b [12]
Plato makes the ethical worth of persuasive speech depend entirely on whether the speaker has sought out what is actually good for the hearer.
This is the Kural's நல்லவை நாடி — the prior search for what is genuinely good — made the precondition of legitimate speech.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela appeals for calm after the killing of Chris Hani
10-13 April 1993 · South Africa
On 10 April 1993 the South African Communist Party leader Chris Hani was assassinated, and the country, then in the middle of negotiations to end apartheid, faced the risk of widespread violence. Nelson Mandela, president of the African National Congress, went on national television that night and again on 13 April, appealing for discipline and calm, asking people to honour Hani through peaceful memorial services and urging that a date be fixed for democratic elections. Negotiations continued, and within weeks the date of the first democratic election, 27 April 1994, was agreed.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Carefully chosen, steadying words from a trusted leader helped hold a country back from violence at one of its most dangerous moments.
The kural says that when a person seeks out what is good to say and says it well, evil wears away and virtue grows, and Mandela's addresses turned a moment of fury toward peaceful action.
