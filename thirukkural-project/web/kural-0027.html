Kural 27

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

சுவைஒளி ஊறுஓசை நாற்றமென்று ஐந்தின் வகைதெரிவான் கட்டே உலகு.
Suvaiyoli oorosai naatramendru aindhin, Vakaitherivaan katte ulagu.

Meaning

The whole world is in the hands of the person who fully understands the five senses: taste, sight, touch, hearing and smell.

Elementary

Everything you know about the world came in through five doors: what you taste, see, feel, hear and smell. This kural says the person who really understands those five doors — how they work and how they fool you — is the one who has the whole world figured out.

University

Valluvar lists the five not as organs but as their objects — suvai (taste), oli (light or form), ooru (touch), osai (sound), naatram (smell) — which is the classical Indian tanmatra scheme, each sense-object paired with one of the five elements. The operative phrase is “vakai therivaan,” the one who discerns the kinds, and “kattu” means a bond, a grip, or a thing held fast: the world is in his hold. Commentators split productively here. Parimelazhagar reads it epistemically — the one who analyses the sense-modes comprehends the world, since the world is nothing but what arrives through them. Others, including modern commentators like Salomon Pappaiah, read it practically — the one who has severed the desires arising through the five has the world at his command. Both readings depend on the same underlying premise, which is arrestingly close to later phenomenology: the world is not encountered apart from the five channels, so mastery of the channels is mastery of the world.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons. They arise from sense perception, O scion of Bharata, and one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [4]
The Sanskrit phrase Krishna uses, mātrā-sparśāḥ, means literally the contacts of the sense-measures — he is telling Arjuna that pleasure and pain are just the coming and going of sense-contact, and that knowing this is what makes them bearable.
Both verses hold that understanding the nature of the five sense-channels is what puts a person on top of experience rather than underneath it.
Christianity
“For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world.”
— 1 John 2:16 (KJV) [5]
John analyses 'the world' into a short taxonomy of appetites, treating it not as a place but as a set of channels through which desire operates on a person.
Like the Kural, it identifies the world with what comes in through the senses, and makes knowing that taxonomy the first step to not being ruled by it.
Islam
“And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, the hearing, the sight and the heart - about all those [one] will be questioned.”
— Qur'an 17:36, Al-Isra (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an makes the sense-faculties themselves accountable, treating hearing and sight as agents that will be interrogated rather than as neutral instruments.
Both verses take the sense-channels as the decisive site of a person's relation to the world, and make knowledge of them the governing requirement.
Judaism
“And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes, after which ye use to go a whoring:”
— Numbers 15:39 (Tanakh, KJV) [5]
The Torah's command of the tzitzit works by installing a counter-sign in the visual field itself, on the explicit reasoning that the eyes and the heart lead a person astray by their own momentum.
It rests on the Kural's premise that the senses are the channel through which the world takes hold of a person, so the remedy has to be applied at that channel.
Buddhism
“What is the All? Simply the eye & forms, ear & sounds, nose & aromas, tongue & flavors, body & tactile sensations, intellect & ideas. This, monks, is called the All. Anyone who would say, ‘Repudiating this All, I will describe another,’ … would be unable to explain, and furthermore, would be put to grief.”
— Sabba Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 35.23 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha defines the totality of what there is exhaustively in terms of the sense-bases and their objects, and rules out in advance any 'world' lying outside them.
This is the Kural's claim made into doctrine: the world simply is the field of the sense-channels, so the one who has discerned them has the world.
Jainism
“The five senses, anger, pride, delusion, and greed--difficult to conquer is one's self; but when that is conquered, everything is conquered.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Answering Indra's challenge to go out and conquer rival kings, the royal sage Nami says that the real enemies are the five senses and the passions, and that whoever masters them has mastered everything.
The Kural says the world is in the grip of the one who fully understands the five, taste, sight, touch, sound and smell. Nami says the same thing: once the five senses are conquered, everything is conquered.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib, Pauri 28, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak recasts the yogi's discipline in inward terms. Whoever masters his own mind has mastered the world, with no need to retreat from it.
The Kural says the world lies in the grip of whoever understands the five senses. Japji says the world is won by whoever wins his own mind.
Greek Mythology
Circe turns Odysseus' crew into swine by way of food and drink; Odysseus alone walks into her hall unharmed because Hermes has given him the herb moly and told him the nature of her drugs in advance, so he drinks and is not transformed.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.275-347 [10]
The crew are undone by sensory pleasure taken without understanding, while Odysseus meets the same pleasure with prior knowledge of what it is and passes through it intact.
It dramatises the Kural's point that it is knowing the nature of sense-experience, not avoiding it, that leaves a person in command.
Philosophy
“Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (trans. George Long) [11]
Epictetus separates the raw sense-impression from the judgement a person adds to it, and locates all disturbance in the second, which is the part within our power.
Like the Kural, it makes analysis of how impressions reach us the lever by which a person stops being at the world's mercy.

Real Life Example

Helen Keller understands the word water
5 April 1887 · Tuscumbia, Alabama, United States
Helen Keller, who had lost her sight and hearing in early childhood, began lessons with her teacher Anne Sullivan in March 1887. On 5 April 1887 Sullivan held Helen's hand under a water pump and spelled w-a-t-e-r into her other hand, and Helen suddenly understood that the finger signs named the water. By that evening she had learned some thirty words, and she went on to become a writer and lecturer.
— Sources: [12] [13]
With two senses closed to her, Keller gained her hold on the whole world by understanding deeply what touch could tell her.
The kural says the world is in the grasp of one who truly discerns the nature of the senses, and Keller's breakthrough came when she grasped what a single sense could carry.
