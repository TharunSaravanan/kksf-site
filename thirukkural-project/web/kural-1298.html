Kural 1298

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

எள்ளின் இளிவாம்என்று எண்ணி அவர்திறம் உள்ளும் உயிர்க்காதல் நெஞ்சு.
Ellin ilivaam-endru enni avarthiram ullum uyirkkaadhal nenju.

Meaning

My heart loves him as much as life itself. It thinks that looking down on him would only shame itself, so it keeps thinking of his good points.

Elementary

When someone you love lets you down, you might want to say mean things about them. But a loving heart thinks, ‘If I say bad things about him, it makes me look bad too,’ and remembers his good side instead. The wife’s heart does exactly that.

University

Eḷḷiṉ iḷivām means ‘if (I) scorn (him), it will be a disgrace (to me)’; avar tiṟam uḷḷum, ‘it thinks of his qualities (or ways)’. Uyirkkātal neñcu is read in two ways: Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah take it as ‘the heart that loves life’ (fearing disgrace as worse than death), while Puliyur Kesikan and Kalaignar take it as ‘the heart that loves him as its own life’. Commentators also differ on avar tiṟam: ‘his noble qualities’ (Varadarasanar, Kesikan, Kalaignar) versus ‘his coming and our union’ (Pappaiah). Pope renders ‘If I contemn him, then disgrace awaits me evermore; / My soul that seeks to live his virtues numbers o’er.’ The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Held captive in Ravana’s grove in Lanka, Sita refuses every attempt to turn her against Rama and keeps her mind on his virtues, declaring that she belongs to him as light belongs to the sun.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (paraphrase) [3]
The Ramayana shows Sita’s devotion holding firm and dwelling on Rama’s goodness even in long separation.
Like Sita, the wife’s heart will not think ill of her absent husband but keeps recalling his good qualities.
Christianity
“Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil;”
— 1 Corinthians 13:5 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as refusing to think the worst of the beloved.
The wife’s heart ‘thinketh no evil’ of her husband and turns to his good qualities instead.
Islam
“O you who have believed, avoid much [negative] assumption. Indeed, some assumption is sin.”
— Qur’an 49:12 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an tells believers to avoid thinking the worst of others.
The wife’s heart refuses to think ill of her husband and chooses to dwell on his merits.
Judaism
“Joshua ben Perahiah used to say: appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion and judge all men with the scale weighted in his favor.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:6 (tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
The sage teaches that one should always give others the benefit of the doubt.
The wife’s heart judges her husband favourably, dwelling on his good qualities rather than his neglect.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada advises not to look for the faults of others or at what they have done or left undone, but only at what one has done or left undone oneself.
— Dhammapada 50 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The verse turns attention away from another’s failings.
The wife’s heart refuses to dwell on her husband’s faults, though for love rather than self-examination.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra lists four attitudes to cultivate: friendliness toward all beings, delight (pramoda) in those with greater virtues, compassion for the afflicted, and equanimity toward the ill-behaved.
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.11 (Digambara numbering; 7.6 in Svetambara) (paraphrase; cf. tr. Vijay K. Jain) [8]
Jain ethics trains the mind to rejoice in others’ good qualities rather than dwell on their faults.
The wife’s heart does something like pramoda, fixing on her husband’s good qualities instead of scorning him.
Sikhism
“Some sing of His Glorious Virtues, Greatness and Beauty.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak describes devotees who dwell on God's qualities and beauty in praise.
The Kural's loving heart refuses to scorn the beloved and dwells on his good qualities instead; Japji describes the devotee's heart dwelling on the Lord's virtues.
Greek Mythology
Alcyone, while her husband Ceyx is away at sea, prays daily to Juno for his safe return and thinks only of his welfare, never of blame.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.573-582 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid portrays a wife whose thoughts of her absent husband are wholly loving.
Like Alcyone, the wife’s heart dwells on her husband with love rather than complaint.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises that when someone treats you badly or speaks ill of you, you should remember that he acted as he thought right, and so you will be gentle with him.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 42 (paraphrase) [11]
The Stoic refuses to let another’s fault provoke him into contempt.
The wife’s heart, too, declines to answer neglect with scorn.

Real Life Example

Nadezhda Mandelstam memorises her husband’s poems
1934–1938 and after · Soviet Union
The Russian poet Osip Mandelstam was arrested by Stalin’s police in 1934 and again in May 1938, and died later that year in a transit prison camp near Vladivostok. His wife, Nadezhda, committed his poems to memory in the hope that one day she could publish them, and she eventually did. She later wrote the memoir Hope Against Hope, which records their life together and preserves his memory.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Through years of danger and loss, Nadezhda Mandelstam chose to carry her husband’s best qualities, his poetry, in her own mind.
The kural says the loving heart, rather than scorning the beloved, dwells on his good qualities, and Nadezhda Mandelstam devoted her life to holding on to her husband’s finest work.
