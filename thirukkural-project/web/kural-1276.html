Kural 1276

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

பெரிதாற்றிப் பெட்பக் கலத்தல் அரிதாற்றி அன்பின்மை சூழ்வ துடைத்து.
Peridhaatrip petpak kalaththal aridhaatri anpinmai soozhva thudaiththu.

Meaning

He hugs me with great love and joy. But this hints that he is about to do the hard thing again and leave me, without caring how I feel.

Elementary

Imagine a parent who gives you an extra-long hug at the door, and you suddenly realise they are about to go on a trip. The heroine notices her husband’s extra-loving embrace and guesses that he is getting ready to leave her again.

University

The wife, speaking to her confidante, reads her husband’s unusually tender union (peritu āṟṟi, ‘showing great love’; peṭpak kalattal, ‘uniting so as to delight’) as a kuṟippu of something else: aritu āṟṟi, doing the hard thing, separation, and anpiṉmai cūḻvatu, contemplating lovelessness. Varadarasanar and Kesikan take it this way, as does Kalaignar, who says the embrace seems to signal another parting. Solomon Pappaiah reads the first half differently, as her having endured the past separation, so that his present embrace makes her think of his lack of love. Pope’s couplet on ytamizh (‘While lovingly embracing me, his heart is only grieved’) follows the separation reading. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Rasa dance, when the gopis grow proud of Krishna’s attention after he has delighted them, he suddenly vanishes from among them, leaving them searching the forest for him.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29-30 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Bhagavata shows an intense union with the beloved followed at once by his disappearance.
The Kural’s wife fears exactly this pattern: closeness that signals an imminent departure.
Christianity
“A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; a time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing;”
— Ecclesiastes 3:5 (KJV) [4]
Ecclesiastes says that embracing and parting each have their season.
The wife in the Kural senses that a time of embracing is about to give way to a time apart.
Islam
“And if a woman fears from her husband contempt or evasion, there is no sin upon them if they make terms of settlement between them - and settlement is best.”
— Qur’an 4:128 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an addresses a wife who senses her husband drawing away and urges reconciliation.
The Kural’s wife likewise detects signs of his withdrawal, though in her case through his very tenderness.
Judaism
“How canst thou say: I love thee, when thy heart is not with me?”
— Judges 16:15 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Delilah confronts Samson because his words of love do not match what she senses in his heart.
The Kural’s wife also senses a gap between loving gestures and an intention to leave.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada teaches that attachment to the dear brings with it the fear of losing them.
The wife’s fear in the Kural, that even his embrace foretells parting, is the fear the Dhammapada says endearment produces.
Jainism
On his wedding procession Arishtanemi turns back, renounces the world and leaves his bride Rajimati, who exclaims: ‘Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him!’
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.14-29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; Rajimati’s words at 22.29) [8]
The Jain story centres on a bridegroom who leaves his bride at the very moment of union.
Rajimati suffers the departure that the Kural’s wife fears behind her husband’s embrace, though Arishtanemi leaves for renunciation, not for another journey.
Sikhism
“Beholding His wondrous beauty, the milk-maid was entranced, and said, "Please don't leave me; please don't come and go!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 338, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir pictures a milk-maid so captivated by the Lord's beauty that, even while with Him, she begs Him not to leave.
In the Kural the wife reads her husband's unusually loving embrace as a hint that he means to leave again; Kabir's milk-maid, too, feels the fear of parting in the very moment of closeness.
Greek Mythology
After Ariadne helps Theseus escape the Labyrinth and sails with him, he abandons her asleep on the island of Naxos.
— Ariadne on Naxos, as told in Ovid, Heroides 10 and Catullus 64 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth tells of a lover who leaves just after a time of closeness.
The Kural’s wife fears that her husband’s tender embrace is a prelude to such a leaving.
Philosophy
Pausanias says the common lover, who loves the body rather than the soul, is not constant, and when the bloom fades he takes wing and flies away.
— Plato, Symposium 183e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato contrasts unstable love, which ends in departure, with lasting love of character.
The wife in the Kural fears that intensity of embrace may signal a love that is about to fly away.

Real Life Example

Lincoln's Farewell Address at Springfield
11 February 1861 · Springfield, Illinois, United States
On 11 February 1861, the day before his 52nd birthday, President-elect Abraham Lincoln spoke to friends and neighbours gathered at the Great Western Railroad depot in Springfield before boarding a train for Washington. He spoke of his sadness at leaving the town where he had lived for 25 years, said he did not know when or whether he would return, and compared the task ahead to the one George Washington had faced. He asked the crowd to pray for him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The unusual tenderness of Lincoln's brief speech reflected the long and uncertain separation that lay ahead.
The kural's wife reads her husband's unusually tender embrace as a sign that a hard parting is coming; the warmth of Lincoln's farewell carried the same sign of a long separation.
