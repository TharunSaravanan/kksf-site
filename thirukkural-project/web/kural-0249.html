Kural 249

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

தெருளாதான் மெய்ப்பொருள் கண்டற்றால் தேரின் அருளாதான் செய்யும் அறம்.
Therulaadhaan meypporul kandatraal therin, Arulaadhaan seyyum aram.

Meaning

If you look closely, the good deeds of an unkind person are like a confused person claiming to understand the deepest truth. They are not real.

Elementary

Imagine someone who has never understood maths saying they solved the hardest puzzle in the book. You would not believe them. Valluvar says good deeds done by someone who does not care about others are just like that: they look right, but there is nothing real inside.

University

The comparison is carefully chosen: therulaadhaan is one whose mind is not clear, and meypporul is ultimate reality or the true meaning of scripture. Such a person may recite the words, but cannot genuinely grasp the truth. In the same way, aram (dharma, virtuous action) performed by one who does not have arul may copy the outward form (gifts, rituals, observances) without the inner substance. The kural thus makes compassion the condition of authenticity for all other virtue, a thesis found in Paul’s ‘if I have not charity’ and in the Talmudic teaching that charity is rewarded only according to the kindness in it. Kant offers a pointed contrast: he held that acts done from sympathy alone lack moral worth and that only acts done from duty have it. Valluvar’s position is closer to virtue and sentiment theorists such as Hume and Schopenhauer, for whom the motive of fellow-feeling is what makes an act good.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And that charity, which is given at the wrong place and wrong time to unworthy persons, without showing respect, or with contempt, is held to be of the nature of nescience.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.22 (trans. Mukundananda) [3]
The Gita classifies giving done with contempt as tāmasic, the lowest grade, belonging to ignorance.
Charity without inner regard for the recipient is, as in the kural, a kind of ignorance dressed up as virtue.
Christianity
“And though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul says even the most extreme acts of generosity are worthless without agapē (love).
Almost exactly the kural: aram performed without arul is empty.
Islam
“O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words, like those who donate their wealth just to show off”
— Quran 2:264 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The verse compares such charity to soil on a bare rock that is washed away by rain.
Giving without inner goodwill has no substance, like the unclear mind’s ‘vision’ of truth.
Judaism
“The reward for charity is paid from Heaven only in accordance with the kindness and generosity included therein”
— Talmud, Sukkah 49b [6]
Rabbi Elazar also teaches that gemilut chasadim (acts of lovingkindness) are greater than tzedakah (charity) in three respects.
The value of a virtuous act is measured by the compassion inside it, which is the kural’s thesis.
Buddhism
“Even though he be well-attired, yet if he is poised, calm, controlled and established in the holy life, having set aside violence towards all beings — he, truly, is a holy man, a renunciate, a monk.”
— Dhammapada 141-142 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The preceding verse (141) says nakedness, matted hair, fasting and ash-smearing cannot purify one who has not overcome inner defilement.
Outward religious practice is empty without the inner disposition of harmlessness, matching the kural’s view of aram without arul.
Jainism
“Those who do not know this (Law), are not awakened, though they fancy themselves awakened; believing themselves awakened, they are beyond the boundary of right faith.”
— Sutrakritanga I.11.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Having just called non-killing 'the quintessence of wisdom', the Sutrakritanga says that those who miss this law only imagine they are enlightened and are in fact outside right faith.
Valluvar likens virtue without compassion to a confused mind claiming to see the truth; the Sutrakritanga likewise says that those ignorant of the law of non-harm only fancy themselves awakened.
Sikhism
“You kill living beings, and call it a righteous action. Tell me, brother, what would you call an unrighteous action?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1103, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir challenges those who sacrifice animals and call it dharma, asking what could count as sin if that is righteousness.
Valluvar says the virtue of someone without compassion is no more real than a muddled mind's claim to have seen truth; Kabir likewise exposes religious acts done without compassion as false righteousness.
Greek Mythology
King Lycaon receives the disguised Jupiter with a show of hospitality, but secretly serves him the flesh of a slaughtered hostage and plots to kill him in his sleep; Jupiter destroys his house and turns him into a wolf, still thirsting for blood.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.209-243 [10]
A sacred duty, hospitality to a guest, is performed in outward form by a man with no pity.
The ‘virtue’ of the pitiless is a hollow performance, as the kural warns; Lycaon’s case is an extreme one.
Philosophy
Kant argues that a philanthropist who helps others out of sympathy acts amiably but without true moral worth; only if sympathy were extinguished and he helped purely from duty would the act have moral worth.
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals 4:398 [11]
For Kant, the moral value of an act lies in its maxim of duty, not in the feeling behind it.
A direct counterpoint: Valluvar holds that virtue without compassion is hollow, whereas Kant holds that virtue from compassion alone is not yet moral. The contrast shows what is distinctive in the Kural’s sentiment-centred ethics.

Real Life Example

The Spanish Requerimiento is read to Indigenous peoples
1513 · Spanish Americas (first read on the coast of present-day Colombia)
Around 1510-1513 the Council of Castile drew up the Requerimiento, a formal text to be read aloud to Indigenous peoples before conquest, asserting the authority of the pope and the Spanish crown and demanding their submission and acceptance of Christian preaching. It was often read only in Spanish, sometimes from a distance, so that its hearers could not understand it, and translation was merely recommended. Historians describe it as a legal formality that served chiefly to justify conquest to the Spanish themselves.
— Sources: [12] [13]
An outward ritual of legality and religion was performed without any real concern for the people it addressed.
The kural says virtue practised without compassion is as unreal as a confused mind claiming to see the truth, and the Requerimiento was a show of righteousness emptied of any regard for its hearers.
