Kural 1145

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

களித்தொறும் கள்ளுண்டல் வேட்டற்றால் காமம் வெளிப்படுந் தோறும் இனிது.
kaLiththoRum kaLLuNdal vaettatraal kaamam veLippatunh thoaRum inidhu

Meaning

Just as a drinker wants more toddy (palm wine) each time it makes him happy, my love grows sweeter each time the rumor makes it known.

Elementary

Some sweets are so tasty that each one makes you want another. The young man says his love is like that. Each time people talk about it, it feels sweeter, and he wants it talked about even more.

University

Parimelazhagar again gives this to the hero and explains vēṭṭaṟṟāl as a shortened form of vēṭkappaṭṭaṟṟāl, ‘as it is desired’: for drinkers, drinking toddy (kaḷ) grows sweeter each time they become merry (kaḷittoṟum), and so the hero’s love grows sweeter each time it is made known (veḷippaṭuntoṟum) by rumour. His point is that the very strength of desire makes even scandal pleasant. Manakkudavar assigns the couplet differently, to the tōḻi answering a hero who asked whether the rumour distressed them. The simile is striking given that chapter 93, kaḷḷuṇṇāmai, condemns drink; here the image describes the lover’s state without endorsing drinking. The text agrees in all three sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Yayati, after a thousand years of enjoyment regained through his son’s youth, declares that desire is never satisfied by enjoying its objects but only blazes higher, like fire fed with clarified butter, and returns his son’s youth.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Sambhava Parva, story of Yayati (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [4]
The Mahabharata uses Yayati to show that every indulgence of desire makes it want more.
The Kural’s drinker wants more with each cup, and its lover finds love sweeter each time it is talked about; Yayati is the classic Indian picture of that endless appetite.
Christianity
“They have stricken me, shalt thou say, and I was not sick; they have beaten me, and I felt it not: when shall I awake? I will seek it yet again.”
— Proverbs 23:35 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs portrays a drunkard who, in spite of every harm, only wants another drink.
The Kural uses the same picture of the drinker who wants more, applying it to a lover who wants his love talked about more and more.
Islam
Ibn al-Farid’s Wine Ode opens with the lovers drinking, in remembrance of the Beloved, a wine with which they were drunk before the vine was created.
— Ibn al-Farid, al-Khamriyya (The Wine Ode), opening verse (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Arberry) [6]
The Sufi poet makes intoxication the image of a love that is endlessly sweet and never sated.
The Kural, too, makes drinking the image of love that becomes sweeter the more it is tasted and revealed.
Judaism
The Song of Songs opens with the beloved saying that her lover’s love is better than wine.
— Song of Songs 1:2 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [7]
The Song compares love and wine and finds love the sweeter.
The Kural, too, sets love beside drink and finds that love, made public, grows ever sweeter.
Buddhism
The Buddha describes a leper who scratches his sores and burns them over a pit of coals; the more he does it, the worse his wounds become, yet he finds a kind of pleasure in it.
— Majjhima Nikaya 75 (Magandiya Sutta) (paraphrase) [8]
Sensual desire, says the Buddha, feeds on what makes it worse and is mistaken for pleasure.
The Kural’s drinker and lover both find sweetness in what only increases their craving; the Buddha calls that the leper’s pleasure.
Jainism
“Pleasures are the thorn that rankles, pleasures are poison, pleasures are like a venomous snake; he who is desirous of pleasures will not get them, and will come to a bad end at last.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.53 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
Nami teaches that the sweetness of pleasure is a trap that leads to more craving and suffering.
The Kural’s lover finds love sweeter each time it is revealed, just as the drinker finds drink sweeter; Jainism warns that this growing sweetness is the poison itself.
Sikhism
“Do not drink the false wine at all, if it is in your power. O Nanak, the True Guru comes and meets the mortal; by His Grace, one obtains the True Wine.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 554, Raag Bihaagraa (Bihaagre ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das forbids ordinary intoxicating drink and offers instead the 'True Wine' of the Name, received through the Guru by grace.
The Kural compares love made public to toddy that grows sweeter each time the drinker is merry; Gurbani takes the same image of intoxication but redirects it to the true, sober intoxication of the Name.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus’ men who taste the lotus lose all wish to return home and want only to stay among the Lotus-eaters, eating more of it.
— Homer, Odyssey 9 (the Lotus-eaters) (paraphrase) [11]
Homer’s lotus is sweet in a way that makes people want nothing but more of it.
The Kural’s drinker and lover are caught by the same sweetness, which grows the more it is tasted.
Philosophy
Lucretius says that love is the one thing of which the more we have, the more fiercely the heart burns with desire.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1088-1090 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.H.D. Rouse) [12]
For Lucretius, love is unlike hunger or thirst, which are satisfied; possessing more of it only makes the craving stronger.
This is the logic of the Kural’s drinker: each taste makes the next one more wanted, and love revealed grows sweeter.

Real Life Example

John Lennon and Yoko Ono turn their honeymoon into a Bed-In
25-31 March 1969 · Amsterdam Hilton, Amsterdam, Netherlands
John Lennon and Yoko Ono married in Gibraltar on 20 March 1969 and, knowing the wedding would draw press attention, spent their honeymoon in bed at the Amsterdam Hilton from 25 to 31 March. Every day from 9 a.m. to 9 p.m. they invited reporters into the room, where they sat in pyjamas talking about peace. A second week-long Bed-In followed in Montreal, where the song Give Peace a Chance was recorded.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Rather than hide their new marriage from the press, the couple invited the world in, again and again.
The kural compares love made public to drink that grows sweeter each time; Lennon and Ono made their love public twice over and welcomed the attention rather than shrinking from it.
