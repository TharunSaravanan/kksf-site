Kural 1269

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

ஒருநாள் எழுநாள்போல் செல்லும்சேண் சென்றார் வருநாள்வைத்து ஏங்கு பவர்க்கு.
Orunaal ezhunaalpoal sellumsaen sendraar varunaal-vaiththu aengu pavarkku.

Meaning

For those who long for the day their faraway love will come back, one day drags by as slowly as seven days.

Elementary

Do you remember waiting for your birthday or a holiday? The last few days feel sooo long! The verse says that when you are waiting for someone you love to come home, one day can feel as long as a whole week.

University

A general maxim (note the plural ēṅkupavarkku, ‘for those who pine’) caps the chapter’s experience: oru nāḷ eḻu nāḷ pōl cellum, ‘one day passes like seven’, for those who have fixed in mind (vaittu) the day (varu nāḷ) of return of one who has gone far (cēṇ ceṉṟār). Mu. Varadarasanar keeps the seven; Solomon Pappaiah generalises to ‘many days’; Kalaignar heightens it to ‘each day like an age (yugam)’. The commentary tradition associated with Parimelazhagar takes it as spoken by the heroine of herself, generalised into a truth. Text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When You go off to the forest during the day, a tiny fraction of a second becomes like a millennium for us because we cannot see You.”
— Bhagavata Purana 10.31.15 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The gopis tell Krishna that every moment without the sight of him stretches into an age.
This is the Kural’s claim, magnified: for the one who waits, a day is seven days, or a moment a millennium.
Christianity
“My soul waiteth for the Lord more than they that watch for the morning: I say, more than they that watch for the morning.”
— Psalm 130:6 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist compares his waiting to night watchmen longing for the dawn, when every hour drags.
Like the Kural’s waiter, the watchman knows how slowly time passes when you are waiting for an arrival.
Islam
“And they urge you to hasten the punishment. But Allah will never fail in His promise. And indeed, a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of those which you count.”
— Qur’an 22:47 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an reminds the impatient that time is measured differently with God than by human counting.
The Kural also says that a day is not always a day: for the yearning heart, it stretches far beyond its count.
Judaism
“And Jacob served seven years for Rachel; and they seemed unto him but a few days, for the love he had to her.”
— Genesis 29:20 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Jacob’s love for Rachel makes seven years of labour feel like a few days.
Both texts say love bends time by the number seven; for Jacob, with his goal in sight, years shrink, while for the Kural’s waiting lover a day swells into seven.
Buddhism
“Long is the night to the sleepless; long is the league to the weary. Long is worldly existence to fools who know not the Sublime Truth.”
— Dhammapada 60 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that time and distance stretch for those who are suffering.
The Kural’s waiting lover lives the first line: her nights and days are long because she is restless with longing.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira reminds Gautama that human life is short and urges him not to waste a single moment.
The Kural’s lover feels each day as long; Jain teaching asks one to feel each moment as precious, a contrasting lesson about the weight of time.
Sikhism
“With each and every breath, I think of You, day and night; I do not forget You, for an instant, even for a moment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 247, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes a mind fixed on the Beloved through every breath, day and night.
The Kural says that for those whose minds are fixed on the day of the beloved's return, one day drags like seven; Guru Arjan likewise speaks of a mind that holds the Beloved in thought through every moment.
Greek Mythology
Theocritus opens an idyll of welcome to a returning beloved by saying that those who long grow old in a single day.
— Theocritus, Idyll 12, line 2 (paraphrase) [10]
Greek pastoral poetry states that longing makes one day as heavy as years.
This is nearly the Kural’s own maxim: for the one who waits, a day is far longer than a day.
Philosophy
Bergson observes that if he wants a glass of sugar-water he must wait until the sugar melts, and that this time of waiting is not a mathematical quantity but coincides with his impatience, a portion of his own lived duration.
— Henri Bergson, Creative Evolution, ch. 1 (paraphrase) [11]
Bergson distinguishes clock time from lived time, which stretches with impatience.
The Kural says the same: by the clock it is one day, but in the waiting heart it is seven.

Real Life Example

Anne Bradstreet's poems to her absent husband
17th century; published 1678 · Massachusetts Bay Colony (now Massachusetts, United States)
The Puritan poet Anne Bradstreet was married to Simon Bradstreet, whose duties as a colonial magistrate frequently took him away from home. She wrote love poems to him, including A Letter to her Husband, absent upon Publick employment, in which she pictures him as the sun and herself as chilled and numb while he stays away, begging him to return. The poems appeared in the Boston edition of her Several Poems in 1678.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bradstreet's poems are among the earliest records in American literature of a wife's longing during her husband's absences on public duty.
The kural says that for those waiting for a faraway beloved's return, one day passes like seven; Bradstreet describes the time of her husband's absence as a long cold season waiting for the sun to come back.
