Kural 422

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

சென்ற இடத்தால் செலவிடா தீதொரீஇ நன்றின்பால் உய்ப்ப தறிவு.
Sendra idaththaal selavidaa theethoreei Nandrinpaal uyppa tharivu.

Meaning

Wisdom means not letting the mind run wherever it wants. It keeps the mind away from evil and guides it toward good.

Elementary

Your mind is a bit like a puppy that wants to run off in every direction. Wisdom is the leash. It stops the puppy from running into trouble and gently pulls it toward good things.

University

Here arivu is defined by its function over the mind (implied as the subject). சென்ற இடத்தால் செலவிடா means 'not letting it go wherever it goes'. தீது ஒரீஇ means 'removing it from evil', and நன்றின்பால் உய்ப்பது means 'leading it toward the good'. Three movements are involved: restraint, withdrawal, and redirection. Wisdom is therefore not only knowing the good but actively governing attention and desire. Parimelazhagan glosses the object as the mind driven by the five senses. The image matches Indian and Greek charioteer models of the psyche (Katha Upanishad, Plato's Phaedrus), where intelligence holds the reins of the senses. The Buddhist 'fletcher straightening the arrow' image is similar. The Kural is notably positive: wisdom does not simply suppress the mind, it 'leads' it (uyppathu) somewhere better.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Katha Upanishad teaches that the good (shreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both approach a person. The wise examine and distinguish them and choose the good; the fool chooses the pleasant. It then compares the body to a chariot, the senses to horses, and buddhi (discernment) to the charioteer holding the reins of the mind.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2; 1.3.3-6 [4]
Yama tells Nachiketas that discernment must choose between competing pulls and must rein in senses that would otherwise run wild, like untamed horses.
Both texts describe wisdom as the faculty that stops the mind from going wherever it pulls and steers it to the good.
Christianity
“Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good.”
— Romans 12:9 (KJV) [5]
Paul sums up the Christian moral life as turning away from evil and holding fast to good.
This mirrors the Kural's two moves: theethu oreei (removing oneself from evil) and nandrinpaal uyppathu (leading toward the good).
Islam
“But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge.”
— Qur'an 79:40-41 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an praises the person who holds the nafs (lower self) back from following its desires.
Both texts stress restraining the self from going wherever its inclinations lead.
Judaism
Ben Zoma asks, 'Who is mighty?' and answers, 'One who subdues his (evil) inclination (yetzer).'
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 [7]
The Mishnah redefines strength as mastery over one's own impulses rather than power over others.
Both texts see the key human capacity as governing the inner drive that pulls toward wrong.
Buddhism
“Just as a fletcher straightens an arrow shaft, even so the discerning man straightens his mind — so fickle and unsteady, so difficult to guard.”
— Dhammapada 33 [8]
The Buddha compares the discerning person to an arrow-maker who takes a crooked, wavering shaft and makes it straight.
In both texts the discerning faculty takes a restless, wandering mind and directs it true.
Jainism
“The unruly, dreadful, bad horse, on which you sit, runs about, Gautama! how comes it to pass that it does not run off with you?” … “I govern it well in its course by the bridle of knowledge; it does not go astray with me, it keeps to the right path.” … “The mind is that unruly, dreadful, bad horse; I govern it by the discipline of the Law (so that it becomes a well-) trained Kambôga-steed.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.55-58 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
In the dialogue between Kesi (of Parshva's line) and Gautama (Mahavira's disciple), the mind is a runaway horse that knowledge reins in and keeps on the right path, until it becomes a well-trained steed.
Valluvar defines wisdom as not letting the mind go wherever it wanders but leading it from evil to the good; Gautama's 'bridle of knowledge' does exactly that: it restrains the mind and steers it onto the right path.
Sikhism
“Taking up the sword of spiritual wisdom, she struggles with her mind, and hope and desire are smoothed over in her mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1022, Raag Maaroo (Solhe), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Raag Maaroo Guru Nanak describes the soul-bride using spiritual wisdom to fight her own mind until its restless desires are stilled.
The Kural defines wisdom as not letting the mind run where it likes, holding it from evil and steering it to good; Guru Nanak shows wisdom wrestling the mind and stilling its cravings.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads the young Heracles meets two women, Vice and Virtue. Vice offers an easy road of pleasure; Virtue offers a harder road of toil and honour. Heracles chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus, 'The Choice of Heracles', as retold in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 [11]
Prodicus's fable presents the moment of choice when desire pulls toward the easy path and judgment must turn it toward the good.
This dramatises the Kural's turning of the mind away from evil and toward good.
Philosophy
Plato's Socrates likens the soul to a charioteer driving two winged horses: one noble and obedient, the other unruly and pulled by appetite. The charioteer (reason) must rein in the unruly horse and guide the pair upward.
— Plato, Phaedrus 246a-254e [12]
Reason's job is to stop appetite from dragging the soul wherever it wants and to direct it toward the good.
This matches the Kural: arivu is the charioteer that keeps the mind from bolting and leads it toward the good.

Real Life Example

Ignatius of Loyola learns to read the movements of his mind
1521-1522 · Loyola, Basque Country, Spain
In 1521 the Basque soldier Inigo de Loyola was badly wounded in the legs by a cannonball while defending Pamplona against the French and was carried to his family's castle at Loyola. During a long convalescence, with only a life of Christ and a book of saints' lives to read, he noticed that daydreams of knightly glory left him empty and unsatisfied, while thoughts of following Christ and the saints left him uplifted. He resolved to change his life, left Loyola in 1522, and the practice of attending to these inner movements, later called discernment, became central to his Spiritual Exercises and to the Society of Jesus he founded.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Ignatius turned close attention to his own thoughts into a lasting method for steering the mind, still taught worldwide.
The kural defines wisdom as not letting the mind go wherever it wanders, but drawing it away from evil and leading it toward the good, which is what Ignatius learned to do by noticing where each kind of thought led him.
