Kural 1203

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

நினைப்பவர் போன்று நினையார்கொல் தும்மல் சினைப்பது போன்று கெடும்.
Ninaippavar poandru ninaiyaarkol thummal sinaippadhu poandru kedum.

Meaning

I felt a sneeze coming, which people say means someone far away is thinking of you, but it faded. Did he start to think of me and then stop?

Elementary

Some people say that if you sneeze, someone far away is thinking of you. The lady in this poem feels a sneeze coming, and then it goes away! She worries: did he start to think of me and then stop? It is a gentle, funny way to show how much she wants to be remembered.

University

The image rests on the folk belief, presupposed by all the glosses, that a sneeze signals that someone far away is thinking of one. The heroine feels a sneeze ‘budding’ (ciṉaippatu, from ciṉai, to sprout) and then subsiding (keṭum), and asks whether her husband, seeming to think of her, has stopped (niṉaippavar pōṉṟu niṉaiyār-kol). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all render it this way, and Pope gives: ‘A fit of sneezing threatened, but it passed away; / He seemed to think of me, but do his fancies stray?’ The couplet’s comedy is tender: a trivial bodily flicker becomes evidence in a lover’s anxious inquiry. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Captive in Ravana’s grove and on the point of despair, Sita feels her left eye, left arm and left thigh throb, auspicious signs for a woman, and takes heart that news of Rama is near; Hanuman soon appears.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, sarga 29 (paraphrase; cf. valmikiramayan.net) [3]
The Ramayana treats involuntary bodily twitches as omens about an absent husband.
Sita reads a throb as the Kural’s heroine reads a sneeze: as a sign of whether her distant husband is near in thought.
Christianity
“O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee? O Judah, what shall I do unto thee? for your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away.”
— Hosea 6:4 (KJV) [4]
God laments that his people’s devotion appears and then vanishes like morning dew.
The heroine fears the same thing: an affection that seemed about to arrive, then went away like the half-sneeze.
Islam
The Prophet taught that when someone sneezes he should say ‘Praise be to God’, and the one who hears should answer ‘May God have mercy on you’, to which the sneezer replies with a prayer for guidance.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6224 (paraphrase) [5]
Islam turns the sneeze into an occasion for praise and mutual blessing rather than an omen.
The Kural reads a sneeze as a sign of a lover’s thought; Islamic etiquette, by contrast, answers every sneeze with remembrance of God.
Judaism
A rabbinic tradition holds that before Jacob a person never fell ill: he would sneeze and his soul would leave him, until Jacob prayed for illness as a warning before death; hence the custom of answering a sneeze with a blessing for life.
— Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, chapter 52 (paraphrase) [6]
Jewish lore treats the sneeze as a portentous moment bound up with life itself.
Like the Kural, this tradition invests a sneeze with hidden meaning, though about life and death rather than love.
Buddhism
When the Buddha sneezed while teaching and the monks loudly called out ‘Long life to you, Lord!’, he asked whether saying so could make anyone live or die, and told them not to say it; later he allowed it in answer to laypeople’s courtesy.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Cullavagga V.33 (paraphrase; cf. tr. T.W. Rhys Davids and H. Oldenberg, SBE 20) [7]
The Buddha gently debunks the belief that words spoken at a sneeze have real power.
Against the Kural’s playful omen-reading, the Buddha questions whether a sneeze means anything at all.
Jainism
The Kalpa Sutra tells how Queen Trishala saw fourteen great dreams, among them an elephant, a bull, a lion and the full moon, which interpreters explained as signs that she would bear a son destined to be a great king or a Jina.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira, the fourteen dreams (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Jain narrative treats signs given to the body and mind as genuine portents of what is coming.
The heroine reads her half-sneeze as a sign; Trishala’s dreams are the classic Jain case of reading omens, though about a birth, not a lover.
Sikhism
“Good omens and bad omens affect those who do not keep the Lord in the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 401, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan rejects reading omens: only those who have forgotten the Lord are troubled by signs of good or bad luck.
The Kural's wife reads a sneeze that fades as a sign her husband has stopped thinking of her; Gurbani would tell her that omens have no hold on one whose mind is fixed on the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
As Penelope wishes aloud that Odysseus would come home, Telemachus sneezes loudly so that the house rings; she laughs and takes it as a sign that the suitors will meet their doom, not knowing that Odysseus is already in the hall in disguise.
— Homer, Odyssey 17.541-547 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
In Homer a sneeze at the right moment is an omen about the absent husband.
Penelope and the Kural’s heroine both read a sneeze as news of a husband far away in body but near in fate or thought.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that anything whatever may accidentally become a cause of hope or fear, and that this is why people interpret chance things as omens.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Proposition 50 and Scholium (paraphrase) [11]
Spinoza explains omen-reading as the mind attaching hope and fear to whatever happens to accompany them.
The heroine’s anxious reading of a sneeze is a perfect instance of hope and fear fastening on a chance sign.

Real Life Example

The first transatlantic telegraph cable falls silent
16 August – September 1858 · Valentia Island, Ireland, to Newfoundland
In August 1858, after several failed attempts, a telegraph cable was completed across the Atlantic between Valentia Island in Ireland and Newfoundland. On 16 August Queen Victoria sent a congratulatory message to President James Buchanan over it. The cable carried 732 messages in about three weeks and then stopped working; a lasting connection came only in 1866.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The 1858 cable was a thrilling sign that distant people could reach each other, followed almost at once by silence.
The kural's heroine feels a sneeze, taken as a sign that someone far away is thinking of her, start and then fade; the 1858 cable was a real-world signal across a great distance that began and then died away.
