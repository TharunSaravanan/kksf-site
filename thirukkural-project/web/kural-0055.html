Kural 55

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

தெய்வம் தொழாஅள் கொழுநன் தொழுதெழுவாள் பெய்யெனப் பெய்யும் மழை.
Theyvam thozhaaaL kozhunan thozhudhezhuvaaL, peyyenap peyyum mazhai.

Meaning

A wife who worships no god but her husband, and honours him as she rises each day, can say ‘Let it rain,’ and the rain will fall.

Elementary

This is a very old saying about how powerful someone's devotion was believed to be: so powerful that even the clouds would listen. People used to tell stories like this to show that a promise kept perfectly gives a person a kind of strength the world has to respect. Today many people read it differently — and disagree with it — which is worth knowing too.

University

This is the most contested couplet in the chapter and arguably in Book I. Read plainly it asserts that a wife's exclusive devotion to her husband confers thaumaturgic power over nature — a claim with deep roots in Tamil literature, where Kannagi's karpu incinerates Madurai and the chaste woman's word commands the elements. Parimelazhagar reads it straightforwardly as establishing 'the power of the woman of karpu' (kaRpudaiyavaLadhu aatral). But modern readings diverge sharply: M. Karunanidhi's widely circulated commentary renders the couplet critically, treating the woman who worships her husband as god as one who has reduced herself to servitude, while other scholars argue the couplet is hyperbolic praise of moral force rather than a prescription of husband-worship, and still others note that Valluvar's own Chapter 1 pointedly refuses sectarian deity-worship, so 'thozhaaaL theyvam' may be less about theology than about single-pointed commitment. The comparative material below shows that the specific motif — a devoted wife's prayer bringing rain — appears independently in the Babylonian Talmud, which is a remarkable convergence. The honest scholarly position is that the couplet encodes a genuinely asymmetric marital theology that many readers today reject while still finding the underlying claim about the force of integrity intelligible.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Savitri, having chosen the doomed Satyavan and kept a three-day vow, follows Yama as he carries off her husband's soul and argues with the god of death until he returns Satyavan alive — her devotion literally overrides the order of death.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Pativrata-mahatmya Parva (the Savitri and Satyavan episode) [4]
The Mahabharata's most famous pativrata narrative makes wifely devotion a force that even a deity must yield to.
This is the Kural's claim in narrative form: her constancy commands what no ordinary petition can.
Christianity
“The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. Elias was a man subject to like passions as we are, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months. And he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain.”
— James 5:16–18 (King James Version) [5]
James offers the New Testament's clearest statement that a sufficiently righteous person's word can govern rainfall.
The same equation as the Kural — perfected righteousness yields command over the rain — though attached to a prophet, not a wife.
Islam
“If I were to order anyone to prostrate to anyone, I would have ordered the woman to prostrate to her husband.”
— Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1159 (also Sunan Ibn Majah 1852), narrated by Abu Hurayra [6]
The hadith is structured as an emphatic refusal — prostration belongs to God alone — while using the marital bond as the measure of how great a claim the husband has.
It sets up the identical tension the Kural does between worship owed to the divine and reverence owed to a spouse.
Judaism
“They went up to the roof. He stood in one corner and she stood in the other corner. Clouds began to form on that side where his wife stood… Because my wife is frequently at home, and she gives bread to the poor, and therefore her provision of benefit to the needy is immediate.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Ta'anit 23b (Koren Noé / William Davidson Edition) [7]
When the Sages come to the miracle-worker Abba Ḥilkiyya to pray for rain, the clouds gather first over his wife — and he explains that her merit outranks his.
An astonishingly close convergence: a devoted wife whose word brings rain before anyone else's.
Buddhism
When the householder Nakulapitā lies gravely ill and fears dying anxious about his family, his wife Nakulamātā speaks to him at length about her own competence and faith, and her words so steady him that his illness subsides — the Buddha later praises her as a teacher in her own right.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 6.16, Nakulapitā Sutta [8]
Early Buddhism records a wife whose words have transforming power, but locates that power in her wisdom and reassurance, not in veneration of her husband.
It is the nearest Buddhist analogue to a wife's word achieving what nothing else could, minus the metaphysics.
Jainism
When the gates of Campā could not be opened, a voice from the sky said they would open only if a true woman (satī) drew water from the well with a sieve on a weak cotton thread and sprinkled it on them; the slandered laywoman Subhadrā did this and the gates opened.
— Āvaśyaka-cūrṇi of Jinadāsa (story of Subhadrā); summarised in Jainpedia, 'Subhadrā' [9]
The tale of Subhadrā, one of the Jain sixteen satīs, makes a wife's proven chastity a power that commands water and stone where everyone else fails.
Valluvar's chaste wife says 'rain' and it rains, and Subhadrā holds water in a sieve and opens sealed gates: the same belief that a chaste wife can command the natural order.
Sikhism
“Do not call them ’Satee’, who burn themselves along with their husbands’ corpses. O Nanak! They alone are known as ’Satee’, who die from the shock of separation.” … “They are also known as ‘Satee’, who abide in modesty and contentment. They serve their Lord, and rise in the early hours to contemplate Him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 787, Raag Soohee, Soohee ki Vaar, Saloks Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das, who opposed the burning of widows, redefines the honoured title sati: the truly faithful woman is not the one who dies on the pyre but the one who lives with modesty and contentment and rises early to remember the Lord.
Like the Kural's devoted wife who rises each day in reverence, the true sati rises early in devotion; but in Sikh teaching that devotion goes to God, not to the husband as a deity.
Greek Mythology
When Ceyx drowns at sea, his wife Alcyone throws herself into the waves in grief; the gods transform them both into halcyon birds, and thereafter Aeolus stills the winds and calms the sea for seven days each winter so that she can nest — the 'halcyon days'.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses XI.410–748 (the Ceyx and Alcyone episode) [11]
Greco-Roman myth's clearest case of the weather itself being ordered to obey the needs of a devoted wife.
Nature bends to a faithful wife, which is precisely the Kural's image of rain falling at her word.
Philosophy
“A wife ought not to make friends of her own, but to enjoy her husband's friends in common with him. The gods are the first and most important friends. Wherefore it is becoming for a wife to worship and to know only the gods that her husband believes in, and to shut the front door tight upon all queer rituals and outlandish superstitions.”
— Plutarch, Conjugalia Praecepta (Advice to Bride and Groom) §19, Moralia 140D (trans. F. C. Babbitt, Loeb Classical Library) [12]
Plutarch's wedding-gift essay advises the bride to adopt her husband's religion exclusively — the same fusion of marital and devotional exclusivity the Kural assumes.
An almost line-for-line Greco-Roman counterpart to 'she worships no other god'.

Real Life Example

Kasturba Gandhi prepares to speak in her husband's place
August 1942 · Bombay (Mumbai), India
Kasturba Gandhi often took her husband's place in public campaigns when he was in prison. When Mahatma Gandhi was arrested at the start of the Quit India movement in August 1942, before he could address a planned public meeting at Shivaji Park in Bombay, he wanted Kasturba to speak in his place; she prepared a message for the crowd but was arrested on her way to the meeting. She was later held with her husband at the Aga Khan Palace in Pune, where she died in February 1944.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Kasturba's lifelong devotion to her husband and his cause gave her a voice and standing of her own that crowds respected.
The kural claims that a wife wholly devoted to her husband commands such power that rain falls at her word, and Kasturba's devotion made her the one chosen to speak with her husband's authority when he could not.
