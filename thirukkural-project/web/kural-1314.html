Kural 1314

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

யாரினும் காதலம் என்றேனா ஊடினாள் யாரினும் யாரினும் என்று.
Yaarinum kaadhalam endraenaa ootinaal yaarinum yaarinum endru

Meaning

When I said, "We love each other more than anyone," she sulked and asked, "More than whom? More than whom?"

Elementary

If you tell your friend, ‘You are my best friend, better than all the others,’ a cheeky friend might reply, ‘Which others? Who else is on your list?’ The wife turns her husband’s compliment into a question to tease him.

University

Yāriṉum kātalam means ‘we are lovers beyond anyone’; the husband meant it as the highest praise, but she seizes on yāriṉum (‘than anyone’) as if it implied a list of other women he loves less, and repeats yāriṉum yāriṉum (‘than whom? than whom?’). Solomon Pappaiah spells out her reading: she took him to mean that he loves many and her the most. The humour depends on the comparative form, which presupposes rivals; Kalaignar notes that she ‘misread a natural remark’. Pope renders it ‘“I love you more than all beside,” ’t was thus I gently spoke; “What all, what all?” she instant cried; and all her anger woke.’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, the sage Narada visits Krishna’s palaces in Dvaraka and finds him in each queen’s house at the same moment, wholly present with every one of them.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.69 (paraphrase) [3]
The story imagines a love so complete that no wife is loved ‘more’ or ‘less’ than another.
It answers the wife’s teasing ‘more than whom?’ by picturing a love that needs no comparison at all.
Christianity
“Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:5 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as slow to take offence and unwilling to read bad meaning into what it hears.
The wife does the opposite in play: she reads a hidden rival into her husband’s loving words.
Islam
“And you will never be able to be equal [in feeling] between wives, even if you should strive [to do so]. So do not incline completely [toward one] and leave another hanging.”
— Qur’an 4:129 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an admits frankly that love cannot be measured out equally between people.
The wife’s ‘more than whom?’ seizes on this very idea that love can be ranked between rivals.
Judaism
“And he went in also unto Rachel, and he loved Rachel more than Leah.”
— Genesis 29:30 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Genesis records Jacob’s love in comparative terms, and the rivalry between the two sisters follows directly from it.
The husband’s ‘more than anyone’ sounds to his wife like Jacob’s ‘more than Leah’, a love that implies a rival.
Buddhism
“He who seeks another’s faults, who is ever censorious — his cankers grow. He is far from destruction of the cankers.”
— Dhammapada 253 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha warns that a mind bent on finding fault keeps feeding its own unrest.
The wife hunts for a fault in a compliment, though here the fault-finding is a loving game.
Jainism
“He who is very fond of a lovely ‘colour,’ hates all others; hence a fool will suffer misery, but a dispassionate sage is not affected by it.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching observes that strong attachment to one thing breeds aversion to everything compared with it.
The wife hears ‘more than anyone’ as a ranking that sets her against unseen others, the comparing mind this verse describes.
Sikhism
“You are the Primal Being, the Most Wonderful Creator. There is no other as Great as You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 11, So Purakh (Raag Aasaa), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das declares that God has no equal and cannot be compared with anyone.
The Kural's wife bristles at 'more than anyone', as if her husband were ranking her against others; Gurbani's praise of the Beloved avoids ranking because no one can stand beside Him.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Zeus tells Hera that desire has never overcome him as it does now, not even for the wife of Ixion, nor Danae, nor Europa, nor Semele, nor Alcmene, nor Demeter, nor Leto, nor Hera herself before.
— Homer, Iliad 14.313-328 (paraphrase) [10]
Zeus tries to flatter Hera by comparing his love for her with a long list of other loves, comically naming the very rivals she resents.
This is the Kural’s joke made explicit: loving someone ‘more than anyone’ invites the question ‘which others?’
Philosophy
Epictetus teaches that people are disturbed not by things themselves but by their judgements about things.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (paraphrase) [11]
A word or event becomes upsetting only through the meaning we give it.
The husband’s compliment upsets the wife only because of the meaning she chooses to read into it.

Real Life Example

One clause of the Treaty of Wichale is read two different ways
2 May 1889 · Wichale (Wuchale), Wallo, Ethiopia
On 2 May 1889 Emperor Menilek II of Ethiopia signed a treaty of friendship and commerce with Italy at Wichale. The Italian text of Article XVII made Rome the channel for all of Ethiopia's foreign relations, while the Amharic text was only permissive, saying Ethiopia could use Italy's help if it wished. Italy used the Italian wording to claim a protectorate over Ethiopia, Menilek rejected that reading, and the treaty was finally cancelled by the Treaty of Addis Ababa in October 1896.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode is a classic example of how a small difference in wording can be read as implying something the other side never meant, with very large consequences.
In this kural the wife seizes on the word than anyone and reads into it a list of rivals the husband never had in mind, just as one side at Wichale read a much bigger meaning into a single clause.
