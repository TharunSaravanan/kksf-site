Kural 520

Selection and Employment · Therindhu Vinaiyaadal · தெரிந்து வினையாடல்

நாடோறும் நாடுக மன்னன் வினைசெய்வான் கோடாமை கோடா துலகு.
Naadorum naaduga mannan vinaiseyvaan Kodaamai kodaa thulagu.

Meaning

A king should check every day how his workers behave. As long as they do not go wrong, the country will not go wrong either.

Elementary

A good coach checks on the team at every practice, not just once at the start of the season. If the players stay on track, the whole team does well.

University

The chapter closes by joining selection to supervision: நாடோறும் நாடுக, examine every day. The logic is causal: if the worker does not swerve (கோடாமை), the world, meaning the realm and its people, will not swerve (கோடாது). Most commentators read it as vigilance over officials, but Kalaignar reads கோடாமை as the worker’s heart not withering, so the king’s daily attention becomes care for the welfare of labour. Kautilya prescribes almost the same thing, that superintendents be examined daily because men are changeable, which gives the Kural a precise parallel in Indian statecraft.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While engaged in work, they shall be daily examined; for men are naturally fickle-minded, and, like horses at work, exhibit constant change in their temper.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 2.9 (Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya says officials must be checked every day because people’s dispositions shift while they work.
This is Kural 520’s நாடோறும் நாடுக almost word for word.
Christianity
“After a long time the lord of those servants cometh, and reckoneth with them.”
— Matthew 25:19 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the talents, the master comes back and asks each servant to account for what he did.
Delegation is followed by accountability, though here after a long time rather than every day.
Islam
“All of you are guardians and responsible for your wards and the things under your care. The Imam (i.e. ruler) is the guardian of his subjects and is responsible for them...”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 893 [5]
The Prophet teaches that every ruler answers for those in his care.
The ruler’s responsibility for his people requires the constant attention to his agents that the Kural prescribes.
Judaism
“Be thou diligent to know the state of thy flocks, And look well to thy herds;”
— Proverbs 27:23 (JPS 1917) [6]
Proverbs tells the owner to keep close, personal watch over everything in his care.
Diligently knowing the state of your charge is the Kural’s daily examination.
Buddhism
“In five ways should a master minister to his servants and employees as the Nadir : (i) by assigning them work according to their ability, (ii) by supplying them with food and with wages, (iii) by tending them in sickness, (iv) by sharing with them any delicacies, (v) by granting them leave at times.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (Narada) [7]
The Buddha tells employers to assign work by ability and look after their workers’ welfare; in return the workers do their duties well.
This fits the whole chapter’s theme of assigning work by ability, and Kalaignar’s welfare reading of Kural 520.
Jainism
“Then he should, in due order, reflect on all transgressions he has committed during the day, with regard to knowledge, faith, and conduct.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 26.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jain monastic practice prescribes a daily review (pratikramana) in which each monk examines and confesses the day's lapses before his guru.
The kural asks the king to examine his workers' conduct every day so that nothing goes crooked; Jain discipline builds the same daily inspection into every monk's evening routine.
Sikhism
“The kings are tigers, and their officials are dogs; they go out and awaken the sleeping people to harass them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1288, Raag Malaar, Malaar Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes a realm where rulers prey on the people and their officials, left unchecked, go out to harass ordinary folk.
The Kural says a king must watch his officials every day, because the world goes crooked when they do; Guru Nanak shows the world that results when officials are not held straight.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says Zeus has countless unseen spirits roaming the earth, watching over human judgments and wrongdoing.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 252-255 [10]
In Greek belief, divine oversight keeps an eye on how people act.
Constant watchfulness over those who act on authority parallels the Kural’s daily review of the king’s agents.
Philosophy
In Xenophon’s Oeconomicus, Ischomachus tells the story of the Persian king who asked what fattens a horse fastest and was told “the master’s eye,” and says the master’s own attention is what makes stewards and estates flourish.
— Xenophon, Oeconomicus 12.20 [11]
The owner’s personal, continuing oversight is what keeps workers diligent.
The master’s eye is the Kural’s daily examination.

Real Life Example

The Yongzheng Emperor and the palace memorial system
1722-1735 · Beijing, Qing China
The Yongzheng Emperor of the Qing dynasty expanded and regularised the palace memorial system, under which trusted officials across the empire sent confidential reports directly to him. He wrote his replies on them personally in vermilion ink before they were returned to the sender. Britannica describes him as an able ruler who checked corruption and personally scrutinised and directed all important matters of government.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Yongzheng kept a continual, personal watch on what his officials were doing, and his reign is known for its campaign against corruption.
The kural tells the ruler to look into the conduct of those doing his work every day, because if they do not go crooked the world will not, and Yongzheng's memorial system was built for that daily scrutiny.
