Kural 820

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

எனைத்தும் குறுகுதல் ஓம்பல் மனைக்கெழீஇ மன்றில் பழிப்பார் தொடர்பு.
Enaiththum kurukudhal ombal manaikkezheei mandril pazhippaar thodarpu.

Meaning

Do not get even a little close to people who are friendly with you in private but say bad things about you in public.

Elementary

Imagine someone who plays nicely with you at your house but makes fun of you in front of the whole class. That’s not a real friend, and it’s wise to keep your distance from them.

University

The chapter closes on the double-faced friend: manaik kezheei (மனைக் கெழீஇ), ‘intimate in the house’, and mandril pazhippaar (மன்றில் பழிப்பார்), ‘those who revile in the manram’, the public hall or assembly where reputations are made. The prescription is the strongest in the chapter: enaiththum kurukudhal ombal (எனைத்தும் குறுகுதல் ஓம்பல்), ‘guard against approaching in any measure at all’. Where 811 and 818 allowed a gradual waning, here even the slightest contact is to be avoided, since such a person turns private trust into public damage. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesigan all read manai literally as ‘home’, while Kalaignar’s gloss refers to private moments. The texts agree; the sandhi-joined edition writes ‘எனைத்துங் குறுகுத லோம்பன்’ and ‘மன்றிற் பழிப்பார்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Avoid him who talks sweetly before you but tries to ruin you behind your back, for he is like a pitcher of poison with milk on top.”
— Chanakya Niti 2.5 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya warns against the person who is sweet in your presence and destructive in your absence.
This is the Kural’s home-friend and public slanderer, and both texts prescribe avoidance.
Christianity
“I speak not of you all: I know whom I have chosen: but that the scripture may be fulfilled, He that eateth bread with me hath lifted up his heel against me.”
— John 13:18 (KJV) [5]
At the Last Supper Jesus applies Psalm 41:9 to Judas, the table companion who will betray him.
Sharing bread at home and turning on someone outside is the pattern the Kural tells us to avoid.
Islam
“The worst people in the Sight of Allah on the Day of Resurrection will be the double faced people who appear to some people with one face and to other people with another face.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6058 [6]
The Prophet names the two-faced person, who shows different faces in different company, as among the worst of people.
The Kural’s friend at home and slanderer in the assembly is exactly this double-faced person.
Judaism
“For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it … But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance. We took sweet counsel together, and walked unto the house of God in company.”
— Psalm 55:12-14 (Hebrew Bible, KJV rendering) [5]
The psalmist says he could have endured an enemy’s reproach, but the one shaming him is a close companion who once shared his confidence.
This is the pain of the Kural’s friend who is intimate in private and reviles in public.
Buddhism
“(i) he approves of his friend’s evil deeds, (ii) he disapproves his friend’s good deeds, (iii) he praises him in his presence, (iv) he speaks ill of him in his absence.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha describes the flatterer, a foe in the guise of a friend, as one who praises to your face and disparages you behind your back.
Items (iii) and (iv) are the Kural’s couplet almost exactly, and the Sutta’s remedy, to avoid such people from afar, matches its counsel.
Jainism
“If he is frequently angry; if he perseveres in his wrath; if he spurns friendly advice; if he is proud of his learning; if he finds fault with others; if he is angry even with friends; if he speaks evil even of a good friend behind his back … then he is called ill-behaved.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.7–9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Among the faults that mark a monk as ill-behaved and keep him from Nirvana, the Uttaradhyayana lists speaking evil of a good friend behind his back.
The Kural warns against coming even slightly close to those who are friendly at home but run you down in public, and the Jain canon condemns that same two-faced disparaging of a friend as a fault that blocks liberation.
Sikhism
“Kabir! I have seen a person who is as shiny as washed wax. He seems very clever and very virtuous, but in reality, he is without understanding, and corrupt.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir 139 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir describes a person whose polished outward show of virtue hides a corrupt nature within.
The Kural warns against even slight contact with those who are friendly in private but revile you in public; Kabir's polished but corrupt man shows the same split between the face he presents and what he is.
Greek Mythology
In one strand of the myth (Euripides’ Orestes; Diodorus Siculus 4.74), Tantalus, honoured as a guest at the gods’ own table, repaid their intimacy by blurting their secrets to mortals, and was punished eternally.
— Euripides, Orestes; Diodorus Siculus, Library of History 4.74 [10]
Tantalus turned the privileges of private friendship into public exposure of his hosts.
He fits the Kural’s pattern of intimacy at home turned against the host in public.
Philosophy
“The dissembler is a man, for instance, who accosts his enemies and engages readily in talk with them, to show that he bears no grudge, and who praises to their faces the very men he slanders behind their backs …”
— Theophrastus, Characters, I (The Dissembler) (tr. Charles E. Bennett and William A. Hammond) [11]
In the first of his character sketches, Theophrastus describes the dissembler, who is friendly in private encounters while running the same people down elsewhere.
This is almost exactly the person the Kural warns against: friendly in private and disparaging in public.

Real Life Example

Dickens portrays his friend Leigh Hunt as Harold Skimpole
1852-1853 · London, England
Charles Dickens and the poet and essayist Leigh Hunt were friends of long standing. In Bleak House, published in monthly parts in 1852-53, Dickens created Harold Skimpole, a charming but irresponsible sponger, and readers who knew Hunt recognised him in the character. In a letter to John Forster in 1853 Dickens called Skimpole an exact portrait of a real man, the caricature caused Hunt much distress, and Dickens later apologised.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A friendship kept up in private was damaged when one friend held the other up to public ridicule in a widely read novel.
The kural warns against coming close to those who are friendly at home but run you down in public, and Dickens's private friendship with Hunt sat beside a public caricature that all their acquaintances recognised.
