Kural 938

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

பொருள்கெடுத்துப் பொய்மேற் கொளீஇ அருள்கெடுத்து அல்லல் உழப்பிக்கும் சூது.
Porulkeduththup poimer koleei arulkeduththu allal uzhappikkum soodhu.

Meaning

Gambling wastes your money, pushes you to lie, kills your kindness, and makes you suffer.

Elementary

First gambling takes your money. Then you start fibbing to cover it up. Then you stop caring about other people. And at the end you are very unhappy. Valluvar lists these four steps like a staircase going down.

University

The couplet is a chain of four causative actions with gambling (soodhu, சூது) held back as the subject until the last word. Porul keduththu (பொருள்கெடுத்து) means 'ruining wealth'; poi mer koleei (பொய்மேற் கொளீஇ) means 'making one take up falsehood', where koleei is the causative alapedai form of kol ('take'); arul keduththu (அருள்கெடுத்து) means 'destroying compassion'. The last verb, uzhappikkum (உழப்பிக்கும், 'causes to toil in affliction'), is also causative. Arul, the compassion celebrated in Arattuppaal's chapter 25 (Aruludaimai), is the highest of the virtues lost. Kesikan follows the Parimelazhagar tradition in reading the misery as belonging to both worlds (இருமையும்), and the Pope prose gloss adds '(here and hereafter)'. Both base sites agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Sabha Parva, Yudhishthira, having lost his wealth, his brothers and himself at dice, stakes and loses Draupadi, who is then dragged into the assembly and humiliated while the court looks on.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (dice-game episode), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
The dice game first takes the Pandavas' wealth and then pushes the players past every limit of compassion and decency.
This is the Kural's descent from lost wealth to lost compassion to deep misery.
Christianity
“For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”
— 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul says love of money leads people away from faith and into many sorrows.
Losing faith and being 'pierced with many sorrows' parallels the Kural's loss of compassion and the misery that follows.
Islam
“Satan only wants to cause between you animosity and hatred through intoxicants and gambling and to avert you from the remembrance of Allah and from prayer...”
— Qur'an 5:91 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says gambling breeds hatred between people and pulls them away from God.
Animosity replacing goodwill is the Kural's arul keduththu, the destruction of compassion.
Judaism
“Since they do not realize the effort required of most people to earn a living they might not be scrupulous about other people’s money, and their testimony cannot be relied upon.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 24b (Steinsaltz's explanatory gloss on Rav Sheshet, Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
The Talmudic discussion explains why a dice-player's word cannot be trusted in court.
The gambler's unreliable testimony is the Kural's poi mer koleei, the turn to falsehood.
Buddhism
“(i) the winner begets hate, (ii) the loser grieves for lost wealth, (iii) loss of wealth, (iv) his word is not relied upon in a court of law...”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), trans. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha's list covers hatred, grief, loss of wealth and loss of credibility.
This covers nearly every step of the Kural's chain: wealth lost, truthfulness doubted, goodwill gone, and grief.
Jainism
“When he is not satisfied with those 'colours,' and his craving for them grows stronger and stronger, he will become discontented, and unhappy by dint of his discontent; misled by greed he will take another's property. When he is overcome by violent desire, takes another's property, and is not satisfied with those 'colours' and their possession, then his deceit and falsehood increase on account of his greed; yet he will not get rid of his misery.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.29–30 (cf. 32.27) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana traces the chain by which unchecked craving leads to discontent, then to taking others' property, then to growing deceit and falsehood, and still to misery; the preceding verse adds that the passionate man torments living beings.
Valluvar lists what gambling does: it ruins wealth, drives a person to lying, kills compassion and leaves him in misery. The Jain verses trace the same chain of craving, lying and lasting misery.
Sikhism
“The truth is perfect and the falsehood a loser gambler running from pillar to post.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 30, Pauri 5 (English translation as published on BaniDB/SikhiToTheMax and searchgurbani.com) [9]
Bhai Gurdas sets truth, which is whole and secure, against falsehood, which he pictures as a gambler who has lost and runs here and there. Lying and losing at dice go together in his image.
The Kural lists gambling as the vice that drives a man to lies, and Bhai Gurdas gives falsehood the very face of a losing gambler.
Greek Mythology
“...I had killed the son of Amphidamas- not of set purpose, but in childish quarrel over the dice.”
— Homer, Iliad 23 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
For the boy Patroclus, a dice game turned into a quarrel and the quarrel into a killing.
This is gambling destroying compassion and bringing lasting sorrow, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“...the other makes gain from his friends, to whom he ought to be giving.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says the gambler takes from his friends when he ought to be generous to them.
Taking from friends where one should give is the Kural's arul keduththu, generosity turned upside down.

Real Life Example

Pete Rose is banned from baseball and denies betting on the game for years
24 August 1989 (admission published January 2004) · New York and Cincinnati, United States
After a six-month investigation found that Pete Rose, baseball's all-time hits leader and manager of the Cincinnati Reds, had bet on baseball games including those of his own team between 1985 and 1987, Commissioner Bart Giamatti placed him on the permanently ineligible list on 24 August 1989. For more than a decade Rose publicly insisted he had never bet on baseball, before admitting it privately to Commissioner Bud Selig in 2002 and publicly in his autobiography in January 2004. The ban kept him out of the Baseball Hall of Fame.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A compulsive gambler who began betting uncontrollably by 1985 lost his place in the game he loved and then spent years living a public lie about it.
The kural says gambling ruins wealth and drives a person to lying, and Rose's gambling cost him his career and led him to deny the truth for fifteen years.
