Kural 1059

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

ஈவார்கண் என்னுண்டாம் தோற்றம் இரந்துகோள் மேவார் இலாஅக் கடை.
Eevaarkan ennundaam thotram irandhukol mevaar ilaaak kadai.

Meaning

If no one wanted to ask and receive, what fame or honor could givers ever have?

Elementary

A goalkeeper can only be a hero if someone kicks the ball. In the same way, a generous person can only shine if someone needs their help.

University

Thotram (தோற்றம், 'appearance, eminence') is glossed as pugazh (புகழ், 'fame') by Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar. Irandhu kol mevaar (இரந்துகோள் மேவார், 'those who wish to take by asking') are the counterpart without whom the giver's eminence cannot exist; ilaaak kadai has the alapedai lengthening ilaaa. Read with 1058, the couplet pair makes askers necessary to the moral economy of giving, a theme going back to Ch. 23 (Eegai) and Ch. 24 (Pugazh), where fame is linked to giving. Pope: 'What glory will there be to men of generous soul, / When none are found to love the askers' role?' The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Karna, famed as the great giver, never refused anyone who came asking. When Indra came disguised as a brahmin and asked for his natural armour and earrings, Karna cut them from his body and gave them, though it cost him his protection, and that gift sealed his fame.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Kundala-harana sub-parva (tr. K. M. Ganguli) (paraphrase) [3]
Karna's lasting glory as dana-vira, the hero of giving, rests on the askers he never turned away.
Without Indra's request there would be no story of Karna's supreme gift, as the Kural says: a giver's glory needs askers.
Christianity
“It is more blessed to give than to receive.”
— Acts 20:35 (KJV) [4]
Paul quotes a saying of Jesus that giving brings a greater blessing than receiving.
If giving is the greater blessing, then receivers make that blessing possible, which is the Kural's point.
Islam
“O people! Give in charity as a time will come upon you when a person will wander about with his object of charity and will not find anybody to accept it”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1411 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet urges people to give now, warning of a time when there will be no one left to accept charity.
He pictures exactly the world the Kural imagines, where givers can find no askers and lose their chance of merit.
Judaism
“Rabbi Akiva said to him: He commands us to sustain the poor, so that through them and the charity we give them we will be saved from the judgment of Gehenna.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 10a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz, via Sefaria) [6]
Answering why God does not feed the poor himself, Rabbi Akiva says the poor exist so that givers may earn merit through them.
The poor are the means of the givers' merit, the Kural's point that askers make givers great.
Buddhism
Prince Vessantara, in the Bodhisattva's last human birth before Gotama, gives away everything he is asked for, from the kingdom's white elephant to his own children, and his perfection of generosity (dana-parami) becomes legendary because of those who came asking.
— Vessantara Jataka (No. 547) (paraphrase) [7]
Vessantara's fame as the perfect giver rests on a series of supplicants.
Without askers, Vessantara's perfection of giving could not have been shown, which is the Kural's claim.
Jainism
Chandana (Chandanbala), a princess sold into servitude and kept in chains, offered boiled lentils to Mahavira at the end of his long fast, fulfilling the strict conditions of his abhigraha vow; her gift made her famous, and she later became head of his order of nuns.
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 10 (life of Mahavira) (tr. Helen M. Johnson) (paraphrase) [8]
In Jain memory Chandana's glory comes from having been the one able to give to the great ascetic.
Her fame as a giver depended on the asker, Mahavira, which is the Kural's point.
Sikhism
“He continues to give, and His Provisions never run short.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 9, So Dar (Rehras), Raag Asa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In So Dar, Guru Nanak praises God as the endless Giver whose gifts to all creatures never run out; this giving is central to how Gurbani praises God's greatness.
The Kural asks what glory givers could have if no one wished to receive; Gurbani likewise praises God above all as the Giver to countless receivers.
Greek Mythology
“you seem to be a chief, foremost among them all; therefore you should be the better giver, and I will tell far and wide of your bounty.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, offers Antinous fame in return for a gift: the beggar will spread the giver's name.
The beggar holds the key to the giver's renown, which is the Kural's idea in story form.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that liberal people are among the most loved of virtuous characters because they are useful to others, and that this depends on their giving.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, the honour of the liberal man rests on his giving to others.
Since that honour depends on giving, it depends on recipients, which matches the Kural's question.

Real Life Example

Mansa Musa's lavish giving in Cairo spreads his fame
1324 · Cairo, Egypt
Mansa Musa, ruler of the Mali Empire, made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 with a vast caravan carrying great quantities of gold. In Cairo he met the Sultan of Egypt, and he and his caravan spent and gave away so much gold that its value in Egypt fell for about twelve years afterwards.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The pilgrimage made Mansa Musa and the wealth of Mali famous across North Africa, the Middle East and Europe.
The kural asks what glory givers could have if there were no one to receive, and Mansa Musa's lasting renown as a giver was spread by the many in Cairo who received his gold.
