Kural 157

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

திறனல்ல தற்பிறர் செய்யினும் நோநொந்து அறனல்ல செய்யாமை நன்று.
Thiranalla tharpirar seyyinum nononthu, Aranalla seyyaamai nandru.

Meaning

Even when others hurt you in ways you did not deserve, it is good to feel sorry for the pain their wrong will bring them, and not to do wrong back.

Elementary

When someone is unfair to you, you might want to be unfair back. This kural says don't. Instead, feel a little sad for them, because doing wrong usually comes back to hurt the person who does it, and keep doing the right thing yourself.

University

The key phrase is நோநொந்து. Mu. Varadarasan reads it as grieving over the suffering (நோ) that will fall on the wrongdoer as the fruit of his act, which makes the couplet an ethic of compassion toward the offender rather than mere self-restraint. Solomon Pappaiah offers a slightly different nuance, grieving at the thought that retaliation would hurt him, but both readings end in refusing அறனல்ல, 'what is not dharma.' The couplet therefore goes beyond tolerance to a positive moral stance: being injured doesn't license you to abandon virtue. Socrates states the same principle in the Crito, that it is never right to return wrong for wrong, and Jesus acts it out on the cross by praying for those who crucify him.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That which is antagonistic to one's own self, should never be applied in respect of another. Briefly even this is virtue.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXIX, Vidura's counsel (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura sums up dharma in one rule: never do to another what you would find hurtful yourself.
Because you know how injury feels, you refuse to inflict it back even when you've been wronged, which is the kural's refusal of 'what is not dharma'.
Christianity
“Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.”
— Luke 23:34 (KJV) [5]
While being crucified, Jesus prays for his executioners and feels for their spiritual danger instead of cursing them.
Grieving for the wrongdoer instead of retaliating is exactly the kural's நோநொந்து.
Islam
“And the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation - his reward is [due] from Allāh. Indeed, He does not like wrongdoers.”
— Qur'an 42:40 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an allows equal retribution but holds up pardon and reconciliation as the better way, rewarded by God.
Both texts steer the injured person away from answering wrong with wrong.
Judaism
“Say not: ‘I will do so to him as he hath done to me; I will render to the man according to his work.’”
— Proverbs 24:29 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs explicitly forbids the tit-for-tat reasoning of paying someone back for what he did to you.
This is the kural's அறனல்ல செய்யாமை: don't answer wrong with wrong.
Buddhism
“Whoever returns insult to one who is insulting, returns taunts to one who is taunting, returns a berating to one who is berating, is said to be eating together, sharing company, with that person.”
— Akkosa Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya 7.2 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
When the brahman Akkosaka abuses him, the Buddha refuses the 'meal' of insults, explaining that anyone who returns abuse joins in the offender's wrongdoing.
Returning wrong for wrong makes you a partner in it, which is why the kural says not to act against dharma even when wronged.
Jainism
“To bear with those that speak contemptuous words; yea, more, to say, ‘Ah, will these sink in the other world to hell, the place of fire, as fruit of their contemptuous words;’ and to grieve, is duty of the perfect man.”
— Nālaṭiyār 58 (ch. 6, 'Renunciation'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [9]
The Nālaṭiyār asks the noble person not only to bear an insult but to grieve over the karmic suffering the insulter is storing up for himself.
Valluvar says that when others wrong us, we should feel sorry for the suffering their wrong will bring them and not retaliate, and the Nālaṭiyār states the same compassion for the wrongdoer's coming fate almost point for point.
Sikhism
“Fareed, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists. Kiss their feet, and return to your own home.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, Salok 7 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid teaches not to hit back at those who hurt us, but to answer them with humility and go home at peace.
The Kural says that even when wronged without cause one should not do anything unrighteous in return; Farid gives the same counsel in concrete form.
Greek Mythology
Old King Priam comes to Achilles' tent to beg for the body of his son Hector. Achilles, who killed Hector and has been abusing his corpse, is moved by Priam, weeps with him, and returns the body.
— Homer, Iliad, Book XXIV (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
At the end of the Iliad, Achilles finally feels for his enemy's grief and stops trying to punish him.
Feeling for the enemy's suffering instead of continuing to strike at him reflects the kural's நோநொந்து.
Philosophy
“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to anyone, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”
— Plato, Crito 49 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
In prison awaiting execution, Socrates refuses to escape unjustly and states that wronging someone in return is never right, however badly you've been treated.
Almost a restatement of the kural: however unfitting the harm received, do nothing unrighteous in return.

Real Life Example

Martin Luther King Jr. bears no ill will toward the woman who stabbed him
20 September 1958 · Harlem, New York City, United States
While signing copies of his book Stride Toward Freedom at Blumstein's department store in Harlem, Martin Luther King Jr. was stabbed in the chest with a letter opener by Izola Ware Curry, a woman who was later judged to be mentally ill. Surgeons at Harlem Hospital removed the blade, which had come close to his aorta. King later stated publicly that he felt no ill will toward Curry and hoped she would receive the help she needed, and he reaffirmed his commitment to nonviolence.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Instead of anger, King answered a near-fatal attack with concern for his attacker's well-being, and he described the experience as deepening his faith in nonviolence.
The kural counsels feeling sorrow for the suffering that awaits the wrongdoer and not returning wrong for wrong; King's response was compassion for the woman who had nearly killed him.
