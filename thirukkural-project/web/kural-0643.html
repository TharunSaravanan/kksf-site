Kural 643

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

கேட்டார்ப் பிணிக்கும் தகையவாய்க் கேளாரும் வேட்ப மொழிவதாம் சொல்.
Kettaarp pinikkum thakaiyavaaik kelaarum Vetpa mozhivadhaam sol.

Meaning

Good speech holds listeners tightly, and it is spoken so well that even people who have not heard it want to hear it.

Elementary

Think of a storyteller so good that the children listening can’t look away, and children walking past stop and want to join in. That is what this kural calls real speaking skill.

University

The key verb piṇittal means to bind or tie (as with a rope), so the listener is pictured as captured by the speech, and vēṭpa means “so as to make them desire”. The contrast kēṭṭār / kēḷār, “those who have heard / those who have not heard”, is read two ways. Parimelazhagar takes kēṭṭār as allies or friends (those already listening) and kēḷār as enemies or those who refuse to listen; this is why Pope’s gloss speaks of binding friends and making even enemies desire. Solomon Pappaiah follows this reading but also records the plainer sense of those who heard before and those who have not yet heard. The couplet is effectively the chapter’s definition of sol-vanmai, ending emphatically with -ām col, “that is speech”.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Hanuman first meets Rama and Lakshmana in the Kishkindha Kanda, Rama praises his speech as faultless, well-ordered and pleasing, saying that such words could win over even an enemy with his sword raised.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 3 [3]
Rama sees Hanuman’s grammar, tone and order as signs of a learned envoy whose words would charm any listener.
An enemy won over by speech is the Kural’s picture of even unwilling hearers longing to listen.
Christianity
“And all bare him witness, and wondered at the gracious words which proceeded out of his mouth.”
— Luke 4:22 (KJV) [4]
In the synagogue at Nazareth the whole audience is held by the grace of Jesus’ words, even those who were sceptical of him.
Listeners held in wonder are what the Kural means by speech that binds those who hear it.
Islam
“Some eloquent speech has the influence of magic.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5146 [5]
After two men from the east gave speeches, the Prophet remarked on how eloquence can move people like a spell, even changing the minds of those who had refused.
The hadith’s magic-like hold on listeners is the Kural’s power to bind hearers.
Judaism
Ecclesiastes says the Preacher sought out pleasing words and wrote truth rightly, and that the words of the wise are like goads and firmly fixed nails.
— Ecclesiastes 12:10-11 (JPS 1917) [6]
Kohelet joins pleasing form with true content, speech that both attracts and holds fast in the mind.
Words that please and stick like nails match speech that draws people in and holds them.
Buddhism
The Brahmāyu Sutta describes the Buddha’s voice as having eight qualities, among them being clear, intelligible, sweet, audible and resonant, so that it reaches the whole assembly and no further.
— Majjhima Nikaya 91 (Brahmāyu Sutta) [7]
Buddhist texts treat the Buddha’s speech as naturally absorbing to every listener in the assembly.
A voice made to reach and hold every listener fits the Kural’s ideal of speech that binds its hearers.
Jainism
“The gods, men, Sabaras, and animals took the language of the Lord for their own.”
— Verse quoted by Devendra's commentary, in Hermann Jacobi's footnote to Uttarādhyayana Sūtra, Lecture 2 (SBE 45, 1895, footnote on p. 8; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jain tradition holds that when Mahavira preached, every listener, human or animal, understood him as if he spoke in their own tongue. Jacobi records this from the commentator Devendra in a note on the Uttaradhyayana.
The kural defines true eloquence as speech that binds those who hear it and draws even those who have not heard it; the Jain image of the Jina's sermon captivating every kind of being is an idealised form of that power.
Sikhism
“Listening-pain and sin are erased.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Jap (Japji Sahib, Pauri 8), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Suniai (Listening) stanzas of Japji, Guru Nanak praises the transforming power of hearing the divine Word, which holds and changes those who attend to it.
The Kural praises speech that binds its hearers and makes others long to hear it; Japji's refrain shows the Sikh sense of a Word so powerful that hearing it alone transforms the listener.
Greek Mythology
Orpheus sings before Hades and Persephone, and his song so enthrals the underworld that the shades weep and the rulers of the dead, who never yield, grant his request.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1-63 [10]
Even the powers most unwilling to listen are captured by Orpheus’ art.
Orpheus moving the unmoved matches the Kural’s speech that makes even those who would not listen long to hear.
Philosophy
In De Oratore, Crassus says nothing seems finer than the power to hold assemblies by speech, win their minds, and move their wills wherever the speaker wishes.
— Cicero, De Oratore 1.30 [11]
Cicero places the orator’s power to capture an audience at the centre of civic life.
Holding an assembly and winning its mind is exactly the binding power the Kural describes.

Real Life Example

Swami Vivekananda addresses the World's Parliament of Religions
11 September 1893 · Chicago, Illinois, United States
On the opening day of the World's Parliament of Religions, held during the Chicago World's Fair, the Hindu monk Swami Vivekananda began his address by greeting the audience as sisters and brothers of America. The crowd of thousands rose and gave him a long ovation, and his short speech on tolerance and the rejection of fanaticism made him one of the stars of the Parliament. Afterwards he lectured widely across the United States on Hindu philosophy and founded the Vedanta Society in New York.
— Sources: [12] [13]
An unknown delegate from India won over a large foreign audience in a few minutes, and the demand to hear him again carried him on lecture tours for years.
The kural describes true eloquence as speech that binds those who hear it and makes even those who have not heard it long to hear it, which is what Vivekananda's Chicago address did.
