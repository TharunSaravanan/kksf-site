Kural 1236

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

தொடியொடு தோள்நெகிழ நோவல் அவரைக் கொடியர் எனக்கூறல் நொந்து.
Thotiyodu thoalnekizha noval avaraik kotiyar enakkooral nondhu.

Meaning

Because my bangles slip and my shoulders are thin, people call him cruel. Hearing him blamed hurts me more than my own pain.

Elementary

Imagine your best friend moves away for a while, and you are sad. If someone says, “Your friend is mean for leaving you,” you might feel even worse, because you still love your friend and don’t want anyone to speak badly of them. That is how the girl in this verse feels.

University

Here the tone turns: after 1235’s accusation, the heroine is pained (nōval, ‘I grieve’) when others, or the confidante, call him koṭiyar (‘cruel’) because of her loosened bangles and thinned shoulders (toṭiyoṭu tōḷ nekiḻa). The final word nontu (‘in sorrow, being hurt’) can be taken with the speakers or with the heroine, and the commentators divide: Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar have the onlookers (kāṇpavar) say it, while Solomon Pappaiah makes the shoulders and bangles themselves the speakers. Pope’s gloss (‘I am greatly pained to hear you call him a cruel man’) addresses the confidante, which accords with the reading associated with Parimelazhagar that the heroine answers her tōḻi. The ethic is the wife’s loyalty (kaṟpu), which forbids hearing the husband reproached even when she is the one who suffers. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Rama, bowing to public gossip, has Sita left in the forest, she sends him a message through Lakshmana that blames no one but her own fate, asks him to care for his people as he would his brothers, and says she grieves less for herself than for the slander against his name.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda, sarga 48 (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki’s Sita refuses to accuse the husband who has caused her suffering and worries instead about his reputation.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Sita is pained by his blame rather than by her own wasting.
Christianity
“Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul’s portrait of love describes a charity that keeps trusting and enduring instead of accusing.
The Kural’s heroine shows that love: she bears the wasting but will not hear her husband condemned.
Islam
The Prophet taught that a believing man should not hate a believing woman: if he dislikes one of her traits, he will be pleased with another.
— Sahih Muslim 1469 (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith asks a spouse not to define the other by a single fault.
The heroine applies this generosity in reverse: she will not let her husband be judged by the one hurt his absence causes.
Judaism
“Joshua ben Perahiah used to say: appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion and judge all men with the scale weighted in his favor.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:6 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah asks that others, especially companions, be judged favourably rather than condemned.
The Kural’s heroine judges her absent husband exactly so, refusing the verdict ‘cruel’ that others pass on him.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada observes that the faults of others are easily seen while one’s own are hard to see, and that people winnow others’ faults like chaff.
— Dhammapada 252 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The verse warns against the quick, public fault-finding that comes so easily to onlookers.
The onlookers in the Kural do just this, pronouncing the husband cruel, and the heroine is hurt by their easy verdict.
Jainism
“Râgîmatî thought: ‘Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him! it is better I should turn nun.’”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Abandoned at her wedding when Arishtanemi renounced the world, Rajimati blames her own life, not him, and resolves to follow him into the ascetic path.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Rajimati suffers the loss but will not call the one who left her cruel.
Sikhism
“Your actions seem so sweet to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 394, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan accepts whatever God does as sweet, the Sikh attitude of living in His will (bhana) without complaint.
The Kural's wife is hurt more by hearing others call her husband cruel than by her own wasting; the Sikh devotee likewise refuses to call the Beloved's doings harsh and finds even hardship from Him sweet.
Greek Mythology
In the underworld, Agamemnon’s shade praises Penelope above all wives because she kept faith with her absent husband through twenty years, so that the gods would make her fame a song for all mankind.
— Homer, Odyssey 24.192-198 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer holds up Penelope as the model of a wife who stays loyal to a husband who has been away for a very long time.
The Kural’s heroine shows the same steadfast loyalty, defending a husband whom others are ready to condemn.
Philosophy
Cicero says a true friend must not only refrain from bringing charges against his friend but must not even believe charges that others bring.
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia 65 (paraphrase) [11]
On Friendship makes loyalty against outside accusation a mark of genuine love.
The Kural’s heroine practises this loyalty, refusing to accept the charge of cruelty that others make against her husband.

Real Life Example

Libbie Custer defends her husband's name for 57 years
1876-1933 · United States
After George Armstrong Custer died at the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876, his widow Elizabeth (Libbie) Bacon Custer learned that President Ulysses S. Grant and senior officers blamed him for the defeat. Widowed at 34 and never remarrying, she spent the rest of her long life protecting his reputation, writing the memoirs Boots and Saddles (1885), Tenting on the Plains (1887) and Following the Guidon (1890). She was buried beside him at the U.S. Military Academy at West Point.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her devotion shaped how Americans remembered Custer for half a century, and critics largely held back while she lived.
The kural's wife is pained more by hearing her husband called cruel than by her own suffering, and Libbie Custer could not bear to hear her husband blamed and spent decades answering his critics.
