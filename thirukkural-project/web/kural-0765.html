Kural 765

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

கூற்றுடன்று மேல்வரினும் கூடி எதிர்நிற்கும் ஆற்ற லதுவே படை.
Kootrudandru melvarinum koodi edhirnirkum aatraladhuve padai.

Meaning

A true army is one that can stand together and fight even if Death himself comes at it in anger.

Elementary

Picture a group of friends holding hands in a line during a strong wind on the beach. Alone, each might be pushed over, but together they stay standing. Valluvar says a real army stands together like that, even against the scariest danger.

University

Kūṟṟu (கூற்று) is Death personified, identified by commentators with Yama, and uṭaṉṟu (உடன்று) means ‘having become enraged’. The key word is kūṭi (கூடி, ‘together, united’): the excellence praised is collective resolve rather than individual heroism, and āṟṟal (ஆற்றல்) is the strength to hold. Parimelazhagar notes that even the god of death cannot be resisted by ordinary means, so the hyperbole stresses unity as the army’s defining power. The two sources agree apart from sandhi (கூடி யெதிர்நிற்கு / மாற்ற லதுவே).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Do thou fight for the sake of fighting, without considering happiness or distress, loss or gain, victory or defeat—and, by so doing, you shall never incur sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.38 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna urges Arjuna to face the battle with equanimity, unmoved by fear of loss or even death.
It reflects the Kural’s resolve to stand firm even when Death advances.
Christianity
“Wherefore take unto you the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to withstand in the evil day, and having done all, to stand.”
— Ephesians 6:13 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the church, addressed collectively, to be equipped to hold its ground on the worst of days.
‘Having done all, to stand’ is the Kural’s collective resistance even against Death.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah loves those who fight in His cause in a row as though they are a [single] structure joined firmly.”
— Qur’an 61:4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The sura named ‘The Ranks’ praises fighters who hold together like a solid building.
The firmly joined structure is exactly the Kural’s kūṭi etirniṟkum, standing together against the onslaught.
Judaism
While rebuilding Jerusalem’s walls under threat, Nehemiah arms the builders, tells them not to fear but to remember the LORD, and orders that wherever the trumpet sounds they should rally together there.
— Nehemiah 4:8–14 (English 4:14–20) [6]
The scattered workers become a single defensive body ready to meet any attack.
It embodies the Kural’s united stand against a threatening enemy.
Buddhism
“What have you heard, Ananda: do the Vajjis assemble and disperse peacefully and attend to their affairs in concord?” ... “So long, Ananda, as this is the case, the growth of the Vajjis is to be expected, not their decline.”
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahaparinibbana Sutta 1.4 (trans. Sister Vajira & Francis Story) [7]
The Buddha tells Ajatasattu’s minister that the Vajjis cannot be conquered while they remain united.
Concord as the source of invincibility is the heart of the Kural’s kūṭi, ‘standing together’.
Jainism
“Having heard (this) from the venerable men who control themselves and subdue their senses, the virtuous and the learned do not tremble in the hour of death.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's teaching on the two kinds of death says that those trained in self-control meet death calmly and without fear.
The Kural praises an army that stands firm even when Death himself comes on in fury, and the Jain verse describes the same fearless steadiness before death.
Sikhism
On Baisakhi in 1699 at Anandpur, Guru Gobind Singh asked for volunteers ready to give their heads. Five came forward, and they became the Panj Pyare, the first initiates of the Khalsa, drinking amrit stirred with a double-edged sword from one bowl and all taking the name Singh.
— The Conversation, 'Why Sikhs celebrate the festival of Baisakhi' (2024) (paraphrase) [9]
The Khalsa was founded as one body of initiates, of mixed backgrounds, who had shown at the start that they would face death together.
The Kural says the true army is one that stands together and faces even Death in fury; the Khalsa began with men ready to die, joined by a shared initiation into a single body.
Greek Mythology
“Divide your men, Agamemnon, into their several tribes and clans, that clans and tribes may stand by and help one another.”
— Homer, Iliad, Book 2 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Nestor advises Agamemnon to array the Achaeans so that kin stand beside kin and support each other.
His counsel aims at the same mutual support that lets the Kural’s army stand together against Death.
Philosophy
“The Moral Law causes the people to be in complete accord with their ruler, so that they will follow him regardless of their lives, undismayed by any danger.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 1.5–6 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu names moral unity between ruler and people as the first factor in war.
Complete accord that holds ‘regardless of their lives’ is the Kural’s united stand even against Death.

Real Life Example

The Knights of St John hold Malta against the Ottoman siege
May-September 1565 · Malta
In May 1565 a large Ottoman force landed on Malta to take the island from the Knights Hospitaller under Grand Master Jean de Valette. The much smaller garrison of knights, soldiers and Maltese defenders held out for nearly four months, losing Fort St Elmo but keeping the main fortifications. When a relief force from Sicily landed in September the Ottomans withdrew, and Malta still marks 8 September as a public holiday.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Britannica notes that the defence halted the Ottoman advance into the western Mediterranean and shattered their reputation for invincibility.
The kural says that is truly an army which can stand together and face the foe even if Death himself comes raging, and Malta's defenders held as one through months when death was daily expected.
