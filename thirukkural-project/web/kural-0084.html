Kural 84

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

அகனமர்ந்து செய்யாள் உறையும் முகனமர்ந்து நல்விருந்து ஓம்புவான் இல்.
Aganamarndhu seyyaal uraiyum muganamarndhu, Nalvirundhu ombuvaan il.

Meaning

Lakshmi, the goddess of good fortune, gladly makes her home in the house of someone who welcomes good guests with a smiling face.

Elementary

Good luck is pictured here as a guest too — and she only stays where she feels welcome. If you greet visitors with a warm, happy face, Fortune decides she likes it in your house and moves in. A frown at the door sends her away again.

University

The verse turns on a deliberate mirroring: அகன் அமர்ந்து ('settling gladly within') describes the goddess, while முகன் அமர்ந்து ('gladdening the face') describes the host — the same verb அமர் binds the host's countenance to the deity's contentment. செய்யாள், 'the red one,' is the Tamil epithet for Lakshmi/Śrī, the goddess of prosperity, invoked here not as an object of worship but as a personified consequence. Crucially, Valluvar specifies நல்விருந்து, the *good* guest, introducing discrimination into hospitality that the chapter elsewhere leaves open. The verse also quietly introduces the chapter's second great theme, which will dominate Kural 90: that the host's face, not the host's larder, is the operative instrument of hospitality. It is one of the few places in the Aṟattuppāl where Valluvar permits himself a mythological figure, and even here Śrī functions less as a deity than as a way of saying that wealth behaves like a guest and stays where it is well received.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him not eat any (dainty) food which he does not offer to his guest; the hospitable reception of guests procures wealth, fame, long life, and heavenly bliss.”
— Manusmriti 3.106 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East) [4]
Manu's householder chapter states the reward of hospitality in strictly worldly terms first — wealth and reputation — before mentioning heaven at all.
The same promise as the Kural: prosperity takes up residence with the householder who receives guests well.
Christianity
“Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.”
— 2 Corinthians 9:7 (King James Version) [5]
Paul, collecting for the Jerusalem church, insists that the disposition of the giver, not the size of the gift, is what is acceptable — and promises that God will make all grace abound toward such a giver.
Both verses make the giver's gladness the decisive quality and attach blessing specifically to it.
Islam
“Then he went to his family and came with a fat [roasted] calf. And placed it near them; he said, ‘Will you not eat?’”
— Qur'an 51:26–27 (Sahih International) [6]
Abraham, meeting unannounced strangers, does not merely permit them to stay but hurries off to his household and returns with the best animal he has, pressing it on them — and the visit ends with the good tidings of a learned son.
The eager, warm host of the Qur'anic account is exactly the Kural's smiling-faced householder, and his house is blessed in consequence.
Judaism
“Hospitality toward guests is greater than receiving the Divine Presence.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 127a (Koren Steinsaltz translation) [7]
Rav Yehuda in the name of Rav derives this from Genesis 18:3, where Abraham, in the middle of an encounter with God, asks God to wait while he attends to three approaching strangers.
Where the Kural says the goddess of fortune settles in the welcoming house, the Talmud says the Divine Presence itself is what the guest displaces — and outranks.
Buddhism
Anāthapiṇḍika, the Sāvatthī merchant whose very name means 'feeder of the destitute,' bought Prince Jeta's grove by covering it with gold coins and kept his house perpetually open to the Sangha and the poor; the canon names him the foremost of lay donors, and he remained one of the wealthiest men in the city.
— Anāthapiṇḍika narratives, Pali Canon (Vinaya Cullavagga VI; Aṅguttara Nikāya) [8]
The Pali tradition consistently presents his fortune and his hospitality as companions rather than competitors, and portrays him as a serene and delighted rather than grudging giver.
Illustrates the Kural's picture of a glad host in whose house prosperity settles and stays.
Jainism
“At that moment the gods caused a rain of perfumed water and flowers, and showered down heavenly treasures; they struck the drums, and in the air they praised the gift.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
After the Brahmin household finally feeds the outcaste-born monk Harikeśa with respect, gods shower treasure on the place of the gift. Jain tradition tells of this kind of shower at worthy almsgiving.
Valluvar pictures Fortune gladly settling in the house of a cheerful host, and the Jain story has wealth literally rain down where a guest is honoured.
Sikhism
“Balwand says that Khivi, the Guru's wife, is a noble woman, who gives soothing, leafy shade to all. She distributes the bounty of the Guru's Langar; the kheer - the rice pudding and ghee, is like sweet ambrosia.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 967, Raag Raamkalee (Ramkali ki Vaar), Satta and Balwand (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
The minstrels Satta and Balwand, in a ballad included in the Guru Granth Sahib, praise Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, for the warmth with which she ran the langar and the abundance it gave everyone.
As the Kural says Fortune dwells in the house of one who welcomes guests gladly, the scripture pictures plenty flowing from a household mistress whose welcome was shade to all.
Greek Mythology
Hestia, goddess of the hearth, was honoured with the first and the last portion of every household libation and was believed to dwell permanently at the centre of the house; the Homeric Hymn calls on her to come into the house and dwell there, bestowing grace on the dwelling.
— Homeric Hymn 29, To Hestia [11]
Greek domestic religion placed a goddess literally inside the home, at the fire where guests were received, making her presence identical with the house being a proper household at all.
Supplies the same image the Kural uses — a goddess taking up residence in the well-kept, welcoming house.
Philosophy
“Hospitality also is a theme of Theophrastus's praise, and rightly so. For, as it seems to me at least, it is most proper that the homes of distinguished men should be open to distinguished guests.”
— Cicero, De Officiis (On Duties), Book II, §64 (tr. Walter Miller (Loeb, 1913)) [12]
Cicero commends hospitality as a fitting use of wealth and adds that, for those seeking honourable influence, generous entertainment of guests brings popularity and standing at home and abroad; he cites Cimon’s open-handedness as an example.
Both texts treat the open, welcoming home as a source of good fortune for the host, though Valluvar stresses the glad face and casts the reward as Fortune herself taking up residence.

Real Life Example

Gander takes in the passengers of 38 diverted airliners
11-15 September 2001 · Gander, Newfoundland, Canada
When North American airspace was closed after the attacks of 11 September 2001, 38 airliners landed at Gander, bringing nearly 7,000 passengers and crew to a town of about 10,000 people. Residents housed the travellers in schools, halls and their own homes for several days, volunteers fed them, local businesses donated supplies and pharmacists filled prescriptions at no charge. The town was later honoured in Washington, and Lufthansa named one of its aircraft Gander-Halifax in recognition of the care given to passengers.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Gander's cheerful, open-handed welcome to thousands of strangers earned it lasting gratitude, honour and goodwill around the world.
The kural says fortune comes to dwell gladly with the one who welcomes guests with a glad face, and Gander's warm welcome brought the town honour and lasting friendship.
