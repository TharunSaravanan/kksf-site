Kural 1050

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

துப்புர வில்லார் துவரத் துறவாமை உப்பிற்கும் காடிக்கும் கூற்று.
Thuppura villaar thuvarath thuRavaamai uppiRkum kaadikkum koottru.

Meaning

If very poor people do not fully give up worldly life, they become like death to other people's salt and rice-water (thin rice soup), only eating up what others have.

Elementary

Imagine someone who has nothing and keeps taking a little salt and soup from the neighbours, but does nothing else with his life. Valluvar says that person should either change his life completely or choose a simple life of giving things up, instead of just using up what others need.

University

Thuppuravu (துப்புரவு) means the things one enjoys or lives by, so thuppuravillaar are ‘those without any means’. Thuvara (‘completely’) thuRavaamai (‘not renouncing’) is the fault named: Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar say the only course left to such people is full renunciation, and failing it they are merely koottru (கூற்று, Yama, death) to uppu (salt) and kaadi (காடி, sour rice-water or gruel; Pope’s ‘vinegar’). Solomon Pappaiah sharpens the social point: by staying in household life they consume the salt and gruel in other people’s homes. Kalaignar reads thuppuravu as orderliness, so the subject becomes those impoverished by disorderly living. The chapter thus ends by pointing toward thuRavu (renunciation), linking the Porutpaal back to the Thuravaraviyal of the first book, and leads into Iravu (begging) in ch. 106. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“...said that poverty is for recluses. Making no provision for the morrow is a practice that suits Rishis.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna reports Nahusha’s verdict that a life without possessions suits only ascetics who have renounced the world.
This matches the Kural’s point: total want is fitting only for those who fully renounce.
Christianity
“For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat.”
— 2 Thessalonians 3:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Thessalonians that those who will not work should not live off others.
Both texts object to the person who simply consumes others’ food, though Paul’s remedy is work, the Kural’s is renunciation.
Islam
“An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign. They do not ask people persistently [or at all].”
— Qur’an 2:273 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises the dignified poor who, through restraint, refrain from begging and burdening others.
Like the Kural, it holds that the destitute should not become a drain on others, commending restraint instead.
Judaism
“Skin a carcass in the market and take payment, but do not say: I am a priest, or: I am a great man, and this matter disgusts me.”
— Talmud Bavli, Pesachim 113a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
Rav tells Rav Kahana to take even the lowliest paid work rather than become dependent on others.
The Kural condemns living off others’ salt and gruel; the Talmud insists on any honest labour to avoid dependence.
Buddhism
“Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth.”
— Dhammapada 204 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada teaches that contentment with little is itself the highest form of wealth.
The Kural tells the destitute to renounce fully; the Buddhist answer is that contentment turns having little into true riches.
Jainism
“An ascetic should not lay by any store, not even so little as the grease (sticking to his alms-bowl); but as a bird with its plumage, so he with his alms-bowl should wander about without desires.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain monk owns only his bowl, stores nothing, and lives on what is freely given for his bare survival.
This is the complete renunciation (thuvarath thuRavu) the Kural says the destitute should embrace instead of lingering as a burden.
Sikhism
“But better than these is dry bread, if one sings the Glorious Praises of the Lord in the Society of the Saints.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 150) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir says that alms-bread eaten while praising God in holy company is better than royal wealth and pleasures. The Sikh Gurus, however, reject living by begging and ask even renunciants to earn by honest work.
The Kural says the utterly destitute should renounce fully rather than live off other people's salt and gruel; Kabir likewise prefers a bare, devotional life to grasping, but Sikh teaching as a whole rejects the renunciant path the Kural recommends.
Greek Mythology
“Both gods and men are angry with a man who lives idle, for in nature he is like the stingless drones...”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 303-304 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod compares the idle man to drones who eat the honey that worker bees produce.
The drone that consumes others’ stores is the Kural’s destitute man who is death to his neighbours’ salt and gruel.
Philosophy
“Provide things relating to the body no further than mere use; as meat, drink, clothing, house, family. But strike off and reject everything relating to show and delicacy.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus counsels limiting one’s bodily needs to bare use and rejecting everything beyond it.
The Stoic minimalism approaches the Kural’s call to renounce rather than to lean on others for more than one needs.

Real Life Example

Gandhi adopts the loincloth at Madurai
22 September 1921 · Madurai, Tamil Nadu, India
On his tour of South India in 1921, while urging people to boycott foreign cloth, Gandhi met many who said they were too poor to buy khadi to replace what they wore. In Madurai on 22 September 1921 he gave up his shirt and cap and adopted the simple loincloth (some accounts say dhoti and shawl), explaining that he hesitated to advise anything he was not prepared to follow himself and wanted to make the change easier for those who could not afford new clothes.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gandhi chose to reduce his own needs to the level of the poorest rather than ask more of them, and the simple dress became his lasting symbol.
The kural says that those who have nothing should embrace full renunciation rather than linger as a burden on others, and Gandhi's decision at Madurai was a voluntary renunciation that identified him with India's poorest.
