Kural 1259

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

புலப்பல் எனச்சென்றேன் புல்லினேன் நெஞ்சம் கலத்தல் உறுவது கண்டு.
Pulappal enachchendraen pullinaen nenjam kalaththal uruvadhu kandu.

Meaning

I went to him meaning to sulk and hold back. But when I saw my heart already rushing to join him, I hugged him.

Elementary

Imagine planning to stay grumpy with a friend who came back late. But when you see them, your heart feels so happy that you forget to be grumpy and give them a big hug instead. That is what happened to the heroine.

University

The couplet turns on the Tamil love convention of ūṭal (lovers’ sulking or feigned pique): pulappal, ‘I will sulk’, was her plan, but pulliṉēṉ, ‘I embraced’, was the result, because she saw her neñcam (heart) already beginning kalattal (union, mingling) with him. The heart acting independently of the self continues the preceding chapter, neñcoṭu kiḷattal (‘speaking to one’s heart’). Mu. Varadarasanar reads ‘my heart leaving me and joining him’, and Solomon Pappaiah elaborates that she stepped away to sulk but, seeing her heart go to him without restraint, gave up and embraced him. Sivayogi Sivakumar’s gloss gives an allegorical (yogic) reading, a minority view. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; Pope’s rendering there (‘I’ll shun his greeting’; saying thus with pride away I went: / I held him in my arms, for straight I felt my heart relent) follows the standard reading.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, Radha, who had held out against Krishna in wounded pride, is urged by her friend to go to him; she finally enters his bower, and her anger dissolves in the joy of seeing him.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, cantos 11-12 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s poem turns on Radha’s māna (proud sulking) giving way when she sees her beloved.
This is the Kural’s situation: she meant to sulk, but on seeing him her resolve melted into embrace.
Christianity
“I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go.”
— Song of Solomon 3:4 (KJV) [4]
After searching anxiously, the bride finds her beloved and holds him fast.
The Kural’s heroine also ends up embracing the one she meant to hold off, because her heart has already gone to him.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an sees affection and mercy between spouses as a sign of God, a force that brings them back to calm together.
The Kural’s heroine experiences that affection overriding her plan to sulk.
Judaism
“And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him; and they wept.”
— Genesis 33:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Esau, who had long held a grievance against Jacob, runs to him and embraces him the moment they meet.
As in the Kural, a resolve to hold back gives way at the meeting, and an embrace takes its place.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that resentment ends only when it is met with its opposite.
The Kural’s heroine finds her small resentment dissolved not by argument but by her own heart’s affection.
Jainism
“Having heard these well-spoken words of the virtuous lady, he returned to the Law like an elephant driven by the hook.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.46 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Rathanemi had set out to win Rajimati, but on hearing her words his intention reverses and he returns to his vows.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Rathanemi meets the other person intending one thing and does the opposite, though his reversal runs from passion to restraint, not from sulking to embrace.
Sikhism
“The bride may be hunch-backed and short, but if she is loved by her Lord Master, she becomes beautiful, and she melts in the Lord's embrace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 527, Raag Dayv Gandhaaree, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that whatever the bride's flaws, once her Lord loves her she melts into His embrace.
The Kural's wife went meaning to sulk but, seeing her heart already rushing to him, embraced him instead; Guru Ram Das's bride likewise melts into her Lord's embrace.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus describes the secret of their bed, Penelope, who had held back from him in doubt, feels her knees and heart give way, bursts into tears, runs to him and throws her arms around his neck.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.205-208 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows a wife’s long-guarded reserve dissolving in a single moment into an embrace.
This is the Kural’s scene: she came holding back, but when her heart went to him she embraced him.
Philosophy
Aristophanes describes how the separated halves of the original human beings, longing for each other, run together and throw their arms about each other, wanting to grow into one.
— Plato, Symposium 191a-b (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato’s speaker presents love as an irresistible drive toward union with one’s other half.
The heroine’s heart going to ‘mingle’ (kalattal) with her husband is this same drive toward union, which overrides her plan to sulk.

Real Life Example

Abraham Lincoln and Mary Todd reconcile and marry
1 January 1841 - 4 November 1842 · Springfield, Illinois, United States
Abraham Lincoln and Mary Todd, engaged in Springfield, broke off their courtship on 1 January 1841, a day Lincoln later called the fatal first of January; Mary's sister and brother-in-law disapproved of the match. After more than a year apart, friends brought them together again and they resumed their courtship in secret. They married on 4 November 1842 at her sister's house, and Mary told her sister they were courting again only on the wedding day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A couple who had separated in hurt found, when brought together again, that their attachment was stronger than their resolve to stay apart.
The kural's heroine set out to sulk but embraced her husband when she saw her heart already running to him; Lincoln and Mary Todd likewise gave up their separation once they met again.
