Kural 851

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இகலென்ப எல்லா உயிர்க்கும் பகலென்னும் பண்பின்மை பாரிக்கும் நோய்.
Ikalenba ellaa uyirkkum pakalennum panbinmai paarikkum noi.

Meaning

Hostility (the wish to fight and oppose others) is called a disease. In every living being, it spreads the bad habit of not getting along with others.

Elementary

Think of a bad cold that spreads from one kid in class to everyone. Grudges work the same way: one person stops getting along with another, and soon nobody can play together. The poet says that kind of falling-out is a sickness, and it can catch anyone.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a definition: ikal (இகல், hostility, mental antagonism) is a noi (நோய், disease) that ‘spreads out’ or ‘nourishes’ (paarikkum, பாரிக்கும்) pakal (பகல், separation, non-union) in all creatures. The wordplay is deliberate: pakal normally means ‘daytime’ but here carries its older sense of ‘division’, which Puliyur Kesigan glosses as kūdāmai (கூடாமை, not coming together) and Mu. Varadarasanar as ‘failing to harmonise with other lives’. The traditional commentators following Parimelazhagar treat ikal as the inner root from which outward enmity (pagai, the subject of the next chapter, Pagaimaatchi) grows, which explains why this chapter sits in the Natpiyal (friendship) section of Porutpal. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads ikal unusually as ‘contempt’ (இகழ்ச்சி) that resists another’s nature; Kalaignar emphasises its social result, people unable to live together. The couplet text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.37 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna names desire-turned-wrath as the one enemy that devours everything in the world.
Valluvar likewise names a single inner disease, hostility, as the common affliction of all living beings.
Christianity
“And if a kingdom be divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand. And if a house be divided against itself, that house cannot stand.”
— Mark 3:24-25 (KJV) [4]
Jesus points out that any household or realm torn by internal division destroys itself.
The Kural’s pakal, disunion, is exactly the division Jesus says no house can survive.
Islam
“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided. And remember the favor of Allah upon you - when you were enemies and He brought your hearts together and you became, by His favor, brothers.”
— Qur’an 3:103 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an commands unity and recalls how enemies were turned into brothers by God’s grace.
Both texts treat division between hearts as the core danger and union as the remedy.
Judaism
“Rabbi Joshua said: an evil eye, the evil inclination, and hatred for humankind put a person out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:11 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah lists hatred of people (sinat ha-briyot) among the three traits that destroy a person.
Like Valluvar, the rabbis see hatred of others as a ruinous inner condition rather than a passing mood.
Buddhism
“There is no fire like lust and no crime like hatred.”
— Dhammapada 202 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada ranks hatred as the worst of wrongs, alongside lust as the worst fire.
Both texts single out hostility as the gravest affliction of living beings.
Jainism
“Anger destroys love, pride destroys modesty, deceit destroys friendship; greed is destructive of everything.”
— Saman Suttam v. 135 (Samyamasūtra; drawn from Daśavaikālika Sūtra 8; English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [8]
The Jain passions (kaṣāyas) are presented as forces that eat away at the bonds between beings, with anger first destroying love.
Valluvar calls hostility a disease that breeds separation in every creature, and the Jain verse likewise makes the passions destroyers of love and friendship, the very bonds hostility breaks.
Sikhism
“I have totally forgotten my jealousy of others, since I found the Saadh Sangat, the Company of the Holy. … No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes a mind cured of jealousy in the company of the holy: it no longer counts anyone as enemy or stranger, and it gets along with all.
The Kural defines hostility as the disease that breeds the habit of not getting along with others; Guru Arjan describes the healthy opposite state, in which one gets along with everyone.
Greek Mythology
Eris, goddess of Strife, uninvited to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, tossed a golden apple marked for the fairest among the guests, setting Hera, Athena and Aphrodite against each other and, through the Judgement of Paris, starting the Trojan War.
— Eris and the Apple of Discord (Cypria, summarized by Proclus; Theoi) [10]
One act of spiteful discord spread until it engulfed gods, heroes and whole cities.
The myth dramatizes the Kural’s idea that discord is a contagion that spreads to all.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Republic, Socrates argues that injustice breeds factions, hatreds and quarrels that make any group, and even a single soul, incapable of acting together and an enemy to itself.
— Plato, Republic I, 351d-352a [11]
Plato holds that internal division paralyses both cities and individuals.
Valluvar’s ‘disease that breeds disunion’ is the same diagnosis of self-defeating division.

Real Life Example

Florence's Guelfs split into Blacks and Whites and Dante is exiled
1300-1302 · Florence, Italy
Around 1300 the Guelf party that governed Florence divided into two hostile factions, the Blacks led by Corso Donati and the Whites, to which the poet Dante Alighieri belonged. In 1302 the Blacks, allied with Pope Boniface VIII, took control of the city and expelled the Whites. Dante, who was away on a diplomatic mission, was condemned and banished, and spent the rest of his life in exile, during which he wrote the Divine Comedy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The feud shows how hostility within a single ruling party could tear a prosperous city apart and drive out even its greatest poet.
The kural calls hostility a disease that feeds the refusal to live together, and in Florence it split even former allies of one party into factions that could no longer share the city.
