Kural 901

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

மனைவிழைவார் மாண்பயன் எய்தார் வினைவிழைவார் வேண்டாப் பொருளும் அது.
Manaivizhaivaar maanpayan eydhaar vinaivizhaivaar ventaap porulum adhu.

Meaning

Men who are ruled by desire for their wives miss the greatest reward in life. People who want to get real work done stay away from this very habit.

Elementary

Imagine a team captain who stops practising because he only wants to hang out at home all day. He may be comfortable, but the team never wins. People who want to do big things keep their comfort in balance with their work.

University

The chapter title penvazhich seral (பெண்வழிச் சேறல்) literally means 'going by a woman's way', and Parimelazhagar frames the whole chapter as advice to a ruler or man of affairs within the Porul (polity) section, placed among other vices to be avoided (chapters 92-94 on wanton women, drink and gambling). In this opening couplet manai-vizhaivaar (மனைவிழைவார், those who crave the wife or household pleasure) fail to reach maan-payan (மாண்பயன், the excellent fruit), which Parimelazhagar glosses as aram (virtue), while vinai-vizhaivaar (வினைவிழைவார், those who desire action) reject such indulgence as a hindrance to porul (wealth-making). Textual note: ytamizh and thirukkural.net read the third word as vinaivizhaiyaar (வினைவிழையார், 'those who do not desire action'), while thirukkural.com, kuraltheni.com and Parimelazhagar's own gloss read vinaivizhaivaar; we follow the latter. Also note that thirukkural.com wrongly prints Kalaignar's gloss for Kural 801 under this verse. Modern critics point out that the chapter assumes a male, elite reader and a patriarchal household; many contemporary readers (including Mu. Varadarasanar's 'considering domestic comfort paramount') take its target to be infatuation and neglect of duty rather than women as such, a reading supported by Valluvar's high praise of the wife in chapter 6 (வாழ்க்கைத் துணைநலம்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [5]
Krishna traces how fixing the mind on pleasing objects grows into attachment and craving that cloud judgment.
The Kural's doting husband is a case of this chain: absorption in domestic pleasure crowds out the higher aim.
Christianity
“But he that is married careth for the things that are of the world, how he may please his wife.”
— 1 Corinthians 7:33 (KJV) [6]
Paul observes that marriage naturally turns a man's attention toward pleasing his spouse and worldly concerns, as against undivided devotion to God.
Like the Kural, it notes that preoccupation with pleasing a wife can draw energy away from a higher calling, though Paul does not condemn marriage.
Islam
“O you who have believed, let not your wealth and your children divert you from the remembrance of Allāh. And whoever does that - then those are the losers.”
— Qur'an 63:9 (Saheeh International) [7]
The Qur'an warns that even legitimate attachments to family and property can distract from the highest purpose.
The Kural similarly warns that domestic attachment can cost a person the 'excellent fruit' of life.
Judaism
The Torah exempts a newly married man from military service and public duties for one year so that he may stay home and bring joy to his wife.
— Deuteronomy 24:5 [6]
Jewish law sanctifies a period of devoted attention to a new spouse, but bounds it in time before public obligations resume.
This offers a counterpoint: devotion to a wife is honored, yet the Kural's concern is with such devotion swallowing a person's duties permanently.
Buddhism
“That is not a strong fetter, the wise say, which is made of iron, wood or hemp. But the infatuation and longing for jewels and ornaments, children and wives — that, they say, is a far stronger fetter, which pulls one downward and, though seemingly loose, is hard to remove.”
— Dhammapada 345-346 (Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada calls infatuation with family and possessions a fetter stronger than any chain.
The Kural's doting husband is held by just such a 'loose but strong' fetter that keeps him from higher attainment.
Jainism
“For a fool who is bound in the fetters of sensuality will be subject to delusion again and again.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The Sutrakritanga's lecture on women says that a man bound by sensual attachment keeps falling back into delusion (moha), which in Jain terms binds fresh karma and blocks liberation.
Like Kural 901, it says that a man given over to desire for a woman loses life's highest goal, which for Jainism is moksha.
Sikhism
“In this world, I have seen love to be false. Whether they are spouses or friends, all are concerned only with their own happiness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 536, Raag Dayv Gandhaaree, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Tegh Bahadur warns that attachment (moh) to spouse and friends cannot be life's goal, since worldly ties are bound up with self-interest and do not lead to God.
The Kural says that doting on a wife costs a man life's highest fruit; Gurbani agrees that attachment can pull one from the highest aim, but it applies this to every worldly tie and to both partners.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus lingers a full year feasting in Circe's palace until his crew remind him of home and he resumes his journey.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 10 [11]
The hero's comfortable stay with an enchantress delays his true task until his companions rouse him.
As in the Kural, absorption in pleasure with a woman stalls the man of action until he recalls his purpose.
Philosophy
Aristotle analyzes akrasia, the condition of the person who knows what is best but is overcome by desire for pleasure and acts against it.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII [12]
For Aristotle, the incontinent person's reason is overpowered by appetite, so his choices fail his own better judgment.
The Kural's man who chooses domestic pleasure over his duties fits Aristotle's portrait of a will weakened by pleasure.

Real Life Example

Edward VIII gives up the throne to marry Wallis Simpson
10-11 December 1936 · Fort Belvedere and London, United Kingdom
Edward VIII succeeded to the throne in January 1936 and made clear that he intended to marry Wallis Simpson, an American who had divorced twice and who the government held would not be acceptable as queen. After Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin rejected a proposed morganatic marriage, Edward signed an Instrument of Abdication on 10 December 1936, witnessed by his three brothers, and it took legal effect the next day. In a radio broadcast he said he could not carry the burden of kingship without the help and support of the woman he loved, and he married her in France in 1937.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
A reigning monarch gave up the highest office in the land rather than give up the woman he wished to marry.
The kural says those wholly given over to desire for a wife miss life's highest fruit, while those intent on their duties avoid that path; Edward chose the marriage over the crown.
