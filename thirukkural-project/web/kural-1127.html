Kural 1127

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

கண்ணுள்ளார் காத லவராகக் கண்ணும் எழுதேம் கரப்பாக்கு அறிந்து.
Kannullaar kaadha lavaraagak kannum ezhudhaem karappaakku arindhu.

Meaning

Because my lover lives in my eyes, I do not even put kohl (dark eyeliner) on them, knowing it would hide him.

Elementary

If you were keeping a tiny friend inside a glass jar, you wouldn't paint the jar, because then you couldn't see them. The girl in this poem doesn't put on eye makeup because she imagines her love is inside her eyes and the makeup would cover him.

University

The heroine continues the eye conceit: since her lover dwells in her eyes (kannullaar), she will not even 'write' her eyes (kannum ezhudhaem), ezhudhu (எழுது) being the Tamil verb for applying mai (மை, kohl or collyrium), for fear of karappu (கரப்பு, concealment) of him. Applying kohl was a basic daily adornment of the akam heroine, so giving it up shows how completely her life is ordered around him. The umm in kannum ('even the eyes') implies she forgoes other adornment too. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar agree on 'lest he be hidden'; Pope: 'My love doth ever in my eyes reside; / I stain them not, fearing his form to hide.' Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa's Cloud Messenger, the exiled yaksha imagines his wife at home giving up her ornaments, her hair bound in a single plain braid, as she waits for him.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Uttaramegha (paraphrase) [3]
In Sanskrit poetry, a devoted woman lays aside adornment while her beloved is absent, because there is no one she wishes to adorn herself for.
The Kural's heroine also gives up adornment for her lover's sake, though for the playful reason that he lives in her eyes.
Christianity
“Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; But let it be the hidden man of the heart”
— 1 Peter 3:3-4 (KJV) [4]
Peter advises that true beauty is the inner person, not outward adornment.
The Kural's heroine sets aside outward adornment for the sake of the one she carries within, a 'hidden man' of her own.
Islam
The Qur'an directs believing women not to display their adornment except to their husbands and close family.
— Qur'an 24:31 (paraphrase) [5]
Adornment is treated as something that belongs within the bond of marriage.
The Kural's heroine arranges her adornment entirely around her beloved; Islam likewise orients a wife's adornment toward her husband.
Judaism
Jeremiah warns faithless Judah that though she paints her eyes and puts on finery to attract lovers, she beautifies herself in vain.
— Jeremiah 4:30 (paraphrase) [6]
The prophet uses eye-paint as the image of adornment that seeks the wrong love.
The Kural's heroine refuses eye-paint out of faithfulness to her one lover, the reverse of the prophet's image of faithless adornment.
Buddhism
On the Uposatha observance days, lay followers take extra precepts, including abstaining from garlands, perfumes and cosmetics.
— Anguttara Nikaya 8.41, Uposatha Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
Setting aside adornment is a Buddhist practice of restraint, done for one's own spiritual progress.
The Kural's heroine also lays aside cosmetics, but out of love for the one in her eyes rather than for restraint.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra lists the use of collyrium on the eyes among the practices forbidden to a Jain monk.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra, ch. 3 (Kshullakacara) (paraphrase) [8]
Even eye-cosmetics count as self-adornment that feeds attachment to the body.
The Kural's heroine gives up kohl out of love; the Jain monk gives it up out of detachment.
Sikhism
“I have applied the healing ointment of spiritual wisdom to my eyes; the Naam, the Name of the Lord, is my food. These are all my decorations.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 542, Raag Bihaagraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan's bride-soul replaces the usual kohl (anjan) and adornments with the Guru's wisdom and the Name, so that her eyes are made ready only for the Beloved.
The Kural's heroine refuses kohl so as not to hide the lover dwelling in her eyes; the Sikh bride-soul sets aside ordinary kohl for the 'ointment of wisdom' that reveals the Lord.
Greek Mythology
When her maid urges Penelope to wash and anoint her face before appearing to the suitors, she refuses, saying the gods destroyed her beauty on the day Odysseus sailed for Troy.
— Homer, Odyssey 18.178-181 (paraphrase) [10]
Penelope's refusal to beautify herself is a sign of loyalty to her absent husband.
Like the Kural's heroine, Penelope lays aside adornment for the one she loves.
Philosophy
In the Gorgias, Socrates calls cosmetics a flattering imitation of gymnastics, giving a false appearance of beauty instead of real health.
— Plato, Gorgias 465b (paraphrase) [11]
Plato distrusts adornment as a counterfeit of genuine good.
The Kural's heroine also sets cosmetics aside, though from love, not from Plato's suspicion of appearances.

Real Life Example

Juliette Drouet gives up the stage for Victor Hugo
1833–1883 · Paris, France
The French actress Juliette Drouet began her relationship with the writer Victor Hugo in 1833, and in the mid-1830s she gave up her stage career to devote herself to him. For about fifty years, until her death in 1883, she remained his companion, travelled with him and copied out his works for his publishers. She wrote him at least one letter a day, about 22,000 in all.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her letters to Hugo form one of the largest bodies of correspondence in the history of literature.
The kural's heroine gives up even the daily adornment of kohl on her eyes so that nothing hides the lover who lives there; Drouet gave up the public life of the stage and ordered her whole life around Hugo.
