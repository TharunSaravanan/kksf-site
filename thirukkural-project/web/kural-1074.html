Kural 1074

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

அகப்பட்டி ஆவாரைக் காணின் அவரின் மிகப்பட்டுச் செம்மாக்கும் கீழ்.
Agappatti aavaaraik kaanin avarin migappattuch chemmaakkum keezh.

Meaning

When mean people meet others who behave even worse than they do, they puff up with pride at being better.

Elementary

Imagine a child who got a bad mark on a test. Instead of trying to do better, he finds someone who did even worse and says, 'Ha, at least I'm better than you!' That is what the Kural means: base people feel big by looking down on someone lower.

University

Keezh (கீழ், 'the low, the base') is the chapter's second word for its subject. The difficult word agappatti (அகப்பட்டி) has divided commentators: Parimelazhagar and Puliyur Kesikan take aavaar as those who behave even more basely, so that the base man sees them and becomes migappattu (மிகப்பட்டு, 'superior, exalted') and semmaakkum (செம்மாக்கும், 'struts, exults'); Solomon Pappaiah glosses it as 'those who live like dogs, as they please'; Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as 'those beneath them'. The common sense is that baseness takes its self-esteem from someone lower. Pope's 'When base men those behold of conduct vile, / They straight surpass them, and exulting smile' follows the 'conduct vile' reading. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I am the richest man, surrounded by aristocratic relatives. There is none so powerful and happy as I am.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.13-15 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita gives the inner voice of the demonic person, who measures himself against others and swells with pride.
The Kural's base man also struts by comparing himself to others he thinks lower.
Christianity
“The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican.”
— Luke 18:11 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus' parable, the Pharisee builds himself up by pointing to a man he considers lower than himself.
This is the Kural's base man exactly: he feels superior by looking down on someone else.
Islam
“O you who have believed, let not a people ridicule [another] people; perhaps they may be better than them”
— Qur'an 49:11 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an forbids mocking others, since those looked down on may be better in God's sight.
It forbids exactly the contemptuous comparison that the Kural says is how the base feel superior.
Judaism
“Who is he that is honored? He who honors his fellow human beings”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma teaches that real honour comes from honouring others, not from rising above them.
This is the opposite of the Kural's base man, who seeks honour by despising those beneath him.
Buddhism
“Whosoever debased by his pride, exalts himself and belittles other — know him as an outcast.”
— Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7, v. 17 (tr. Piyadassi Thera) [7]
In the Discourse on Outcasts, the Buddha says one becomes truly low by deeds, including exalting oneself and belittling others.
The Buddha's 'outcast' and the Kural's keezh are the same figure: the man who rises in his own eyes by putting others down.
Jainism
“the base man is mighty in speaking harsh words. He regards no one, laughs at the misery of others, grows more and more enraged and will continually reproach others.”
— Naladiyar 35.8, chapter 'Meanness' (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Naladiyar describes the base man who respects no one and laughs at others' misfortunes.
Like the Kural's keezh, he gains his sense of superiority from looking down on those worse off.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself good - goodness shall not draw near him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan warns that self-praise is a sign of haumai (ego), and that goodness keeps away from the person who declares himself good.
The Kural shows the base swelling with pride when they meet people worse than themselves; Guru Arjan says that whoever rates himself as good is thereby cut off from goodness.
Greek Mythology
Theophrastus' sketch of the arrogant man shows someone who looks down on everyone, makes others wait on him and treats those he thinks beneath him with contempt.
— Theophrastus, Characters, 'The Arrogant Man' (tr. R. C. Jebb) [10]
The Greek character sketch catalogues the small habits of a man who builds himself up by belittling others.
It shows the same comparative pride as the Kural's base man, though less sharply focused on those even lower.
Philosophy
Hobbes defines 'sudden glory' as the passion behind laughter, caused either by something one does oneself or by seeing some defect in another, by comparison with which one suddenly applauds oneself; he adds that it is most common in those who know they have few abilities.
— Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, ch. 6 (1651) [11]
Hobbes explains contemptuous laughter as a way the weak feel strong by noticing the weakness of others.
This is the Kural's psychology exactly: the base exult on finding someone lower than themselves.

Real Life Example

Emperor Commodus renames Rome after himself
about 190–192 CE · Rome, Roman Empire
The Roman emperor Commodus, son of Marcus Aurelius, presented himself as the god Hercules, posed with the lion skin and club, and appeared in the arena as a fighter. According to the historian Cassius Dio, he ordered that Rome itself be called Commodiana, and the months of the year were renamed after his own titles. His misrule ended with his murder at the end of 192 CE.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Commodus became a classic example of a ruler puffed up with self-glory rather than real achievement.
The kural describes the base swelling with pride and strutting; Commodus renaming the city and the calendar after himself is that strutting on an imperial scale.
