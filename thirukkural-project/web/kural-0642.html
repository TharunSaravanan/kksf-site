Kural 642

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

ஆக்கமுங் கேடும் அதனால் வருதலால் காத்தோம்பல் சொல்லின்கட் சோர்வு.
Aakkamung kedum adhanaal varudhalaal Kaaththombal sollinkat sorvu.

Meaning

Both success and ruin can come from what we say. So be very careful never to make a slip in your speech.

Elementary

Words are like the steering wheel of a car: turn it well and you get home safely, jerk it carelessly and you crash. Because words can build things up or break them down, we should be careful never to say the wrong thing.

University

Ākkam (growth, prosperity) and kēṭu (ruin) are the paired outcomes that Valluvar often sets against each other, and here both are said to arrive “by it” (atanāl), meaning speech. The verb phrase kāttu ōmpal, “guarding and protecting”, is a doubled imperative of care, and cōrvu names a lapse, slip or momentary negligence, not deliberate lying. Parimelazhagar reads the couplet as advice to the minister, whose words in council can raise or ruin the king’s affairs, and so the lapse to be guarded against is any fault in word choice or timing. Modern commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar, Kalaignar) widen it into a universal maxim of verbal care. The older edition prints the first line in sandhi form (ஆக்கமுங் கேடு மதனால் வருதலாற்), with no difference in meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Udyoga Parva, Vidura warns Dhritarashtra that wounds from arrows and axes heal, but wounds from harsh words do not, and that a wise person never lets such arrows of speech fly.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura Niti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Vidura’s political counsel treats careless speech as a direct cause of lasting enmity and ruin in a royal household.
Like the Kural, Vidura tells a ruler’s counsellor to guard every word because ruin can come from speech.
Christianity
“For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.”
— Matthew 12:37 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that a person’s words decide their standing, for good or ill.
Words bring either justification or condemnation, just as the Kural says they bring either gain or ruin.
Islam
“O you who have believed, fear Allah and speak words of appropriate justice.”
— Qur’an 33:70 (Sahih International) [5]
The verse commands qawlan sadīdan, straight and well-aimed speech, and the next verse promises that God will then set right the believers’ deeds.
The Qur’an ties good outcomes to carefully aimed speech, as the Kural ties prosperity to guarding one’s words.
Judaism
Proverbs 18:21 teaches that death and life are in the power of the tongue, and that those who love it will eat its fruit.
— Proverbs 18:21 (JPS 1917) [6]
The sage says the tongue decides between life and death, and people reap the results of how they use it.
Death and life in Proverbs, like ruin and gain in the Kural, both come through speech.
Buddhism
In the Kokālika Sutta the Buddha says that a person is born with an axe in the mouth, with which the fool cuts himself when he speaks wrongly.
— Sutta Nipata 3.10 (Kokālika Sutta) [7]
The monk Kokālika slanders the chief disciples and suffers terribly for it; the verses warn that reckless words wound the speaker most of all.
The axe in the mouth is a vivid picture of the Kural’s warning that ruin can come through one’s own careless speech.
Jainism
“A monk or a nun, well considering, should not use speech whether truth or untruth, or truth mixed with untruth, if it be sinful, blamable, rough, stinging, coarse, hard, leading to sins, to discord and factions, to grief and outrage, to destruction of living beings.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.4.1.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the Acaranga's lecture on modes of speech, every word is weighed before it is spoken, because speech can lead to sin, discord, grief and even the destruction of life.
The kural warns that both gain and ruin come through speech, so one must guard against any slip; the Jain rule turns that same care into a discipline of considering each word first.
Sikhism
“Those words are acceptable, which, when spoken, bring honor. Harsh words bring only grief. Listen, O foolish and ignorant mind!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that speech is the hinge of a person's standing: words that bring honour are accepted, while harsh or insipid words bring only ruin and grief.
The Kural says both prosperity and ruin come through speech, so one must guard against any slip of the tongue; Guru Nanak names the same two outcomes, honour and grief, as the fruit of what one says.
Greek Mythology
Niobe publicly boasts that her many children make her greater than the goddess Leto, and in response Apollo and Artemis kill her children one by one.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.146-312 [10]
A single proud speech in front of the people of Thebes brings total ruin on her household.
Niobe’s story shows the ruin side of the Kural: one slip of the tongue destroys everything.
Philosophy
In the Encomium of Helen, Gorgias calls speech a mighty lord that with a tiny body does divine works, and compares its effect on the soul to drugs on the body: some cure, some kill.
— Gorgias, Encomium of Helen 8-14 [11]
Gorgias argues that speech can bring either healing or harm, depending on how it is used.
Speech as both cure and poison is Gorgias’ version of the Kural’s gain and ruin.

Real Life Example

A loosely worded order sends the Light Brigade down the wrong valley
25 October 1854 · Balaklava, Crimea
During the Battle of Balaklava in the Crimean War, Lord Raglan sent a written order for the cavalry to advance rapidly to the front and try to prevent the enemy carrying away the guns. The cavalry commanders could not see which guns he meant from their position, and the Light Brigade charged instead at a Russian battery at the end of a heavily defended valley. Of the roughly 670 men who charged, about 260 were killed or wounded, and the episode became one of the most infamous blunders in British military history.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The order made perfect sense from Raglan's viewpoint on the heights but was dangerously ambiguous to those who had to carry it out, and no one stopped to clarify it.
The kural warns that both prosperity and ruin come through words, so one must guard against any lapse in speech, and the vague wording of a single order at Balaklava turned into disaster.
