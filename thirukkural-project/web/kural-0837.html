Kural 837

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

ஏதிலார் ஆரத் தமர்பசிப்பர் பேதை பெருஞ்செல்வம் உற்றக் கடை.
Edhilaar aarath thamarpasippar pedhai perunjelvam utrak kadai.

Meaning

When a fool becomes very rich, strangers eat their fill while the fool's own family goes hungry.

Elementary

Imagine a boy who wins a huge bag of candy and hands it all out to kids he barely knows, while his little sister at home gets none. Having lots of something doesn’t help your family if you don’t know how to share it wisely.

University

The couplet contrasts edhilaar (ஏதிலார், strangers, those with no tie) who aara (ஆர, eat their fill) with thamar (தமர், one’s own kin) who pasippar (பசிப்பர், go hungry). Puliyur Kesigan follows the traditional explanation that the fool’s wealth comes from past merit (munvinai), while the lack of judgment in spending it is his own; Parimelazhagar’s tradition reads the strangers as flatterers and parasites who gather round new wealth. Kalaignar’s gloss stresses that outsiders ‘roll up’ the money while affectionate kin go without. Pope renders edhilaar as ‘foes’, a stronger word than the Tamil requires; Drew’s ‘neighbours’ is closer. The text is the same in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And charity performed at an impure place, at an improper time, to unworthy persons, or without proper attention and respect is said to be in the mode of ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.22 (As It Is) [3]
The Gita classes giving to the wrong people, at the wrong time and place, as charity in ignorance.
Valluvar’s fool likewise lavishes his wealth on the wrong people while his own go short.
Christianity
“But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.”
— 1 Timothy 5:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that neglecting one’s own household is a basic moral failure.
The Kural’s fool commits exactly this failure, feeding strangers and starving kin.
Islam
“And give the relative his right, and [also] the poor and the traveler, and do not spend wastefully.”
— Qur’an 17:26 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an puts relatives first among those with a claim on one’s wealth and forbids squandering it.
Valluvar’s fool reverses this order, wasting wealth on outsiders while kin go hungry.
Judaism
“A man to whom God hath given riches, wealth, and honour, so that he wanteth nothing for his soul of all that he desireth, yet God giveth him not power to eat thereof, but a stranger eateth it: this is vanity, and it is an evil disease.”
— Ecclesiastes 6:2 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Ecclesiastes laments the man whose great wealth ends up feeding a stranger rather than himself.
Both texts picture abundant wealth consumed by strangers instead of those it should serve.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta, the Buddha warns young Sigala of the channels through which wealth is squandered, such as drink, gambling and bad companions, and teaches that one should support friends and relatives with gifts and kindly care.
— Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31) [6]
The sutta pairs wise use of wealth with duties to one’s own circle.
Valluvar’s fool fails at both: his wealth leaks to outsiders and his kin go hungry.
Jainism
“The bat will not go to the rough-stemmed wood-apple tree, though near and fruitful. So the riches of those who, though they be very near to one, have no greatness of soul, have not the excellence of being considered as profitable.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 27 ('Riches without goodness'), st. 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaṭiyār says the wealth of small-souled people is of no use even to those closest to them, like a fruitful tree that the bat will not visit.
Valluvar says that when a fool gets great wealth his own people go hungry; the Nālaṭiyār similarly observes that riches in the hands of those without greatness of soul do nothing for those near them.
Sikhism
“Your wealth shall not go with you; why do you cling to it, you fool? Children, friends, family and spouse - who of these shall accompany you? … The fool does not acknowledge the One who gave this; forgetting the Naam, O Nanak! He will repent in the end.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 288, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib (ashtapadi 19, stanza 5), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan asks the fool why he clings to wealth that will not stay with him and forgets the One who gave it.
The Kural says that when a fool gains great wealth, strangers enjoy it while his own go hungry; Guru Arjan also shows the fool's wealth slipping away from him and his family.
Greek Mythology
In Odysseus’s absence, the suitors occupy his house and feast day after day on his herds and wine, while Penelope and Telemachus look on helplessly.
— Homer, Odyssey 1-2 [9]
Homer makes the wasting of a household’s wealth by outsiders a central wrong of the Odyssey.
It mirrors the Kural’s picture of strangers feasting while the family suffers.
Philosophy
Aristotle says the prodigal gives to the wrong people, enriching flatterers and others who deserve nothing, while failing to give to those who should receive.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 [10]
For Aristotle, prodigality is a failure to give to the right people in the right amounts.
Valluvar’s fool shows exactly this vice: outsiders grow fat while kin go hungry.

Real Life Example

Khedive Ismail's debts bring Egypt under European financial control
1863-1879 · Egypt
Khedive Ismail of Egypt financed costly modernisation projects and other ambitions largely with loans from European financiers, and the national debt rose many times over during his reign. In 1875 he sold Egypt's Suez Canal shares to Britain, and in 1876, unable to meet payments, Egypt's finances were placed under the supervision of a European debt commission and British and French controllers. In June 1879 the Ottoman sultan, pressed by Britain and France, deposed Ismail in favour of his son Tawfiq.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Ismail's borrowing enriched foreign bondholders while the burden of repayment fell on Egypt, and it ended in the loss of control over the country's own finances.
The kural says that when a fool comes into great wealth, strangers feast while his own people go hungry, and Egypt's wealth under Ismail flowed to foreign creditors while Egyptians bore the cost.
