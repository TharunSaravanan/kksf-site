Kural 338

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

குடம்பை தனித்துஒழியப் புள்பறந் தற்றே உடம்பொடு உயிரிடை நட்பு.
Kudambai thaniththu ozhiyap pul parandhatre Udambodu uyiridai natpu.

Meaning

The friendship between the body and the soul is like a bird that flies away and leaves its egg (or nest) behind, empty and alone.

Elementary

A baby bird grows inside an egg, but one day it breaks out and flies off, leaving the empty shell behind. This kural says our life and our body are like that: one day life flies off, and the body is left behind like the shell.

University

The meaning turns on the word “kudambai”, which Parimelazhagar and many commentators gloss as ‘egg’ (so the chick breaks out and leaves the shell), while others read it as ‘nest’ (so a grown bird abandons its home); both readings agree that the container is left “thaniththu ozhiya” (abandoned, alone). Calling the relationship “natpu” (friendship) is pointed, since it is a friendship of convenience that ends the moment the occupant departs. The egg reading adds the idea that the body was the soul's place of formation, not its permanent home, which prepares for kural 340. The image of soul and body as distinct, one of them leaving the other, parallels the Gita's worn-out garments, Ecclesiastes' spirit returning to God, and Plato's definition of death in the Phaedo as the separation of soul from body.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As a person sheds worn-out garments and wears new ones, likewise, at the time of death, the soul casts off its worn-out body and enters a new one.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.22 [3]
Krishna explains that the soul discards the body at death as one discards old clothes.
In both images the soul leaves and the body is left behind like an empty covering: garments in the Gita, shell or nest in the Kural.
Christianity
“We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord.”
— 2 Corinthians 5:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul speaks of leaving the body behind at death to be with Christ.
Both texts see the body as something the self eventually leaves, not the self's permanent place.
Islam
“No! When the soul has reached the collar bones ... And the dying one is certain that it is the [time of] separation”
— Quran 75:26, 75:28 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran describes the soul rising in the dying body, and the dying person knowing it is time for separation.
‘Separation’ (al-firaq) is the Kural's parting of bird and shell, soul and body.
Judaism
“Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it.”
— Ecclesiastes 12:7 (KJV) [4]
Ecclesiastes describes death as the body returning to dust and the spirit returning to God.
Body and spirit part ways at death: the body stays behind like the Kural's empty shell, while the spirit goes on.
Buddhism
“Ere long, alas! this body will lie upon the earth, unheeded and lifeless, like a useless log.”
— Dhammapada 41 [6]
The Buddha reminds his followers that the body will soon lie lifeless and ignored, like a discarded log.
The abandoned body, left alone and useless, is the Kural's deserted shell or nest.
Jainism
“(The soul) cannot be apprehended by the senses, because it possesses no corporeal form, and since it possesses no corporeal form it is eternal. The fetter of the soul has been ascertained to be caused by its bad qualities, and this fetter is called the cause of worldly existence.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.19 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
Answering the materialist claim that the soul is produced by and perishes with the body, Bhṛgu's sons say the soul is formless and eternal, only temporarily bound to embodiment by karma.
The Kural pictures body and soul as egg (or nest) and bird, the one abandoned when the other leaves; the Jain verse likewise holds the soul to be distinct from the body and only temporarily joined to it.
Sikhism
“When the soul-swan flies away, by the Command of the Lord, her dust mingles with dust.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1111, Raag Tukhaari, Chhant, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak pictures death as the soul, imagined as a swan, flying off at God's command while the body it leaves behind returns to dust.
The Kural compares the bond of body and soul to a bird leaving its egg or nest empty behind it; Guru Nanak uses the same image of a bird flying from an abandoned body.
Greek Mythology
When Patroclus is struck down by Hector, his soul leaves his limbs and flies down to the house of Hades, mourning its fate and the youth and strength it leaves behind.
— Homer, Iliad 16.855-857 [9]
Homer pictures the psyche flying out of the body at the moment of death.
The soul flying away and leaving the body behind is the Kural's bird deserting its shell.
Philosophy
“And is this anything but the separation of soul and body? And being dead is the attainment of this separation; when the soul exists in herself, and is parted from the body and the body is parted from the soul-that is death?”
— Plato, Phaedo 64c (Jowett translation) [10]
Socrates defines death as the soul and the body parting from each other.
Plato's definition is the philosophical form of the Kural's image: two companions whose ‘friendship’ ends when one departs.

Real Life Example

Benjamin Franklin writes his own mock epitaph
1728 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, British America
As a young printer of about 22, Benjamin Franklin wrote a humorous epitaph for himself, describing his body in the grave as the cover of an old book whose contents had been torn out and whose lettering and gilding had been stripped away. He added that the work itself would not be lost but would appear again in a new and better edition. Several autograph copies survive, and in his will he asked for a simple inscription on his actual gravestone instead.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Franklin's famous image treats the body as an outer case that is left empty once its real contents depart. It became one of the best-known epitaphs in American literature.
The kural compares body and life to an egg or nest left empty and alone when the bird flies off, and Franklin pictured his own body as an empty book cover left behind.
