Kural 1150

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

தாம்வேண்டின் நல்குவர் காதலர் யாம்வேண்டும் கௌவை எடுக்கும்இவ் வூர்.
thaamvaeNtin nalkuvar kaadhalar yaamvaendum kavvai edukkum-iv voor

Meaning

The town is now spreading the very rumor we wanted. So if we ask him, our lover will gladly agree to what we want.

Elementary

Imagine you have been hoping your parents will let you go on a trip with your best friend. Then everybody starts saying, ‘Those two always go everywhere together!’ The talk makes the trip seem natural, and you feel sure your friend will say yes when you ask. That is how the girl’s friend feels in this verse.

University

In Parimelazhagar’s reading the speaker is now the tōḻi, addressing the heroine while the hero is within earshot, to make him favour elopement (uṭaṉpōkku). ‘The rumour we have long wanted’ (yām vēṇṭum kauvai), wanted because it gives a reason for leaving together, ‘the town itself is raising’ (eṭukkum); kātalar, ‘the lover’, chosen to suggest that because he loves her he will not refuse, ‘will grant it if we ask’ (tām vēṇṭiṉ nalkuvar). He closes his commentary by noting that the twenty couplets of chapters 114 and 115 are all puṇartal nimittam, occasions leading toward union. Manakkudavar reads the second clause differently, that the family will now themselves agree to give her to the lover; modern glosses (Pappaiah: he will marry me; Varadarasanar: he will grant it) vary between marriage and elopement.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Rukmini, whose brother has promised her to Shishupala, sends a brahmin with a letter asking Krishna to come and carry her away; Krishna agrees at once and takes her from the temple before the wedding.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.52-53 (paraphrase) [4]
The Bhagavata tells of a woman who asks her beloved to take her away against her family’s plans, and of the lover who readily consents.
The tōḻi in the Kural is sure the lover will agree to take the heroine away if asked, just as Krishna answers Rukmini.
Christianity
“My beloved spake, and said unto me, Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away.”
— Song of Solomon 2:10 (KJV) [5]
The lover in the Song invites the beloved to leave with him.
The Kural’s tōḻi expects the lover to give just such an invitation now that the town’s talk has made the way clear.
Islam
Ibn Ishaq relates that Khadija, admiring Muhammad’s character, sent word through an intermediary proposing marriage, and he accepted.
— Ibn Ishaq, Sirat Rasul Allah, marriage of Khadija (paraphrase; cf. tr. A. Guillaume) [6]
In this account the woman takes the initiative, confident her proposal will be welcomed, and it is.
The Kural’s tōḻi is likewise confident the lover will grant what the heroine asks, now that the town has made their bond known.
Judaism
When Abraham’s servant asks to take Rebekah to Isaac, her family say they will call the girl and ask her, and when asked whether she will go with this man, she answers, I will go.
— Genesis 24:57-58 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [7]
Rebekah consents to leave her home and go to the man she is to marry.
The Kural, too, turns on a question of consent to leave home, with the tōḻi sure the answer will be yes.
Buddhism
The Pali commentaries tell that Patacara, a rich man’s daughter, fell in love with a family servant, and when her parents arranged another marriage she ran away with him to live in a distant village.
— Therigatha commentary and Dhammapada commentary, story of Patacara (paraphrase) [8]
Patacara’s story is a Buddhist account of an elopement, which later ends in tragedy and her conversion.
The Kural’s tōḻi is urging exactly such an elopement (uṭaṉpōkku), though the Buddhist story shows where its sorrows can lead.
Jainism
When Arishtanemi leaves his wedding to become a monk, Rajimati, his bride, resolves to follow him, takes the vows herself, and goes to the mountain where he is practising austerities.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [9]
In the Jain story the bride follows the man she loves, but into renunciation rather than married life.
The Kural’s heroine is urged to follow her lover away from home; Rajimati also follows hers, though to a very different end.
Sikhism
“Whatever I ask for, I receive; I have total faith in my Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 619, Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan expresses complete confidence that the Master will grant whatever His devotee asks.
The Kural's companion is sure that, with the town raising the very rumour they wanted, the lover will gladly grant what they ask; Gurbani shows the same confident trust that the Beloved will give what is asked.
Greek Mythology
Medea, having helped Jason win the Golden Fleece, flees her father’s house by night and sails away with him, after he swears to make her his wedded wife.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.C. Seaton) [11]
The Argonautica tells one of the classic elopements of Greek myth.
The Kural’s tōḻi hopes for just such a flight with the lover, now that the rumour has made it seem natural.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus, the beloved, receiving the lover’s affection, comes to feel a returning love (anteros), an image of the lover’s own, and begins to desire his company in turn.
— Plato, Phaedrus 255b-e (paraphrase) [12]
Plato describes how love, once shown, awakens an answering love in the beloved.
The Kural’s tōḻi trusts that the lover’s own love (he is kātalar, ‘the one who loves’) will make him agree to what the heroine asks.

Real Life Example

Bertha Kinsky secretly marries Arthur von Suttner and leaves with him for the Caucasus
1876 · Vienna, Austria, and the Caucasus
Bertha Kinsky became engaged to Baron Arthur von Suttner, seven years her junior, but his family strongly opposed the match, so in 1876 she took a post in Paris as secretary to Alfred Nobel. After only a week or so she returned to Vienna, secretly married Arthur, and the couple left at once for the Caucasus, where they lived for nine years by teaching and writing. In 1885 they returned to Austria, welcomed by his now relenting family; she later won the 1905 Nobel Peace Prize.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Faced with family opposition, the couple chose to marry and leave together, and in time the family accepted them.
In the kural the confidante urges the couple to go away together, sure that the lover will agree; Bertha and Arthur did just that, marrying secretly and leaving together despite his family's opposition.
