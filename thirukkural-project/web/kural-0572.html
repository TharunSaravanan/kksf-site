Kural 572

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

கண்ணோட்டத் துள்ளது உலகியல் அஃதிலார் உண்மை நிலக்குப் பொறை.
Kannottath thulladhu ulagiyal aqdhilaar Unmai nilakkup porai.

Meaning

All the everyday business of the world runs on kindness toward others. People without it are just a burden on the earth.

Elementary

Imagine a class where everyone shares pencils and helps each other. One child never helps anyone and only takes. The class works because of the helpers; the one who never helps just makes things heavier for everyone. Valluvar says people without kindness are like extra weight the earth has to carry.

University

உலகியல் means ‘the conduct or course of the world’, i.e., everyday social transactions, and Parimelazhagar takes the first line to mean that all such dealings are sustained by kannottam. The second line’s உண்மை (‘existence’, not ‘truth’ here) and பொறை (‘burden’) make a stark judgement: a person without kindly regard contributes nothing and simply weighs on the land, an image the Tamil tradition also uses of the ungrateful and the idle. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both render பொறை as சுமை/பாரம் (load), while Kalaignar stresses the ‘embracing with love’ that such people lack. The couplet thus moves from the cosmic claim of 571 to its negative corollary. ytamizh.com and thirukkural-thamizh.org agree; the latter prints the sandhi-joined form (துள்ள துலகிய லஃதிலா / ருண்மை).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…nonviolence; truthfulness; freedom from anger; renunciation; tranquillity; aversion to faultfinding; compassion for all living entities; freedom from covetousness; gentleness…”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna lists dayā bhūteṣu (compassion toward all beings) and mārdava (gentleness) among the qualities of the divine nature (daivī sampat).
The Gita, like the Kural, sees compassion as the mark of a life that upholds the world rather than burdens it.
Christianity
“Behold, these three years I come seeking fruit on this fig tree, and find none: cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?”
— Luke 13:7 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus’ parable, a fig tree that bears no fruit is said only to take up space and exhaust the soil.
The fruitless tree that ‘cumbers the ground’ is almost exactly the Kural’s person without kindness who is a burden on the earth.
Islam
“Allah will not be merciful to those who are not merciful to mankind.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7376 [5]
The Prophet makes mercy toward people the condition for receiving God’s mercy.
Both texts judge the person lacking kindly regard as having no rightful place, whether on earth or before God.
Judaism
“Shammai used to say: make your [study of the] Torah a fixed practice; speak little, but do much; and receive all men with a pleasant countenance.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:15 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Even the famously strict Shammai required that every person be received with a pleasant face, making warm regard a basic rule of social life.
Receiving everyone with a kindly face is the everyday ‘worldly conduct’ that the Kural says rests on kannottam.
Buddhism
“Even as a mother protects with her life / Her child, her only child, / So with a boundless heart / Should one cherish all living beings”
— Karaniya Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.8 (trans. Amaravati Sangha) [7]
The Metta Sutta asks for a boundless, protective kindness toward every being without exception.
This all-embracing goodwill is what the Kural says the whole world runs on.
Jainism
“Benevolence (maitrī) towards all living-beings (sattva), joy (pramoda) at the sight of the virtuous (guṇādhika), compassion and sympathy (kāruṇya) for the afflicted (kliśyamāna), and tolerance (mādhyasthya) towards the insolent and ill-behaved (avineya) are the other observances.”
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.11 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The four bhavanas (contemplations) of friendliness, joy, compassion and equanimity are given to sustain the vows of non-violence and the rest.
The Kural says all the world's business runs on kindly regard; the Jain sutra makes goodwill toward every being a daily discipline underlying all conduct.
Sikhism
“Those who forget the Naam, the Name of the Lord, are a burden on the earth.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses the Gurus included in the scripture, says that a life cut off from the divine Name is mere dead weight on the earth.
The Kural says that people without kindly regard merely exist as a burden on the earth; Farid uses the same image of a worthless life weighing on the earth.
Greek Mythology
Zeus and Hermes, disguised as travellers, are turned away by every house in a Phrygian village except that of the poor old couple Baucis and Philemon; the gods flood the pitiless village and turn the couple’s hut into a temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 [10]
Ovid’s tale shows the unkind community swept away while the kindly household is preserved and honoured.
The villagers without regard for strangers are, as the Kural says, a burden the earth can do without.
Philosophy
“… as men, too, are born for the sake of men, that they may be able mutually to help one another; in this direction we ought to follow Nature as our guide, to contribute to the general good by an interchange of acts of kindness, by giving and receiving …”
— Cicero, De Officiis (On Duties), Book I, §§22, 29 (tr. Walter Miller (Loeb, 1913)) [11]
Cicero says human society is held together by mutual kindness, and a few sections later calls those who keep to their own affairs and give society nothing traitors to social life.
As in the Kural, the world's business runs on kindly regard, and those who withhold it contribute nothing to the life they share.

Real Life Example

Clara Barton founds the American Red Cross
21 May 1881 · Washington, D.C., United States
Clara Barton, who had nursed and supplied soldiers during the American Civil War and later ran an office that traced missing soldiers, learned of the European Red Cross movement in Switzerland in 1869 and worked with it during the Franco-Prussian War. On 21 May 1881 she founded the American Red Cross in Washington, D.C., and in 1882 the United States ratified the Geneva Convention. She led the organisation for 23 years, and her push for peacetime disaster relief led the International Red Cross Conference to adopt the so-called American Amendment in 1884.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Barton made organised compassion a permanent part of everyday national life, so that help after floods, fires and wars no longer depended on chance.
The kural says the ordinary business of the world runs on kindly regard, and Barton's Red Cross turned that regard into an institution that ordinary communities still rely on in every disaster.
