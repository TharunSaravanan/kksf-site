Kural 1044

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

இற்பிறந்தார் கண்ணேயும் இன்மை இளிவந்த சொற்பிறக்கும் சோர்வு தரும்.
IRpiRandhaar kaNNaeyum inmai iLivandha soRpiRakkum soarvu tharum.

Meaning

Poverty makes even people from good families so tired and weak that low, shameful words come out of their mouths.

Elementary

When you are very tired and hungry, you might snap at someone even though you are usually polite. Valluvar says being very poor can make even well-brought-up people so worn down that unkind or embarrassing words slip out.

University

IRpiRandhaar (இற்பிறந்தார்) are ‘those born in a (good) house’, i.e. of noble family, and the -um of kaNNaeyum (‘even in them’) concedes that breeding is no protection. Inmai gives soarvu (சோர்வு, ‘slackness, exhaustion, lapse of vigilance’) from which iLivandha sol (இளிவந்த சொல், ‘words that bring disgrace’) is born. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read it this way: poverty causes the lapse, and the lapse produces demeaning speech. Puliyur Kesikan, notably, reverses the causation, saying that the slackness which leads to base acts is itself what brings poverty even to good families, a reading that turns the couplet into a warning rather than a lament. Pope’s ‘Oblivion that gives birth to words that bring disgrace’ takes soarvu as forgetfulness of one’s station. The text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“King Nahusha, having done many wicked acts in a state of poverty, cried fie on that state and said that poverty is for recluses.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna cites the royal Nahusha, who was driven to wicked acts when reduced to poverty.
Even a king of illustrious line fell into base conduct through want, just as the Kural says of those nobly born.
Christianity
“Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: Lest I be full, and deny thee... or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain.”
— Proverbs 30:8-9 (KJV) [4]
Agur prays to be spared poverty because it might push him to steal and to profane God’s name.
The prayer names exactly the Kural’s danger: want driving a decent person to shameful deeds and words.
Islam
“Satan threatens you with poverty and orders you to immorality, while Allah promises you forgiveness from Him and bounty.”
— Qur’an 2:268 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an pairs the fear of poverty with the pull toward shameful conduct, both ascribed to Satan’s whispering.
Like the Kural, it sees poverty, or the dread of it, as the doorway to disgraceful behaviour.
Judaism
“Three matters cause a person to act against his own will and the will of his Maker, and they are: Gentiles, and an evil spirit, and the depths of extreme poverty.”
— Talmud Bavli, Eruvin 41b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
The Talmud lists crushing poverty among forces that make a person act against both his own nature and God’s will, and therefore asks that we pray for such people.
This is the Kural’s point precisely: poverty can make even the well-born behave unlike themselves.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta the Buddha relates how a king who failed to give wealth to the destitute let poverty spread, and from poverty came theft, then weapons, killing and lying, so that a whole people sank into degraded conduct.
— Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta, DN 26 [7]
The sutta traces a causal chain in which poverty is the first step toward social and moral collapse.
It extends the Kural’s point from one family to a whole society: want breeds base acts and base words, including lying.
Jainism
“A houseless and poor monk who wanders from village to village may become tired of ascetic life: he should bear this trouble.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira names discontent (arati), the weariness of the poor wandering monk, as a trouble to be recognised and overcome.
Jainism acknowledges the very soarvu (weariness) the Kural describes, but asks that it be mastered rather than allowed to degrade one.
Sikhism
“The hungry person does not care about honor, dishonor or harsh words.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 317, Raag Gauri, Gauri ki Vaar, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Gauri ki Vaar, Guru Arjan observes that someone driven by hunger stops weighing honour and shame and will put up with insults or speak without restraint to get food; he uses this as a simile for the seeker's single-minded longing.
The Kural says poverty breeds, even in the well-born, a weariness from which mean and shameful words come; Guru Arjan likewise notes that hunger erases a person's sense of honour and dishonour.
Greek Mythology
“An evil shame is the needy man’s companion, shame which both greatly harms and prospers men: shame is with poverty, but confidence with wealth.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 317-319 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod observes that need brings with it a crippling sense of shame, while wealth brings confidence.
Both poets see poverty as robbing a person of the dignity that normally governs speech and conduct.
Philosophy
“Wealth, I said, and poverty; the one is the parent of luxury and indolence, and the other of meanness and viciousness, and both of discontent.”
— Plato, Republic IV, 421e-422a (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates tells Adeimantus that poverty breeds meanness and vice, which is why the guardians must keep both extremes out of the city.
Plato’s ‘poverty, the parent of meanness’ is the Kural’s claim that want gives birth to base conduct.

Real Life Example

Mozart's letters to Michael Puchberg asking for loans
1788-1791 · Vienna, Austria
From June 1788, at a hard time for musicians in Vienna, Mozart began writing a series of letters to his friend and fellow Freemason, the merchant Michael Puchberg, asking for money. In one he admitted that he still owed Puchberg eight ducats and could not repay them, yet asked for a hundred gulden more; later letters asked for far larger sums. Puchberg lent smaller amounts again and again, and the appeals continued until a few months before Mozart's death in December 1791.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters show one of the most gifted and cultivated artists of his age reduced by money troubles to repeated, apologetic pleas.
The kural says that want breeds, even in the well-born, a weariness from which humbling words come out, and Mozart's pleading letters to Puchberg are a documented case of a refined man pressed into such speech.
