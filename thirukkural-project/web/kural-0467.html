Kural 467

Acting after Due Consideration · Therindhu Seyalvakai · தெரிந்து செயல்வகை

எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம் துணிந்தபின் எண்ணுவம் என்பது இழுக்கு.
Ennith thunika karumam thunindhapin, Ennuvam enbadhu izhukku.

Meaning

Think it through first, and then decide what to do. Saying “we will think about it after we start” is a mistake.

Elementary

Do your thinking first, then make up your mind and go. Starting first and figuring it out later is a big mistake.

University

Probably the most-quoted couplet of the chapter. Its force comes from the sequence எண்ணி → துணிக (reflect → resolve) and the rejection of the reversed order துணிந்தபின் எண்ணுவம் (‘having resolved, we shall reflect’). Two faults are excluded at once. One is beginning without deliberation. The other, implied, is wavering and reopening the question after committing, which dissipates resolve. துணிவு means both decision and courage, so the couplet unites prudence and boldness: deliberation belongs before action, and firmness belongs after decision. இழுக்கு (a slip, a blemish) casts the failure as a lapse of character, not merely of technique.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After eighteen chapters of dialogue, Arjuna tells Krishna that his delusion is destroyed, his doubts are gone, and he stands firm, ready to act on Krishna’s word.
— Bhagavad Gita 18.73 [4]
The whole Gita is one long pause to think before battle. Only when Arjuna’s doubts are cleared does he commit to action.
The Gita’s structure (deliberation first, then firm resolve) is the Kural’s sequence.
Christianity
No man, having put his hand to the plough, and looking back, is fit for the kingdom of God.
— Luke 9:62 (KJV) [5]
Once you’ve committed to something important, you mustn’t keep looking back and second-guessing.
Matches the Kural’s condemnation of reconsidering after resolving.
Islam
…and consult them in the matter. And when you have decided, then rely upon Allah.
— Qur’an 3:159 (Sahih International) [6]
God tells the Prophet: talk things over with your people first, but once you’ve decided, act with trust and don’t hesitate.
An exact counterpart: consult and deliberate, then resolve and proceed firmly.
Judaism
Let thine eyes look right on, and let thine eyelids look straight before thee. Ponder the path of thy feet, and let all thy ways be established.
— Proverbs 4:25–26 (Hebrew Bible; KJV rendering) [5]
Think carefully about where you are going, then walk steadily ahead without turning aside.
Pondering first and then holding firmly to the path is the Kural’s order.
Buddhism
The Buddha describes the resolve of a disciple who has set out on the path: willingly let only skin, sinews and bones remain, let flesh and blood dry up; energy will not be relaxed until the goal is attained.
— Kitagiri Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 70 [7]
Once a Buddhist practitioner decides to seek awakening, they commit completely and don’t turn back.
Illustrates the unwavering resolve that should follow deliberation.
Jainism
“Vratas or a vow is a solemn resolve made after deliberation to observe a particular rule of conduct … A vow affords stability to the will and guards its votary from the evils of temptation or of unguarded life”
— Vilas A. Sangave, 'Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code', section on the twelve vratas (jainworld.com) [8]
Jain ethics defines the vow, the basic unit of a layman's discipline, as a resolve taken only after deliberation, which then keeps the will steady.
The Kural's order (reflect, then resolve, and do not reopen the question afterwards) is how the Jain vow works: deliberation comes first, and steadiness follows.
Sikhism
“If you desire to play this game of love with Me, then step onto My Path with your head in hand. When you place your feet on this Path, give Me your head, and do not pay any attention to public opinion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaran Thay Vadheek (salok 20), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak asks that the full cost be accepted before the first step on the path of love; once the step is taken, there is no holding back.
The Kural says to think first and then resolve, not to begin and think afterwards; Guru Nanak asks the seeker to settle the price before setting foot on the path, then give without hesitation.
Greek Mythology
Trapped in the Cyclops’s cave, Odysseus first thinks of stabbing Polyphemus as he sleeps, but a second thought stops him: no one could move the boulder from the door. He devises the stake-and-ram plan and carries it out without hesitation.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.299–305 and following [10]
Odysseus thinks through his options before he acts, then sticks to the plan he chooses.
Odysseus’s metis is the Kural’s ‘think, then resolve’ in action.
Philosophy
Descartes’s second maxim: be as firm and resolute in action as possible once a course is chosen, like travellers lost in a forest who should walk straight in one direction rather than wander in circles.
— René Descartes, Discourse on Method (1637), Part 3 [11]
Descartes says that once you’ve picked a direction, keep going; changing your mind every few steps gets you nowhere.
Matches the Kural’s rule against reopening deliberation after resolving.

Real Life Example

Construction of the Sydney Opera House begins before the design is resolved
1959-1973 · Sydney, New South Wales, Australia
Work on the Sydney Opera House began in 1959, pressed forward before a state election, long before Jorn Utzon's design for the roof shells and their supports had been worked out. As each new design was produced the cost rose and the completion date moved back, and the estimated cost of 3.5 million pounds had reached 13.7 million by 1962. After a dispute with the state government over payments, Utzon resigned in 1966, and other architects completed the building, which opened in 1973.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The building became a world icon, but starting before the plan was settled led to years of delay, a vastly higher cost and the departure of its architect.
The kural calls it a disgrace to say we will think it through after we have begun, and the Opera House was begun on precisely that basis.
