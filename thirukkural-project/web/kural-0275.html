Kural 275

Inconsistent Conduct · Koodaavozhukkam · கூடாவொழுக்கம்

பற்றற்றேம் என்பார் படிற்றொழுக்கம் எற்றெற்றென்று ஏதம் பலவுந் தரும்.
Patratrem enbaar padittrozhukkam etretrendru Edham palavum tharum.

Meaning

People who say, “We have given up all wanting,” but live falsely will face many troubles that make them cry, “What have we done! What have we done!”

Elementary

If you tell everyone you gave up sweets, but secretly eat them all, one day you’ll be caught — and you’ll feel terrible and say, “Why did I do that?” Pretending costs more in the end than being honest.

University

The kural records the hypocrite’s own claim — பற்றற்றேம் (“we are free of attachment”) — and sets against it his eventual cry, எற்று எற்று (“what [have we done], what!”), an onomatopoeic doubled lament unusual in the Kural. Valluvar’s focus is on consequences: படிற்றொழுக்கம் (disguised conduct) produces ஏதம் பலவும், “many evils,” culminating in bitter self-reproach. The phrase may refer both to worldly ruin when the imposture is exposed and to karmic suffering afterwards. The pattern — pretended renunciation leading to anguished regret — is echoed in Manu’s consignment of ‘cat’ and ‘heron’ ascetics to hell and in Jesus’ ‘weeping and gnashing of teeth’ for hypocrites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those Brahmanas who act like herons, and those who display the characteristics of cats, fall in consequence of that wicked mode of acting into (the hell called) Andhatamisra.”
— Manusmriti 4.197 (Bühler translation) [3]
Manu says religious pretenders who behave like the ‘cat’ or the ‘heron’ (feigning piety to catch prey) fall into a dark hell.
Same moral arithmetic as the Kural: sham renunciation leads to great suffering for the pretender.
Christianity
“…and appoint him his portion with the hypocrites: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.”
— Matthew 24:51 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus’ parable, the unfaithful servant is sent to share the fate of hypocrites, a place of weeping and grinding teeth.
The hypocrite’s end is anguished lament — the Kural’s cry of ‘What have we done!’
Islam
“The hypocrites will call to the believers, ‘Were we not with you?’ They will say, ‘Yes, but you afflicted yourselves and awaited [misfortune for us] and doubted, and wishful thinking deluded you…’”
— Qur’an 57:14 (Sahih International) [5]
On the Day of Judgement the hypocrites plead that they were outwardly with the believers, and are told that their self-deception ruined them.
Captures the Kural’s scene of the pretender’s too-late realisation that his false show has destroyed him.
Judaism
“The sinners in Zion are afraid; fearfulness hath surprised the hypocrites. Who among us shall dwell with the devouring fire?”
— Isaiah 33:14 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah describes hypocrites suddenly seized with terror when judgement arrives.
Mirrors the Kural’s picture of the pretender overtaken by distress and crying out in dismay.
Buddhism
“Just as kusa grass wrongly handled cuts the hand, even so, a recluse’s life wrongly lived drags one to states of woe.”
— Dhammapada 311 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says a monk’s life lived falsely injures the one who lives it, like grass grabbed the wrong way slicing the hand.
Exactly the Kural’s warning: the renunciant’s life, when faked, turns against the faker and brings him grief.
Jainism
“Thus the self-willed sinner who leaves the road of the highest Ginas, who with the appetite of an osprey is desirous of pleasure, will grieve in useless sorrow.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.50 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The lecture's verdict on the false monk is that his hidden craving for pleasure ends in bitter and fruitless grief.
Valluvar says those who falsely claim to be free of attachment will end crying 'What have we done!'; the Jain text likewise foresees useless sorrow for the monk who hides his appetites.
Sikhism
“He dyes his robes with saffron dye, and wearing these robes, he goes out begging. … From house to house he goes begging, and tries to teach the world; but his mind is blind, and so he loses his honor.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1012, Raag Maaroo, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Raag Maaroo Guru Nanak describes a man who puts on the saffron robe of renunciation and begs from door to door, preaching to others while his own mind stays blind, until he loses his honour.
The Kural warns that those who falsely claim to have given up all attachment will end up crying 'what have we done!'; Guru Nanak likewise shows the false renunciant ending in disgrace.
Greek Mythology
“A Wolf resolved to disguise himself in order that he might prey upon a flock of sheep without fear of detection. So he clothed himself in a sheepskin… But that very night… the shepherd… laid hands on the Wolf in mistake for a Sheep, and killed him with his knife on the spot.”
— Aesop, “The Wolf in Sheep’s Clothing” (tr. V. S. Vernon Jones) [9]
The wolf’s disguise works — so well that it gets him slaughtered as a sheep.
The disguise itself becomes the source of the deceiver’s ruin, as the Kural predicts of false renunciation.
Philosophy
“A good conscience welcomes the crowd, but a bad conscience, even in solitude, is disturbed and troubled.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 43.5 (tr. Gummere) [10]
Seneca says a person living falsely is haunted and uneasy even when alone.
Describes the inner torment that the Kural’s ‘What have we done!’ expresses.

Real Life Example

The fall of Cardinal Wolsey
1529-1530 · Hampton Court, London and Leicester, England
Thomas Wolsey, a cardinal of the Church and Henry VIII's Lord Chancellor, accumulated enormous wealth, with an income at its peak estimated at many times that of the richest nobleman, and built Hampton Court Palace. When he failed to obtain the pope's annulment of Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, he fell from favour in 1529 and was stripped of his offices except the archbishopric of York. In November 1530 he was arrested on a charge of treason and died at Leicester Abbey on his way south to face it.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A churchman vowed to spiritual service who had amassed worldly power and riches lost all of it within a single year.
The kural warns that those who claim to have renounced attachment while clinging to the world will meet many sorrows, and Wolsey's sudden ruin fits that warning.
