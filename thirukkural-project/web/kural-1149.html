Kural 1149

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

அலர்நாண ஒல்வதோ அஞ்சலோம்பு என்றார் பலர்நாண நீத்தக் கடை.
alarnhaaNa olvadhoa anjaloambu endraar palarnhaaNa neeththak kadai

Meaning

He once told me, 'Do not be afraid,' and now he has left me, shaming me in front of many. How can I still feel ashamed of the town's gossip?

Elementary

Imagine a friend promised, ‘Don’t worry, I’ll always stay with you,’ and then went away, so everyone could see you were left alone. You might think, ‘Everyone already knows I’ve been left. Why should I be embarrassed about what they say now?’ That is how the girl in this verse feels.

University

Parimelazhagar sets this when the hero has gone away, leaving the marriage still pending; the heroine, aware he has returned and is within earshot, answers the tōḻi who told her to endure for fear of the rumour. Añcal ōmpu (அஞ்சல் ஓம்பு), ‘guard against fear, do not fear’, recalls the hero’s promise at their first meeting, ‘I will not leave you’; now he has abandoned her ‘so that the many are ashamed’ (palar nāṇa), and she asks whether she can still feel nāṇ before alar. He paraphrases her meaning: ‘We, who are in a state that makes onlookers blush, where is our shame?’ Manakkudavar takes the ‘many’ as the tōḻi, the foster-mother (cevili) and the household. Solomon Pappaiah’s gloss reads the line differently, as the hero having said ‘fear not’ even as he departed; the text itself agrees in all three sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Mirabai sings that she has thrown away the shame of the world (lok-laj): her family scolds her and people call her mad, but she will not hide her love for Krishna.
— Mirabai, padas (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Alston, The Devotional Poems of Mirabai) [4]
The bhakti poet makes indifference to public shame a sign of a love that has gone beyond caring what people say.
The Kural’s heroine reaches the same point: having been left before everyone, she can no longer be ashamed of their gossip.
Christianity
“Peter said unto him, Though I should die with thee, yet will I not deny thee. Likewise also said all the disciples.” Later that night: “Then all the disciples forsook him, and fled.”
— Matthew 26:35, 26:56 (KJV) [5]
Those who promised never to abandon Jesus leave him at the moment of public shame.
The Kural’s heroine has suffered the same: the one who promised ‘fear not’ has left her exposed before everyone.
Islam
Al-Hujwiri describes the Malamatiyya, the ‘people of blame’, Sufis who deliberately accept people’s reproach so that public opinion has no hold on their love for God.
— Al-Hujwiri, Kashf al-Mahjub, chapter on blame (malama) (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.A. Nicholson) [6]
For these Sufis, being freed from concern about blame is part of loving sincerely.
The Kural’s heroine, abandoned in public, also reaches the point where the town’s blame can no longer shame her.
Judaism
Lamentations pictures Jerusalem as a woman weeping in the night, who among all her lovers has none to comfort her, for all her friends have dealt treacherously with her.
— Lamentations 1:2 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [7]
The city is portrayed as a woman abandoned by those who had pledged themselves to her, exposed before the nations.
The Kural’s heroine is likewise abandoned by the one who promised to stay, and left exposed before everyone.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha teaches that the wise person stands firm whatever others say.
The Kural’s heroine also stops being shaken by blame, though out of grief rather than wisdom.
Jainism
“Happy are we, happy live we who call nothing our own; when Mithilâ is on fire, nothing is burned that belongs to me.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
Nami, having let go of everything, is untouched by the uproar and fire in his city.
The Kural’s heroine, having lost what mattered most, is likewise beyond being hurt by the town’s talk, though she has not chosen that loss as Nami did.
Sikhism
“You are my honor; You are my decoration.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 181, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan places all his honour (pat) and adornment in the Lord alone, so that what the world says can neither give nor take it away.
The Kural's heroine, already shamed by her lover's leaving, asks why she should still fear the town's gossip; Gurbani answers that one whose honour rests in the Beloved need not fear what people say.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s Heroides, Ariadne, abandoned on the island of Naxos by Theseus after she saved him, writes reproaching him for the promises he made and for leaving her alone and disgraced.
— Ovid, Heroides 10 (Ariadne to Theseus) (paraphrase) [11]
Ariadne is the classic figure of the woman abandoned by the man who swore to protect her.
The Kural’s heroine has suffered the same broken promise of ‘do not fear’, and the same public exposure.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises that if someone tells you a person has spoken ill of you, you should not defend yourself, but answer that he must not have known your other faults, or he would have mentioned them too.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 (paraphrase) [12]
For the Stoic, other people’s talk is outside our control and should not disturb us.
The Kural’s heroine also refuses to be ashamed of the town’s talk, though from grief rather than Stoic calm.

Real Life Example

Kierkegaard breaks his engagement to Regine Olsen
10 September 1840-12 October 1841 · Copenhagen, Denmark
Søren Kierkegaard became engaged to Regine Olsen on 10 September 1840, but soon decided that he could not marry her. Despite her strong opposition, he broke the engagement for good on 12 October 1841, causing her great despair and provoking widespread indignation in Copenhagen, and he left for Berlin for a time. Regine married Johan Frederik Schlegel in 1847, and Kierkegaard, who never married, left her his remaining possessions in his will.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A promise of marriage was withdrawn in a way that exposed the young woman to public talk, though she later built her own life.
The kural's heroine says that the man who once told her not to fear has left her shamed before many, so why should she still fear the town's gossip; Regine was left by her betrothed in a break that all Copenhagen discussed.
