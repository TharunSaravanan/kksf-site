Kural 674

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

வினைபகை என்றிரண்டின் எச்சம் நினையுங்கால் தீயெச்சம் போலத் தெறும்.
Vinai pagai endru irandin echcham ninaiyungaal Theeyechcham polath therum.

Meaning

Think about it: an unfinished task and an enemy not fully beaten are like leftover sparks of a fire. They can grow back and destroy you.

Elementary

When you put out a campfire, you pour water until every last glowing coal is out, because one hidden ember can start a big fire later. A job left half-done, or a quarrel only half-settled, is like that ember.

University

Echcham means 'remainder, leftover', and theeyechcham 'the remnant of a fire'; therum means 'will burn, will destroy'. Parimelazhagar notes that an unfinished undertaking and a partly subdued enemy both look small and harmless, but grow unnoticed, like smouldering embers, until they destroy the one who neglected them; Mu. Varadarasanar stresses this unseen growth. The thought is a commonplace of Indian niti: the Mahabharata's counsel of Kanika (Shanti Parva 12.138.58, critical edition) lists the remnant of a debt, of a fire and of an enemy as things that grow again and again if left unchecked. The older sandhi text reads வினைபகை யென்றிரண்டி னெச்ச நினையுங்காற் / றீயெச்சம் with no change in meaning. Modern readers often apply the enemy half to unresolved conflicts or problems rather than literal foes.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“ṛṇaśeṣo'gniśeṣaśca śatruśeṣastathaiva ca / punaḥ punarvivardheta svalpo'pyanivāritaḥ”
— Mahabharata 12.138.58 (critical edition, Sanskrit text) [3]
In this Sanskrit verse, Kanika tells King Shatrumtapa that what is left over of a debt, a fire or an enemy, however small, grows again and again if it is not dealt with.
This is the same image and the same pairing as the Kural: a leftover fire and a leftover enemy both come back to destroy.
Christianity
“Lest haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all that behold it begin to mock him, Saying, This man began to build, and was not able to finish.”
— Luke 14:29-30 (KJV) [4]
Jesus describes a builder who starts a tower without the means to complete it and ends up a laughing stock.
Luke shows the cost of the first half of the Kural's warning: an unfinished task does not stay harmless but turns against the one who left it.
Islam
The Prophet is reported to have said that God loves it when any of you does a piece of work, that you do it with itqan, that is, thoroughly and completely.
— Hadith in al-Bayhaqi, Shu'ab al-Iman (graded hasan by al-Albani, al-Silsila al-Sahiha 1113) [5]
This hadith makes finishing and perfecting a task a quality God loves.
It supports the task half of the Kural: work should be taken to completion, not left in pieces.
Judaism
King Saul is commanded to finish the war with Amalek but spares King Agag and the best of the spoil; Samuel tells him that because of this incomplete obedience the kingdom will be torn from him.
— 1 Samuel 15 [4]
Saul's half-finished campaign becomes the turning point of his downfall, and later tradition links the leftover Agagite line to Haman.
Like the Kural, the story treats an enemy left partly dealt with as a smouldering danger that later consumes the one who left it.
Buddhism
“Truly, an evil deed committed does not immediately bear fruit, like milk that does not turn sour all at once. But smoldering, it follows the fool like fire covered by ashes.”
— Dhammapada 71 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha warns that consequences can lie hidden like embers under ash before flaring up.
The same image of fire covered by ash carries the Kural's warning about remnants that look dead but are not.
Jainism
“One should not be complacent with a small debt, slight wound, spark of fire and slight passion, because what is small (today) may become bigger (later).”
— Saman Suttam 134 (cf. 133), English tr. on jainworld.com [7]
The preceding verse warns that even one who has repressed all his passions may fall again, so one must not be complacent while remnants of passion remain.
The kural's image of an unfinished task and a half-defeated foe as embers that grow back and destroy is matched almost exactly by this Jain warning about a spark of fire and the remnants of passion.
Sikhism
“If someone focuses on the Lord beforehand, then why should he be punished? The kings had lost their higher consciousness, reveling in pleasure and sensuality.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Ashtapadi (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Writing of Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak says the rulers who ignored the gathering danger while absorbed in pleasure were overtaken by disaster that foresight could have prevented.
The Kural warns that a half-dealt-with enemy grows unnoticed like embers until it destroys the careless; Guru Nanak's lament shows rulers ruined by exactly such neglect of a danger they should have attended to in time.
Greek Mythology
When Heracles cuts off the heads of the Lernaean Hydra, two grow back for each one; only when Iolaus burns each stump with firebrands can the monster be finished off.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5.2 [9]
The Hydra shows that an enemy only partly destroyed regrows stronger unless the job is completed.
The Hydra's regrowing heads are the Kural's echcham: a remnant that multiplies unless it is fully dealt with.
Philosophy
Machiavelli advises that if an injury must be done to an enemy it should be of such a kind that there is no fear of revenge, since men avenge slight injuries but cannot avenge heavy ones.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 3 [10]
He argues that half-measures against opponents are the worst policy because they provoke without disabling.
Machiavelli reaches the Kural's conclusion from political calculation: a half-handled enemy is a future danger.

Real Life Example

Malaria returns to Sri Lanka after near-elimination
1963-1969 · Sri Lanka (then Ceylon)
An intensive spraying campaign cut malaria in Sri Lanka from about 92,000 cases in 1953 to just 17 cases in 1963. In the belief that the disease was nearly gone, control was scaled back: many DDT spraying teams were disbanded when a consolidation phase began in 1964, and surveillance did not react to the rising numbers. Cases grew from 1965 onward into a countrywide epidemic, with more than half a million cases reported in 1969, and the country was not certified malaria-free until 2016.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Sri Lanka's experience became a classic warning in public health that a disease brought low but not finished can return with great force.
The kural warns that an unfinished task is like embers left burning that grow back and destroy, and Sri Lanka's malaria, reduced to a handful of cases and then left unwatched, flared back into an epidemic.
