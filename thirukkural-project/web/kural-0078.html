Kural 78

The Possession of Love · Anbudaimai · அன்புடைமை

அன்பகத் தில்லா உயிர்வாழ்க்கை வன்பாற்கண் வற்றல் மரந்தளிர்த் தற்று.
Anbagath thillaa uyirvaazhkkai vanbaarkan, Vatral marandhalirth thatru.

Meaning

A life without love in the heart is like a dead, dried-up tree in a desert trying to grow green leaves.

Elementary

Picture a dry stick standing in hot sand suddenly growing green leaves. It could not really happen, and if it looked like it did, something would be wrong. This kural says a life with no love in it is like that: it might look like it is going fine, but it is not really alive.

University

The comparison is doubly impossible and that is the point. வற்றல் மரம் is a tree already dried dead, and வன்பாற்கண் is the வன்புலம் or hard, waterless tract — the பாலை landscape of classical Tamil poetics, the desert region associated in akam convention with separation and deprivation. A dead tree sprouting in the desert is not merely unlikely; the couplet's force is that it is a contradiction dressed as a phenomenon. Valluvar is not saying the loveless life is unhappy — he is saying it is not life (உயிர்வாழ்க்கை) at all, only its outward semblance. The use of the tiṇai landscape vocabulary quietly imports the whole emotional register of பாலை (barrenness, loss, the unwatered heart) into an ethical argument, a characteristic Valluvar move of compressing a poetic tradition into seven words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Teaching his son Śvetaketu, Uddālaka points to a great tree: if the living essence leaves one branch, that branch withers; if it leaves a second and a third, they wither too; but when it leaves the whole tree, the whole tree withers and dies.
— Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.11.1–2 (Uddālaka to Śvetaketu) [3]
The Upaniṣad uses the withering tree to show that what looks like a living structure is only alive so long as an invisible animating essence remains in it.
Same image, same argument: the outward form of a life persists for a while after the thing that made it alive has gone.
Christianity
“If a man abide not in me, he is cast forth as a branch, and is withered; and men gather them, and cast them into the fire, and they are burned.”
— Gospel of John 15:6, King James Version [4]
In the vine discourse — which culminates in the command to love one another — Jesus says a branch severed from its source does not slowly decline but simply becomes dead wood.
The Kural's withered tree and John's withered branch make the identical point: separation from the source of love leaves only the shape of life.
Islam
“…a good word is like a good tree, whose root is firmly fixed and its branches [high] in the sky… And the example of a bad word is like a bad tree, uprooted from the surface of the earth, not having any stability.”
— Qurʾān 14:24–26 (Sūrat Ibrāhīm), Sahih International translation [5]
The Qurʾān contrasts two trees — one deeply rooted and perpetually fruiting, one torn loose and unable to stand — as images of speech and the inner state behind it.
Both texts test a life by whether its tree is genuinely rooted and watered or only standing there without any source of life.
Judaism
“For he shall be like the heath in the desert, and shall not see when good cometh; but shall inhabit the parched places in the wilderness, in a salt land and not inhabited.”
— Jeremiah 17:6 (in context of 17:5–8), King James Version [4]
Jeremiah contrasts the person who trusts in mere human strength — a shrub in a salt desert — with one rooted by water, whose leaf stays green even in drought.
Jeremiah's desert shrub and Valluvar's dry tree in the வன்பால் are very nearly the same image used to make the same judgement.
Buddhism
“Like a beautiful flower full of colour but without fragrance, even so, fruitless are the fair words of one who does not practise them.”
— Dhammapada 51, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [6]
The Dhammapada judges a display that has everything except the one quality that would make it real — colour without scent, speech without practice.
Both verses describe an outward flourishing that is hollow because the inward animating quality is missing.
Jainism
“He is empty like a clenched fist, (of no value) like an uncoined false Kârshâpana or like a piece of glass resembling turquoise, he is held lightly by men of discernment.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.41–42 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
An ascetic who keeps the outward forms but neglects vows, penance and self-control is like a hollow fist, a counterfeit coin, or glass passed off as a gem.
Valluvar's dead tree sprouting in a desert is another picture of apparent life with nothing real behind it, which is what this verse describes.
Sikhism
“My body is like the simmal tree; seeing me, other people are fooled.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak compares a life lacking true virtue to the tall, imposing simmal (silk-cotton) tree, whose appearance promises fruit that never satisfies.
Like Valluvar's dead tree putting out shoots in the desert, it pictures a life with the look of flourishing but nothing real inside.
Greek Mythology
Narcissus, who has rejected every lover including the nymph Echo, falls in love with his own reflection in a pool; unable to love anything outside himself, he refuses food and drink, wastes away beside the water, and is found only as a flower.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses III.339–510 (Narcissus and Echo) [9]
Ovid gives the incapacity for outward love a literal physiological result: a beautiful young man dies of thirst beside a spring.
Narcissus is the Kural's withered tree in the barren place — abundant water and beauty all around, and no life in him at all.
Philosophy
“But he who is unable to live in society, or who has no need because he is sufficient for himself, must be either a beast or a god: he is no part of a state.”
— Aristotle, Politics I.2, 1253a, trans. Benjamin Jowett [10]
Aristotle argues that human beings are constitutively political and relational, so a person genuinely needing no one has stepped outside the human category altogether.
Aristotle, like Valluvar, denies the self-enclosed person the status of a real human life rather than merely criticising it.

Real Life Example

The world learns of Romania's state orphanages
1990 · Romania
After the fall of the Ceausescu government at the end of 1989, the outside world learned of large state institutions in which abandoned children were raised with severe psychosocial deprivation; one Bucharest institution held about 850 children under three in 1989. From 2000 the Bucharest Early Intervention Project compared institutionalised children with those placed in foster families. It found that children who stayed in institutions scored far below the others in development, and that foster care, especially when begun early, improved cognitive and emotional outcomes.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The Romanian findings showed that bodily care without love and attention produces only the outward form of a growing child, not a flourishing one.
The kural compares a loveless life to a dead tree putting out shoots in the desert, an appearance of life with nothing behind it, and the orphanage children's stunted development proved how much love is needed for real growth.
