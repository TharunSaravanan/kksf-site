Kural 730

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

உளரெனினும் இல்லாரொடு ஒப்பர் களன்அஞ்சிக் கற்ற செலச்சொல்லா தார்.
Ulareninum illaarodu oppar kalananjik Katra selachchollaa thaar.

Meaning

People who fear a group and so cannot share what they have learned in a way that reaches others are like the dead, even though they are alive.

Elementary

A lamp that is never switched on might as well not be there. When fear stops you from ever sharing what you know, it is as if that part of you isn’t there at all.

University

Uḷar eṉiṉum (“though they exist, are alive”) and illārotu oppar (“they are equal to those who are not”) set being against non-being. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read illār as the dead. Kaḷaṉ (kaḷam, “arena, place of assembly”) stands in for avai for variety, and añci (“fearing”) names the cause. The closing phrase kaṟṟa cela-c-collātār repeats the formula of 722 and 724 and ties the chapter together: to learn but not to pass it on is a kind of death. The sandhi text reads “உளரெனினு மில்லாரொ டொப்பர் களனஞ்சிக்.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for one who has been honored, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna warns Arjuna that shrinking from his duty out of fear would bring a disgrace worse than dying.
In the Gita as in the Kural, a failure of nerve leaves a man worse than dead.
Christianity
“I know thy works, that thou hast a name that thou livest, and art dead.”
— Revelation 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The risen Christ tells the church at Sardis that it has the reputation of being alive but is in fact dead.
‘Alive in name but dead in fact’ is almost the Kural’s own wording for the silent scholar.
Islam
“And is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him light by which to walk among the people like one who is in darkness, never to emerge therefrom?”
— Qur’an 6:122 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes someone given life and light to walk among people, in contrast with someone still in darkness.
Real life means bringing one’s light among people, and the Kural says those who cannot are like the dead.
Judaism
Jeremiah says that when he decided to stop speaking God’s word, it became like a fire shut up in his bones, and he grew weary of holding it in and could not.
— Jeremiah 20:9 [6]
For the prophet, keeping a message unspoken is a torment that eats him from inside.
Knowledge held back instead of spoken makes life unbearable, which is close to the Kural’s ‘alive yet like the dead.’
Buddhism
“Heedfulness is the path to the Deathless. Heedlessness is the path to death. The heedful die not. The heedless are as if dead already.”
— Dhammapada 21 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada calls the heedless as good as dead even while they live.
It uses the same ‘living yet dead’ judgement the Kural passes on the fearful scholar.
Jainism
“Those who having investigated the Law expound it, are awakened and put an end to mundane existence; able to liberate both (themselves and others), they answer the well-deliberated questions.”
— Sutrakritanga I.14.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Nirgrantha lecture praises those who have studied the doctrine and then expound it, answering questions so that both they and others are freed.
The Kural says those who cannot pass on what they learned are like the dead; the Jain verse puts the same value the other way round, praising those who do expound their learning as fully awakened.
Sikhism
“They alone are truly alive, whose minds are filled with the Lord. O Nanak, no one else is truly alive;”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 142, Raag Maajh, Majh ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that only those in whom the Lord dwells are really living; the rest only appear to be alive.
The Kural says those who cannot pass on their learning for fear are like the dead though alive; Guru Nanak uses the same living-yet-dead contrast.
Greek Mythology
After his quarrel with Agamemnon, Achilles sits by his ships and goes neither to the assembly, where men win glory, nor to war, and he lets his heart waste away.
— Homer, Iliad 1.488-492 [10]
Cut off from both assembly and battle, the greatest hero lives on but wastes away.
Withdrawing from the assembly is a slow death of the self, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
Seneca writes that if wisdom were given to him on condition that he keep it shut up and never declare it, he would refuse it, because no good thing is pleasant to possess unless it can be shared.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 6.4 [11]
For Seneca, wisdom that can never be passed on is not worth having.
Learning that is never spoken counts for nothing, the same verdict as the Kural’s.

Real Life Example

Fermat's marginal note and his unpublished mathematics
c. 1637; notes published 1670 · Toulouse, France
Pierre de Fermat, a lawyer and councillor in Toulouse, wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica that he had found a remarkable proof of what became known as Fermat's Last Theorem, but that the margin was too small to contain it. Attempts to have his work published during his lifetime failed largely because he never wanted to put it into polished form. His marginal notes became known only after his son Samuel published them in an edition of the Arithmetica in 1670, and the theorem was not proved until the 1990s.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Fermat's methods and proofs, which he rarely set out in full for others, were largely lost to his contemporaries, and mathematicians spent centuries trying to recover what he claimed to know.
The kural says that those who cannot pass on what they have learned so that it reaches others are like the dead though alive, and Fermat's unshared proofs left his contemporaries with little more than tantalising claims.
