Kural 1028

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

குடிசெய்வார்க் கில்லை பருவம் மடிசெய்து மானங் கருதக் கெடும்.
Kudiseyvaark killai paruvam madiseydhu maanang karudhak kedum.

Meaning

People who work for their family cannot wait for the right time. If they are lazy or worry too much about their pride, the family falls apart.

Elementary

If you wait for the ‘perfect day’ to do your chores, it never comes, and the mess just grows. Valluvar says people who care for a family can't wait for a perfect time or think a job is beneath them; they just have to get going.

University

Paruvam (பருவம், ‘season, proper time’) is denied to the kudiseyvaar (குடிசெய்வார், ‘those who make the family’): any time is the time. The second line names two enemies, madi (மடி, ‘sloth’, the theme of chapter 61) and maanam karudhal (மானம் கருதல், ‘considering one's pride or honour’), either of which makes the family kedum (கெடும், ‘perish’). Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read maanam as standing on one's dignity, refusing humble work; Karunanidhi reads it as being shaken by insults directed at oneself. Pope gives ‘Wait for no season, when you would your house uprear; / ’Twill perish, if you wait supine, or hold your honour dear.’ The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Perform your prescribed duty, for doing so is better than not working. One cannot even maintain one’s physical body without work.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.8 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna says work is always better than inaction and that even bodily survival needs it.
Like the Kural, it rejects idleness, since nothing can be kept going without work.
Christianity
“He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the clouds shall not reap.”
— Ecclesiastes 11:4 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher warns that the farmer who keeps waiting for perfect weather never plants or harvests.
This is the Kural's ‘there is no season’: waiting for the ideal time means ruin.
Islam
“There are two blessings that many people are deceived into losing: health and free time.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6412 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet warns that people waste their health and spare time, thinking there will always be more.
Like the Kural, it warns against letting time slip by in idleness.
Judaism
“Say not: ‘when I shall have leisure I shall study;’ perhaps you will not have leisure.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:4 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
Hillel warns against putting off important work until a convenient time that may never come.
This is the Kural's point that the family-builder has no ‘proper season’ to wait for.
Buddhism
“Who says it is too hot, too cold, too late, and leaves things undone, the opportunities for good go past such men. But he who does not regard cold or heat any more than a blade of grass and who does his duties manfully, does not fall away from happiness.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (tr. Narada Thera, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha warns the householder that making excuses about weather and time loses every chance of prosperity.
It is almost the Kural itself: the one who raises a family has no season to wait for.
Jainism
“As the fallow leaf of the tree falls to the ground, when its days are gone, even so the life of men (will come to its close); Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira tells Gautama that life falls like a dead leaf, so he must never be careless for a moment.
The Jain call to constant heedfulness (apramada) matches the Kural's rejection of waiting and idling, though it aims at liberation rather than household welfare.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, that which you have to do tomorrow - do it today instead; and that which you have to do now - do it immediately! Later on, you will not be able to do anything, when death hangs over your head.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 138) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir warns against putting off what must be done, because the right moment does not wait.
The Kural says those who raise a family have no ‘right season’ to wait for, and that sloth will ruin the family; Kabir says the same of every duty, do it now, for later never comes.
Greek Mythology
“Do not put your work off till to-morrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work: industry makes work go well”
— Hesiod, Works and Days, passage at ll. 405-413 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, sacred-texts.com) [10]
Hesiod warns his brother that the farmer who delays his work ends with an empty barn.
Like the Kural, it says a household is ruined by sloth and by waiting for a later time.
Philosophy
Seneca opens his letters to Lucilius by urging him to seize each hour, because while we put things off, life runs past us.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 1 [11]
The Stoic argues that postponement wastes the one thing we truly own, our time.
This matches the Kural's warning that waiting for a right season ruins the work.

Real Life Example

Maria Sklodowska works as a governess to put her sister through medical school
c. 1885-1891 · Poland (then part of the Russian Empire)
Young Maria Sklodowska, later Marie Curie, made an agreement with her elder sister Bronislawa that Maria would work first to pay for Bronya's medical studies in Paris, and Bronya would later help her in turn. Maria spent several years as a tutor and live-in governess, including a post with a family in the countryside north of Warsaw, before she was finally able to enrol at the Sorbonne in 1891.
— Sources: [12] [13]
She put her own ambitions on hold and took humble work at once so that the family could advance, and the plan succeeded for both sisters.
The kural says that those who raise a family cannot wait for the right season or stand on their dignity, and Maria took modest work immediately rather than wait for better times.
