Kural 269

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

கூற்றம் குதித்தலும் கைகூடும் நோற்றலின் ஆற்றல் தலைப்பட் டவர்க்கு.
Kootram kudhiththalum kaikoodum notralin Aatral thalaippattavarkku.

Meaning

People who have gained the power that comes from strict self-control can even escape death itself.

Elementary

Everyone is scared of death, like a giant monster nobody can beat. Valluvar says a person who has become really strong through tapas can even jump right over that monster.

University

கூற்றம் (Kūṟṟam), ‘the one who separates [soul from body]’, is the Tamil personification of Death, identified with Yama; குதித்தல் means to leap over or escape. The inclusive உம் (‘even’) signals this as the ultimate limit case of tapas-power, following the curse-and-blessing powers of 264. The medieval commentator Manakkudavar explicitly glosses the verse with the legend of Markandeya, the boy-devotee who escaped Yama’s noose, while Parimelazhagar identifies ஆற்றல் as the power of curse and grace. Philosophically, the verse can also be read as conquest of the fear of death, or as liberation from the cycle of births — interpretations that let the verse speak beyond its mythological frame.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Markandeya, destined to die at sixteen, clings to the Shiva linga in devotion and austerity; when Yama casts his noose, Shiva emerges, kicks Yama away, and grants the boy eternal youth.
— Markandeya legend (Shaiva Puranas; Tirukkadavur tradition), cited by Manakkudavar on Kural 269 [4]
Manakkudavar’s own commentary on this Kural cites Markandeya’s escape from death.
The traditional illustration of ‘leaping beyond Kūṟṟam’ through tapas.
Christianity
“O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?”
— 1 Corinthians 15:55 (KJV) [5]
Paul proclaims death conquered for the faithful through resurrection.
Shares the claim that death can be overcome; in Christianity through grace rather than accumulated austerity.
Islam
“And never think of those who have been killed in the cause of Allah as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision.”
— Qur’an 3:169 (Sahih International) [6]
Those who give themselves wholly for God are not truly subject to death.
Supreme self-sacrifice transcends death, a partial echo of the Kural.
Judaism
The Talmud relates that the Angel of Death could not take King David because his mouth never ceased from Torah study; only by distracting him for a moment could it approach.
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 30b [7]
Unbroken spiritual discipline held death itself at bay.
Close to the Kural’s image of disciplined power holding off Death.
Buddhism
“Heedfulness is the path to the Deathless. Heedlessness is the path to death. The heedful die not. The heedless are as if dead already.”
— Dhammapada 21 [8]
Sustained vigilance leads to the Deathless (amata), Nibbana.
Discipline conquers death — understood as release from the round of rebirth.
Jainism
“If a sage truly performs these two kinds of austerities, he will soon be thoroughly released from the Circle of Births.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
After listing the six external and six internal austerities, the lecture promises that their true practice frees the soul entirely from repeated birth and death.
Valluvar says the power of tapas lets one leap even beyond Death; for Jainism, austerity ends the cycle of birth and death altogether, so death no longer has a hold.
Sikhism
“Remembering God, death is eliminated.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 262, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 1, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the opening section of the Sukhmani Guru Arjan says that remembering God drives death away.
The Kural says those with the power of penance can leap even beyond Death; Guru Arjan says the same for one who remembers God.
Greek Mythology
Heracles wrestles Thanatos (Death) at the tomb of Alcestis and forces him to give her back to life.
— Euripides, Alcestis [11]
The much-enduring hero physically overpowers Death.
A mythic ‘leap over Death’ by one whose strength was forged through labours.
Philosophy
“Other people are likely not to be aware that those who pursue philosophy aright study nothing but dying and being dead.”
— Plato, Phaedo 64a (trans. H. N. Fowler) [12]
Socrates says the philosopher’s discipline of detaching soul from body is a rehearsal for death, leaving him unafraid of it.
The disciplined life overcomes death by mastering it in advance.

Real Life Example

Shackleton brings every man of the Endurance expedition home alive
1915-1916 · Weddell Sea, Elephant Island and South Georgia, Southern Ocean
After the ship Endurance was crushed by Antarctic pack ice, Ernest Shackleton's 28 men camped on the ice and reached Elephant Island in lifeboats. Shackleton and five companions sailed the small boat James Caird about 800 miles to South Georgia, and three of them crossed the island's mountains on foot to reach the Stromness whaling station on 20 May 1916. On 30 August 1916 the Chilean tug Yelcho rescued the 22 men left on Elephant Island, and not one member of the ship's crew died.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
The expedition became a classic example of endurance and self-discipline defeating seemingly certain death.
The kural says those who gain the strength of disciplined endurance can leap even beyond Death, and Shackleton's men survived an ordeal that should have killed them.
