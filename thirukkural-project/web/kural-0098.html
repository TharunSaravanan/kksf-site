Kural 98

Charming Utterance · Iniyavai Kooral · இனியவை கூறல்

சிறுமையுள் நீங்கிய இன்சொல் மறுமையும் இம்மையும் இன்பம் தரும்.
Sirumaiyul neengiya insol marumaiyum, Immaiyum inbam tharum.

Meaning

Kind words with no meanness in them bring joy both in this life and in the next.

Elementary

Some people say something nice but slip a little insult inside it, like a stone hidden in a cake. This kural says the kind words that count are the ones with nothing mean tucked inside — and those are the ones that make you happy now and keep on paying you back later.

University

சிறுமை (sirumai) means smallness, and by extension pettiness, meanness or baseness; the phrase சிறுமையுள் நீங்கிய ('having departed from smallness') therefore screens out the backhanded compliment, the sweetly delivered slight, the kindness offered as a display of superiority. Valluvar then makes his only eschatological claim in the chapter, invoking the standard Tamil pairing இம்மை / மறுமை (immai / marumai, this birth and the next). The pairing is significant for the Kural's interpretive history: it has been read by Jain scholars as evidence of a rebirth framework, by Saiva commentators as reference to heaven, and by twentieth-century secular readers as a rhetorical intensifier meaning simply 'always'. The formula 'in this world and the next' is one of the most durable rhetorical structures in world ethics, recurring near-verbatim in the Dhammapada, the Gita and the Mishnah.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Son of Pṛthā, a transcendentalist engaged in auspicious activities does not meet with destruction either in this world or in the spiritual world; one who does good, My friend, is never overcome by evil.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.40 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [4]
Krishna reassures Arjuna that the effort spent on good conduct is never wasted, in either of the two arenas of existence.
It uses the Kural's exact two-world structure to guarantee the durability of good action.
Christianity
“But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned.”
— Matthew 12:36–37 (King James Version) [5]
Jesus makes speech itself the basis of eschatological verdict, treating words as fully weighted moral acts rather than as incidental noise.
It carries the Kural's speech into the next world as decisively as into this one.
Islam
“Anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should talk what is good or keep quiet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6018 (narrated by Abu Hurairah) [6]
The Prophet makes good speech a direct corollary of belief in the hereafter, placing every utterance inside the horizon of final accounting.
It links kind speech to the world to come precisely as the Kural does with marumai.
Judaism
The Mishnah lists certain acts — honouring parents, deeds of loving-kindness, and making peace between people — as those whose fruits a person consumes in this world while the capital remains intact for the world to come.
— Mishnah Peah 1:1 (cf. Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 127a) [7]
The financial metaphor is precise: these acts pay a dividend immediately without depleting the principal reserved for the hereafter.
It is the Kural's double payoff — happiness in this life and the next — expressed as an accounting relationship.
Buddhism
“Here he rejoices, hereafter he rejoices. In both worlds the doer of good rejoices. He rejoices, he is exceedingly glad, seeing the purity of his own deeds.”
— Dhammapada 16 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's twin verses set the wrongdoer and the doer of good in exact parallel, each receiving his result in both worlds.
Structurally and substantively the same promise the Kural makes for speech free of meanness.
Jainism
“He should not tell anything sinful or meaningless or hurtful, neither for his own sake nor for anybody else's, nor without such a motive.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.25 (cf. 1.15) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The Uttarādhyayana's rules of discipline forbid hurtful or empty speech for any motive. The same lecture promises that one who masters himself 'will be happy in this world and in the next' (1.15).
Valluvar promises joy in this birth and the next for kind words free of meanness, and the Jain text joins a ban on hurtful speech to the same promise of happiness in both worlds.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, speaking insipid words, the body and mind become insipid. He is called the most insipid of the insipid; the most insipid of the insipid is his reputation. The insipid person is discarded in the Court of the Lord, and the insipid one's face is spat upon.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak warns that harsh, graceless speech sours the speaker inwardly, ruins his name among people and leaves him rejected in God's court.
It states the Kural's promise in reverse: kind words free of meanness bring joy in this life and the next, while their opposite brings disgrace in both.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod records that the righteous race of heroes was not extinguished but settled by Zeus at the ends of the earth on the Isles of the Blest beside deep-swirling Ocean, where the grain-giving earth bears honey-sweet fruit three times a year.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 167–173 [11]
Greek myth does contain a picture of continued blessedness after death, but it is awarded for heroic worth and divine favour rather than for everyday moral conduct.
It offers the nearest Greek analogue to the Kural's marumai — happiness continuing past this life — without linking it to speech.
Philosophy
Socrates closes the Gorgias with a myth of judgment in which souls are stripped of body and reputation and judged naked, and argues from it that rhetoric is worth having only if it is used to serve justice, since the orator's own soul is what is finally at stake.
— Plato, Gorgias 523a–527e [12]
Plato subordinates the entire question of persuasive speech to the speaker's condition in a post-mortem reckoning.
It reproduces the Kural's move of evaluating speech by its consequences in both this life and the next.

Real Life Example

Abraham Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address
4 March 1865 · Washington, D.C., United States
As the Civil War neared its end, Abraham Lincoln took the oath of office for a second time and delivered a short address at the Capitol. He named slavery as the cause of the war but avoided triumph over the defeated South, closing with the words with malice toward none, with charity for all, and calling on the nation to bind up its wounds. The address is inscribed on the wall of the Lincoln Memorial.
— Sources: [13] [14]
At the moment of victory Lincoln chose words free of pettiness or revenge, and the speech remains one of the most admired in American history.
The kural praises kind words with no trace of meanness, and Lincoln's address spoke to a defeated enemy without spite at the very moment he could have gloated.
