Kural 1223

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

பனிஅரும்பிப் பைதல்கொள் மாலை துனிஅரும்பித் துன்பம் வளர வரும்.
Paniarumpip paidhalkol maalai thuniarumpith thunpam valara varum.

Meaning

The pale evening comes as drops of dew begin to form. With it, my distress begins to grow, and my sorrow keeps getting bigger.

Elementary

Picture tiny dewdrops forming on leaves as the sun goes down. The wife says her sadness grows the same way, drop by drop, every evening, until it feels much bigger than it did in the morning.

University

The couplet is built on a parallel of two ‘buddings’: paṉi arumpi (dew forms like buds) and tuṉi arumpi (aversion or distress begins to sprout), so that the evening’s dew and the wife’s grief swell together. Paital koḷ describes the evening taking on a sallow, faded hue, recalling the wife’s own pasalai (love-pallor) of chapter 119. Commentators divide on time: Varadarasanar and Kesikan read the whole as the present evening, while Solomon Pappaiah and Mu. Karunanidhi contrast an earlier evening that came ‘trembling and pale’ while the husband was near with the present one that brings mounting sorrow; Pope’s couplet explanation follows the latter (‘once came in with trembling and dimness’). Pope: ‘With buds of chilly dew wan evening’s shade enclose; / My anguish buds space and all my sorrow grows.’ Both web texts agree on the wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda, Radha’s friend tells Krishna that in his absence the cool things of evening, sandal paste, moonlight and the soft breeze, feel to Radha like fire and poison, and her suffering grows night by night.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 4 (paraphrase) [3]
The great Sanskrit poem of viraha (love in separation) makes the soothing features of dusk into torments for the waiting beloved.
The Kural’s dewy evening, which should cool, instead makes the wife’s sorrow grow, exactly the reversal Jayadeva paints for Radha.
Christianity
“When I lie down, I say, When shall I arise, and the night be gone? and I am full of tossings to and fro unto the dawning of the day.”
— Job 7:4 (KJV) [4]
Job, in his suffering, dreads the coming of night because it stretches his misery until dawn (the KJV margin notes the Hebrew as ‘the evening be measured’).
Like Job, the Kural’s wife finds that the evening does not bring rest but lengthens and deepens her distress.
Islam
“He said, ‘I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know.’”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob, still separated from Joseph, voices a grief that has kept growing, and turns it toward God.
The wife’s sorrow likewise keeps ‘growing’; Islam’s model is to pour that mounting grief out before God rather than before the evening.
Judaism
“I am weary with my groaning; Every night make I my bed to swim; I melt away my couch with my tears.”
— Psalms 6:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria; 6:6 in KJV numbering) [6]
The Psalmist describes sorrow that returns and swells each night.
The Kural’s wife also finds that each nightfall brings a fresh and larger flood of grief.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha traces grief back to the affection from which it grows.
The Kural shows grief ‘budding’ and growing each evening; the Dhammapada names the root it grows from.
Jainism
“In the same way he who hates ‘colours,’ incurs a long succession of pains; when his mind is filled with hatred, he accumulates Karman which in the end again produces misery.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that aversion to what we perceive is as binding as desire and lengthens our suffering.
The wife now loathes the very sight of evening, and Jain teaching would say that this aversion is itself what makes her sorrow keep growing.
Sikhism
“In the month of Poh, the cold does not touch those, whom the Husband Lord hugs close in His Embrace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 135, Raag Maajh, Barah Maha, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha (song of the twelve months), Guru Arjan says the frost of the winter month cannot touch the bride whom her Lord holds close.
The Kural's pale evening arrives beaded with dew, and the wife's sorrow buds with it; Guru Arjan implies the converse, that cold and frost wound only the bride whose Husband is away.
Greek Mythology
After her son Memnon is killed at Troy, Aurora (Eos), goddess of the dawn, never stops mourning him, and Ovid says her tears still fall over the whole world as the morning dew.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 13.576-622 (paraphrase) [10]
Greek myth explains dew as a goddess’s tears of grief for one she has lost.
The Kural’s ‘dew budding’ at dusk, rising alongside the wife’s sorrow, links dew and grief in the same way.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines longing (desiderium) as the desire to possess a thing that is fed by remembering it and, at the same time, checked by remembering other things that exclude its presence.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Definitions of the Affects 32 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.H.M. Elwes) [11]
For Spinoza, longing is a painful tension between memory of the beloved and awareness that the beloved is absent.
Each evening reminds the wife both of her husband and of his absence, so by Spinoza’s account her longing must grow, as the Kural says.

Real Life Example

Dante writes La Vita Nuova after the death of Beatrice
c. 1293 · Florence, Italy
As a boy of nine, Dante Alighieri glimpsed Beatrice Portinari and fell in love with her, and he remained in love with her until her death in 1290. Stricken with grief, he wrote La Vita Nuova (The New Life) around 1293, a book of sonnets and other poems linked by prose that traces his love from their first meeting through her death and his mourning.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The book is a founding work of Italian literature and a lasting record of how grief for a lost beloved can grow and take over a life.
The kural describes sorrow budding and growing in the one left alone, and Dante's grief after Beatrice's death grew into a whole book of mourning.
