Kural 722

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

கற்றாருள் கற்றார் எனப்படுவர் கற்றார்முன் கற்ற செலச்சொல்லு வார்.
Katraarul katraar enappaduvar katraarmun Katra selachchollu vaar.

Meaning

People who can share what they have learned with other learned people, in a way that really reaches them, are called the most learned of all.

Elementary

Lots of kids can read a book. The one who can explain it clearly to the whole class, even with the teacher listening, is the one everyone calls the real expert.

University

The line repeats kaṟṟār (“the learned”) four times. The word-play mirrors the idea that learning is measured among the learned. The key term is cela-c-colluvār, “those who speak so that it goes (cel-)” into the hearers. Parimelazhagar glosses it as speaking so that the matter enters the listeners’ minds, and Mu. Varadarasanar likewise says “so that it settles in their hearts.” The judgement is about rank: standing among scholars comes from being able to explain things well before one’s peers, not from private learning. The sandhi-joined text reads “கற்றாருட் கற்றா ரெனப்படுவர் கற்றார்முற்,” in which ட் and ற் are sound-changes of ள் and ன் before க.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Janaka offers a thousand cows, with gold on their horns, to the most learned Brahmin at his sacrifice. When the others hesitate, Yajnavalkya claims the cows and then answers one questioner after another from the assembled scholars, including Gargi.
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.1-3.9 (trans. F. Max Müller, Sacred Books of the East 15) [3]
Yajnavalkya’s standing as the foremost knower is settled in public, by answering the learned in front of the learned.
This is the Kural’s “most learned of the learned” shown as an actual event.
Christianity
“And it came to pass, that after three days they found him in the temple, sitting in the midst of the doctors, both hearing them, and asking them questions. And all that heard him were astonished at his understanding and answers.”
— Luke 2:46-47 (KJV) [4]
The twelve-year-old Jesus sits among the teachers of the Law, listening, asking and answering, and they are amazed.
A young learner speaks before the learned and is recognised as learned himself.
Islam
“He said, ‘O Adam, inform them of their names.’ And when he had informed them of their names, He said, ‘Did I not tell you that I know the unseen [aspects] of the heavens and the earth?’”
— Qur’an 2:33 (Saheeh International) [5]
The angels have admitted that they know only what they were taught. Adam, who has been taught the names of things, then lays out that knowledge before them.
Knowledge proves its worth when it is set out before a learned assembly, here the angels.
Judaism
The sons of Bathyra, leaders in Jerusalem, could not recall whether the Passover offering overrides the Sabbath. Hillel, a newcomer from Babylonia, proved the answer before them by formal reasoning and tradition, and they appointed him their head (Nasi).
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 66a [6]
Hillel’s leadership was granted because he could lay out his learning convincingly before the learned.
This is exactly the Kural’s claim: those who can explain before scholars are ranked first among scholars.
Buddhism
“That monk who has control over his tongue, is moderate in speech, unassuming and who explains the Teaching in both letter and spirit — whatever he says is pleasing.”
— Dhammapada 363 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada praises the monk who can expound the teaching accurately and pleasingly.
Its standard of excellence, explaining the teaching so that it is well received, matches the Kural’s cela-c-collutal.
Jainism
“(If the pupil does not understand his short explanation), he should explain at greater length. When the pupil has heard it, he will correctly understand the Truth.”
— Sutrakritanga I.14.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The same lecture tells the teacher to fit his explanation to the hearer and to lengthen it when a short account does not get through, so that the listener really grasps the truth.
The Kural praises those who speak so that their learning actually reaches the hearers; the Jain teacher is likewise judged by whether his words reach and settle in the listener.
Sikhism
“The Siddhas formed an assembly; sitting in their Yogic postures, they shouted, ‘Salute this gathering of Saints.’ … I offer my salutation to the One who is true, infinite and incomparably beautiful.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 938, Raag Ramkali, Siddh Gosht, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Siddh Gosht opens with Guru Nanak facing an assembly of learned yogis; he answers their questions one by one, setting out his teaching so that it reaches them.
The Kural calls 'most learned of the learned' those who can set out their learning before the learned so that it reaches them; Siddh Gosht shows Guru Nanak doing this before the Siddhas' assembly.
Greek Mythology
“when he raised his voice, and the words came driving from his deep chest like winter snow before the wind, then there was none to touch him, and no man thought further of what he looked like.”
— Homer, Iliad 3 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Antenor remembers Odysseus coming to Troy as an envoy. At first he stood stiff with his eyes on the ground, but once he began to speak no one could match him.
Odysseus proved himself foremost by speaking before an assembly of Trojan elders.
Philosophy
Quintilian takes up Cato’s definition of the orator as “a good man skilled in speaking” (vir bonus dicendi peritus) and makes it the goal of his whole course of education.
— Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria 12.1.1 [11]
For Quintilian, the complete man of learning is the one who can bring his knowledge and character to bear in public speech.
Like the Kural, it treats effective speech before an audience as the crown of learning.

Real Life Example

David Hilbert presents his problems to the International Congress of Mathematicians
8 August 1900 · Paris, France
At the second International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris, David Hilbert gave a talk in which he described ten problems from a list of twenty-three unsolved questions he believed would shape mathematics in the coming century. The full list appeared in the published paper. Several of the problems became central to twentieth-century mathematics, and the organisers later placed his paper among the plenary lectures because of its importance.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Before an audience of the world's leading mathematicians, Hilbert set out his vision so clearly that it directed much of their research for generations.
The kural says the most learned of the learned are those who can present their learning before the learned so that it truly reaches them, which is what Hilbert's address achieved.
