Kural 920

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

இருமனப் பெண்டிரும் கள்ளும் கவறும் திருநீக்கப் பட்டார் தொடர்பு.
Irumanap pendirum kallum kavarum thiruneekkap pattaar thodarbu.

Meaning

Two-faced women, alcohol and gambling are the friends of people whom good luck has left behind.

Elementary

Imagine a kid who spends all their allowance on silly gambling games at the fair, sugary junk, and ‘friends’ who only like them for their money. Soon the money is gone and so are the ‘friends’. Valluvar says these habits are a sign that someone’s good luck is slipping away.

University

The closing couplet groups three ruinous attachments: irumanap pendir (இருமனப் பெண்டிர், ‘women of two minds’, whose heart is in one place and body in another, as Solomon Pappaiah explains), kal (கள், toddy or liquor) and kavaru (கவறு, dice). Their thodarbu (தொடர்பு, ‘association’) belongs to those thiru-neekkap-pattaar (திருநீக்கப் பட்டார்), ‘those from whom Thiru has been removed’; Thiru is both fortune and the goddess Lakshmi (Thirumagal), which is how Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesigan read it. Parimelazhagar notes that the couplet links this chapter to the next two, Kallunnaamai (93, not drinking) and Soodhu (94, gambling), so it works as a hinge for the whole cluster of vices. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Drinking, dice, women, and hunting, these four (which have been enumerated) in succession, he must know to be the most pernicious in the set that springs from love of pleasure.”
— Manusmriti 7.50 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu warns a king that these are the worst of the vices born of love of pleasure.
Three of Manu’s four (drink, dice, women) are exactly Valluvar’s triad of ruin.
Christianity
“And not many days after the younger son gathered all together, and took his journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living.”
— Luke 15:13 (KJV) [4]
The Prodigal Son loses his inheritance through careless living; his brother later accuses him of spending it on harlots (Luke 15:30).
The prodigal is the thiru-neekkap-pattaan, the man whose fortune leaves him through such company.
Islam
“O you who have believed, indeed, intoxicants, gambling, [sacrificing on] stone alters [to other than Allah], and divining arrows are but defilement from the work of Satan, so avoid it that you may be successful.”
— Qur’an 5:90 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an groups intoxicants and gambling as works of Satan to be avoided for success.
Two of Valluvar’s three ruinous companions appear here, set against ‘success’ (falah), close to Valluvar’s thiru.
Judaism
“He that loveth pleasure shall be a poor man; He that loveth wine and oil shall not be rich.”
— Proverbs 21:17 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs links the love of pleasure and wine to poverty.
Valluvar likewise says such loves are the company of those whom fortune has left.
Buddhism
“Dice, women, liquor, dancing, singing, sleeping by day, sauntering at unseemly hours, evil companions, avarice — these nine causes ruin a man.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha lists, among causes of ruin, the very triad Valluvar names.
Dice, women, liquor appear at the head of the list, mirroring Kural 920’s triad of lost fortune.
Jainism
Jain codes of lay conduct (shravakachara texts) list seven vices (vyasanas) that a layman must give up completely: gambling, drinking alcohol, eating meat, sexual promiscuity (visiting prostitutes), hunting, theft and adultery.
— Saptavyasana (seven vices) of the Jain shravakachara literature; glossary entry 'Vyasana', JAINpedia (Institute of Jainology) [8]
The seven-vice list ties prostitution, drink and gambling together as addictions that ruin a householder.
Kural 920 groups exactly these, double-minded women, liquor and dice, as the companions of those whom fortune has abandoned.
Sikhism
“The drunkards gamble and look at the wives of others.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 34, Pauri 16 (English translation as published on BaniDB/SikhiToTheMax and searchgurbani.com) [9]
Bhai Gurdas puts drink, gambling and lust for other men’s wives in one line as a single cluster of sins, before saying that even these are less grave than turning one’s back on the Guru. The same three ruinous habits recur together in Sikh moral teaching.
This is the Kural’s own triad, double-minded women, liquor and dice, named together as the companions of the ruined.
Greek Mythology
Elpenor, the youngest of Odysseus’ crew, drinks heavily in Circe’s house, sleeps on the roof, and in the morning stumbles off it and breaks his neck.
— Homer, Odyssey 10-11 [10]
Homer’s small tragedy shows indulgence bringing sudden, pointless ruin.
Elpenor is a minor Greek example of the drink in Kural 920’s triad of misfortune.
Philosophy
Epicurus writes that a pleasant life is produced not by continuous drinking and revelry, nor by sensual indulgence or luxurious tables, but by sober reasoning that examines every choice and avoidance.
— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus (Diogenes Laertius 10.132) [11]
Even the philosopher of pleasure rejects drinking, revelry and indulgence as the path to happiness.
Kural 920’s ruinous companions are the very things Epicurus says do not make life good.

Real Life Example

Hogarth publishes A Rake's Progress
25 June 1735 · London, England
William Hogarth painted a series of eight pictures, now in Sir John Soane's Museum, and published engravings of them in 1735, after Parliament passed the copyright act known as Hogarth's Act. The series follows Tom Rakewell, a young heir who squanders his inheritance on a dissolute life, including a night of revelry at the Rose Tavern and the loss of a second fortune at the gaming table. He ends in debtors' prison and finally in Bethlem Hospital, known as Bedlam.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
One of the most famous moral satires in British art traces how drink, dissolute company and gambling together ruin a young man of fortune.
The kural names double-minded women, strong drink and dice as the companions of those whom good fortune has abandoned, and Hogarth's series shows all three leading his hero to ruin.
