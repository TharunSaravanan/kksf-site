Kural 1078

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

சொல்லப் பயன்படுவர் சான்றோர் கரும்புபோல் கொல்லப் பயன்படும் கீழ்.
Sollap payanpaduvar saandror karumbupol kollap payanpadum keezh.

Meaning

Good people help you as soon as you tell them your need. Mean people are like sugarcane: they give their juice only when they are crushed.

Elementary

To get juice from sugarcane, you have to squeeze and crush it hard. A good person helps you as soon as you ask nicely. A mean person is like the sugarcane: he gives nothing until he is squeezed.

University

The couplet sets the saandror (சான்றோர், 'the noble, the perfect in virtue', the subject of ch. 99) against the keezh. The noble are solla (சொல்ல, 'on being told') useful: as Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah gloss it, they give as soon as the needy describe their want. The base, karumbu pol (கரும்புபோல், 'like sugarcane'), yield only kolla (கொல்ல, 'on being crushed, killed'). Parimelazhagar takes kolla as being pressed to the point of torment, as cane is crushed in a mill; Pope's prose gloss follows ('only when they are tortured to death'). The image recurs in Tamil didactic poetry, though there the sweetness of crushed cane is sometimes used for the noble who stay sweet under pressure. Pope's verse: 'The good to those will profit yield fair words who use; / The base, like sugar-cane, will profit those who bruise.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That gift which is given out of duty, at the proper time and place, to a worthy person, and without expectation of return, is considered to be charity in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.20 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita describes the highest, sattvic giving: given freely, out of duty, with no thought of return.
This is how the Kural's saandror give, as soon as they are asked, in contrast to the base who must be crushed.
Christianity
“And he would not for a while: but afterward he said within himself, Though I fear not God, nor regard man; Yet because this widow troubleth me, I will avenge her, lest by her continual coming she weary me.”
— Luke 18:4-5 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus' parable, an unjust judge finally grants a widow's plea only because her constant pressure wears him down.
The unjust judge is the Kural's sugarcane: he gives only when squeezed.
Islam
“[Saying], "We feed you only for the face [i.e., approval] of Allāh. We wish not from you reward or gratitude.”
— Qur'an 76:9 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an praises the righteous who feed the needy freely, seeking nothing in return.
These are the Kural's saandror, who give at a word, unlike the base who must be crushed like cane.
Judaism
“He who desires that he himself should give, and that others should give: he is a pious man; He who desires that he himself should not give and that others too should not give: he is a wicked man.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:13 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah sorts givers by their willingness, calling the willing giver pious and the one who wants no giving at all wicked.
Like the Kural, it judges people by whether they give freely or have to be forced.
Buddhism
“A person of integrity gives a gift with a sense of conviction. A person of integrity gives a gift attentively. A person of integrity gives a gift in season. A person of integrity gives a gift with an empathetic heart.”
— Sappurisadana Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.148 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes how a good person gives: with conviction, care, good timing and sympathy.
The Kural's saandror give in this way, promptly and with empathy, as soon as they hear of a need.
Jainism
“The high-born, even when they have not anything, will approach those who are in want, and be a prop when they totter.”
— Naladiyar 15.10, chapter 'High Birth' (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Naladiyar says that the truly noble seek out the needy and support them even when they themselves have little.
This is the Kural's saandror, who give as soon as they learn of need, the opposite of the cane that yields only when crushed.
Sikhism
“One who performs selfless service, without thought of reward, shall attain his Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 286, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan praises seva done freely, without any wish for return.
The Kural says the noble help as soon as they are told of a need, while the base give only when crushed like sugarcane; Guru Arjan praises service given freely, with no need to be forced or rewarded.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop's fable of the walnut tree, a tree by the roadside bears a rich crop, and passers-by beat its branches with sticks and stones to get the nuts.
— Aesop's Fables, 'The Walnut-Tree' (tr. George Fyler Townsend) [10]
The tree complains that it is beaten by the very people who enjoy its fruit.
The fable shares the Kural's image of a giver from whom goods come only by beating, though Aesop's sympathy lies with the tree.
Philosophy
Seneca teaches that a benefit should be given readily and without delay, since a gift that has to be dragged out of the giver, or that clings long to his hands, loses most of its value and earns little gratitude.
— Seneca, On Benefits 2.1-2 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
For Seneca, promptness is part of what makes a gift a real benefit.
Seneca's ideal giver is the Kural's saandror; the gift wrung out under pressure is the base man's.

Real Life Example

Tsar Nicholas II issues the October Manifesto
17 October 1905 (30 October, New Style) · St Petersburg, Russia
During the Revolution of 1905 a general political strike spread across Russia, shutting factories, transport and newspapers. Under that pressure Tsar Nicholas II issued the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties and an elected Duma. When the Fundamental Laws were issued in April 1906, the rights actually granted were narrower than the manifesto had promised.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The manifesto ended unlimited autocracy in Russia on paper, but it was a concession forced out of the government rather than freely given.
Like sugarcane that gives juice only when crushed, the tsar granted reforms only when a nationwide strike pressed hard, and then gave less than he had promised.
