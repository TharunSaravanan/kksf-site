Kural 685

The Envoy · Thoodhu · தூது

தொகச்சொல்லித் தூவாத நீக்கி நகச்சொல்லி நன்றி பயப்பதாந் தூது.
Thogachchollith thoovaadha neekki nagachcholli Nandri payappadhaan thoodhu.

Meaning

A good envoy sums things up briefly, leaves out harsh words, speaks so pleasantly that people smile, and so brings good to their king.

Elementary

If you need to tell a friend they broke your toy, you don’t list every bad thing they ever did. You say it short, you leave out the mean words, and you say it kindly, maybe with a smile. Then they are much more likely to help fix it.

University

Parimelazhagar reads the three verbs as two techniques. Tokac colli is condensing: when many demands must be made, state the one cause from which they all follow, so the other side agrees because the point is short and has to be worked out. Tūvāta nīkki naka colli applies to unwelcome demands: drop the harsh words (veyya col) and put things so pleasantly that the listener agrees without feeling the sting. Both serve naṉṟi payattal, securing benefit for one’s own king. Manakkudavar says the couplet teaches ‘how to speak’. Kalaignar glosses tūvāta as what would provoke anger, and Varadarasanar as what is useless. The couplet repeats the ideals of chapter 65 on speech, applied to diplomacy. On the text: all sources agree; the final தூது follows நன்றி பயப்பதாம் with the m assimilated (பயப்பதாந்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [5]
The Gita defines disciplined speech as true, pleasant, helpful, and free of anything that disturbs the listener.
The Kural gives the envoy the same pattern: leave out words that wound, speak pleasantly, and bring benefit.
Christianity
“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man.”
— Colossians 4:6 (KJV) [6]
Paul tells believers to speak graciously and aptly to outsiders so that they can answer each person well.
Gracious, well-seasoned speech to outsiders is the envoy’s craft the Kural describes.
Islam
“Go, both of you, to Pharaoh. Indeed, he has transgressed. And speak to him with gentle speech that perhaps he may be reminded or fear [Allah].”
— Qur’an 20:43-44 (Sahih International) [7]
God sends Moses and Aaron as envoys to a tyrant and tells them to speak gently so that he might listen.
Softening a hard message so that a hostile ruler will listen is exactly the Kural’s tūvāta nīkki.
Judaism
“By long forbearing is a prince persuaded, and a soft tongue breaketh the bone.”
— Proverbs 25:15 (KJV) [6]
The proverb teaches that patience and gentle speech can persuade a ruler and overcome even the hardest resistance.
The Kural’s envoy likewise wins over a foreign king with soft words.
Buddhism
The Buddha says speech with five qualities is well spoken: it is said at the right time, it is true, it is said gently, it is beneficial, and it is said with a mind of goodwill.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.198, Vācā Sutta [8]
Good speech in the Buddha’s teaching is timely, true, gentle and beneficial.
Gentle and beneficial speech is the Kural’s naka colli … naṉṟi payattal.
Jainism
“Disobedient, rough speaking, ill-behaved pupils will exasperate even a gentle teacher; but those will soon win even a hot-tempered teacher who humour him and are polite.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The verse contrasts rough speech, which alienates even the gentle, with courteous, agreeable speech, which wins over even the irritable.
As the Kural's envoy avoids harsh words and speaks pleasantly to win advantage for his master, the Jain text teaches that politeness, not rough speech, wins a superior's goodwill.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are in the Guru Granth Sahib, forbids even one harsh word on the ground that the Lord dwells in every listener.
The Kural's true envoy drops harsh words and speaks so pleasantly that listeners agree; Farid's rule against any harsh word gives that diplomatic courtesy a spiritual basis.
Greek Mythology
Agamemnon’s offer to Achilles ends with a demand that Achilles yield to him as the more kingly man. When Odysseus repeats the offer to Achilles, he lists the gifts but quietly leaves out that insulting demand.
— Homer, Iliad 9.158-161 and 9.225-306 [11]
Odysseus edits his king’s message and drops the part that would only anger the hearer, so the offer has a better chance.
This is tūvāta nīkki, leaving out the harsh words, carried out by Homer’s model envoy.
Philosophy
François de Callières, a French diplomat, advised that a good negotiator should put things agreeably, keep his temper, and never offend a prince with harsh words, because a well-turned phrase wins more than a blunt demand.
— François de Callières, De la manière de négocier avec les souverains (1716) [12]
Callières’ early handbook of diplomacy treats pleasant, tactful speech as a working tool.
His advice closely matches the Kural’s envoy, who speaks so as to please while pursuing his master’s gain.

Real Life Example

Ralph Bunche negotiates the 1949 armistice agreements on Rhodes
1949 · Rhodes, Greece
After the UN mediator Count Folke Bernadotte was killed in September 1948, his chief aide Ralph Bunche became acting mediator in the Arab-Israeli conflict. Working from the island of Rhodes through eleven months of almost continuous negotiation, he obtained signatures on separate armistice agreements between Israel and the individual Arab states. Time magazine credited the first agreement largely to his ability, patience, tact and unflagging good humour, and in 1950 he received the Nobel Peace Prize.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Bunche kept bitterly opposed parties at the table by tact and humour rather than pressure, and the agreements he secured stood as the framework for nearly two decades.
The kural praises the envoy who avoids harsh words and speaks so pleasantly that his hearers are won over, and Bunche's tact and good humour at Rhodes are a modern example.
