Kural 579

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

ஒறுத்தாற்றும் பண்பினார் கண்ணும்கண் ணோடிப் பொறுத்தாற்றும் பண்பே தலை.
Oruththaatrum panbinaar kannum kannodip Poruththaatrum panbe thalai.

Meaning

The highest goodness is to show kindness and patiently put up with even those who like to hurt us.

Elementary

If a classmate keeps being mean to you, the easy thing is to be mean back. Valluvar says the very best thing is to stay kind and patient with them anyway. That is the hardest kind of kindness, and so it is the greatest.

University

ஒறுத்து ஆற்றும் பண்பினார் means ‘those whose nature is to cause harm/afflict’, and the matching பொறுத்து ஆற்றும் பண்பு is ‘the nature that bears it and carries on’; the verbal rhyme ஒறுத்து/பொறுத்து sets the injurer’s nature against the forbearer’s. கண்ணும் (‘even towards’) extends kannottam from familiar friends to enemies, the chapter’s highest reach. Commentators differ slightly on the object: Mu. Varadarasanar reads ‘even those who deserve punishment’, stressing a judge’s leniency, while Solomon Pappaiah and the English gloss read ‘those who grieve or harm us’, stressing personal forbearance; Parimelazhagar’s royal reading allows both. தலை (‘head’, i.e., foremost) ranks this above all other forms of kindness. Both texts agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has கண்ணுங்கண் for ytamizh’s கண்ணும்கண்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not envious but is a kind friend to all living entities, who does not think himself a proprietor and is free from false ego, who is equal in both happiness and distress, who is tolerant…”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.13-14 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes the devotee dear to him as maitraḥ karuṇaḥ (friendly and compassionate) and kṣamī (forgiving) toward all beings.
Kindness and forbearance toward all, including those who harm us, is the Kural’s supreme virtue.
Christianity
“Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.”
— Luke 23:34 (KJV) [4]
On the cross, Jesus prays for the very people who are putting him to death.
This is the Kural’s highest virtue at its limit: kindly bearing with those who are actively harming one.
Islam
“And not equal are the good deed and the bad. Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend.”
— Quran 41:34 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran commands answering hostility with something better, promising that an enemy may become a close friend.
Returning kindness for harm is exactly the forbearing kannottam the Kural ranks highest.
Judaism
The Talmud tells of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi, who was plagued by a heretic and waited for the moment of divine anger to curse him, but dozed off; on waking he concluded that cursing people is not proper, since God’s mercy is over all his creatures, even sinners.
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 7a [6]
The sage learns that even toward someone who torments him, the right course is mercy rather than retaliation.
Rabbi Yehoshua’s turn from cursing to forbearance is the Kural’s kindly patience toward those who harm us.
Buddhism
“Overcome the angry by non-anger; overcome the wicked by goodness; overcome the miser by generosity; overcome the liar by truth.”
— Dhammapada 223 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada teaches meeting each vice with its opposite virtue rather than in kind.
Overcoming the wicked by goodness is the Kural’s forbearing kindness toward those who hurt us.
Jainism
“Ceasing to use the stick (i.e. cruelty) against living beings, abandoning the care of the body, the houseless (Mahâvîra), the Venerable One, endures the thorns of the villages (i.e. the abusive language of the peasants), (being) perfectly enlightened.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.9.3.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org, where it is numbered Lecture 8, Lesson 3) [8]
In the pathless Ladha country villagers beat Mahavira, set dogs on him and abused him, and he bore it all without retaliating.
The Kural ranks forbearing kindness toward those who hurt us as the highest virtue; Mahavira's patience under the Ladha villagers' blows and insults is the Jain model of that forbearance.
Sikhism
“Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir places forgiveness at the highest level, as the very place where God is present.
The Kural calls it the highest virtue to bear patiently with those whose nature is to hurt us; Kabir likewise exalts forgiveness above all, as the dwelling of God Himself.
Greek Mythology
When the aged Priam comes by night to Achilles’ tent to ransom the body of Hector, the son who killed Patroclus, Achilles, moved by pity, weeps with him, returns the body and grants a truce for the funeral.
— Homer, Iliad 24 [10]
The greatest warrior of the Iliad extends compassion to the father of his worst enemy.
Achilles’ pity for Priam is kindly regard shown to one bound up with the very person who hurt him.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius reminds himself that benevolence is invincible if it is genuine, and that the most insolent man can do nothing to one who stays kind to him and gently corrects him.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.18 [11]
For the Stoic, unshakeable goodwill toward offenders is both the highest and the strongest stance.
Marcus’s invincible kindness toward those who wrong us is the Kural’s supreme forbearance.

Real Life Example

J. R. Jayewardene urges forgiveness of Japan at the San Francisco Peace Conference
6 September 1951 · San Francisco, California, United States
At the conference called to make peace with Japan after the Second World War, Ceylon's finance minister J. R. Jayewardene addressed the delegates in the War Memorial Opera House. Citing the Buddha's teaching that hatred is ended not by hatred but by love, he argued that Japan should be allowed to rejoin the community of nations as a free country, and Ceylon renounced its claim to reparations. Japanese leaders have repeatedly acknowledged the speech in the decades since.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A country that had suffered wartime attack chose to answer its former enemy with forbearance rather than retribution.
The kural calls it the highest virtue to show kindly regard and patiently bear with even those who have hurt us, and Ceylon's stand at San Francisco extended that regard to a former enemy.
