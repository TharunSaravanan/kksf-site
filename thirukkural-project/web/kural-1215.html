Kural 1215

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

நனவினால் கண்டதூஉம் ஆங்கே கனவுந்தான் கண்ட பொழுதே இனிது.
Nanavinaal kandadhooum aange kanavundhaan kanda pozhudhae inidhu.

Meaning

Seeing him while I was awake was sweet only while it lasted. Seeing him in a dream is just the same, sweet for the moment I see him.

Elementary

An ice-cream cone is lovely while you are eating it, and then it’s gone. A dream about someone you love is the same: it is just as lovely while it lasts. This woman says both kinds of seeing, awake and dreaming, are sweet, but only in the moment.

University

Kaṇṭatūum is kaṇṭatu, ‘what was seen’, with uyir-aḷapeṭai, the metrical prolongation of the vowel (-ūum) that Tamil prosody uses to fill out a foot. Āṅkē, ‘just then, at that time’, and kaṇṭa poḻutē, ‘at the very moment of seeing’, set the two experiences on a level: both sweetnesses are bounded by the moment. Varadarasanar stresses the limitation (‘only at that time… only at the time it is seen’), whereas Pappaiah and Kalaignar stress the equality (‘both were pleasures to me’); the couplet can carry both, a consolation tinged with the knowledge that waking joy too was fleeting. Pope: ‘As what I then beheld in waking hour was sweet, / So pleasant dreams in hour of sleep my spirit greet.’ Both base sources print the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Mandukya Upanishad describes the dream state (taijasa) as a condition of consciousness turned inward that enjoys its own subtle objects, as the waking state enjoys gross ones.
— Mandukya Upanishad 4 (paraphrase) [3]
Vedantic analysis treats dreaming and waking as parallel states of experience, each with its own real enjoyment.
The Kural’s heroine reaches the same parity from the side of feeling: the dream-sight of her husband is enjoyed as fully as the waking one was.
Christianity
“I sat down under his shadow with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste.”
— Song of Solomon 2:3 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song of Solomon savours the sweetness of being near her beloved.
The Kural measures dream-sight against exactly this remembered sweetness of being with the beloved, and finds it equal.
Islam
The Prophet taught that nothing of prophethood remains after him except glad tidings, and explained these as good, true dreams.
— Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of the Interpretation of Dreams, on al-mubashshirat (paraphrase) [5]
In the Sunni tradition, a good dream is a real portion of blessing granted to the believer.
The Kural likewise refuses to discount the dream: its sweetness is as real as waking joy.
Judaism
“When the LORD brought back those that returned to Zion, We were like unto them that dream. Then was our mouth filled with laughter, and our tongue with singing.”
— Psalms 126:1-2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist likens the joy of a longed-for homecoming to the joy of dreaming.
Both texts set dream-joy and real joy side by side as equally sweet, the psalm by simile, the Kural by experience.
Buddhism
The closing verse of the Diamond Sutra bids the disciple regard all conditioned things as a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, as dew or a flash of lightning.
— Diamond Sutra (Vajracchedika), closing verse (paraphrase) [7]
Mahayana teaching places waking experience and dreams on the same fleeting footing.
The Kural’s heroine arrives at the same parity, waking sweetness and dream sweetness both lasting only ‘while seen’, but she treasures what the sutra counsels letting go.
Jainism
“These my excellent and pre-eminent dreams shall not be counteracted by other bad dreams.” Accordingly she remained awake to save her dreams by means of (hearing) good, auspicious, pious, agreeable stories about gods and religious men.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §§54-55 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Queen Trisala treasures her auspicious dreams so much that she stays awake to keep their sweetness intact.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Trisala holds that a good dream is a real treasure whose sweetness belongs to the time it is seen and must be guarded.
Sikhism
“The world is a drama, staged in a dream. In a moment, the play is played out.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 18, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that worldly life is as passing as a dream, a show that ends in a moment.
The Kural's wife finds that seeing her husband awake was sweet only while it lasted, just like seeing him in a dream; Gurbani goes further and calls waking life itself a dream that is over in a moment.
Greek Mythology
“This very night methought there was one lying by my side who was like Ulysses as he was when he went away with his host, and I rejoiced, for I believed that it was no dream, but the very truth itself.”
— Homer, Odyssey 20 (tr. Samuel Butler, via the Internet Classics Archive) [10]
Penelope tells how, in a dream, her long-absent husband seemed beside her as he was when he left, and her joy was as full as if it were real.
This is the Kural’s claim lived out: the dream-sight of the absent husband was, while it lasted, as sweet as waking union.
Philosophy
Socrates asks Theaetetus how anyone could prove whether we are now asleep or awake, noting that in dreams we believe what we see just as firmly as in waking life.
— Plato, Theaetetus 158b-d (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato observes that, at the time it is had, a dream experience carries the same conviction as waking experience.
The Kural’s heroine makes the emotional version of Plato’s point: in the moment of seeing, dream and waking are equally sweet.

Real Life Example

Kekulé describes the dream behind the benzene ring
11 March 1890 (recalling 1865) · Berlin, Germany, recalling his time at Ghent, Belgium
In 1865, while a professor at Ghent, August Kekulé published his proposal that the six carbon atoms of benzene form a ring. At a celebration in Berlin in 1890 marking the 25th anniversary of the theory, he told his audience that the idea had come to him while dozing by the fire, when he saw a snake seize its own tail. Most historians accept the account, though some suspect he was strengthening his claim to priority.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A fleeting dream image, gone the moment he woke, became one of the foundations of organic chemistry.
The kural says that a dream meeting is sweet for exactly the moment it is seen, just as a waking one was; Kekulé's brief dream image mattered only in the moment of seeing it, and he had to seize it before it vanished.
