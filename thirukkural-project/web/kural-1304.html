Kural 1304

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

ஊடி யவரை உணராமை வாடிய வள்ளி முதலரிந் தற்று.
Ooti yavarai unaraamai vaatiya valli mudhalarin thatru.

Meaning

Not making up with the one who is sulking is like cutting the root of a vine that is already drooping.

Elementary

A thirsty plant droops when it needs water. If, instead of watering it, someone cut its root, it would never recover. Someone who is sulking is like that drooping plant: they need care, not to be ignored.

University

Ūṭiyavarai uṇarāmai, ‘not making those who sulk understand (and so ending the sulk),’ is likened to cutting at the root (mutal arital) a valli creeper that is already wilted (vāṭiya). Uṇartal here is the technical term for appeasing a lover’s sulk by explanation and tenderness; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss ‘without showing love,’ while Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah add that the creeper had already withered for want of water, so the cutting destroys any chance of recovery. The image completes the previous couplet: the sulk is a sign of a love already parched by separation, and neglect kills it outright. Pope renders the explanation ‘Not to reconcile those who have feigned dislike is like cutting a faded creeper at its root.’ The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Where the female relations live in grief, the family soon wholly perishes; but that family where they are not unhappy ever prospers.”
— Manusmriti 3.57 (tr. Georg Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
The Laws of Manu tie the survival of a household to the contentment of its women.
The Kural’s withering creeper is the same warning in a single image: a grieving partner left uncomforted means the family’s love dies at the root.
Christianity
“A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench, till he send forth judgment unto victory.”
— Matthew 12:20 (KJV) [4]
Matthew applies Isaiah’s prophecy to Jesus, who does not crush what is already weak and fading.
The Kural’s plant image says the same: what is already drooping should be restored, not cut down.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes marriage as a bond meant to be sustained by affection and mercy.
Neglecting a sulking partner withholds the very mercy that keeps this bond alive, as the Kural’s cut creeper shows.
Judaism
“Therefore, behold, I will allure her, And bring her into the wilderness, And speak tenderly unto her.”
— Hosea 2:16 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Hosea pictures God, the estranged husband of Israel, winning back his alienated bride with tender words rather than abandoning her.
The Kural asks the husband to do the same: woo back the one who has withdrawn instead of letting the bond wither.
Buddhism
When the monks of Kosambi quarrel bitterly, the Buddha urges them to reconcile and teaches that hatred is never appeased by hatred, but only by non-hatred.
— Vinaya Mahavagga 10 and Dhammapada 5 (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha treats lingering estrangement as something that destroys community unless met with goodwill.
The Kural similarly warns that a quarrel left unhealed destroys the relationship at its root.
Jainism
In the Jain Ramayana, Prince Pavananjaya, stung by a slight on his wedding day, neglects his wife Anjana for years while she pines; on campaign he sees a chakravaka bird crying for its lost mate, realizes his cruelty, and hurries back to her.
— Vimalasuri, Paumacariya (Anjana-Pavananjaya episode; paraphrase; also in Hemacandra’s Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra, book 7) [8]
The story shows a husband who nearly destroys his marriage through stubborn neglect and is brought back by an image of grieving love.
Pavananjaya’s neglect of the pining Anjana is the Kural’s withered creeper cut at the root, narrowly averted.
Sikhism
“Fareed, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Salok Farid, Bhagat Shaikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Shaikh Farid tells the seeker to meet hurt with kindness rather than anger.
The Kural warns that failing to soothe a sulking partner is like cutting a wilting creeper at the root; Farid likewise asks us to heal a wounded bond with kindness instead of harshness.
Greek Mythology
Echo, rejected by Narcissus, wastes away in grief until only her voice remains.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid shows love that is spurned withering until nothing of the lover is left.
The Kural’s wilting creeper cut at the root is the same fate: love ignored does not survive.
Philosophy
Aristotle quotes the saying that lack of conversation has broken many a friendship, noting that friends who do not meet and share their lives let affection fade.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.5 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.D. Ross) [11]
For Aristotle, even good friendships need active upkeep or they die away.
The Kural says the same about love: if the sulking partner is not drawn back, the bond withers at its root.

Real Life Example

Charles Dickens separates from his wife Catherine
May 1858 · London, United Kingdom
Charles Dickens married Catherine Hogarth in 1836, and they had nine children who survived infancy. After years of growing unhappiness he separated from her in May 1858, and from then on she lived apart from him. A painful public scandal followed, during which, according to Britannica, Dickens did not act with tact, patience or consideration; he even printed a public statement about the separation in his magazine Household Words on 12 June 1858, while Catherine kept a dignified silence.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Instead of seeking to soothe a wife who was already hurt, Dickens cut her off completely and publicly.
The kural says that failing to soothe and win back the one who is hurt is like cutting at the root a creeper that is already wilting, and Dickens’s handling of the separation cut the marriage off at its root.
