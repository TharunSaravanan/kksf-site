Kural 147

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

அறனியலான் இல்வாழ்வான் என்பான் பிறனியலாள் பெண்மை நயவா தவன்.
Araniyalaan ilvaazhvaan enbaan piraniyalaal, Penmai nayavaa thavan.

Meaning

A man who does not desire a woman who belongs to another is the one called a true family man, living the right way.

Elementary

What makes someone a good member of a family? This kural gives a simple test: a good family person is happy with their own family and never wishes for someone else's husband or wife. Being loyal is part of being good at home.

University

Here Valluvar turns from warning to definition: the true இல்வாழ்வான் (householder), the central figure of the whole இல்லறவியல் section, is identified by a negative mark, not desiring பிறனியலாள் ('she who moves within another's sphere'). The word இயல் appears twice, linking அறன்இயலான் ('one whose conduct is shaped by virtue') to பிறன்இயலாள் ('one whose life is joined to another'), so the couplet balances two kinds of belonging. By defining domestic virtue through fidelity, the Kural treats chastity as a duty of husbands as much as of wives, which some modern commentators highlight as notably even-handed for its era. The idea that the good household is built on the spouse's exclusive devotion appears in many traditions' ideals of marriage.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, Rama is honoured as ekapatnivrata, a man vowed to one wife. Even when he performs a great royal sacrifice after Sita is gone, he does not remarry but uses a golden image of Sita in her place.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda (narrative summary) [3]
Rama is held up as the ideal householder precisely because his devotion belongs to his wife alone.
The Kural defines the virtuous householder by his fidelity; Rama is the Hindu tradition's model of exactly that.
Christianity
“For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. Love worketh no ill to his neighbour.”
— Romans 13:9-10 (KJV) [4]
Paul says all the commandments, starting with the ban on adultery, are summed up in loving one's neighbour.
The Kural's good householder shows his virtue by not wanting his neighbour's wife; Paul says this is simply what love for one's neighbour looks like.
Islam
“And they who guard their private parts ... But whoever seeks beyond that, then those are the transgressors.”
— Qur'an 23:5, 23:7 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the successful believers as those who guard their chastity except within marriage, and calls anyone who goes beyond this a transgressor.
Both texts define the good person by keeping desire within one's own marriage.
Judaism
“Drink waters out of thine own cistern, and running waters out of thine own well. ... Let them be only thine own, and not strangers' with thee. Let thy fountain be blessed: and rejoice with the wife of thy youth.”
— Proverbs 5:15, 5:17-18 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs uses the image of drinking from one's own well to urge a man to find joy only with his own wife.
The Kural's ideal householder desires only his own; Proverbs pictures that same faithful household as a blessed fountain.
Buddhism
“In five ways, young householder, should a wife as the West be ministered to by a husband: (i) by being courteous to her, (ii) by not despising her, (iii) by being faithful to her, (iv) by handing over authority to her, (v) by providing her with adornments.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [6]
In his code for lay householders, the Buddha lists faithfulness among a husband's duties to his wife.
Both texts make fidelity a defining duty of the good householder.
Jainism
Jain ethics makes 'contentment with one's own wife' (svadārasantoṣa) the fourth of the five minor vows (aṇuvrata) taken by every lay householder. The Tattvārthasūtra lists 'intercourse with an unchaste married woman' among the transgressions that break it.
— Tattvārthasūtra 7.28, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Jainism asks only monks for total celibacy. For the householder, chastity means being faithful to his own spouse, and this vow is one of the marks of a proper lay follower (śrāvaka).
Valluvar defines the true householder as the man who does not desire another's wife, and Jainism builds that same restraint into the vows that define its lay householder.
Sikhism
“The Sikh ought to treat beautiful women of others as his mothers, sisters, and daughters.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 29, Pauri 11 (English translation as published on BaniDB and searchgurbani.com) [8]
In a pauri on what a Sikh ought to do, Bhai Gurdas makes regarding other men's women as mother, sister or daughter a mark of the true disciple.
The Kural defines the true householder by his not desiring another's wife; Bhai Gurdas defines the true Sikh, himself a householder, by the same restraint.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus finally returns home in the Odyssey, Penelope tests him by asking for their bed to be moved. Odysseus reveals that he built the bed himself around a living olive tree, so it cannot be moved, and she knows he is truly her husband.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 23 (narrative summary) [9]
The immovable marriage bed stands for the unbreakable bond between a faithful husband and wife.
The Kural's true householder keeps his bond whole; Homer's rooted bed is the Greek image of that same faithful home.
Philosophy
“So too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle says character is built by repeated action: we become temperate by consistently acting with self-control.
The Kural defines the virtuous householder by what he consistently does not desire; Aristotle explains that such character is formed by habit.

Real Life Example

The Hongzhi Emperor keeps Empress Zhang as his only consort
1487-1505 · Beijing, Ming China
The Hongzhi Emperor of the Ming dynasty, who reigned from 1487 to 1505, kept Empress Zhang as his only wife from his time as a prince until his death, although Chinese emperors customarily kept many consorts. His ministers repeatedly urged him to take more consorts to secure the succession, and he refused. Historians describe him as the only monogamous emperor in Chinese history.
— Sources: [11] [12]
In a court where an emperor's household was expected to include many women, Hongzhi defined his own household by fidelity to one wife.
The kural identifies the true householder as the man who does not desire a woman who belongs to another; Hongzhi went further and refused even the consorts his position allowed.
