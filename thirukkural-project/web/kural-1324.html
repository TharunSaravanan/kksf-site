Kural 1324

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

புல்லி விடாஅப் புலவியுள் தோன்றுமென் உள்ளம் உடைக்கும் படை.
Pulli vidaaap pulaviyul thondrumen Ullam udaikkum padai.

Meaning

In a sulk that ends with a hug that will not let go, a weapon appears that breaks down my firm mind.

Elementary

Imagine deciding to stay cross with your best friend all afternoon, and then they do something so sweet that your crossness just melts. Their kindness is like a magic key that unlocks your grumpy mood. The wife says her husband has that kind of key.

University

The couplet describes a pulavi (‘sulk’) that leads to pulli viṭā (‘embracing and not letting go’, the long vowel in viṭāa marking aḷapeṭai lengthening for metre), within which there arises a paṭai (‘weapon, army’) that uṭaikkum (‘shatters’) eṉ uḷḷam (‘my heart, my firmness’). Commentators identify the weapon with the husband’s tender pleading or caresses, which break the wife’s resolve to keep sulking; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar gloss uḷḷam as uṟuti (‘firmness, determination’). Pope’s ‘a weapon lurks, which quite breaks down my mind’ follows the same line. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the tenth song of the Gita Govinda, Krishna pleads with the sulking Radha, begging her to place her tender foot on his head as an ornament, and her pride begins to melt.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 10 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s poem makes Krishna’s humble pleading the force that overcomes Radha’s māna (proud sulk).
The lover’s pleading is the ‘weapon’ that, in the Kural too, breaks the sulking partner’s firmness.
Christianity
“Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me: thy hair is as a flock of goats that appear from Gilead.”
— Song of Solomon 6:5 (KJV) [4]
The lover in the Song of Songs confesses that the beloved’s gaze has overpowered him.
The beloved’s eyes are the same kind of weapon the Kural names, one that breaks down the will of the one who sees them.
Islam
The Prophet taught that a believing man should not hate a believing woman: if he dislikes one of her traits, he will be pleased with another.
— Sahih Muslim 1469 (paraphrase) [5]
Affection is meant to outweigh passing irritation between spouses.
In the Kural, too, irritation gives way because something lovable in the other overcomes it.
Judaism
“Scarce had I passed from them, When I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go, Until I had brought him into my mother’s house, And into the chamber of her that conceived me.”
— Song of Songs 3:4 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
After searching the city for her beloved, the woman of the Song of Songs finds him and holds on.
‘I held him, and would not let him go’ is almost the Kural’s pulli viṭā, the embrace that will not let go.
Buddhism
Mara’s three daughters, Craving, Discontent and Passion, try every kind of charm on the newly awakened Buddha, but he remains unmoved and they leave defeated.
— Samyutta Nikaya 4.25 (Mara’s Daughters; paraphrase) [7]
The story shows a mind so free that even the strongest charms cannot shake it.
The Kural’s wife freely admits the opposite: love’s weapon breaks her resolve, which is exactly the hold the Buddha escapes.
Jainism
“The five senses, anger, pride, delusion, and greed --difficult to conquer is one’s self; but when that is conquered, everything is conquered.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Nami teaches that self-conquest is the hardest and greatest victory.
The Kural’s wife is conquered by love’s weapon, the reverse of the Jain ideal of a self that cannot be overcome.
Sikhism
“The Bani of the Lord's Love is the pointed arrow, which has pierced my mind, O Lord King.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 449, Raag Aasaa (Chhant), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das describes the Lord's loving Word as an arrow that pierces the heart and overcomes it.
The Kural speaks of a weapon hidden in the lover's embrace that breaks down the wife's resolve to keep sulking; Gurbani likewise pictures love as a weapon that pierces and conquers the mind.
Greek Mythology
“As she spoke she loosed from her bosom the curiously embroidered girdle into which all her charms had been wrought- love, desire, and that sweet flattery which steals the judgement even of the most prudent.”
— Homer, Iliad 14 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Aphrodite lends Hera the girdle whose charms can overpower even Zeus.
Homer’s girdle that ‘steals the judgement’ is the Kural’s weapon that breaks the heart’s firmness.
Philosophy
Pascal wrote that the heart has its reasons which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées 277 (tr. W. F. Trotter; paraphrase) [11]
Pascal holds that the heart moves by its own logic, which reasoning cannot control.
The wife’s reasoned decision to keep sulking is undone by her heart, as Pascal would expect.

Real Life Example

Johnny Cash proposes to June Carter on stage
22 February 1968 · London, Ontario, Canada
During a concert at the London Gardens arena in London, Ontario, on 22 February 1968, Johnny Cash proposed marriage to June Carter on stage in front of about 7,000 people. Cash had proposed to her on stage many times before, and this time she said yes. The two were married on 1 March 1968.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The moment became one of the most famous proposals in music history and is now marked by a public mural in London, Ontario.
The kural speaks of a weapon hidden in the lover's pleading that breaks down the beloved's resolve, and Cash's repeated proposals finally overcame June Carter's long reluctance.
