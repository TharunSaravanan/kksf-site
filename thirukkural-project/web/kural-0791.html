Kural 791

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

நாடாது நட்டலிற் கேடில்லை நட்டபின் வீடில்லை நட்பாள் பவர்க்கு.
Naadaadhu nattalir kedillai nattapin veedillai natpaal bavarkku.

Meaning

Nothing is more harmful than making friends without first checking a person carefully. For loyal people, once a friendship is made, they cannot walk away from it.

Elementary

Imagine tying your shoelace to a friend’s shoelace for a three-legged race. Once the knot is tight, you have to run together wherever the other person goes. So before you tie the knot, make sure your partner is someone who runs the right way.

University

The chapter opens on a paradox: the very loyalty that makes a person a good friend is what makes a careless friendship dangerous. Natpaalbavar (நட்பாள்பவர்) are ‘those who maintain friendship’, people for whom a friend once made cannot be dropped; veedu (வீடு) here means ‘release’ or ‘leaving off’, not ‘house’. Parimelazhagar draws the inference the couplet implies: since the good never abandon a friend, there is no evil equal to friendship formed without naadal (நாடல், inquiry), and so the examination must come before, not after. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah read it the same way, while Kalaignar paraphrases it as ‘a bad friendship taken up without inquiry brings harm one cannot escape’. The text is the same in ytamizh, thirukkural.net and the sandhi-joined edition at thirukkural-thamizh.org.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Do not put your trust in a bad companion nor even trust an ordinary friend, for if he should get angry with you, he may bring all your secrets to light.”
— Chanakya Niti 2.6 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya warns that trust given too freely, even to an ordinary friend, can be turned against you once the relationship sours.
Both texts counsel caution before intimacy, because a bond once given cannot simply be taken back.
Christianity
“If thou wouldst get a friend, try him before thou takest him, and do not credit him easily. For there is a friend for his own occasion, and he will not abide in the day of thy trouble.”
— Ecclesiasticus (Sirach) 6:7-8, Douay-Rheims [5]
Ben Sira tells his student to test a would-be friend before accepting him, because some friends stay only while it suits them. Sirach is deuterocanonical: it is Scripture for Catholic and Orthodox Christians but not part of the Protestant or Jewish canon.
Ben Sira and Valluvar give the same order of operations: test first, then befriend.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not take as intimates those other than yourselves, for they will not spare you [any] ruin.”
— Qur’an 3:118 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse warns believers against taking as close confidants people whose hostility has already shown itself.
Like the Kural, it treats a badly chosen intimate as a source of ruin and urges discernment before closeness.
Judaism
Jonathan and David bind themselves in a covenant of friendship, and the bond outlives Jonathan: years later David, now king, seeks out Jonathan’s lame son Mephibosheth and keeps him at his table for Jonathan’s sake.
— 1 Samuel 18:1-3; 20:42; 2 Samuel 9 (Tanakh) [7]
In 1 Samuel 18 and 20 the two men swear a lasting covenant, and in 2 Samuel 9 David honours it even after Jonathan’s death.
The story shows Valluvar’s premise from the positive side: a true friendship, once formed, is never released, which is exactly why it must be formed with care.
Buddhism
“these four as enemies the wise behold, avoid them from afar as paths of peril.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
After listing four kinds of foes who look like friends, the Buddha tells Sigala to recognise them and keep them at a distance.
The Buddha, like Valluvar, puts the discernment before the relationship: spot the false friend and avoid him from afar.
Jainism
“To join oneself to many, and strive many days and examine dispositions, and take (for friends) those who are worthy, is proper. Even with a deadly serpent, to associate and afterwards to part from it will be painful.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 10 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar tells the reader to examine people's dispositions over many days before taking them as friends, because once a bond is formed, even with something as deadly as a serpent, breaking it is painful.
The Kural warns that nothing is worse than friendship formed without inquiry, since a loyal person cannot get free of it afterwards, and the Naladiyar makes the same point that examination must come first because parting later hurts.
Sikhism
“Kabir! Associate with the Holy people, who will take you to Nirvana in the end. Do not associate with the faithless cynics; they would bring you to ruin.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir 93 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir's salok treats companionship as something that carries a person all the way to the end, for good or ill: the company of the holy stays with you to the last, while the company of the saakat (the faithless cynic) brings ruin.
The Kural warns that a friendship, once made, cannot be shaken off, so choosing badly is the worst harm; Kabir likewise says the company you take up decides where you end, so it must be chosen before it binds you.
Greek Mythology
“Let the wage promised to a friend be fixed; even with your brother smile—and get a witness; for trust and mistrust, alike ruin men.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 370-372 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod advises his brother Perses to keep clear terms even with friends and kin, because misplaced trust can ruin a man as surely as suspicion.
Hesiod shares the Kural’s practical caution about giving trust before it has been earned.
Philosophy
“Wherefore, I must again and again repeat, you must satisfy your judgment before engaging your affections: not love first and judge afterwards.”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia §22 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [12]
Laelius complains that people choose friends carelessly and then have to break off friendships later, when it is painful to do so.
Cicero states Valluvar’s rule almost word for word: judge first, then love.

Real Life Example

The eunuch Wang Zhen leads the Zhengtong Emperor into disaster at Tumu
1449 · Tumu, near present-day Huailai, Hebei, China
The young Zhengtong Emperor of the Ming dynasty placed his complete trust in the eunuch Wang Zhen, his first teacher, who came to dominate the government. In 1449, when the Oirat Mongol leader Esen invaded, Wang Zhen persuaded the emperor to lead an army against him in person, overriding the objections of senior ministers and officers. At the Tumu post station the ill-prepared Ming army was surrounded and largely destroyed, and the emperor was captured and held by the Mongols for about a year.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The disaster weakened the Ming dynasty and showed how costly a ruler's unexamined trust in a favourite could be.
The kural warns that nothing is more harmful than friendship formed without careful examination, because a loyal person cannot afterwards break free of it, and the emperor's unquestioning bond with Wang Zhen led him straight into captivity.
