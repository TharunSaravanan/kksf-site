Kural 152

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

பொறுத்தல் இறப்பினை என்றும் அதனை மறத்தல் அதனினும் நன்று.
Poruththal irappinai endrum adhanai, Maraththal adhaninum nandru.

Meaning

Always put up with the wrongs people do to you, even when they go too far. Forgetting those wrongs completely is even better.

Elementary

If a friend breaks your favorite toy, being patient about it is good. This kural says something is even better: letting it go so completely that you stop thinking about it. Holding onto a hurt is like carrying a heavy rock in your pocket, and forgetting it is like finally throwing the rock away.

University

The couplet sets up two steps: பொறுத்தல் (bearing a wrong) and, higher still, மறத்தல் (forgetting it). The key word இறப்பு here means 'transgression, going beyond bounds.' Traditional commentators such as Parimelazhagan and Mu. Varadarasan read it as an excessive wrong, not as 'death,' although some popular modern glosses take it as 'until death.' Valluvar's point is psychological: if you endure a wrong but still keep a record of it, the endurance is incomplete, because the remembered wrong keeps feeding resentment. Real forbearance lets the grievance go altogether. Rabbinic law draws a similar line between taking vengeance and bearing a grudge (Leviticus 19:18, discussed in Talmud Yoma 23a), and Nietzsche later observed that some strong natures 'forgive' simply by forgetting.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who beareth not malice towards others but is kind to all, who being weak disputeth not with others, who speaketh not arrogantly, and forgeteth a quarrel, is praised everywhere.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXIII, Vidura's counsel (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura advises King Dhritarashtra that the person everyone praises holds no malice and actually forgets his quarrels instead of keeping them alive.
Like the kural, it puts forgetting a dispute above merely putting up with it.
Christianity
“Charity suffereth long, and is kind; … is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil;”
— 1 Corinthians 13:4-5 (KJV) [5]
Paul says love is long-suffering and 'thinketh no evil.' The Greek phrase (ou logizetai to kakon) means love doesn't keep a record of wrongs done to it.
'Suffereth long' corresponds to the kural's bearing, and 'thinketh no evil' (not keeping a ledger of injuries) to its forgetting.
Islam
“and let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allāh should forgive you? And Allāh is Forgiving and Merciful.”
— Qur'an 24:22 (Sahih International) [6]
According to the classical exegetes, this verse was revealed after Abu Bakr swore to stop supporting a relative who had joined in slandering his daughter Aisha. The Qur'an tells him to pardon (ʿafw) and also to overlook (ṣafḥ), that is, to turn the page completely.
Pardoning and then overlooking follows the same two steps as the kural's bearing and then forgetting.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD.”
— Leviticus 19:18 (JPS 1917) [7]
The Torah forbids two separate things: taking revenge, and bearing a grudge. The Talmud (Yoma 23a) explains that even helping someone while reminding them of their past wrong counts as bearing a grudge.
Not taking vengeance matches the kural's bearing; not bearing a grudge, not even keeping the memory alive, matches its forgetting.
Buddhism
“‘He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.’ Those who do not harbor such thoughts still their hatred.”
— Dhammapada, verse 4 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's opening verses teach that hatred ends only when we stop going over the wrongs done to us.
This is the kural's 'forgetting': letting go of the memory of the injury so it no longer controls the mind.
Jainism
“If a monk hears bad words, cruel and rankling ones, he should silently overlook them, and not take them to heart.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Among the twenty-two hardships (parīṣaha) a Jain monk must bear, abuse is met not only with silence but by refusing to let the words lodge in the mind.
Valluvar tells us to bear even wrongs that go beyond all limits and, better still, to forget them, and the Uttarādhyayana likewise moves from bearing ('silently overlook') to letting go ('not take them to heart').
Sikhism
“So erase your duality and hold tight to forgiveness,”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 343, Raag Gauri, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir tells the seeker to give up the divided, restless mind and hold firmly to forgiveness.
The Kural asks us to bear even extreme wrongs and better still to forget them; Kabir likewise asks for a forgiveness held fast, not grudgingly.
Greek Mythology
“Ajax, will you not forget and forgive even in death, but must the judgement about that hateful armour still rankle with you?”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book XI (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
In the Underworld, Odysseus meets the ghost of Ajax, who is still furious about losing the armour of Achilles to him. Odysseus begs him to forget. Ajax turns away without a word.
This is the mirror image of the kural: a grievance held even past death shows what refusing to forget costs.
Philosophy
“Mirabeau, who had no memory for any insults and meannesses which were practised on him, and who was only incapable of forgiving because he forgot.”
— Friedrich Nietzsche, The Genealogy of Morals, First Essay, §10 (trans. Horace B. Samuel) [12]
Nietzsche praises strong natures who can't take their enemies' offences seriously for long. His example is Mirabeau, who didn't need to forgive because he simply forgot.
Nietzsche arrives at the kural's conclusion, that forgetting a wrong goes beyond forgiving it, though for a different reason.

Real Life Example

Eric Lomax meets and forgives his wartime interrogator Nagase Takashi
1993 · Kanchanaburi, Thailand
Eric Lomax, a British signals officer captured at the fall of Singapore, was tortured by his captors while working as a prisoner on the Siam-Burma railway after prisoners were found to have built a radio. For decades he was haunted by the memory of the interpreter at his interrogations, Nagase Takashi, who after the war devoted himself to acts of atonement and paid for a temple of peace near the River Kwai bridge. In 1993 the two men met at Kanchanaburi, and Lomax later gave Nagase a letter saying that, although he could never forget, he offered him total forgiveness; he told the story in his memoir The Railway Man.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Lomax had lived for nearly fifty years with bitterness, and the meeting is remembered as a rare personal reconciliation between a victim and one of those who had wronged him.
The kural urges bearing even wrongs that go beyond all limits and, better still, letting them go; Lomax moved from enduring a grave wrong to releasing it through forgiveness.
