Kural 123

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

செறிவறிந்து சீர்மை பயக்கும் அறிவறிந்து ஆற்றின் அடங்கப் பெறின்.
Serivarindhu seermai payakkum arivarindhu Aatrin adangap perin.

Meaning

If a person learns what is worth knowing and controls themselves in the right way, good people will notice, and it will bring them respect.

Elementary

Think of a kid who quietly does the right thing in class without showing off. At first nobody seems to notice, but the teacher does, and soon everyone respects that kid. Quiet goodness gets noticed in the end.

University

The couplet turns on two uses of arindhu (‘having known’). In arivarindhu, the person first understands what true knowledge is; Parimelazhagar reads this as grasping that restraint itself is our wisdom. In serivarindhu, that restraint comes to be known and recognized, by the wise, in Parimelazhagar’s reading. The result is seermai, excellence or eminence. The qualifier aatrin (‘in the proper way’) rules out showy or grudging restraint. Manakkudavar adds a sensory reading: one must know the five sense-objects (taste, sight, touch, sound, smell) and restrain the five organs that seek them. Joining knowledge to restraint echoes the Delphic ‘Know thyself,’ which Greek thought tied to sophrosyne, and Aristotle’s view that the temperate person desires ‘as reason directs.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who restrains his senses, keeping them under full control, and fixes his consciousness upon Me, is known as a man of steady intelligence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.61 [4]
Krishna says that sense-restraint is the mark by which a person of steady wisdom is known.
Both link restraint to wisdom and say it is what makes a person recognized as wise.
Christianity
“Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in due time:”
— 1 Peter 5:6 (KJV) [5]
Peter promises that those who humble themselves will be lifted up at the proper time.
Same arc as the Kural: restraint and humility practised now bring honour and distinction later.
Islam
“And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily, and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace,”
— Qur’an 25:63 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an identifies the true servants of God by their gentle bearing and restrained response to provocation.
Restrained conduct is what distinguishes these people and earns them their honoured title, much as the Kural says restraint brings distinction.
Judaism
“Now the man Moses was very meek, above all the men that were upon the face of the earth.”
— Numbers 12:3 (JPS 1917) [7]
When Miriam and Aaron criticize Moses, the Torah records his exceptional humility, and God then publicly vindicates him.
Moses’ restraint is noticed and honoured from on high, the same recognition the Kural promises to the restrained.
Buddhism
“Even the gods hold dear the wise one, whose senses are subdued like horses well trained by a charioteer, whose pride is destroyed and who is free from the cankers.”
— Dhammapada 94 [8]
The Buddha says the wise one with tamed senses and no pride is cherished even by the gods.
Both texts join wisdom with sense-restraint and humility and say such a person wins high regard.
Jainism
“An intelligent man who has learned (the sacred texts) takes his duties upon himself, and he becomes renowned in the world; as the earth is the dwelling of all beings, so he will be a dwelling of all duties.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.45 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
In the lecture on discipline, the pupil who joins learning to disciplined, willing conduct gains renown and becomes a support for every duty.
The Kural promises distinction, recognized by the good, to one who knows what true knowledge is and is restrained in the right way; this verse likewise pairs learning with disciplined conduct and promises renown.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, he is a brave warrior, who conquers and subdues his vicious inner ego.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 86, Siree Raag, Siree Raag ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das redefines heroism as the conquest of one's own haumai.
The Kural says restraint, understood as wisdom, wins recognition from the good; Gurbani gives the person who masters himself the honoured title of true hero.
Greek Mythology
The maxims ‘Know thyself’ and ‘Nothing in excess’ were inscribed at Apollo’s temple at Delphi. In Plato’s Charmides, Critias argues that this self-knowledge is the very heart of sophrosyne (temperance).
— Delphic maxims, discussed in Plato, Charmides 164d–165a (paraphrase) [11]
Greek religious tradition joined knowing oneself to keeping within limits.
The Kural’s ‘knowing that restraint is wisdom’ matches the Delphic joining of self-knowledge and self-limitation.
Philosophy
“the temperate man craves for the things he ought, as he ought, and when he ought; and this is what rational principle directs”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.12, 1119b (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
For Aristotle, temperance is desire rightly ordered by reason, neither suppressed blindly nor indulged.
Aristotle’s ‘as he ought’ matches Valluvar’s aatrin, restraint practised in the proper way and guided by understanding.

Real Life Example

Albert Luthuli receives the Nobel Peace Prize
1960 (awarded); December 1961 (received) · Groutville, South Africa, and Oslo, Norway
Albert Luthuli, a teacher and chief who was elected president-general of the African National Congress in December 1952, became the spokesman of a campaign of nonviolent civil disobedience against South Africa's racial segregation laws, inspired by Gandhi's philosophy. He was dismissed as chief, repeatedly banned and confined to his rural district, and arrested in the treason arrests of 1956, yet he continued to appeal for nonviolence and a shared society. The Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded him the 1960 Nobel Peace Prize for his non-violent struggle against apartheid, making him Africa's first Peace Prize laureate; he received it the following year.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Luthuli's disciplined refusal to meet persecution with violence was recognised by the wider world even while his own government silenced him.
The kural says that restraint understood as wisdom and practised rightly will be recognised by the good and bring distinction; Luthuli's restraint earned the world's highest honour for peace.
