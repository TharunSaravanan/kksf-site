Kural 668

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

கலங்காது கண்ட வினைக்கண் துளங்காது தூக்கங் கடிந்து செயல்.
Kalangaadhu kaNda vinaikkaN thuLangaadhu ThookkanG kadindhu seyal.

Meaning

Once you have clearly decided what to do, do it without wavering and without delay.

Elementary

When you and your friends decide to build a treehouse, first think carefully. But once you’ve decided, don’t keep changing your mind or saying “maybe tomorrow.” Grab the hammer and start building!

University

The couplet moves in three steps: kalaṅkātu kaṇṭa vinai, an undertaking seen or decided without confusion (kalaṅku, to be muddied or agitated); tuḷaṅkātu, without wavering (tuḷaṅku, to tremble or sway); and tūkkam kaṭintu, having cast off tūkkam. Parimelazhagar takes tūkkam here as procrastination or delay (nīṭṭittal), not literal sleep, and the modern commentators follow him (Pappaiah: ‘without faltering and without delay’); Pope’s ‘mind unslumbering’ keeps the literal sense. The logic is that deliberation belongs before the decision, and firmness after it, which echoes the rule about deliberating and then acting in terintu ceyal vakai (ch. 47). The sandhi text reads viṉaikkaṭ ṭuḷaṅkātu, the same words written with the sandhi applied.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Arjuna said: My dear Kṛṣṇa, O infallible one, my illusion is now gone. I have regained my memory by Your mercy. I am now firm and free from doubt and am prepared to act according to Your instructions.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.73 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [4]
After long deliberation, Arjuna’s confusion clears and he declares himself firm and ready to act.
Clear decision (kalaṅkātu kaṇṭa) followed by unwavering action is exactly the Kural’s sequence.
Christianity
“Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord.”
— 1 Corinthians 15:58 (KJV) [5]
Paul urges believers to hold firm and keep working, confident their work is not wasted.
‘Stedfast, unmoveable’ answers to the Kural’s tuḷaṅkātu, never swaying once the work is begun.
Islam
“...and consult them in the matter. And when you have decided, then rely upon Allah. Indeed, Allah loves those who rely [upon Him].”
— Quran 3:159 (Sahih International) [6]
The Prophet is told to take counsel, and then, once a decision is made, to act on it with trust instead of hesitating.
Consultation, then firm and prompt action, is the Kural’s own sequence.
Judaism
“He [also] used to say: If I am not for myself, who is for me? But if I am for my own self [only], what am I? And if not now, when?”
— Pirkei Avot 1:14 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [7]
Hillel’s last question rejects putting off what ought to be done.
‘If not now, when?’ is the Kural’s tūkkam kaṭintu, casting off delay.
Buddhism
“Hasten to do good; restrain your mind from evil. He who is slow in doing good, his mind delights in evil.”
— Dhammapada 116 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada warns that delay in doing good leaves room for the mind to drift to harm.
Both texts treat delay as the enemy of a good act already decided on.
Jainism
“Now you have entered on the path from which the thorns have been cleared, the great path; walk in the right path; Gautama, &c. Do not get into an uneven road like a weak burden-bearer; for you will repent of it afterwards”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 10.32-33 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira tells Gautama that, the path now being clear, he should walk it steadily and not stray onto an uneven road, ending each verse with the refrain to be careful all the while.
The kural's counsel to carry out without wavering a course settled with a clear mind matches this call to keep to a path already seen clearly.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, that which you have to do tomorrow - do it today instead; and that which you have to do now - do it immediately!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, urges acting at once rather than putting off what must be done.
The Kural says that once a course is settled with a clear mind it should be carried out without wavering or delay; Kabir gives the same call against procrastination.
Greek Mythology
At Gordium, Alexander is shown the famous knot said to promise rule of Asia to whoever undoes it; according to the popular version, he does not hesitate but cuts it through with his sword.
— Plutarch, Life of Alexander 18 (trans. Bernadotte Perrin) [11]
The ‘Gordian knot’ has become the byword for decisive action that refuses to be stalled.
Alexander acts without wavering or delay once his course is set, as the Kural urges.
Philosophy
“In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present- I am rising to the work of a human being.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius argues himself out of lingering in bed by recalling that he exists for action.
This is tūkkam kaṭintu in its literal and figurative sense: shake off sloth and do the work.

Real Life Example

Julius Caesar crosses the Rubicon
10-11 January 49 BCE · Rubicon River, northern Italy
In January 49 BCE the Roman Senate, led by Caesar's opponents, demanded that Julius Caesar give up his army and return to Rome as a private citizen. Caesar instead led his troops, reportedly a single legion, across the small river Rubicon, the boundary between his province of Cisalpine Gaul and Italy proper, an act that began a civil war. He then advanced rapidly through Italy, and the phrase crossing the Rubicon has since meant passing a point of no return.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Once Caesar had made his choice he acted at once and without hesitation, and the speed of his advance gave his opponents no time to organise.
The kural says that once a course has been settled with a clear mind it should be carried out without wavering or delay, and Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon has become the world's shorthand for exactly such an irreversible, unhesitating commitment.
