Kural 576

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

மண்ணோ டியைந்த மரத்தனையர் கண்ணோ டியைந்துகண் ணோடா தவர்.
Mannodu iyaindha maraththanaiyar kannodu iyaindhukan nodaa thavar.

Meaning

People who have eyes but never look kindly at others are no better than trees standing in the ground.

Elementary

A tree stands in one place and can’t notice when someone near it is crying. Valluvar says a person who has eyes but never looks kindly at others is just like that tree: standing there, but not really caring about anyone.

University

The couplet is built on a careful parallel: மண்ணோடு இயைந்த மரம் (‘a tree joined to the soil’) and கண்ணோடு இயைந்து கண்ணோடாதவர் (‘those who, though joined to eyes, do not let the eye run out [in kindness]’). The verb கண்ணோடு is the root of the chapter’s title, so the line plays on having கண் (eyes) but not doing கண்ணோட்டம். Parimelazhagar is usually cited as noting that trees too were said to have ‘eyes’ (buds or nodes), so that the unkind are no better than eyed trees; this pun is reported in commentary tradition and should be treated with some caution. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses ‘like a tree that has eyes but does not see’, while Solomon Pappaiah stresses that such people move about but are as insensible as a tree fixed in the earth. The two texts agree word for word, with the line break falling inside கண்ணோ / டியைந்து in both.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Pride, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness and ignorance – these qualities belong to those of demoniac nature, O son of Pṛthā.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.4 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
The Gita lists pāruṣya (harshness, hardness) among the traits of the āsurī (demonic) nature, the opposite of the gentle divine nature.
The hard, unfeeling person the Kural likens to a tree is the Gita’s harsh āsuric type.
Christianity
“Having eyes, see ye not? and having ears, hear ye not? and do ye not remember?”
— Mark 8:18 (KJV) [4]
Jesus rebukes his disciples for having working eyes and ears yet failing to perceive what is before them.
‘Having eyes yet not seeing’ is the same paradox the Kural sets out: joined to eyes, yet not using them as eyes should be used.
Islam
“For indeed, it is not eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts which are within the breasts.”
— Quran 22:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran says true blindness lies not in the eyes but in hearts that will not respond.
The Kural’s tree-like people have seeing eyes but unfeeling hearts, exactly the blindness this verse describes.
Judaism
“They have mouths, but they speak not: eyes have they, but they see not… They that make them are like unto them; so is every one that trusteth in them.”
— Psalm 115:5-8 (KJV) [4]
The Psalm mocks idols of wood and metal that have eyes without sight, and says their makers become like them.
The Kural’s people with eyes who are no better than trees resemble the Psalm’s lifeless images with unseeing eyes.
Buddhism
“The man of little learning grows old like a bull. He grows only in bulk, but, his wisdom does not grow.”
— Dhammapada 152 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada compares a person who never develops inwardly to an ox that merely grows bigger.
Like the Kural’s tree-like person, this one has bodily existence without the inner quality that makes a human being.
Jainism
Jain teaching classes plants among the ekendriya beings, souls that possess only one sense, touch; human birth with all five senses is counted a rare chance that should not be wasted.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.5-9 and 10.17, with Jacobi's note on the ekendriyas (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
In Jain cosmology a tree is a living soul limited to a single sense, while a human being has the full faculties needed for right conduct.
The Kural compares people who have eyes but show no kindly regard to trees standing in the soil; on Jain terms, such a person lives with five senses but, morally, no better than a one-sensed being.
Sikhism
“What can cold do to a stone?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 143, Raag Maajh (Maajh ki Vaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak uses the stone that feels no cold as an image of the person on whom nothing outside can make an impression.
The Kural says those who have eyes yet never look kindly on others are like trees rooted in the soil, with form but no feeling; Guru Nanak's unfeeling stone makes the same point about outward form with no inner response.
Greek Mythology
The Gorgon Medusa’s gaze turns everyone who meets it to stone, until Perseus beheads her using a mirrored shield.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.604-803 [9]
Medusa’s look is the opposite of a kindly regard: it does not warm or soften others but deadens them.
A gaze with no warmth is lifeless and makes others lifeless, like the Kural’s eyed yet wooden people.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius writes that a branch cut off from the branch next to it is cut off from the whole tree, and so a person who separates himself from even one fellow human has cut himself off from the whole community.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.8 [10]
For the Stoic emperor, withdrawing one’s goodwill from others amounts to self-amputation from the living body of humanity.
Both use tree imagery to say that a person without fellow-feeling is cut off from true human life.

Real Life Example

The Evian Conference on refugees from Nazi Germany
July 1938 · Evian-les-Bains, France
At President Franklin Roosevelt's initiative, delegates from 32 countries and a number of aid organisations met for nine days at Evian on Lake Geneva to discuss Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi Germany and annexed Austria. Nearly all the governments represented declined to open their doors to more refugees, and the Dominican Republic was the only participant that offered to accept significant additional numbers. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum concludes that the conference had very little impact on the refugees' plight.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The conference is remembered as a moment when the world's governments looked directly at people in danger and, apart from rare exceptions, did not act.
The kural says those who have eyes but never look kindly are like trees rooted in the soil, having the form of seeing without the feeling, which is what most delegations at Evian displayed.
