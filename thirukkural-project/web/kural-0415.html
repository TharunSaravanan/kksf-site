Kural 415

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

இழுக்கல் உடையுழி ஊற்றுக்கோல் அற்றே ஒழுக்கம் உடையார்வாய்ச் சொல்.
Izhukkal udaiyuzhi ootrukkol atre Ozhukkam udaiyaarvaaich chol.

Meaning

The words of people who live the right way are like a walking stick for someone walking on slippery ground.

Elementary

When you walk on a muddy, slippery path, a strong stick helps you not fall. Advice from good, honest people works the same way — it keeps you steady when life gets slippery.

University

The image is precise: இழுக்கல் is slipperiness (and by extension moral lapse), ஊற்றுக்கோல் a staff one leans on. The kural qualifies the source of hearing — not any speaker, but ஒழுக்கம் உடையார், those of upright conduct. Parimelazhagar emphasises that the words of the virtuous are trustworthy because their conduct confirms them, and that such words prevent a fall before it happens. The kural links the chapter to the earlier chapter on ஒழுக்கமுடைமை (conduct) and grounds the authority of what one hears in the moral character of the speaker.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Arise, awake; approach the great and learn. The path is sharp as a razor’s edge, difficult to cross, hard to tread — so say the wise.
— Katha Upanishad 1.3.14 [4]
The Katha Upanishad describes the spiritual path as treacherous and tells the seeker to approach and hear the great teachers.
A hazardous path and the counsel of the wise as one’s support — the same structure as the Kural’s slippery ground and staff.
Christianity
“Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock:”
— Matthew 7:24–25 (KJV) [5]
The one who hears and acts on Jesus’ words stands firm when rain, floods and winds come.
Heard words from a righteous teacher as the stable footing against collapse — the Kural’s staff on slippery ground.
Islam
“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided.”
— Qur’an 3:103 (Sahih International) [6]
Commentators interpret the ‘rope of Allah’ as the Qur’an and the guidance transmitted through it — something to grip to avoid falling.
A physical support image (rope / staff) for guidance received from a trustworthy source.
Judaism
“The law of his God is in his heart; none of his steps shall slide.”
— Psalm 37:31 (KJV) [5]
The Psalmist says the one who has taken God’s teaching into his heart will not slip.
Teaching received and held keeps one’s steps from sliding — near-identical imagery to the Kural’s slippery ground.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure. It is always better, and never worse, to cultivate such an association.”
— Dhammapada 76 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha urges following the corrective words of the wise, who save one from error.
Words of the wise as a guide that prevents a fall — the Kural’s supporting staff.
Jainism
“A needle with a thread (in it) does not get lost even when it falls in a heap of rubbish, so a person endowed with scriptureal knowledge does not lose his self, even if involved in transmigratory cycle.”
— Saman Suttam v. 248 (Samyagjnanasutra) (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [8]
The Saman Suttam's verses on right knowledge compare scriptural knowledge to the thread that keeps a needle from being lost. Through it the soul keeps hold of itself even in the confusion of samsara.
Valluvar compares the words of the virtuous to a staff for someone walking on slippery ground. The Jain image of the threaded needle likewise presents received teaching as what keeps a person from being lost amid danger.
Sikhism
“I am blind; Your Name, O Creator Lord, is my only anchor and support.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 727, Raag Tilang, Bhagat Namdev (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Tilang Bhagat Namdev calls God's Name the support of a blind man; the Gurmukhi word tek is glossed by Sahib Singh as a walking stick.
The Kural calls the words of the upright a staff for one walking on slippery ground; Namdev calls the Name the walking stick of the blind.
Greek Mythology
As Odysseus walks toward Circe’s house, Hermes meets him, warns him of her drugs and gives him the herb moly with instructions; heeding this counsel, Odysseus avoids being turned into a swine like his men.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.275–306 [10]
A trustworthy divine adviser’s words save the hero at the moment of greatest danger.
Counsel heard at the right moment keeps one from ‘slipping’ into ruin.
Philosophy
Seneca advises Lucilius to choose some man of high character and keep him always before his eyes, living as if he were watching — ‘we need someone against whom our characters can measure themselves’.
— Seneca, Epistulae Morales 11.8–10 [11]
Seneca argues that most wrongdoing is prevented when a witness of good character stands near the one about to err.
The upright person serves as the support that keeps one from slipping — Valluvar’s staff.

Real Life Example

Augustine listens to the sermons of Ambrose in Milan
c. 384-387 · Milan, Western Roman Empire (now Italy)
In the mid-380s Augustine, a gifted teacher of rhetoric from North Africa who had followed the Manichaean sect for years, took up a publicly paid professorship in Milan, then the imperial capital of the West. There he became a regular listener at the preaching of the city's bishop, Ambrose, whose allegorical method of interpreting Scripture helped remove his objections to Christianity. In the summer of 386 he gave up his professorship, and Ambrose baptised him at Easter 387.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sermons of a respected bishop helped carry one of the most influential thinkers in Western history through a long period of doubt and indecision.
The kural compares the words of upright people to a staff for one walking on slippery ground, and Ambrose's preaching gave the wavering Augustine something firm to lean on.
