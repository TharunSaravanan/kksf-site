Kural 1261

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

வாளற்றுப் புற்கென்ற கண்ணும் அவர்சென்ற நாளொற்றித் தேய்ந்த விரல்.
Vaalatrup purkendra kannum avarsendra naalotrith thaeyndha viral.

Meaning

My eyes have lost their shine from watching the road for him. My fingers are worn out from touching the marks I made to count the days since he left.

Elementary

Imagine someone you love goes on a long trip. Every day you stand at the window looking down the road, and every night you make a mark on the wall and count them all again. After many days your eyes are tired from looking and your finger is sore from counting. That is how much the lady in this verse misses her husband.

University

Chapter 127, avarvayiṉ vitumpal (‘eager longing for him’; vitumpal = impatient yearning), continues the kaṟpu (wedded love) section: the time the husband (talaivaṉ) fixed for his return is passing, and the wife (talaivi) speaks her impatience; in two couplets (1268, 1270) the husband’s own eagerness is voiced, which is why Pope titles the chapter ‘Mutual Desire’. Here the heroine offers two physical proofs: her eyes are vāḷ aṟṟu (stripped of their lustre, vāḷ = brightness) and puṟkeṉṟa (dimmed, unable to see fine things, as Solomon Pappaiah glosses), and her finger is worn from nāḷ oṟṟi, pressing the day-marks she has drawn on the wall; Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all supply the wall (சுவர்) on which the days are scored. The commentary tradition associated with Parimelazhagar reads the couplet as the heroine telling her friend (tōḻi) that she can no longer bear the delay. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s Cloud Messenger, the exiled yaksha pictures his wife at home counting the months still left of their separation by laying flowers on the threshold, one for each, and wasting with longing.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta (Uttaramegha), the yaksha’s description of his wife (paraphrase) [3]
Classical Sanskrit love poetry gives the separated wife a counting ritual very like the Kural’s day-marks on the wall.
Both wives keep a tally of the days apart, and both are worn down by the counting itself.
Christianity
“I am weary of my crying: my throat is dried: mine eyes fail while I wait for my God.”
— Psalm 69:3 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes waiting so long and so hard that his eyes give out.
The Kural’s heroine says the same of her eyes: they have lost their light from watching for the one she waits for.
Islam
“And he turned away from them and said, ‘Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,’ and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.”
— Qur’an 12:84 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s long grief for his absent son Joseph costs him his sight.
As in the Kural, years of longing for an absent loved one are written in the eyes, which lose their brightness.
Judaism
“Mine eyes fail for Thy word, saying: ‘When wilt Thou comfort me?’”
— Psalm 119:82 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The psalmist’s eyes wear out as he keeps asking when the promised comfort will come.
The heroine’s dimmed eyes carry the same unspoken question: when will he come back?
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names not seeing the one you love as one of the basic forms of suffering.
The heroine’s worn eyes and fingers are exactly the pain of not seeing the beloved, though the Buddha’s answer is to loosen the attachment rather than keep counting.
Jainism
“When the daughter of the king heard of the ordination of the Gina, laughter and gaiety forsook her, and she was overwhelmed with affliction.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati, betrothed to Arishtanemi, is left grieving when he turns away from their wedding to become a monk.
Rajimati’s lost gaiety is the Jain counterpart of the heroine’s lost brightness of eye: both are women worn down by a beloved’s absence.
Sikhism
“Looking for Him, my eyes have become blood-shot.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 341, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir describes the seeker who has searched for the Lord so long and so hard that the eyes themselves are worn and reddened.
The wife in the Kural says her eyes have grown dim from watching the road for her husband; Kabir's eyes are likewise worn out by looking for the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Penelope weeps for Odysseus as she listens to the disguised stranger, her cheeks melting with tears like snow thawing on the mountains, though her husband sits beside her unrecognised.
— Homer, Odyssey 19 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer’s faithful wife has spent nearly twenty years waiting, and her grief shows in her face.
Penelope’s tear-worn face and the heroine’s dimmed eyes are the same mark of long waiting for a husband.
Philosophy
Roland Barthes, describing the lover’s experience of waiting, suggests that the lover is defined as the one who waits, and that waiting itself is a kind of enchantment that fixes the lover in place.
— Roland Barthes, A Lover’s Discourse: Fragments, ‘Waiting’ (paraphrase) [11]
Barthes treats waiting not as empty time but as the very shape that love takes in absence.
The heroine’s whole existence has become waiting: eyes on the road, finger on the calendar.

Real Life Example

Hachiko waits at Shibuya Station for his master
1925 - c. 1935 · Shibuya, Tokyo, Japan
Hachiko was an Akita dog owned by Hidesaburo Ueno, a professor of agricultural engineering at the University of Tokyo, and he used to wait at Shibuya Station for Ueno to come home. Ueno died suddenly at work on 21 May 1925, but Hachiko kept going to the station to wait for him for about ten more years. A statue of Hachiko now stands at Shibuya Station.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Hachiko's daily vigil became a national symbol of faithful waiting for someone who has gone away.
The kural's heroine has worn out her eyes watching the road for her husband's return; Hachiko watched the station day after day for a master who never came back.
