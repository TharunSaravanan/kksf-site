Kural 617

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

மடியுளாள் மாமுகடி என்ப மடியிலான் தாளுளாள் தாமரையி னாள்.
Madiyulaal maamugadi enba madiyilaan Thaalulaal thaamaraiyinaal.

Meaning

People say the dark goddess of bad luck lives with the lazy. The goddess of good fortune, who sits on a lotus, lives with the hard work of someone who is not lazy.

Elementary

Pretend Good Luck and Bad Luck are two visitors looking for a home. Bad Luck loves a messy room where someone is napping all day. Good Luck knocks on the door of the kid who’s busy building and practising. Where you work, good luck moves in.

University

Valluvar borrows two goddesses of popular belief: மாமுகடி, the ‘great dark Mugadi’ (மூதேவி, Sanskrit Jyeṣṭhā or Alakṣmī), goddess of misfortune, and தாமரையினாள், ‘she of the lotus’, Tirumagal or Lakshmi; the verb என்ப (‘they say’) signals that he is citing received wisdom. Parimelazhagar notes that sloth is the ‘dwelling’ of the one and effort (தாள்) the dwelling of the other, so fortune is located not in fate but in conduct; Kalaignar demythologises the pair entirely, treating the goddess names as figures for the industrious and the idle. There is a textual point: ytamizh.com prints தாளுளான் (masculine), but the parallelism with மடியுளாள் and the sandhi form றாளுளா டாமரையி னாள் in thirukkural-thamizh.org (தாளுளாள் + தாமரை) confirm the feminine தாளுளாள், ‘she dwells in the effort’, which is the reading adopted here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A famous Hitopadesha verse says that Lakshmi, goddess of fortune, approaches the lion-like man of effort, and only cowards say that fortune is handed out by fate; so set fate aside and exert yourself with your own strength.
— Hitopadesha, Introduction, verse 33 [3]
The Sanskrit maxim (udyoginaṃ puruṣasiṃham upaiti lakṣmīḥ) names the same goddess and the same condition for her favour.
This is almost the Kural itself: the lotus-goddess comes to dwell with the diligent.
Christianity
In the parable of the talents, the servants who trade with their master’s money double it and are rewarded, while the one who buries his talent out of fear and sloth is called ‘wicked and slothful’ and loses even what he had.
— Matthew 25:14-30 [4]
Jesus’ parable shows increase coming to the active and loss to the idle.
Blessing dwells with the diligent and deprivation with the lazy, as in Valluvar’s two goddesses.
Islam
“Nobody has ever eaten a better meal than that which one has earned by working with one’s own hands. The Prophet of Allah, David used to eat from the earnings of his manual labor.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2072 (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet praises self-earned sustenance as the best, citing even a prophet-king who lived by his own labour.
Blessing is found in one’s own labour, the ‘dwelling’ the Kural gives to the goddess of fortune.
Judaism
Rabbi Yitzhak teaches that if someone tells you, ‘I laboured and did not find,’ do not believe him; ‘I did not labour yet found,’ do not believe him; but ‘I laboured and found,’ believe him.
— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 6b [6]
The Talmud treats real attainment as something that only comes with toil.
Success lodging only where there is labour is the Kural’s fortune that ‘dwells in effort’.
Buddhism
“Ever grows the glory of him who is energetic, mindful and pure in conduct, discerning and self-controlled, righteous and heedful.”
— Dhammapada 24 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada links steadily increasing glory to energy and heedfulness.
Glory growing with the energetic parallels fortune taking up residence with the diligent.
Jainism
Jain tradition knows the goddess Śrī (Lakṣmī) seated on a lotus: she is the fourth of the fourteen auspicious dreams Queen Triśalā saw before Mahavira's birth. But Jain doctrine credits fortune and misfortune to a person's own karma and conduct, not to goddesses of luck.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §36 (the dream of the goddess Śrī) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Jainism keeps Śrī as an auspicious symbol, but in its non-theistic karma theory prosperity and adversity follow one's own deeds, a view close to Valluvar's actual point about effort and sloth.
The Kural's image of the lotus goddess living in effort and Misfortune living in sloth fits Jainism only as metaphor: in Jain teaching one's own conduct, not a deity, brings fortune.
Sikhism
“Cheated and plundered by Maya, you do not think of God, and you waste your life in laziness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 92, Siree Raag, Bhagat Trilochan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Bhagat Trilochan included in the Guru Granth Sahib, the idle person is pictured as robbed and ruined, his life squandered through laziness.
The Kural says misfortune dwells in sloth; Trilochan's hymn likewise shows laziness leaving a person plundered and his life wasted.
Greek Mythology
“work, high-born Perses, that Hunger may hate you, and venerable Demeter richly crowned may love you and fill your barn with food; for Hunger is altogether a meet comrade for the sluggard.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 299-302 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod pairs the grain-goddess Demeter, who favours the worker, with Hunger, the sluggard’s companion.
Demeter and Hunger are an exact Greek counterpart to the lotus-goddess and the dark Mugadi.
Philosophy
Samuel Smiles opens Self-Help with the maxim ‘Heaven helps those who help themselves’, arguing that the spirit of self-help is the root of all genuine growth in the individual.
— Samuel Smiles, Self-Help (1859), ch. 1 [11]
The Victorian moralist made personal industry the true source of what people call good fortune.
Like the Kural, Smiles says fortune favours the self-reliant worker.

Real Life Example

Alexander Fleming notices the mould that leads to penicillin
September 1928 · St Mary's Hospital, London, United Kingdom
Returning from holiday on 3 September 1928, the bacteriologist Alexander Fleming began sorting through culture plates of Staphylococcus bacteria in his laboratory. On one plate contaminated by a mould he saw that the bacteria around the mould had been destroyed, and he went on to show that the mould produced a substance, which he named penicillin, that killed many kinds of bacteria. He published his findings in 1929, and penicillin was later developed into a medicine by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their Oxford team; the three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A chance contamination became a medical revolution only because an experienced researcher was at work, paying attention, and followed up what he saw.
The kural says good fortune dwells in the effort of the industrious, and Fleming's lucky mould brought its fortune to the world through a working scientist who was ready to recognise it.
