Kural 337

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

ஒருபொழுதும் வாழ்வது அறியார் கருதுப கோடியும் அல்ல பல.
Oru pozhudhum vaazhvadhu ariyaar karudhupa Kodiyum alla pala.

Meaning

People cannot know if they will live even one more moment, yet they make countless plans, more than ten million of them.

Elementary

Someone might plan what they'll do next week, next year, and in ten years, even though nobody knows for sure what will happen tomorrow. This kural gently laughs at how many plans we make when we can't even be sure of the next hour.

University

The couplet's force comes from the mismatch of scale: “oru pozhudhu” (a single moment or watch of the day) against “kodiyum alla pala” (not a crore, i.e. ten million, but more). People who cannot guarantee a single stretch of time still carry countless projects, desires and calculations. Commentators note the progression within the chapter from “yesterday/today” (336) to an even smaller unit, “one moment” (337), tightening the frame of uncertainty. The critique is not of planning as such but of a mind that lives in imagined futures while forgetting its own fragility. The Gita's portrait of the deluded man counting his gains ‘today’ and ‘tomorrow’, Jesus' parable of the rich fool, and Aeschylus' ‘blind hopes’ all diagnose the same condition.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The demoniac persons think, "I have gained so much wealth today, and I shall now fulfill this desire of mine. This is mine, and tomorrow I shall have even more.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.13-15 [3]
Krishna describes deluded people who endlessly plan their gains for today and tomorrow, as though their lives were secure.
This is the Kural's ‘more than ten million’ calculations, made by people who cannot be sure of the next moment.
Christianity
“And I will say to my soul, Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry. But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided?”
— Luke 12:19-20 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus' parable, a rich man plans years of comfort and dies that very night.
The rich fool is the Kural's planner: many years of plans, not even one secure night.
Islam
“And no soul perceives what it will earn tomorrow, and no soul perceives in what land it will die.”
— Quran 31:34 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran states that no one knows what tomorrow will bring or where they will die.
Both texts point to the basic uncertainty of the future that makes endless planning foolish.
Judaism
“Boast not thyself of to morrow; for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth.”
— Proverbs 27:1 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns against confident talk about tomorrow, since no one knows what a single day will bring.
Both texts set human certainty about the future against ignorance of even one day ahead.
Buddhism
“Here shall I live during the rains, here in winter and summer” — thus thinks the fool. He does not realize the danger (that death might intervene).”
— Dhammapada 286 [6]
The Buddha pictures a fool making plans for every season without realising death may come first.
The fool's seasonal planning is the Kural's ‘more than ten million’ thoughts in someone unsure of a single moment.
Jainism
“I have this, and I have not that; I must do this, and I should not do that! While he talks in this strain, the robbers (viz. time) drag him away. What foolishness is this!”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
A man busy reckoning his possessions and plans is carried off by time before he finishes.
Valluvar mocks those who cannot be sure of one moment yet make endless plans; the Jain verse shows the planner dragged away by time mid-sentence.
Sikhism
“The self-willed manmukhs make their calculations and plans, but only the actions of the Creator come to pass.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 60, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak contrasts the endless schemes of the self-centred person with the reality that events unfold by the Creator's will (hukam).
The Kural mocks those who cannot be sure of one more moment yet make countless plans; Guru Nanak likewise sets human plan-making against our real powerlessness over what will happen.
Greek Mythology
“I caused mortals to cease foreseeing their doom. ... I caused blind hopes to dwell within their breasts.”
— Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 248-250 (Smyth translation) [9]
Prometheus says he stopped humans from foreseeing their deaths by giving them ‘blind hopes’.
In both texts, people forget how fragile life is and fill their minds with countless hopes and plans.
Philosophy
Seneca tells Paulinus that people live as if destined to live forever, never thinking of their own frailty or of how much time has already gone, and put off real living to their fiftieth or sixtieth year.
— Seneca, On the Shortness of Life (De Brevitate Vitae) 3 [10]
In On the Shortness of Life, Seneca criticises people who put off living and make distant plans while forgetting that they are mortal.
Seneca's forgetful planners are the Kural's people who cannot secure one moment yet plan without end.

Real Life Example

Antoni Gaudi dies with the Sagrada Familia unfinished
June 1926 · Barcelona, Spain
The architect Antoni Gaudi, who had devoted more than four decades to designing and building the Sagrada Familia basilica, was struck by a tram in Barcelona on 7 June 1926 while walking to church. He died at the Hospital de la Santa Creu on 10 June and was buried in the crypt of the basilica. The building, planned to take generations, was still far from complete and construction continued long after his death.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Gaudi's vast, long-range design stands in sharp contrast to the sudden, ordinary accident that ended his life. His plans outlived him by a century, carried on by others.
Valluvar remarks that people who cannot be sure of the next moment still carry countless plans, and Gaudi's enormous project was cut short in an instant by a passing tram.
