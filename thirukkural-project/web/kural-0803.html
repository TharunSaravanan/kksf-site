Kural 803

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

பழகிய நட்பெவன் செய்யுங் கெழுதகைமை செய்தாங்கு அமையாக் கடை.
Pazhakiya natpevan seyyung kezhudhakaimai seydhaangu amaiyaak kadai.

Meaning

What good is a friendship of many years if you will not accept what your close friend does, as if you had done it yourself?

Elementary

Imagine your best friend signs both your names on a birthday card for your teacher without asking. If you get cross, what was all that friendship for? A real friend says, ‘Good idea, that’s what I would have done.’

University

The couplet is a rhetorical question: evan seyyum (எவன் செய்யும், ‘what will it do?’) asks what use a long-practised friendship (pazhakiya natpu) has if one does not seydhaangu amaidhal (செய்தாங்கு அமைதல்), ‘acquiesce as though one had done it oneself’, in what the friend did by kezhudhakaimai (intimate right). Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar take this as owning the friend’s act; Solomon Pappaiah adds that this holds even when one does not personally agree with it. Puliyur Kesigan reads the line from the other side: if the same intimacy is not present in the friend too, the long acquaintance is useless. That reading shows the couplet can be taken as a statement about reciprocity. The text is the same in both base sources (with the sandhi form seyyung).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And if, jestfully, I treated You with disrespect, while playing, resting, sitting, eating, when alone, or before others—for all that I crave forgiveness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 11.42 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Arjuna remembers years of casual companionship with Krishna, joking, eating and resting together, and Krishna never took offence.
Krishna accepted his friend’s familiar behaviour without complaint, which is the acceptance the Kural says gives an old friendship its value.
Christianity
Writing to his friend Philemon, Paul sends back the runaway Onesimus, now a Christian, and asks Philemon to receive him as he would receive Paul himself, offering to repay any debt and noting that Philemon owes Paul his own self.
— Philemon 1:17-19 [4]
Paul uses the freedom of an old friendship to ask a great deal and trusts Philemon to treat the request as his own.
This is the Kural’s ideal in practice: a friend’s act of familiarity received as if one had done it oneself.
Islam
When Abu Bakr and ‘Umar fell out, the Prophet told those present not to leave his companion exposed to blame, reminding them that Abu Bakr had believed in him when others called him a liar.
— Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of the Companions, narration of Abu al-Darda’ [5]
The Prophet defended the standing of a long-standing companion against criticism of his conduct.
He treated an old friend’s place as his own concern, which is close to the Kural’s ideal of owning a friend’s acts.
Judaism
On the day David kills Goliath, Jonathan’s soul is bound to David’s. Jonathan makes a covenant with him and, without being asked, gives David his own robe, armour, sword, bow and belt.
— 1 Samuel 18:1-4 [6]
The prince hands over the symbols of his rank to the friend he loves.
Jonathan treats what is his as David’s, the complete identification of interests the Kural asks of old friends.
Buddhism
“He reveals his secrets to you. He keeps your secrets.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.35, Mitta Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Among the marks of a friend worth keeping, the Buddha lists mutual confidence: sharing secrets and guarding the other’s.
That kind of mutual trust is what lets a friend act for you and have it accepted as your own act.
Jainism
“Is it right utterly to abandon friends, who ought not to be forsaken though guilty of evil deeds? … will men cut off their hand because it has struck the eye?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 6 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet compares a friend's offence to one's own hand striking one's own eye: nobody cuts off the hand, because it is part of oneself.
The Kural asks what use an old friendship is if one will not accept a friend's act as though one had done it oneself, and the Naladiyar's image of the hand and the eye makes the friend's act literally one's own.
Sikhism
“My Guru is always with me, near at hand. … Your actions seem so sweet to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 394, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In this Aasaa shabad Guru Arjan speaks of the Guru as an ever-present companion and says that whatever the Beloved does is sweet to him.
The Kural asks what use long friendship is if one will not accept what the friend does by right of closeness as though one had done it oneself; Guru Arjan's line shows that attitude of owning and welcoming whatever the closest companion does.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Iphigenia among the Taurians, Orestes and Pylades each insist on dying in the other’s place, and each treats the other’s fate as his own.
— Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris [10]
The two cousins and companions are so close that neither will accept survival at the other’s cost.
Their friendship makes no distinction between ‘his act’ and ‘mine’, which is the identification the Kural requires.
Philosophy
“Every one likes ceteris paribus to use the horse to which he has been accustomed, rather than one that is untried and new.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 19 (§68), trans. E. S. Shuckburgh [11]
Cicero argues that habit and long familiarity give old friendships a weight that new ones do not have.
Valluvar asks what long familiarity is worth, and Cicero answers that its worth lies in the ease that habit brings.

Real Life Example

Louis Howe writes and speaks for Franklin Roosevelt during his illness
1921-1928 · New York, United States
After Franklin Roosevelt was paralysed by illness in 1921, his old friend and adviser Louis Howe kept Roosevelt before the public by meeting supporters, attending meetings on his behalf and writing editorials and magazine articles, some published under Roosevelt's own byline. Roosevelt kept Howe as his closest counsellor, valued his frank advice and, after winning the presidency, made him his personal secretary with a room in the White House. Howe continued to speak bluntly to Roosevelt until his death in 1936.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Howe's work in Roosevelt's name helped carry him from apparent political ruin to the governorship of New York and the presidency.
The kural asks what use a long friendship is if one will not accept what the friend does by right of closeness as though one had done it oneself, and Roosevelt allowed Howe's words to go out under his own name.
