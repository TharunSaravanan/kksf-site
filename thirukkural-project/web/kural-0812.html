Kural 812

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

உறின்நட்டு அறினொருஉம் ஒப்பிலார் கேண்மை பெறினும் இழப்பினும் என்.
Urinnattu arinoruum oppilaar kenmai perinum izhappinum en.

Meaning

People who are not worth having as friends become close when they can get something and leave when they cannot. It makes no difference whether you gain or lose such friends.

Elementary

Imagine a classmate who sits with you only on days you bring cookies, and walks away when you don’t. Having that ‘friend’ or not having them is really the same, because they were only ever friends with the cookies.

University

The couplet is built on two paired verbs: urin nattu (உறின் நட்டு), ‘when there is gain, befriending’, and arin oruum (அறின் ஒரூஉம்), ‘when it ends, departing’; ar- here means ‘to cease or be cut off’, and oruu is the lengthened (alapedai) form of ‘leave’. The subjects are oppilaar (ஒப்பிலார்), which Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesigan take as ‘the unworthy’ or ‘those of unequal nature’, while Solomon Pappaiah reads it with a twist, ‘those who have no equal in doing this’. The closing ‘en’ (என், ‘what of it?’) is a rhetorical shrug: such friendship is neither gain nor loss. Texts differ in spelling the second word: ytamizh has ‘அறினொருஉம்’, thirukkural.net prints ‘அறின்ஙருஉம்’ (apparently a typographic slip), and the sandhi-joined edition gives ‘உறினட் டறின்னொரூஉ’; the meaning is unaffected.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who loses his money is forsaken by his friends, his wife, his servants and his relations; yet when he regains his riches those who have forsaken him come back to him.”
— Chanakya Niti 15.5 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya observes, with some cynicism, that many relationships simply follow wealth, leaving when it goes and returning when it comes back.
This is precisely the Kural’s friend who comes with gain and leaves with loss.
Christianity
“Wealth maketh many friends; but the poor is separated from his neighbour.”
— Proverbs 19:4 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs notes that prosperity attracts friends and poverty drives them off.
Both describe friendship that tracks advantage, which is why the Kural says losing it costs nothing.
Islam
“And of the people is he who worships Allah on an edge. If he is touched by good, he is reassured by it; but if he is struck by trial, he turns on his face [to the other direction].”
— Qur’an 22:11 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse describes loyalty that holds only while things go well and turns away at the first trial.
The same fair-weather pattern the Kural sees in unworthy friends: present in gain, gone in loss.
Judaism
“All love that depends on a something, [when the] thing ceases, [the] love ceases; and [all love] that does not depend on anything, will never cease.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:16 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
The Mishnah distinguishes love tied to some benefit, which ends when the benefit ends, from love that depends on nothing and lasts, citing David and Jonathan as the lasting kind.
This is the Kural’s gain-based friendship exactly, and it explains why its loss is no loss.
Buddhism
“One is a bottle friend; one says, ‘friend, friend’ only to one’s face; one is a friend and an associate only when it is advantageous.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [8]
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha lists kinds of hollow companions, including the one who is a friend only when it pays.
This is the Kural’s urin nattu, arin oruum: friend while there is advantage, gone when there is none.
Jainism
“men will remain until they have done their work in an old house the thatch of which is untied, keeping out the water br a dam, and being drenched with the rain falling down upon them. Thus will friends! remain with one until their business is finished.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 24 'Improper Friendship', stanza 1 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The poet compares self-serving friends to men who put up with a leaking old house only until their work there is finished, and then leave.
The Kural describes the unworthy who befriend you while there is gain and leave when it ends, and the Naladiyar's friends who stay only until their business is done describe the same thing.
Sikhism
“The true society is the company of those who meditate on the Name of the Lord. Do not associate with those, O Nanak! Who look out only for their own interests.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 520, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan contrasts true company, built around the Name, with association with people who are only after their own advantage, and tells the Sikh to avoid the latter.
The Kural describes unworthy people who befriend you when there is gain and leave when it ends; Guru Arjan names the same self-interested companions and says not to keep their company at all.
Greek Mythology
“He who shares the danger ought to share the prize.”
— Aesop, The Two Travelers and the Axe (tr. G. F. Townsend) [11]
In Aesop’s fable of the Two Travelers and the Axe, one man says ‘I have found an axe’, not ‘we’, but when the owner gives chase he cries ‘we are undone’; his companion tells him to keep saying ‘I’.
The finder wants partnership only in loss, the mirror image of the Kural’s friend who wants it only in gain; both are the same selfish calculation.
Philosophy
Seneca tells Lucilius that a friendship entered for one’s own advantage is a bargain, and that the man who begins to be your friend because it pays will also stop being your friend because it pays.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 9 [12]
For Seneca, friendship made for profit carries its own ending inside it, because the reason for it can disappear.
Valluvar draws the same conclusion: since such friendship comes and goes with gain, having it or losing it is all one.

Real Life Example

Marshal Marmont takes his corps over to the Allies
5 April 1814 · Essonne, near Paris, France
Auguste Marmont had first met Napoleon at Toulon, served as his aide-de-camp, sailed back with him from Egypt and rose under him to become a marshal and duke of Ragusa. In 1814, as the Allied armies closed in, Marmont capitulated at Paris on 30 March and on 5 April went over to the Allied side with his army corps, which had been guarding the road to Fontainebleau. Napoleon abdicated the next day, and under the restored Bourbon king Marmont was made a peer of France, while his title gave French the verb raguser, meaning to betray.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Marmont had shared in Napoleon's rise for twenty years, but when there was no more to be gained from him he left at the moment of greatest need.
The kural describes those who befriend you while there is gain and leave when the gain is gone, and Marmont's change of sides came exactly when Napoleon's fortunes collapsed.
