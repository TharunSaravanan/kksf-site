Kural 1258

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

பன்மாயக் கள்வன் பணிமொழி அன்றோநம் பெண்மை உடைக்கும் படை.
Panmaayak kalvan panimozhi androa-nam penmai udaikkum padai.

Meaning

My love is a clever thief with many tricks. Aren't his soft, sweet words the army that breaks down my firmness as a woman?

Elementary

Imagine building a strong sandcastle wall, and then a friend says something so sweet and kind that you happily knock the wall down yourself. The heroine says her husband’s gentle words are like that: they break down her strong ‘I won’t give in’ feeling.

University

The heroine calls her beloved pal-māyak kaḷvaṉ, ‘a thief of many wiles’, an affectionate reproach familiar from Tamil love poetry, and names his paṇimoḻi (humble, coaxing words) as the paṭai (army, weapon) that breaks her peṇmai (womanliness, here the firmness that resists). Mu. Varadarasanar glosses peṇmai as a fortress (அரண்) and Solomon Pappaiah as the fort of self-restraint (மன அடக்கமாகிய கோட்டை), sustaining the military image of breaking. Puliyur Kesikan reads the couplet as past (‘were they not, on that day…’), referring to their courtship, while others read it as a present truth. Pope’s rendering on ytamizh.com (‘The words of that deceiver, versed in every wily art, / Are instruments that break through every guard of woman’s heart!’) pushes kaḷvaṉ toward ‘deceiver’, though the Tamil glosses keep the tone fond and playful. The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the tenth canto of the Gita Govinda, Krishna comes to Radha while she is still angry and pleads with her in the sweetest words, begging her to give up her anger and set her foot upon his head.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 10 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva shows a lover’s gentle, humble pleading overcoming a woman’s proud resistance.
Krishna’s words are the Kural’s paṇimoḻi: coaxing speech that breaks down a woman’s firmness.
Christianity
“With her much fair speech she caused him to yield, with the flattering of her lips she forced him.”
— Proverbs 7:21 (KJV) [4]
The proverb describes how smooth, flattering words can overcome a person’s resolve.
The Kural uses the same idea: sweet, coaxing speech is the weapon that breaks down firmness.
Islam
“Some eloquent speech has the influence of magic.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5146 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet observed that well-chosen words can move people to do what they had refused to do.
The Kural’s heroine says the same of her husband’s coaxing words, which work on her firmness like a spell.
Judaism
“My beloved spoke, and said unto me: ‘Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away.’”
— Song of Songs 2:10 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The Song’s beloved wins the woman over with tender, coaxing words.
In the Kural too, the beloved’s gentle words are what overcome the woman’s reserve.
Buddhism
After the Buddha’s awakening, Mara’s three daughters, Craving, Discontent and Passion, approach him with flattering words and alluring forms to break his resolve, but he is unmoved.
— Samyutta Nikaya 4.25, Dhitaro Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The sutta shows coaxing words used as a weapon against a person’s firmness, and the firmness holding.
The Kural’s heroine faces the same weapon of coaxing speech, but, unlike the Buddha, she gives way.
Jainism
“Meekly and politely they approach him with their manifold arts to win his heart; and talking sweetly in confidential conversation they make him do (what they like).”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga warns monks that gentle, sweet speech is the means by which their resolve is overcome.
The Kural names the same weapon, humble and sweet words used with many arts, as what breaks the heroine’s firmness.
Sikhism
“So listen, O my sisters - the Enticer has enticed my mind. My body and mind are drenched with Nectar.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 764, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's bride tells her companions that the Beloved, the Enticer, has won her mind completely.
The Kural's wife calls her beloved a thief of many wiles whose coaxing words broke down her womanly firmness; Guru Nanak's bride likewise confesses to her friends that the Enticer has captured her mind.
Greek Mythology
When Hera borrows Aphrodite’s embroidered band, Homer says it holds love, desire, and the persuasive whispering that steals the wits even of the wise.
— Homer, Iliad 14.214-217 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
In Homer, lovers’ persuasive speech is literally a divine weapon that can overcome even Zeus.
The Kural’s heroine names her beloved’s sweet words as the weapon that breaks her firmness, the same power Aphrodite’s band contains.
Philosophy
Gorgias argues that Helen cannot be blamed if she was persuaded by speech, because speech is a mighty lord that, with the smallest and least visible body, achieves the most divine works.
— Gorgias, Encomium of Helen 8 (paraphrase) [11]
Gorgias makes the power of persuasive words a defence of the one who yielded to them.
The heroine offers a similar plea: it was her beloved’s coaxing words, a whole army, that broke her firmness.

Real Life Example

Pierre Curie's letters persuade Marie Sklodowska to return to Paris
1894-1895 · Paris, France, and Poland
Marie Sklodowska met the physicist Pierre Curie in Paris in the spring of 1894. Her plan had always been to return to Poland and teach, and in the summer of 1894 she went home unsure whether she would come back; she later wrote that marriage would have meant abandoning her country and family. Pierre's heartfelt letters helped persuade her to return to Paris and work alongside him, and they married in a simple civil ceremony at Sceaux on 25 July 1895.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Marie's firm plan to return home gave way before Pierre's patient, persuasive words, beginning one of the most famous partnerships in science.
The kural's heroine says her beloved's gentle, coaxing words were the army that broke down her firmness; Pierre's letters did the same to Marie's resolve to leave France.
