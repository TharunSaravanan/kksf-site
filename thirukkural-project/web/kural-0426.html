Kural 426

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

எவ்வ துறைவது உலகம் உலகத்தோடு அவ்வ துறைவ தறிவு.
Evva thuraivathu ulagam ulagaththodu Avva thuraiva tharivu.

Meaning

Wisdom is living in step with the world, behaving the way the best people in the world behave.

Elementary

Every place has its own ways of doing things. A wise person notices how good people around them behave and fits in with them, instead of being odd or rude just to stand out.

University

The couplet turns on the parallel எவ்வது / அவ்வது ('in whatever way / in that way') and on the verb உறைதல் (to dwell or abide). Wisdom is to abide with the world in the way the world abides. As in 425, Parimelazhagan takes ulagam to mean the uyarndhor, the worthy or great of the world, not the crowd. The Kural is therefore not a call to conformity with the majority but to conduct in step with the accepted norms of the good. It still carries a real social insight: discernment includes reading context and custom and not acting eccentrically. This complements 423. There, wisdom judges claims independently; here, wisdom adapts one's conduct to the settled ways of good society. Parallels range from the Talmudic 'never deviate from local custom' to Paul's 'all things to all men' and Descartes's provisional maxim of following his country's customs.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“So as not to disrupt the minds of ignorant men attached to the fruitive results of prescribed duties, a learned person should not induce them to stop work. Rather, by working in the spirit of devotion, he should engage them in all sorts of activities.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.26 [4]
Krishna advises the wise not to set themselves apart and unsettle ordinary people. They should act alongside them in the accepted way (compare 3.21: whatever the great do, others follow).
Both texts see wisdom as moving in step with the world instead of standing apart from it.
Christianity
“I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:22 (KJV) [5]
Paul describes adapting his conduct to Jews, Gentiles, and the weak alike: living as they live, so as to reach them.
Both texts treat adapting to the ways of those around you as wise and effective.
Islam
Ali ibn Abi Talib said: speak to people according to what they understand. Do you want Allah and His Messenger to be denied?
— Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Knowledge, no. 127 (saying of Ali) [6]
Bukhari records Ali's teaching in the Book of Knowledge: the wise adapt what they say to the capacity and ways of their audience.
Both texts see wisdom as fitting oneself to the people among whom one lives and speaks.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that a person should never deviate from local custom. When Moses ascended on high he did not eat bread, and when the angels descended to visit Abraham they appeared to eat.
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 86b [7]
Even Moses and the angels adjusted their behaviour to the realm they were in, and the Talmud draws a general rule of wise conduct from this.
This is nearly the Kural's point: live as the world you are in lives.
Buddhism
The Buddha says: 'I do not dispute with the world; the world disputes with me.' What the wise in the world agree does not exist, he too says does not exist; what they agree exists, he too says exists.
— Puppha Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 22.94 [8]
The Buddha presents himself as not contrary for its own sake. He aligns his statements with what the wise of the world accept.
This matches the Kural and Parimelazhagan's reading that one should abide as the wise of the world abide.
Jainism
Hemacandra's Yogaśāstra opens its account of lay conduct with the qualities of the mārgānusārī, the householder who is 'following the path' even before taking the vows. Among them are earning wealth honestly and conforming to the proper, approved customs of one's society and country.
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, ch. 1 (the mārgānusārī qualities of the householder); paraphrased [9]
For Jain teachers, a layperson's spiritual path begins with being a decent and well-adjusted member of society, living by the accepted good customs around them, before any higher discipline is taken up.
Valluvar's wisdom of 'living as the (worthy) world lives' corresponds to the Jain picture of the householder whose first virtues include fitting in with the sound customs of his land.
Sikhism
“In the midst of this world, do seva, and you shall be given a place of honor in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Siree Raag Guru Nanak teaches that the spiritual life is lived in the world through service, not by leaving it.
The Kural defines wisdom as living in harmony with the world as the worthy do; Guru Nanak likewise places the right life within the world, through seva.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus is introduced as polytropos, 'the man of many turns', who 'saw the cities of many men and learned their mind'. He survives by adapting his manner to each people and court he encounters.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.1-5 [11]
His defining trait is adaptability: reading each new society and conducting himself accordingly.
He illustrates the Kural's wisdom of moving with the world as the world moves.
Philosophy
The first maxim of Descartes's provisional moral code is to obey the laws and customs of his country, and to govern himself by the most moderate opinions of the most sensible people he would live among.
— René Descartes, Discourse on the Method, Part 3 [12]
While doubting everything in theory, Descartes decides that in practice a wise person lives by the settled customs of sensible people around him.
This closely matches the Kural, including its implicit restriction to the sensible or worthy, as Parimelazhagan reads it.

Real Life Example

Matteo Ricci adopts the ways of Chinese scholars
1583-1610 · Southern China and Beijing, Ming China
The Italian Jesuit Matteo Ricci entered China in 1583, when the interior was closed to most foreigners. He learned Chinese, studied the Chinese classics, and in the mid-1590s exchanged the robes of a Buddhist monk for the dress of a Chinese scholar, winning a hearing among officials and literati through his learning in mathematics, astronomy and geography. In January 1601 he was allowed to settle in Beijing, where he lived and taught until his death in 1610.
— Sources: [13] [14]
By living as the respected scholars of China lived, Ricci became one of the first Europeans accepted into Chinese intellectual life, opening a long exchange of knowledge between Europe and China.
The kural defines wisdom as conducting oneself as the worthy of the world conduct themselves, and Ricci succeeded by shaping his dress, speech and learning to the ways of China's scholar-officials.
