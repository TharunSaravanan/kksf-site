Kural 168

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அழுக்காறு எனஒரு பாவி திருச்செற்றுத் தீயுழி உய்த்து விடும்.
Azhukkaaru ena oru paavi thiruchchetruth Theeyuzhi uyththu vidum.

Meaning

Jealousy is a wicked thing like no other. It wipes out a person's riches and pushes them into a place of terrible suffering.

Elementary

Envy is like a sneaky thief who first steals everything you have, then pushes you onto a dangerous road. Once envy moves in, it takes your good things away and leads you somewhere bad.

University

Valluvar personifies envy as a பாவி (sinner, evil being) with no equal (ஒரு), and gives it two acts: it destroys wealth (திருச்செற்று, with திரு again evoking Lakshmi from the previous couplet) and drives its host into தீயுழி. The term means “evil place” or “evil path”; Parimelazhagar and Solomon Pappaiah read it as hell, others as a life of wrongdoing. The double sense covers both this-worldly ruin and afterlife punishment, a pattern also seen in the Dhammapada's “suffers here and hereafter.” Parallels such as Korah and Devadatta, whose envy ended with the earth swallowing them, show how widely traditions have linked envy to a downward plunge.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who are envious and mischievous, who are the lowest among men, I perpetually cast into the ocean of material existence, into various demoniac species of life.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.19 (Prabhupada translation, via Vedabase) [3]
Krishna says the envious (dviṣataḥ) and cruel are repeatedly cast into lower, demonic births.
Both texts say envy drives a person downward to the worst destination.
Christianity
“For wrath killeth the foolish man, and envy slayeth the silly one.”
— Job 5:2 (KJV) [4]
Eliphaz tells Job that envy kills the simple person who harbors it.
Like the Kural's “sinner without equal,” envy is pictured as a killer of its host.
Islam
“And his soul permitted to him the murder of his brother, so he killed him and became among the losers.”
— Qur'an 5:30 (Saheeh International; narrative at 5:27-31) [5]
The Qur'an tells of Adam's son who, after his brother's offering was accepted and his was not, killed him and became “among the losers.”
Envy of a brother's acceptance leads to the gravest sin and lasting loss, the path the Kural describes.
Judaism
“They, and all that appertained to them, went down alive into the pit, and the earth closed upon them: and they perished from among the congregation.”
— Numbers 16:33 (KJV); cf. Pirkei Avot 5:17 on Korah's dispute [4]
Korah and his followers, who challenged Moses and Aaron out of jealousy of their position, are swallowed by the earth with all their possessions.
Envy destroys wealth (“all that appertained to them”) and plunges the envier into the pit, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
“The evil-doer suffers here and hereafter; he suffers in both the worlds. The thought, “Evil have I done,” torments him, and he suffers even more when gone to realms of woe.”
— Dhammapada 17 (trans. Buddharakkhita); Devadatta story from the Dhammapada Commentary [6]
The commentarial tradition attaches this verse to Devadatta, whose envy of the Buddha led to schism and assassination attempts, and who was swallowed by the earth into hell.
Envy ruins in this life and drives to hell hereafter, the two-stage fall the Kural describes.
Jainism
“A cut-throat enemy will not do him such harm as his own perversity will do him; the man without pity will feel repentance in the hour of death.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.48 (cf. 20.46) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
The young ascetic who calls himself “without a protector” tells King Śreṇika that the worst enemy a person can have is his own perverted disposition, which brings him to grief at death and, as the preceding verses say, to hell.
Valluvar personifies envy as a sinner without equal that wrecks a man's wealth and drives him to the fire of hell, and the Jain verse likewise ranks the inner vice above any outward foe as the agent of ruin.
Sikhism
“In the worlds of Shiva, Brahma and Indra, I wandered around, burning up with envy.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1219, Raag Saarang, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes wandering even through the heavens of the gods while burning with envy, so that even those exalted realms bring no relief.
The Kural calls envy a matchless sinner that drives a man into the fire of hell; Guru Arjan's image of a soul burning with envy wherever it goes echoes that fire.
Greek Mythology
Aglauros, poisoned by Envy, blocks Mercury from reaching her sister; the god turns her into a dark stone statue, stained by her envious mind.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.812-832 (trans. A. S. Kline) [9]
Envy destroys Aglauros's life and fortune completely, ending in her punishment by a god.
Like the Kural's “sinner,” Envy wrecks its host and delivers her to a terrible end.
Philosophy
Aristotle groups envy with spite and shamelessness, and with adultery, theft, and murder, as things that “imply by their names that they are themselves bad”; for all of them, “one must always be wrong.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2.6 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
For Aristotle, envy is not a feeling to moderate but a vice that always leads a person wrong.
The Kural's “sinner without equal” and Aristotle's “always wrong” both treat envy as corrupt at its root.

Real Life Example

The Pazzi conspiracy against the Medici
26 April 1478 · Florence Cathedral, Florence, Italy
Members of the Pazzi family, a rival Florentine banking house, joined a plot to overthrow the ruling Medici, and on 26 April 1478 conspirators attacked Lorenzo and Giuliano de' Medici during Mass in Florence Cathedral, killing Giuliano and wounding Lorenzo. The people of Florence rallied to the Medici, the plot collapsed, and the conspirators were pursued and punished. The Pazzi family was stripped of its riches and banished, while Lorenzo emerged with his power strengthened.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Instead of competing fairly, the Pazzi tried to seize a rival's position by crime, and the attempt destroyed their own wealth and standing.
The kural says envy destroys a man's wealth and drives him onto the path of evil, and the Pazzi lost their fortune after turning to a criminal plot against their rivals.
