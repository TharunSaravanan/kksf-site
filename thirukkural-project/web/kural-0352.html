Kural 352

Knowledge of the True · Meyyunarthal · மெய்யுணர்தல்

இருள்நீங்கி இன்பம் பயக்கும் மருள்நீங்கி மாசறு காட்சி யவர்க்கு.
Irulneengi inbam payakkum marulneengi Maasaru kaatchi yavarkku.

Meaning

For people who are free from confusion and see the truth clearly, that clear sight drives away darkness and brings true joy.

Elementary

When you switch on the light in a dark room, the scary shape in the corner turns out to be just a coat on a chair, and suddenly you feel safe and happy. People who see the truth clearly are like that: the light of truth chases away the darkness and fear, and joy comes in.

University

The couplet is built on a matched pair: irul neengi (darkness departs) and marul neengi (delusion departs). When inner confusion (marul) is removed, outer darkness (irul, which commentators gloss as the darkness of hell or of future births) also lifts. The key phrase maasaru kaatchi, ‘stainless seeing’, frames liberation as a kind of vision (compare the Sanskrit darshana) rather than mere intellectual agreement, and Parimelazhagar takes the resulting inbam (bliss) as the bliss of release (veedu). The light/darkness metaphor for knowledge and ignorance is shared with the Upanishads (tamaso ma jyotir gamaya), with the Buddha’s first sermon (‘illumination arose’), and with Plato’s ascent from the cave. What stands out in Valluvar is the ethical precondition built into the seeing itself: the vision must be free of stain (maasu).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When, however, one is enlightened with the knowledge by which nescience is destroyed, then his knowledge reveals everything, as the sun lights up everything in the daytime.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.16 [3]
Krishna compares true knowledge to the rising sun: once ignorance is destroyed, everything is seen clearly, as objects become visible at dawn.
Same movement as the Kural: when ignorance (marul) is removed, darkness (irul) goes and clear vision takes its place.
Christianity
“Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God.”
— Matthew 5:8 (KJV) [4]
In the Beatitudes, Jesus promises that people whose hearts are pure and undivided will see God.
The Kural’s ‘maasaru kaatchi’ (stainless seeing) and ‘inbam’ (bliss) match the Beatitude’s purity, seeing, and blessedness almost exactly.
Islam
“Allāh is the Ally of those who believe. He brings them out from darknesses into the light.”
— Qur’an 2:257 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes God leading believers out of layers of darkness into light, while false guides lead people the other way.
The same passage from darkness to light that the Kural promises to those freed from delusion.
Judaism
“For with thee is the fountain of life: in thy light shall we see light.”
— Psalm 36:9 (KJV) [4]
The Psalmist says that true sight is only possible within God’s own light, which is also the source of life.
Like the Kural, the Psalm links clear seeing, light, and the fullness of life.
Buddhism
“Vision arose, insight arose, discernment arose, knowledge arose, illumination arose within me with regard to things never heard before: ‘This is the noble truth of stress.’”
— Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
In his first sermon the Buddha describes his awakening to the Four Noble Truths as vision and illumination, ending with the certainty that there would be no further birth.
The Kural’s ‘stainless seeing’ that removes darkness corresponds to the Buddha’s ‘vision… illumination’; both ground liberation in seeing truth directly.
Jainism
“By the teaching of true knowledge, by the avoidance of ignorance and delusion, and by the destruction of love and hatred, one arrives at final deliverance which is nothing but bliss.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 184) [7]
Mahāvīra's sermon opens by naming the path: true knowledge, the removal of ignorance and delusion, and the end of passion lead to a liberation that is pure bliss.
Valluvar promises that when delusion departs and stainless vision arises, darkness lifts and bliss follows. The Jain verse makes the same sequence explicit: delusion avoided, knowledge gained, 'final deliverance which is nothing but bliss'.
Sikhism
“The Divine Light shines in their hearts, and like the sun which removes the darkness of night, it dispels the darkness of ignorance.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 87, Siree Raag (Sri Raag ki Vaar), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das says that when the Guru's teaching is taken to heart, divine light dawns within and drives out ignorance as the sun drives out night.
The Kural says that for those freed from delusion, a clear vision drives away the darkness; Guru Amar Das uses the same image of inner light dispelling the dark.
Greek Mythology
The Homeric Hymn to Demeter closes by declaring blessed the person on earth who has seen the Eleusinian Mysteries, while the uninitiated has no such good lot after death, down in the darkness and gloom.
— Homeric Hymn to Demeter, lines c. 480–482 [9]
Initiates at Eleusis were promised a happier fate after death because of what they had ‘seen’ in the secret rites.
As in the Kural, a saving ‘sight’ separates those who reach bliss from those left in darkness.
Philosophy
“The instrument of knowledge can only by the movement of the whole soul be turned from the world of becoming into that of being, and learn by degrees to endure the sight of being, and of the brightest and best of being, or in other words, of the good.”
— Plato, Republic VII, 518c (trans. Jowett) [10]
Plato says education is not putting sight into blind eyes but turning the whole soul away from shifting appearances toward true being, which it learns to look at directly.
The Kural’s ‘marul neengi… maasaru kaatchi’ (delusion gone, pure vision gained) is Plato’s turn from becoming to being, from shadow to light.

Real Life Example

Wang Yangming's awakening in exile in Guizhou
1508 · Longchang, Guizhou province, Ming China
After protesting against the imprisonment of officials by a powerful court eunuch, the Ming official and scholar Wang Yangming was beaten and banished in 1506-1507 to a minor post in remote Guizhou. There, in hardship and isolation, he reached in 1508 a sudden philosophical awakening: that the principles of things are to be sought within one's own mind rather than in external objects, as the school of Zhu Xi had taught. He went on to teach the unity of knowing and acting, and his ideas became one of the most influential schools of thought in China and Japan.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Wang's years of confusion about how to find truth ended not through more outward study but through a clear inner insight, which he then spent his life teaching.
The kural promises that for those freed of delusion and granted stainless sight, darkness gives way to joy, much as Wang's awakening in a bleak exile cleared his long confusion and set the course of his life.
