Kural 1158

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

இன்னாது இனன்இல்ஊர் வாழ்தல் அதனினும் இன்னாது இனியார்ப் பிரிவு.
Innaadhu inanil oor vaazhdhal adhaninum innaadhu iniyaarp pirivu.

Meaning

It is painful to live in a town where no one is close to you. It is even more painful to be apart from the one you love.

Elementary

Being the new kid in a school where you don’t know anyone feels lonely. But being far away from the person you love most feels even lonelier, even if you are surrounded by people. That is what this couplet says.

University

The couplet is built on a graded comparison: iṉṉātu (இன்னாது, ‘painful’) is repeated, first for living in an iṉaṉ il ūr (இனன் இல் ஊர்), ‘a town without one’s own kind or kin’, and then, with ataṉiṉum (அதனினும், ‘more than that’), for iṉiyār pirivu (இனியார்ப் பிரிவு), ‘separation from the sweet one’. Iṉam can mean kin, friends of one’s own sort, or (for the heroine) companions such as the confidante, which Puliyur Kesikan specifies; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as ‘those who love us as our own’. The repetition of iṉṉātu echoes the device of the Iṉṉā Nāṟpatu, a didactic work among the Eighteen Minor Classics. Pope: ‘’Tis sad to sojourn in the town where no kind kinsmen dwell; / ’Tis sadder still to bid a friend beloved farewell.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Separated from Sita, Rama wanders the shores of Lake Pampa in spring, and the beauty of the season, which would delight others, only deepens his grief for her.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 1 (Rama’s lament at Pampa) (paraphrase) [3]
In the Kishkindha Kanda, even a beautiful place feels desolate to Rama without his wife.
Rama’s lament shows the Kural’s ranking: separation from the beloved makes any place more painful than mere loneliness would.
Christianity
“But we, brethren, being taken from you for a short time in presence, not in heart, endeavoured the more abundantly to see your face with great desire.”
— 1 Thessalonians 2:17 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Thessalonians that his separation from them, though brief and only in body, has made his longing to see them stronger.
Paul, like the Kural, finds being parted from those dear to him one of the keenest pains, however short.
Islam
“Hearken to the reed-flute, how it complains, / Lamenting its banishment from its home.”
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book I, Prologue (tr. E.H. Whinfield) [5]
Rumi opens the Masnavi with the reed cut from its reed-bed, whose music is a lament for its lost origin, a symbol of the soul separated from the Beloved.
The reed’s exile is the Kural’s ‘town without one’s own’, and its deeper grief is for the Beloved it was parted from.
Judaism
“How shall we sing the LORD’S song In a foreign land?”
— Psalm 137:4 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The exiles in Babylon cannot bring themselves to sing, because they are cut off from Zion, the place they love.
The psalm joins both pains the Kural compares: living in a foreign place, and being cut off from what one loves most.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names two pains: being with those we do not love, and being without those we do.
The Kural ranks the same two pains and finds the second, absence of the beloved, the worse.
Jainism
“And we were gods having great power, in the regions of the gods. This is our sixth birth, in which we are separated from each other.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Brahmadatta recalls five past lives spent together with his brother Citra and grieves that in this sixth life they have been parted.
After every hardship they shared together, it is separation that Brahmadatta names as the sorrow of this life, as the Kural ranks it above all else.
Sikhism
“Many talk of the pain and suffering of separation; O pain, you are the ruler of all. Fareed, that body, within which love of the Lord does not well up - look upon that body as a cremation ground.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 36) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid calls the pain of separation (birha) the sovereign over every other pain, and says a body that never feels it is no better than a cremation ground.
The Kural ranks pains, saying living among strangers is bitter but parting from the beloved is bitterer still; Farid likewise crowns the pain of separation as the ruler of all sorrows.
Greek Mythology
On Calypso’s island, where a goddess loves him and offers him immortality, Odysseus spends his days sitting on the shore weeping, longing for home and for Penelope.
— Homer, Odyssey 5 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer shows that no comfort can make up for being parted from one’s own.
Odysseus lives the Kural’s comparison: a strange place is hard, but being parted from the beloved is harder still.
Philosophy
Cicero’s Laelius says that life without friendship is hardly worth living, and that nothing in nature is so opposed to solitude as human beings.
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia (On Friendship) (paraphrase) [11]
On Friendship argues that human beings are made for companionship, and that being cut off from those we love takes the savour out of life.
Cicero supports the Kural’s first claim about friendless places, and the Kural adds that losing the one dearest is worse still.

Real Life Example

Ovid is banished to Tomis on the Black Sea
8 CE · Tomis (now Constanta, Romania)
In 8 CE the Roman emperor Augustus banished the poet Ovid from Rome to Tomis, a town on the far shore of the Black Sea. In exile Ovid wrote the Tristia, or Sorrows, poems that include letters addressed to his wife, along with long letters and poems appealing to be allowed to return to Rome. He was never recalled and died in Tomis in 17 CE.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ovid's exile poems became the classic record of a person cut off from home, friends and spouse and forced to live among strangers.
The kural ranks living in a town where no one is close to you as painful and parting from one's beloved as more painful still, and Ovid suffered both together in Tomis.
