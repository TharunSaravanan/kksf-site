Kural 193

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

நயனிலன் என்பது சொல்லும் பயனில பாரித் துரைக்கும் உரை.
Nayanilan enbadhu sollum payanila, Paarith thuraikkum urai.

Meaning

When a person goes on and on about useless things, their talk itself shows everyone that they are not a good person.

Elementary

You can often tell what kind of person someone is just by listening to them. If a person keeps talking on and on about useless things, their own words are like a sign on their back that says, “I am not a good person.” They don’t even need anyone else to say it.

University

The grammar is striking: the subject of சொல்லும் (“will declare”) is the talk itself (உரை, urai), so speech bears witness against the speaker. பாரித்து (paarittu, “spreading out, amplifying”) condemns not a single slip but the habit of stretching trivial matter into long discourse. The key term நயனிலன் (nayanilan, “one without nayan”) is glossed differently by modern commentators: Mu. Varadarasan reads it as lacking virtue (aram), Solomon Pappaiah as lacking justice (neethi), and Kalaignar as simply a ‘useless person’. All of these readings agree that speech works as a public index of inner character. The same idea appears in Solon’s maxim that speech is the image of actions, and in the Buddha’s teaching that discernment is revealed through conversation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Keshav, what is the disposition of one who is situated in divine consciousness? How does an enlightened person talk? How does he sit? How does he walk?”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.54 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Arjuna asks how inner realisation shows outwardly, and the first sign he asks about is speech.
Both assume that speech shows a person’s inner state. The Kural uses the idea negatively (empty talk exposes a lack of virtue), and the Gita positively.
Christianity
“For a dream cometh through the multitude of business; and a fool’s voice is known by multitude of words.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:3 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher says a fool can be recognised by the sheer quantity of his words.
In both texts, piling up empty words identifies the kind of person who speaks them.
Islam
“Indeed among the excellence of a person’s Islam is that he leaves what does not concern him.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2317 (sunnah.com) [5]
This widely taught saying (also no. 12 in al-Nawawi’s Forty Hadith) makes leaving aside irrelevant matters, including irrelevant talk, a measure of how good someone’s faith is.
Both texts read engagement with trivial matters as a measure of inner quality.
Judaism
“A small coin in an empty barrel calls: Kish, kish.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 85b (William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz trans., Sefaria) [6]
Ulla quotes a folk proverb: one coin in an empty jar rattles loudly, while a full jar makes no noise. (The words ‘small’ and ‘empty’ are the translator’s clarifying additions.)
Both texts say that noisy talk shows inner emptiness.
Buddhism
“It’s through discussion that a person’s discernment may be known, and then only after a long period, not a short period.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.192, Thana Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha says a small fish can be told from the ripples it makes, and in the same way a dull mind can be told from how a person talks.
Both texts treat speech as the window through which others read what a person is like inside.
Jainism
“Indulging in meaningless and unrestrained talk out of self-importance is maukharya.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.32, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Pūjyapāda's commentary defines maukharya (garrulity), which Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.32 lists as a lapse in the layperson's vow to avoid purposeless harm (anarthadaṇḍa-virati).
Valluvar says long talk about worthless things itself announces the speaker's lack of goodness, and Jain lay ethics names that very habit, meaningless talk born of self-importance, as a breach of a vow.
Sikhism
“Whatever is in the mind, comes forth; spoken words by themselves are just wind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa ki Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok preserved in Asa ki Vaar, Guru Angad teaches that what a person truly is inside will show itself, while words on their own are only air.
The Kural says that long talk about worthless things itself announces the speaker's lack of goodness; Guru Angad likewise holds that the inner self comes out and that mere words are empty wind.
Greek Mythology
“Oho, my friend, it’s you, is it? I, too, should have been afraid if I hadn’t heard your voice.”
— Aesop, ‘The Ass in the Lion’s Skin’ (trans. V. S. Vernon Jones, 1912) [10]
In Aesop’s fable an ass dressed in a lion’s skin scares everyone until he brays, and the fox recognises him at once.
Speech gives the speaker away. In the Kural, empty talk exposes a man without worth just as the bray exposes the ass.
Philosophy
“He used to say, too, that speech was the image of actions.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 1.58 (Solon) (trans. C. D. Yonge) [11]
Diogenes Laertius records Solon’s maxim that a man’s words mirror his deeds and character.
Solon’s ‘speech is the image’ is the Kural’s point in general terms: empty speech reflects an empty moral life.

Real Life Example

The Tichborne Claimant undone by his own testimony
1871-1874 · London, England, United Kingdom
A man who came from Australia claiming to be Sir Roger Tichborne, the lost heir to an English baronetcy, sued for the family estates in a civil trial that opened in May 1871 and ran for 102 days. Under a cross-examination by Sir John Coleridge that lasted more than three weeks, his long testimony failed to hold up, and his case collapsed in March 1872 after evidence of tattoos that the real Roger had and he did not. He was then tried for perjury, found to be Arthur Orton, a butcher's son from Wapping, and sentenced on 28 February 1874 to fourteen years' penal servitude.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The claimant's own words, poured out at enormous length in court, were what exposed him.
The kural says that long talk spread over worthless matter itself announces the speaker's lack of worth, and the claimant's weeks of testimony are what proved him an impostor.
