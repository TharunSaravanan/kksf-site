Kural 434

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

குற்றமே காக்க பொருளாகக் குற்றமே அற்றந் தரூஉம் பகை.
Kutrame kaakka porulaakak kutrame Atran tharooum pakai.

Meaning

Make guarding against faults your main goal, because faults are the very enemy that brings ruin.

Elementary

We often worry about enemies outside us. This kural says the most dangerous enemy is a bad habit or mistake inside ourselves, because that is what can truly destroy us. So guarding against our own faults should be our main job.

University

The repetition of குற்றமே (‘fault itself’) in both lines is emphatic: the ēkāram singles out fault, not external foes, as the true enemy. பொருளாக can be read as ‘as the essential aim’ or, with a pun on பொருள் (wealth, the theme of the whole Porutpāl), ‘as though it were your treasure’: guarding against faults is to be protected like wealth. அற்றம் means ruin or destruction, and தரூஉம் is the prolonged (alapedai) form of தரும், ‘that which brings’. (The two digitised editions consulted print ‘த்ரூஉம்’, a typographical slip for தரூஉம்.) The political reading (Solomon Pappaiah) is that a state is destroyed less by invading armies than by the ruler’s own moral failings, which is the governing thesis of this chapter.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.37 [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that the real enemy is not outside him but the desire and anger within.
The Kural and the Gita both locate the decisive enemy inside the self; the Kural calls it fault, the Gita names lust turning into wrath.
Christianity
“If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.”
— Genesis 4:7 (KJV) [4]
God warns Cain that sin is crouching at his door like a predator, and that he must master it.
Both texts picture wrongdoing as an enemy lying in wait, and both command vigilant guarding against it.
Islam
“And I do not acquit myself. Indeed, the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon which my Lord has mercy. Indeed, my Lord is Forgiving and Merciful.”
— Quran 12:53 (Sahih International) [5]
In the story of Joseph, the speaker admits that his own soul constantly urges him toward evil, and only God’s mercy protects him.
The Kural’s enemy within is the Quran’s nafs that ‘persistently enjoins evil’; both demand watchfulness over oneself.
Judaism
“So too the Holy One, Blessed be He, said to Israel: My children, I created an evil inclination, which is the wound, and I created Torah as its antidote.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 30b [6]
The Talmud describes the evil inclination as a wound inside each person, and Torah study as the bandage that keeps it from festering; it cites Genesis 4:7, ‘Sin crouches at the door’.
The yetzer hara is the Kural’s inner enemy, and the constant remedy of Torah is the constant guarding the Kural commands.
Buddhism
“Whatever harm an enemy may do to an enemy, or a hater to a hater, an ill-directed mind inflicts on oneself a greater harm.”
— Dhammapada 42 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says that no external enemy can hurt you as badly as your own badly directed mind.
Almost exactly the Kural’s claim: one’s own fault is a worse enemy than any outside foe.
Jainism
“Self is the one invincible foe, (together with the four) cardinal passions, (viz. anger, pride, deceit, and greed, they are five) and the (five) senses (make ten). These (foes), O great sage, I have regularly vanquished.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Asked who the thousand foes attacking him are, Gautama answers that the real enemies are within: the unsubdued self, the four passions and the five senses.
Valluvar insists that it is fault itself that is the enemy bringing ruin; Gautama likewise locates the true enemy in one's own faults rather than in any outside attacker.
Sikhism
“Within this body dwell the five thieves: sexual desire, anger, greed, emotional attachment and egotism.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 600, Raag Sorath, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das teaches that the real robbers are not outside but within: the five vices that plunder the treasure of the Naam.
The Kural says fault itself is the enemy that brings ruin and must be guarded against; Sikh teaching likewise locates the true enemy in the five thieves inside the self.
Greek Mythology
Agamemnon, reconciling with Achilles, says his ruinous insult was the work of Atē, the goddess of blind folly, who walks on men’s heads and brings them to harm.
— Homer, Iliad 19.85-94 [10]
Agamemnon admits a fault of his own nearly destroyed the Greek army, and describes it as a goddess of ruin that seized him.
The Iliad personifies fault as a destroying power, as the Kural calls fault an enemy that brings ruin; Agamemnon’s near-defeat came from his own error, not from Troy.
Philosophy
“...know this of a truth - that no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death.”
— Plato, Apology 41d (tr. Jowett) [11]
Facing execution, Socrates insists that nothing outside can truly harm a good person.
If no outside evil can ruin the good, then, as the Kural says, the only real enemy is one’s own fault.

Real Life Example

Sakichi Toyoda builds looms that stop themselves at the first fault
1896-1924 · Japan
In 1896 the inventor Sakichi Toyoda produced a power loom with a device that automatically stopped the machine when a weft thread broke, so that it would not go on weaving defective cloth. His Type G automatic loom of 1924 included stopping devices for broken warp or weft threads among its many automatic mechanisms. Toyota later named this principle jidoka, stopping immediately when an abnormality is detected, and made it one of the two pillars of the Toyota Production System.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Toyoda's idea was that a fault caught at once costs little, while a fault left running multiplies into waste, and the principle became central to modern quality control.
The kural tells us to make guarding against faults our chief aim because fault itself is the enemy, and Toyoda built that vigilance into the very machine.
