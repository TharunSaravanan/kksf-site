Kural 725

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

ஆற்றின் அளவறிந்து கற்க அவையஞ்சா மாற்றங் கொடுத்தற் பொருட்டு.
Aatrin alavarindhu karka avaiyanjaa Maatrang koduththar poruttu.

Meaning

Study the right way to reason and argue, so that you can answer questions in front of a group without fear.

Elementary

If you want to answer tricky questions without getting nervous, practise thinking things through step by step. When you know how to reason, a hard question doesn’t scare you.

University

Parimelazhagar reads āṟṟin aḷavu aṟintu kaṟka as “learn, by the proper path (of grammar), the aḷavai-nūl,” that is, the science of pramāṇa (means of valid knowledge), or logic. Solomon Pappaiah paraphrases it as tarka śāstra, the logic of proofs, and Kalaignar as grammar together with logic. Māṟṟam koṭuttal means “giving a reply,” so the purpose of this study is avai añcā, not fearing the assembly, above all when being cross-questioned. Some modern readers take āṟṟin aḷavu more loosely as “the measure of one’s ability” or “the scope of the subject.” The sandhi text “ஆற்றின் னளவறிந்து” shows a metrical doubling, and the words are the same.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Nyaya Sutra opens by listing sixteen subjects whose knowledge leads to the highest good. They include the means of valid knowledge (pramana), the parts of a formal argument, discussion (vada), wrangling, cavil, fallacies, and the ‘points of defeat’ at which a debater loses.
— Nyaya Sutra of Gautama 1.1.1 [3]
Indian logic was built largely as a discipline for winning and judging debates in learned assemblies.
This is the aḷavai (pramāṇa) learning that Parimelazhagar says one must master in order to answer fearlessly.
Christianity
“be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear:”
— 1 Peter 3:15 (KJV) [4]
Peter tells believers to be prepared at any time to explain and defend their faith.
Being ready to give a reasoned answer is the Kural’s māṟṟam koṭuttal.
Islam
“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best.”
— Qur’an 16:125 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an tells the believer to argue in the best way, which assumes skill in reasoning and debate.
Like the Kural, it treats well-trained argument as the way to face opponents.
Judaism
“Be diligent in the study of the Torah; And know how to answer an epicuros”
— Pirkei Avot 2:14 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar tells students to study hard so that they can reply to a skeptic.
This is the Kural almost word for word in purpose: study so that you can give an answer.
Buddhism
“In the same way, when astute aristocrats, brahmins, householders, or ascetics come to see me with a question already planned, the answer just strikes me on the spot.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 58, Abhayarājakumāra Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha compares himself to a charioteer who knows every part of a chariot. Because his understanding is thorough, answers come to him at once, even to questions prepared in advance.
Thorough learning is what makes a ready, fearless reply possible, which is the Kural’s argument.
Jainism
“The knowledge (of the seven categories) is attained by means of pramāṇa and naya.”
— Tattvartha Sutra 1.6, with Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Umasvati makes valid means of knowledge (pramana) and partial standpoints (naya) the method for knowing reality, and Pujyapada's commentary adds that pramana is 'for oneself' and 'for others', with knowledge put into words serving as pramana for others.
Parimelazhagar reads this Kural as urging study of the science of pramana, logic, so as to answer without fear in an assembly; Jainism makes that same science of pramana and naya the basis of all sound knowledge and argument.
Sikhism
“Contemplate and reflect upon knowledge, and you will become a benefactor to others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 356, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak values learning that has been reflected on (vichar), because it equips a person to serve others.
The Kural tells the student to learn the method of reasoning properly so as to answer fearlessly in assembly; Guru Nanak too asks for learning that is reflected on and put to use.
Greek Mythology
“You knew nothing neither of war nor of the arts whereby men make their mark in council, and he sent me with you to train you in all excellence of speech and action.”
— Homer, Iliad 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Phoenix reminds Achilles that he was sent to train him in speaking in council as well as in fighting.
Greek heroes also had to be trained for the assembly, which is the kind of preparation the Kural recommends.
Philosophy
In his essay on studies, Bacon says that reading makes a full man, conference (discussion) makes a ready man, and writing makes an exact man.
— Francis Bacon, Essays, “Of Studies” [11]
Bacon holds that practice in discussion is what makes a person quick and ready in reply.
Disciplined preparation is what produces a ready reply, as the Kural argues.

Real Life Example

Thurgood Marshall, trained at Howard, wins Brown v. Board of Education
1933 to 1954 · Howard University Law School and the U.S. Supreme Court, Washington, D.C., United States
Thurgood Marshall graduated first in his class from Howard University Law School in 1933, where his mentor Charles Hamilton Houston encouraged students to use the law for social change. Marshall went on to argue thirty-two cases before the U.S. Supreme Court and win twenty-nine of them. The most famous was Brown v. Board of Education, decided in 1954, in which the Court declared racial segregation in public schools unconstitutional.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Marshall's long training in legal reasoning allowed him to argue repeatedly and successfully before the highest court in the land.
The kural urges study of the proper method of reasoning so that one can answer in an assembly without fear, and Marshall's disciplined legal training made him a fearless advocate before the Supreme Court.
