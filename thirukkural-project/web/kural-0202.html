Kural 202

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

தீயவை தீய பயத்தலால் தீயவை தீயினும் அஞ்சப் படும்.
Theeyavai theeya payaththalaal theeyavai, Theeyinum anjap padum.

Meaning

Bad deeds lead to more bad things, so we should fear bad deeds even more than we fear fire.

Elementary

Fire can burn you, so you keep away from it. But a bad deed is even worse than fire — one bad deed leads to another bad deed, and then another, like sparks starting more fires. So we should be even more careful about doing bad things than about touching fire.

University

The couplet is built on a deliberate pun: தீ means both “evil” and “fire,” and தீயவை (“evil things”) sounds like “fiery things.” Valluvar’s argument is comparative — fire burns only what it touches and can be put out, but evil propagates, generating consequences for both the victim and the doer (Pulavar Kesigan: ‘to others and to oneself’). Evil is therefore self-replicating in a way fire only resembles. The same insight — that wrongdoing breeds more wrongdoing — appears in the rabbinic dictum that ‘one transgression leads to another’ and in Aeschylus’s image of the evil deed that begets its own offspring.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Thus the wise living entity’s pure consciousness becomes covered by his eternal enemy in the form of lust, which is never satisfied and which burns like fire.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.39 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [4]
Krishna names desire (kāma) as the root that drives people to sin, likening it to a fire that only grows the more it is fed.
Both texts use fire as the image of evil’s self-feeding growth; the Kural goes further, saying evil is worse than fire.
Christianity
“Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death.”
— James 1:15 (KJV) [5]
James describes a chain of generation: desire gives birth to sin, and sin, fully grown, gives birth to death.
Same logic as ‘evil yields evil’ — wrongdoing is fertile and its offspring are destructive.
Islam
“Indeed, those who devour the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming into their bellies fire. And they will be burned in a Blaze.”
— Qur’an 4:10 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an describes an evil deed as literally being fire in disguise: the unjust gain that seems like nourishment is burning the wrongdoer from within.
Like Valluvar, it equates evil with fire — and implies that evil is the more dangerous because it is not recognized as fire.
Judaism
“For one commandment leads to another commandment, and transgression leads to another transgression; For the reward for performing a commandment is another commandment and the reward for committing a transgression is a transgression.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:2 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Ben Azzai observes that sin has momentum: each transgression makes the next one easier, so the ‘wage’ of sin is simply more sin.
Exactly the Kural’s reasoning — evil is to be feared because it produces more evil.
Buddhism
“Truly, an evil deed committed does not immediately bear fruit, like milk that does not turn sour all at once. But smoldering, it follows the fool like fire covered by ashes.”
— Dhammapada 71 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha compares an unripened evil deed to embers hidden under ash — quiet for now, but still burning and certain to scorch the one who carries it.
Fire and evil are fused in one image, and the danger is precisely that evil keeps burning after the act.
Jainism
“Even such a thing as ghee when approached by the flame of fire will cause intolerable pain by fierce burning; of many bad actions will they become guilty who, though not crooked, become so, and associate with those who are bad.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 13 ('The fear of misconduct'), st. 4 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [9]
In its chapter titled 'The fear of misconduct', the Tamil Jain Nālaṭiyār uses the image of fire to warn that one wrong step, here keeping bad company, draws a person into many more bad actions.
Valluvar says evil should be feared more than fire because it breeds more evil; the Nālaṭiyār also pairs fire with the way wrongdoing multiplies.
Sikhism
“Virtue and vice do not come by mere words; actions repeated, over and over again, are engraved on the soul. You shall harvest what you plant.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 4, Japji Sahib, Pauri 20, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak teaches that each deed leaves its mark on the soul, so that repeated actions shape a person and bear their own harvest.
The Kural says evil must be feared more than fire because evil gives rise to further evil; Guru Nanak likewise shows deeds engraving themselves and growing into their own crop.
Greek Mythology
“It is the evil deed that afterwards begets more iniquity like its own breed; but when a house is righteous, the lot of its children is blessed always.”
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 757–760 (trans. H. W. Smyth) [11]
The chorus of Agamemnon reflects on the curse of the house of Atreus, in which each crime (Atreus’s feast, Iphigenia’s sacrifice) breeds the next murder.
The same principle as ‘evil yields evil’, dramatized across generations.
Philosophy
In the Gorgias, Socrates defends the startling thesis that doing injustice is the greatest of evils — worse even than suffering it — because it corrupts the soul of the doer.
— Plato, Gorgias 469b ff. (paraphrase) [12]
For Socrates, physical harms like burns or wounds damage only the body, while wrongdoing damages the soul, the most precious thing a person has.
Parallels the Kural’s ranking: evil is to be feared more than fire because its harm is deeper and self-inflicted.

Real Life Example

The Watergate cover-up brings down President Nixon
17 June 1972 - 9 August 1974 · Washington, D.C., United States
After men connected to President Richard Nixon's re-election committee were caught breaking into the Democratic National Committee offices in the Watergate complex in June 1972, Nixon and his aides worked to hide the White House's links to the break-in. A recording of 23 June 1972 captured Nixon approving a plan to have the CIA ask the FBI to halt its investigation. When the Supreme Court ordered the tapes released, this conversation became public in August 1974, his remaining support collapsed, and he resigned on 9 August 1974, the only US president to do so.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Watergate is the classic example of a cover-up doing more damage than the original wrongdoing, as each new concealment created new offences.
The kural says evil must be feared more than fire because it gives birth to further evil, and the Watergate break-in led step by step to obstruction of justice and the downfall of a presidency.
