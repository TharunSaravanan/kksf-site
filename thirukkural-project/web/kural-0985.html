Kural 985

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

ஆற்றுவார் ஆற்றல் பணிதல் அதுசான்றோர் மாற்றாரை மாற்றும் படை.
Aatruvaar aatral panidhal adhusaandror maatraarai maatrum padai.

Meaning

The strength of capable people is their modesty. It is the weapon good people use to turn their enemies into friends.

Elementary

When someone is cross with you, shouting back usually makes it worse. But if you speak gently and say sorry, the angry person often calms down and even becomes your friend. Valluvar says being humble is the strongest tool a good person has.

University

The couplet plays on the root aatru (ஆற்று, 'to accomplish, to be able'): aatruvaar aatral (ஆற்றுவார் ஆற்றல்), 'the power of those who can achieve', is panidhal (பணிதல், 'bending, humility'). In the second line maatraar (மாற்றார், 'adversaries') are 'changed' (maatrum, மாற்றும்) by this padai (படை, 'weapon, army'), a deliberate pun. Most modern commentators (Varadarasanar, Karunanidhi) read the second line as humility turning enemies away from enmity; Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan, nearer the older commentarial line of Parimelazhagar, read the first line more concretely as the achiever's skill in winning over helpers by bowing to them. Pope's 'Submission is the might of men of mighty acts; the sage / With that same weapon stills his foeman's rage' captures both senses. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Humility; pridelessness; nonviolence; tolerance; simplicity; approaching a bona fide spiritual master; cleanliness; steadiness; self-control; renunciation of the objects of sense gratification; absence of false ego; ... all these I declare to be knowledge, and besides this whatever there may be is ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 13.8-12 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna places humility and pridelessness first in his list of the marks of true knowledge.
Like the Kural, it makes humility the first strength of the accomplished person.
Christianity
“A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger.”
— Proverbs 15:1 (KJV) [4]
The proverb observes that gentleness defuses anger while harshness inflames it.
This is the Kural's claim that humility is the weapon that turns adversaries from hostility.
Islam
“And not equal are the good deed and the bad. Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend.”
— Qur'an 41:34 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an teaches that answering hostility with something better can turn an enemy into a close friend.
This matches the Kural's claim that the noble person's weapon changes enemies.
Judaism
Avot de-Rabbi Natan expands the Mishnah's question 'Who is mighty?' by answering that the mightiest of the mighty is the one who makes an enemy into a friend.
— Avot de-Rabbi Natan (Version A) 23 [6]
Rabbinic tradition redefines strength as the power to win over one's adversary.
This is close to the Kural's idea of the noble person's power to change enemies.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that enmity ends only when it is met without enmity.
Like the Kural, it says the way to end hostility is not counter-force but a gentler disposition.
Jainism
“Bravo! you have conquered anger; bravo! you have vanquished pride; bravo! you have banished delusion; bravo! you have subdued greed. Bravo for your simplicity, O saint! bravo for your humility, O saint! bravo for your perfect patience! bravo for your perfect liberation!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.56-57 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
After testing King Nami, who had renounced his throne, the god Sakra praises him for conquering pride and for his humility and patience.
Nami's victory, won by humility rather than armies, parallels the Kural's view that humility is the true strength of the capable.
Sikhism
“Humility is my spiked club. My dagger is to be the dust of all men’s feet. No evil-doer can withstand these weapons.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 628, Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan calls humility his weapon: the club and dagger of the Gurmukh are lowliness and being the dust of everyone's feet, and no evil-doer can stand against them.
The Kural calls humility the strength of the capable and the weapon by which the noble turn enemies from enmity, and Guru Arjan gives the very same image of humility as an unbeatable weapon.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop's fable of the North Wind and the Sun, the Wind tries to strip a traveller's cloak by force and only makes him clutch it tighter, while the Sun's gentle warmth makes him take it off willingly.
— Aesop's Fables, The North Wind and the Sun (Perry Index 46) [10]
The fable teaches that gentleness achieves what force cannot.
Like the Kural, it presents a gentle, unforced approach as the stronger weapon.
Philosophy
“That whereby the rivers and seas are able to receive the homage and tribute of all the valley streams, is their skill in being lower than they;--it is thus that they are the kings of them all.”
— Laozi, Tao Te Ching 66 (trans. James Legge) [11]
Laozi teaches that the sea rules the streams because it lies lower than they do, and that the sage leads by placing himself below others.
This is the Kural's paradox in Daoist form: lowliness is the source of real power.

Real Life Example

Willy Brandt kneels at the Warsaw Ghetto memorial
7 December 1970 · Warsaw, Poland
On 7 December 1970, during a visit to Poland to sign the Treaty of Warsaw, West German Chancellor Willy Brandt asked to lay a wreath at the Monument to the Ghetto Heroes. After laying it he knelt silently before the memorial, a gesture he later said had not been planned. The gesture was controversial in West Germany at the time, but the Norwegian Nobel Committee cited it when awarding him the 1971 Nobel Peace Prize, and it became a lasting symbol of German-Polish reconciliation.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A head of government humbling himself before the victims of his nation's crimes did more to open the way to reconciliation with a former enemy than any show of strength could have done.
The kural calls humility the true strength of the capable and the weapon that turns enemies from enmity, and Brandt's kneeling helped change relations between peoples long divided by war.
