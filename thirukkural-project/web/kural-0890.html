Kural 890

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

உடம்பாடு இலாதவர் வாழ்க்கை குடங்கருள் பாம்போடு உடனுறைந் தற்று.
Utampaatu ilaadhavar vaazhkkai kutangarul paampotu utanurain thatru.

Meaning

Living with people who do not truly agree with you is like sharing a small hut with a snake.

Elementary

Imagine sleeping in a tiny tent with a snake curled up in the corner. You could never relax, because you would always be wondering when it might bite. Living with someone who secretly dislikes you feels the same way.

University

The closing couplet gives the chapter its most vivid image. Utampaatu ilaadhavar (உடம்பாடு இலாதவர்) are 'those without inner agreement', people who share one's life without sharing one's mind. To live with them is kutangarul paampotu utanurainthatru (குடங்கருள் பாம்போடு உடனுறைந்தற்று), 'like dwelling together with a snake inside a kutangar'. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah gloss kutangar as a small hut (குடிசை), and Kalaignar stresses the moment-by-moment fear of such a life. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as a dark cave, an interpretive variant. Pope's 'dwelling in a shed with snake for company' and thirukkural.net's 'share a hut with a cobra' agree on the hut reading. The text is identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“internal troubles like the fear from a lurking snake are more serious than external troubles.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book IX (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya advises a king to deal with internal danger first, comparing it to a snake hidden close by.
The Arthashastra and the Kural use the same image: an internal enemy is a snake inside one's own dwelling.
Christianity
“Can two walk together, except they be agreed?”
— Amos 3:3 (KJV) [4]
The prophet Amos asks a rhetorical question: true companionship requires real agreement.
Both texts hold that a shared life without inner accord cannot be safe or real.
Islam
The Prophet said that a believer is not stung twice from the same hole.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6133 [5]
The hadith uses the image of a snake or scorpion hole to teach vigilance against a known source of harm.
It shares the Kural's snake imagery and its counsel to stay alert to danger close at hand.
Judaism
“Better is a dry morsel, and quietness therewith, than an house full of sacrifices with strife.”
— Proverbs 17:1 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs values a peaceful home with little over a rich home full of conflict.
Both texts hold that sharing a home without harmony makes life unbearable.
Buddhism
“Should a seeker not find a companion who is better or equal, let him resolutely pursue a solitary course; there is no fellowship with the fool.”
— Dhammapada 61 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada advises going alone rather than living with an unsuitable companion.
Like the Kural, it treats companionship without real accord as worse than solitude.
Jainism
“For those who are wise, to become companions of the wicked is like the nâgâ playing with the female cobra.”
— Nāladiyār 24.10 (English translation serialised in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The closing quatrain of the Nāladiyār's chapter on improper friendship says that for a wise person, keeping close company with the wicked is as dangerous as sporting with a cobra.
The Kural likens living with those who do not share one's mind to sharing a small hut with a snake, and the Jain Tamil quatrain uses the same serpent image for intimacy with the ill-disposed.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, do not associate with the faithless cynics; run far away from them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Kabeer, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Kabir, whose verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, warns against close company with those cut off from God, whose influence is harmful.
The Kural compares living with people who do not truly agree with you to sharing a hut with a snake, and Kabir's counsel is to keep away from such company.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop's fable, a farmer finds a snake frozen with cold, warms it in his bosom, and is bitten to death once it revives.
— Aesop's Fables, The Farmer and the Snake (Perry 176) [9]
The fable warns that taking a hostile nature into one's closest space invites a deadly bite.
It is the Kural's snake in the hut: closeness with a hostile companion is mortal danger.
Philosophy
Seneca advises Lucilius to avoid crowds and keep company with those who will make him better, because close association with others' vices does harm.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 7 [10]
The Stoic letter treats one's companions as a decisive influence on peace of mind and character.
Like the Kural, it warns that sharing life with the wrong people costs one's safety and peace.

Real Life Example

The First Triumvirate of Caesar, Pompey and Crassus
60-49 BCE · Rome, Roman Republic
Around 60 BCE Julius Caesar, Pompey and Crassus formed an unofficial and at first secret political alliance, now called the First Triumvirate, each seeking his own ends. The partnership was renewed at Luca in 56 BCE, but the death of Julia, Caesar's daughter and Pompey's wife, in 54 BCE and of Crassus in 53 BCE removed its bonds, and Pompey drew closer to Caesar's opponents in the Senate. In January 49 BCE the Senate decreed a state of emergency against Caesar, and he crossed the Rubicon, beginning a civil war in which Pompey was defeated and killed.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The three men shared power without sharing purpose, and their uneasy partnership ended in war between two of them.
The kural likens living with those who lack inner agreement to sharing a hut with a snake, which describes the alliance that ended with Caesar and Pompey at war.
