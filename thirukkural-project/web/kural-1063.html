Kural 1063

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

இன்மை இடும்பை இரந்துதீர் வாமென்னும் வன்மையின் வன்பாட்ட தில்.
Inmai idumbai irandhudheer vaamennum vanmaiyin vanpaatta thil.

Meaning

Nothing is harsher than the idea that you can end the pain of being poor by begging instead of working.

Elementary

If your bike breaks, you could keep asking friends to lend you theirs, or you could learn to fix your own. The Kural says choosing to ask forever instead of fixing things yourself is the worst choice of all.

University

The couplet plays on vanmai (வன்மை), 'hardness, toughness, boldness': the 'toughness' of deciding inmai idumbai (இன்மை இடும்பை, 'the affliction of want') will be ended by asking (irandhu theervaam) is called harder than any hardness (vanmaiyin vanpaattadhu il). Following Parimelazhagar's line, Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar supply the implied contrast with effort (muyarchi): the fault is abandoning work and choosing to live by others' gifts. Solomon Pappaiah adds the parenthesis '(without working it off)', and Pope's explanation speaks of remedying poverty 'by begging (rather than by working)'. Pope renders it 'Nothing is harder than the hardness that will say, / 'The plague of penury by asking alms we'll drive away'.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna to perform his prescribed work, because action is better than inaction and even the maintenance of the body cannot be achieved without work.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.8 (cf. Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) (paraphrase) [3]
The Gita holds that no one can live, even physically, without doing one's own work.
The Kural applies the same principle: the remedy for want is one's own work, not living off others.
Christianity
“Neither did we eat any man's bread for nought; but wrought with labour and travail night and day, that we might not be chargeable to any of you:”
— 2 Thessalonians 3:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul says he and his companions worked night and day so as not to live off anyone's bread.
Like the Kural, Paul treats working, not relying on others' bread, as the answer to need.
Islam
“It is better for anyone of you to take a rope (and cut) and bring a bundle of wood (from the forest) over his back and sell it and Allah will save his face (from the Hell-Fire) because of that, rather than to ask the people who may give him or not.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1471 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet says that even hauling firewood to sell is better than asking people for help.
This is the Kural's exact contrast: remove poverty by honest labour, however humble, not by begging.
Judaism
“And anyone who does not need to take [charity] and yet takes, will not depart from this world before he actually needs [charity] from others.”
— Mishnah Peah 8:9 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah warns that whoever takes charity without real need will end up truly needing it.
The Mishnah, like the Kural, condemns choosing to live by others' gifts instead of one's own means.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta, the Buddha lists the dangers of idleness, such as refusing to work because it is too cold, too hot, too early or too late, so that new wealth is not earned and existing wealth runs out.
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha warns lay people that excuses for not working lead to poverty.
The Kural's target is the same: the person who gives up effort and expects poverty to be solved some other way.
Jainism
“Since it is a means of removing the affliction of poverty for one to beg for himself, then let affliction be my portion, and let precious wealth depart from me.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 6 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet would rather keep his poverty than remove it by begging.
This answers the Kural's very phrase inmai idumbai irandhu theervaam: the noble refuse to cure the affliction of want by asking.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself a guru or a spiritual teacher, while he goes around begging - don't ever touch his feet. One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Sarang, Sarang ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Sarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak refuses respect to the holy man who lives by begging and praises the one who earns his food by work and shares part of it. This is the basis of kirat karni (honest work) and vand chhakna (sharing).
The Kural calls it the hardest folly to think poverty can be cured by begging instead of effort; Guru Nanak likewise rejects living by begging and points to honest labour as the true path.
Greek Mythology
“Work the work which the gods ordained for men, lest in bitter anguish of spirit you with your wife and children seek your livelihood amongst your neighbours, and they do not heed you.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 397-400 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells his idle brother Perses to work, or he will end up begging from neighbours who will stop listening.
Hesiod, like the Kural, rejects begging as a remedy for want and insists on work.
Philosophy
In Xenophon's Memorabilia, Socrates advises Aristarchus, whose house is full of destitute kinswomen, to set them to useful work such as making clothes, and the household is soon both fed and happier.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.7 (paraphrase) [11]
Socrates' answer to a household's poverty is productive work, not dependence or borrowing.
The Kural likewise insists that want should be met by effort, not by asking.

Real Life Example

South Korea launches the Saemaul Undong self-help movement
1970-1979 · Republic of Korea
In 1970 South Korea launched Saemaul Undong, the New Village Movement, a rural development campaign built on the principles of diligence, self-help and cooperation, in which villagers worked together to improve their own communities. In 2013 UNESCO inscribed the movement's archives on its Memory of the World Register, noting that the campaign helped Korea move from being one of the world's poorest countries toward becoming a major economy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The movement is widely studied as an example of a poor country reducing want through organised local effort, and its methods have been shared with other developing nations.
The kural says there is no greater folly than thinking the misery of want can be cured by begging instead of effort, and Saemaul Undong was founded on curing rural poverty through villagers' own work.
