Kural 258

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

செயிரின் தலைப்பிரிந்த காட்சியார் உண்ணார் உயிரின் தலைப்பிரிந்த ஊன்.
Seyirin thalaippirindha kaatchiyaar unnaar, Uyirin thalaippirindha oon.

Meaning

People who see clearly, free of confusion and fault, will not eat the flesh of a body that has lost its life.

Elementary

When your eyes are clear, you see things as they really are. People whose minds are clear and free from wrong ideas look at meat and see a body that was once alive — so they don’t eat it.

University

The couplet is built on an elegant repetition: தலைப்பிரிந்த (“separated from”) occurs in both lines — the wise are separated from செயிர் (fault, delusion), while the flesh is separated from உயிர் (life). The parallel suggests a moral symmetry: purity of vision is achieved by the same act of separation that, done to a living being, produces flesh. காட்சியார் (“those of vision”) links the virtue to perception, continuing kural 257’s theme that seeing clearly ends the desire for meat. Parimelazhagar glosses செயிர் as the delusion (mayakkam) that makes one mistake the body for the self, placing this couplet within the ascetic epistemology of the துறவறவியல்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is beyond duality and doubt, whose mind is engaged within, who is always busy working for the welfare of all sentient beings, and who is free from all sins, achieves liberation in the Supreme.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.25 [3]
The Gita describes sages whose impurities are destroyed (kṣīṇa-kalmaṣāḥ) and who delight in the welfare of all beings (sarva-bhūta-hite ratāḥ).
The Kural’s “those freed from fault” are the same figures, and their concern for all beings excludes eating them.
Christianity
“But let all except the very weak and the sick abstain altogether from eating the flesh of four-footed animals.”
— Rule of St Benedict, ch. 39 [4]
St Benedict’s monastic rule forbids monks the meat of four-footed animals, a practice kept for centuries in Western monasticism.
As in the Kural, those who commit to purity of life and vision renounce flesh.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not kill game while you are in the state of ihram.”
— Quran 5:95 (Sahih International) [5]
Pilgrims in the consecrated state of ihram are forbidden to hunt or kill game.
Islam links a heightened state of purity with refraining from killing animals — a limited echo of the Kural’s pure-sighted who refuse flesh.
Judaism
The Talmud records Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi’s teaching that an ignoramus is prohibited from eating meat, deriving from Leviticus 11:46 (“This is the torah of the beast and of the fowl”) that only one who engages in Torah study may eat animals and fowl.
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 49b [6]
Rabbinic tradition ties the permission to eat meat to spiritual knowledge and discipline.
Both texts connect meat and wisdom — but in opposite directions: the Talmud limits meat to the learned; the Kural says the truly wise abstain.
Buddhism
“He is not noble who injures living beings. He is called noble because he is harmless towards all living beings.”
— Dhammapada 270 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha defines the noble one (ariya) by harmlessness toward all beings.
Like the Kural, spiritual excellence is defined by refusing to harm living creatures.
Jainism
“You are irreligious, unworthy men, devoted to foolish pleasures, who say that partaking heartily of this meat you are not soiled by sin. All who partake of such food, commit sins in their ignorance; but the wise do nothing of the kind.”
— Sutrakritanga II.6.38-39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Ardraka rejects the claim that one can eat meat without moral stain, saying meat-eating comes from ignorance and the wise do not do it.
Valluvar says those whose vision is free of delusion will not eat lifeless flesh; Ardraka likewise contrasts the ignorance of meat-eaters with the wise, who abstain.
Sikhism
“The fools argue about flesh and meat, but they know nothing about meditation and spiritual wisdom. What is called meat, and what is called green vegetables? What leads to sin?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1289, Vaar Malaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Vaar Malaar Guru Nanak mocks those who make meat-eating the test of holiness while knowing nothing of meditation or spiritual wisdom.
The Kural says those whose vision is free of delusion will not eat flesh; Guru Nanak agrees that clear vision is what matters but places it in spiritual wisdom, not in avoiding meat.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod’s golden race of mortals lived “like gods,” free from toil and grief, while the fruitful earth gave them its harvest unforced; later Pythagorean writers took this age as a model of bloodless living.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 109-120 [10]
The first, purest human race lived on what the earth freely gave.
The purest humans of myth, like the Kural’s fault-free sages, live without killing for food.
Philosophy
“Can you really ask what reason Pythagoras had for abstaining from flesh? For my part I rather wonder both by what accident and in what state of soul or mind the first man who did so, touched his mouth to gore”
— Plutarch, Moralia, On the Eating of Flesh I.1 (993A-B; Loeb trans.) [11]
Plutarch reverses the burden of proof: it is not the abstainer but the meat-eater whose state of mind needs explaining.
Matches the Kural’s assumption that a clear, undeluded mind naturally refuses flesh.

Real Life Example

Plutarch delivers his discourses On the Eating of Flesh
late 1st century CE · Boeotia, Greece (Roman Empire)
The Greek writer Plutarch composed two discourses, On the Eating of Flesh, preserved in his Moralia, which argue for abstaining from meat. He asked why people find it strange that Pythagoras refused flesh, argued that the human body is not built like a carnivore's, and condemned the cruelty of killing animals for luxurious meals. The Loeb editors note that these speeches probably date from his youth.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Plutarch's essay is one of the earliest surviving arguments in the West that clear thinking about what meat is should lead a person to refuse it.
The kural says those whose vision is freed from delusion will not eat flesh separated from life, and Plutarch argued that only habit and blindness keep people eating it.
