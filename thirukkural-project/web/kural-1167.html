Kural 1167

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

காமக் கடும்புனல் நீந்திக் கரைகாணேன் யாமத்தும் யானே உளேன்.
Kaamak kadumpunal neendhik karaikaanaen yaamaththum yaanae ulaen.

Meaning

I swim the wild flood of love but see no shore. Even in the middle of the night, I am the only one awake.

Elementary

Imagine swimming and swimming in a huge lake at night, looking for the edge, and never finding it, while everyone else is fast asleep in bed. That is how the wife feels: her missing-him feeling seems to have no end, and at midnight she is the only one still awake.

University

Kaṭum puṉal (கடும்புனல்) is a ‘fierce flood’ or torrent, extending the sea of 1164 and 1166 into a current she must swim; karai kāṇēṉ (கரைகாணேன்) is ‘I see no shore’. Yāmam (யாமம்) is the midnight watch, and yāṉē uḷēṉ (யானே உளேன்), ‘I alone am’, is read by all four modern glossators as ‘I alone remain awake’, while Pope’s commentary-based prose adds ‘still I live’, catching the other shade of uḷēṉ (‘I exist, I survive’). The couplet introduces the night theme that the next two couplets develop. Pope’s verse: ‘I swim the cruel tide of love, and can no shore descry, / In watches of the night, too, ’mid the waters, only I!’ The text is identical on both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Nala abandons Damayanti while she sleeps in the forest, she wakes alone in the night, searches for him in vain, and wanders through the wilderness crying out his name.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase) [3]
Damayanti’s night of waking to find herself alone is one of the great scenes of separation in the Mahabharata.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Damayanti is the one left awake and alone in the night, with no end to her searching in sight.
Christianity
“Deep calleth unto deep at the noise of thy waterspouts: all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me.”
— Psalms 42:7 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist, longing for God, feels himself overwhelmed by wave after wave.
The Kural’s heroine also feels herself under a flood of longing that has no end.
Islam
“Or [they are] like darknesses within an unfathomable sea which is covered by waves, upon which are waves, over which are clouds - darknesses, some of them upon others. When one puts out his hand [therein], he can hardly see it.”
— Qur’an 24:40 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes a person without light as lost in a deep, dark sea, wave on wave, with nothing visible.
The heroine’s midnight flood with no shore in sight is the same picture of being lost in dark waters.
Judaism
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth; I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Songs 3:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved in the Song lies awake at night searching for her absent lover without finding him.
This is the Kural’s heroine at midnight: awake, searching, and unable to reach the end of her longing.
Buddhism
“The misguided man in whom the thirty-six currents of craving strongly rush toward pleasurable objects, is swept away by the flood of his passionate thoughts.”
— Dhammapada 339 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada pictures craving as a set of currents that carry the mind away like a flood.
The heroine’s ‘fierce flood of love’ is this same current, and she, too, finds no shore.
Jainism
“The flood is old age and death, which carry away living beings; Law is the island, the firm ground, the refuge, the most excellent shelter.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.68 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Asked whether there is any firm ground for beings swept off by the great flood, Gautama names the Law as the island.
The heroine swims a flood and sees no shore; the Jain text names a shore, but for the flood of birth and death rather than love.
Sikhism
“I cannot endure the night, and sleep does not come, without the Sight of the Beloved Guru's Court.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 97, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan writes of nights that will not end and sleep that will not come while he is parted from his Beloved Guru; Sikh tradition holds that these verses were sent as letters to his father, Guru Ram Das, while he was kept away in Lahore.
The Kural's heroine swims a flood of love that has no shore and lies alone awake through the midnight watch; Guru Arjan likewise describes the sleepless night of one parted from the beloved.
Greek Mythology
Leander swims the Hellespont each night to reach Hero, guided by her lamp, until one stormy night the lamp is blown out and he drowns before reaching the shore.
— Ovid, Heroides 18-19 (Leander and Hero) (paraphrase) [10]
The story of Hero and Leander makes love literally a night swim through a dangerous sea.
The Kural’s heroine swims love’s flood at midnight and sees no shore, just as Leander fails to reach his.
Philosophy
In The Anatomy of Melancholy, Robert Burton lists among the symptoms of love-melancholy that lovers cannot sleep, but lie awake through the night sighing and thinking of the absent beloved.
— Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy, Part 3, Section 2 (Love-Melancholy) (paraphrase) [11]
Burton’s encyclopaedic study treats sleeplessness as a classic sign of love-sickness.
The Kural’s heroine, alone and awake at midnight, shows exactly this symptom.

Real Life Example

Pliny the Younger's letter to his absent wife Calpurnia
early 2nd century CE · Rome, Italy
The Roman senator and author Pliny the Younger wrote a short letter to his wife Calpurnia while she was away from Rome. He told her that he missed her intensely, that he stayed awake through much of the night thinking of her, and that at the hours when he used to visit her his feet carried him to her room, only for him to turn away from the empty doorway. He added that his only relief was his work in the law courts.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Pliny's letter is one of the earliest surviving descriptions, in a husband's own words, of sleepless longing for an absent spouse.
The kural's heroine swims a flood of love with no shore in sight and is the only one awake in the middle of the night, which is the state Pliny described in his letter.
