Kural 1171

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

கண்தாம் கலுழ்வ தெவன்கொலோ தண்டாநோய் தாம்காட்ட யாம்கண் டது.
Kandhaam kaluzhva thevan-koloa thandaanoi thaamkaatta yaamkan dadhu.

Meaning

This endless love-sickness came only because my eyes showed him to me. So why do those same eyes now cry and beg to see him?

Elementary

Imagine you begged your friend to let you hold their puppy, and then you cried when it was time to give it back. Your friend might say, ‘But you were the one who wanted to hold it!’ The lady in this couplet scolds her own eyes the same way: they were the ones who looked at him first, so why are they crying now?

University

Chapter 118 continues the karpiyal (married love) sequence on separation, and its whole conceit is that the heroine turns on her own eyes: kaṇ (eye) plus vituppu (eager longing, impatience) plus aḻital (wasting away), i.e. the eyes are worn out by their own craving to see the absent husband. In the traditional reading associated with Parimelazhagar she speaks to her confidante (tōḻi), half-blaming, half-pitying the eyes as if they were separate persons. Kaluḻvatu (கலுழ்வது) is ‘weeping’, taṇṭā nōy (தண்டாநோய்) ‘the disease that does not abate’, and tām kāṭṭa yām kaṇṭatu ‘we saw it because they showed it’: the eyes are both cause and complainant. Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar sharpen the irony by making the eyes weep to be shown him again, while Pope renders: ‘They showed me him, and then my endless pain / I saw: why then should weeping eyes complain?’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As a strong wind sweeps away a boat on the water, even one of the roaming senses on which the mind focuses can carry away a man’s intelligence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.67 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna warns that a single sense, once the mind follows it, can drag the whole person off course.
The heroine’s complaint is exactly this: one sense, her eyes, carried her into a love-sickness she cannot now steer out of.
Christianity
“And if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell.”
— Matthew 5:29 (KJV) [4]
Jesus speaks of the eye as a member that can lead the whole person into ruin, and so may be treated as an offender.
Like Jesus’ saying, the Kural treats the eye as a culprit separate from the self, the one that ‘caused’ the trouble.
Islam
“The adultery of the eyes is the sight (to gaze at a forbidden thing), the adultery of the tongue is the talk, and the inner self wishes and desires…”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6243 (Book of Asking Permission) [5]
The Prophet describes how desire begins with the eyes’ looking, which the inner self then turns into longing.
The Kural makes the same chain of cause: the eyes looked first, and the heart’s unending longing followed.
Judaism
“The heart and the eyes are the ‘spies’ of the body — they act as its agents for sinning: the eye sees, the heart covets and the body commits the sin.”
— Rashi on Numbers 15:39 (tr. M. Rosenbaum and A.M. Silbermann, via Sefaria) [6]
Rashi, citing the Midrash, explains that the eyes are the body’s scouts: they go first, and the heart’s desire follows what they report.
The heroine likewise names her eyes as the scouts that went ahead and led her heart into its present suffering.
Buddhism
“The eye is aflame. Forms are aflame. Consciousness at the eye is aflame. Contact at the eye is aflame.”
— Adittapariyaya Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 35.28 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Fire Sermon the Buddha teaches that seeing, and everything that arises from the eye’s contact with forms, burns with passion and brings sorrow and lamentation.
The Kural’s heroine discovers the same thing in her own life: what began with the eye’s contact has become a fire of grief.
Jainism
“He who passionately hates (a colour), will at the same moment suffer pain. It is the fault of an undisciplined man that he is annoyed (by a colour); it is not the ‘colour’ itself that annoys him.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that suffering from what the eye perceives comes from one’s own undisciplined attachment or aversion, not from the object seen.
The heroine likewise refuses to blame anyone outside herself: the fault lies with her own eyes, which looked and clung.
Sikhism
“These are the very eyes which used to see the extremely beautiful form of the beloved Lord and satisfying their desire would absorb themselves in the spiritual bliss. ... These are the eyes that used to suffer the most at the time of separation of the Lord, the Master of my life.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabitt Savaiye 669, English translation as given in the BaniDB database [9]
Bhai Gurdas devotes a whole kabitt to the eyes: the same eyes that once feasted on the Beloved's form are the ones that suffer most when He is gone. His Kabitt Savaiye are early exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural's heroine says her eyes showed her the beloved and so brought on her endless sickness, yet now they weep to see him; Bhai Gurdas likewise singles out the eyes as both the first to delight and the worst to suffer.
Greek Mythology
Orpheus wins Eurydice back from the underworld on condition that he not look at her until they reach the upper air, but at the last moment, longing to see her, he turns his eyes and loses her for ever, and then weeps for her.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1-77 (paraphrase) [10]
In Ovid’s telling, it is Orpheus’s own longing look that costs him the beloved he then mourns.
As in the Kural, the eyes that looked out of love become the cause of the grief they then weep over.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that the pleasure of the eye is the beginning of love: no one falls in love who has not first been delighted by the beloved’s appearance, though delight in looks alone is not yet love.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.5 (1167a) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, sight is the starting point of erotic love, which then grows into longing for the beloved’s presence.
The heroine traces her ‘unending disease’ to that same beginning: her eyes’ first delighted look.

Real Life Example

Mozart weeps after Aloysia Weber turns away from him
29 December 1778 · Munich, Bavaria (now Germany)
On his way to Paris in 1777 the young Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart met the Weber family in Mannheim and fell in love with the gifted singer Aloysia Weber, who was his pupil. When he reached Munich on his way home in late 1778, she had turned away from him, though she had once returned some of his affection. In a letter to his father dated 29 December 1778, Mozart wrote that today he could only weep and that his heart was too full of tears; four years later he married her sister Constanze.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mozart's own letter records how a love that began with delight in seeing and hearing Aloysia ended, for the moment, in tears.
The kural asks why the eyes that first showed the heroine her beloved now weep for him, and Mozart's tears in Munich came from the same love his eyes had first welcomed in Mannheim.
