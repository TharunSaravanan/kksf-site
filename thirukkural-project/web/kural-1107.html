Kural 1107

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

தம்மில் இருந்து தமதுபாத்து உண்டற்றால் அம்மா அரிவை முயக்கு.
Thammil irundhu thamadhupaaththu undatraal ammaa arivai muyakku.

Meaning

Holding this lovely young woman feels like living in your own home and eating food you earned yourself, after sharing it with others.

Elementary

Remember how good it feels to eat a meal you helped cook, at your own table, after giving some to your friends? It feels honest and happy and safe. The lover says being close to the one he loves gives that same warm, at-home happiness.

University

The simile draws on the householder ideal of the Arattuppal: tammil iruntu (‘dwelling in one’s own home’), tamatu (‘one’s own’, i.e. what one has earned) pāttu uṇṭaṟṟāl (‘like eating after dividing and sharing it’). Mu. Varadarasanar glosses pāttu as distributing one’s earnings to others before eating, Solomon Pappaiah as sharing with one’s own people, and Puliyur Kesikan more narrowly as eating one’s own portion; all agree that the pleasure is one of independence, rightful ownership and generosity. Ammā is read as am (‘beautiful’) plus māmai (‘the dark-gold complexion praised in Tamil poetry’), and arivai (அரிவை) is a young woman, also the name of one of the seven traditional stages of womanhood. Muyakku (முயக்கு) is ‘embrace’. Pope: ‘As when one eats from household store, with kindly grace / Sharing his meal: such is this golden maid’s embrace.’ Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The devotees of the Lord are released from all kinds of sins because they eat food which is offered first for sacrifice. Others, who prepare food for personal sense enjoyment, verily eat only sin.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.13 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita praises eating only after first offering the food, and condemns cooking only for oneself.
The Kural’s ideal of eating one’s own food only after sharing it, which it uses as the measure of love’s joy, rests on this same ethic.
Christianity
“For thou shalt eat the labour of thine hands: happy shalt thou be, and it shall be well with thee. Thy wife shall be as a fruitful vine by the sides of thine house.”
— Psalm 128:2-3 (KJV) [4]
The psalm joins the happiness of eating one’s own earnings with the blessing of a wife at home.
Both halves of the Kural’s simile, one’s own earned food and the beloved wife, appear together in this verse.
Islam
“Nobody has ever eaten a better meal than that which one has earned by working with one’s own hands.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2072 [5]
The Prophet praises food earned by one’s own work as the best food of all, citing David who lived by his own labour.
The Kural measures love’s joy by the sweetness of one’s own earned food, which this hadith calls the best meal.
Judaism
“Drink waters out of thine own cistern, And running waters out of thine own well... Let thy fountain be blessed; And have joy of the wife of thy youth.”
— Proverbs 5:15, 18 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs compares faithful joy in one’s own wife to drinking from one’s own well.
Like the Kural, Proverbs likens love for one’s own spouse to the satisfaction of using what is rightfully one’s own.
Buddhism
“There is the case where the son of a good family, using the wealth earned through his efforts & enterprise... righteous wealth righteously gained, partakes of his wealth and makes merit... he experiences bliss, he experiences joy.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.62, Anana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells Anathapindika that a householder knows real bliss in enjoying and giving from wealth he has honestly earned.
This is the very pleasure the Kural uses as its measure for the joy of the beloved’s embrace.
Jainism
Among the vows of the Jain householder is atithi-samvibhaga, sharing one’s food and goods with a worthy guest or mendicant before eating oneself.
— Tattvartha Sutra, chapter 7, list of lay vows (tr. Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is) [8]
The Jain layperson is taught to divide what is his own and give a share away as a religious duty.
The Kural’s picture of eating one’s own food only after sharing it reflects this same household ethic, which Jainism made a formal vow.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang, Sarang ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak says the person who lives on honest earnings and shares part of them with others is the one who knows the true path (kirat karni and vand chhakna).
The Kural compares the beloved's embrace to eating one's own earned food in one's own home after sharing it; this is the Sikh ideal of honest work and sharing almost word for word.
Greek Mythology
Ovid tells how the old couple Baucis and Philemon, poor but devoted to each other, share their humble meal in their own cottage with two strangers, who turn out to be Jupiter and Mercury.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 [10]
Their happiness lies in a loving marriage, a small home of their own and a generous table.
The Kural’s simile, the joy of sharing one’s own food at home, is lived out by this loving pair.
Philosophy
Aristotle writes that friendship between husband and wife seems to exist by nature, includes both utility and pleasure, and that the two help each other by putting their own gifts into the common stock.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.12, 1162a (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
For Aristotle a good marriage is a partnership in which each shares what is their own.
The Kural’s joy of sharing what is one’s own is, for Aristotle, built into the friendship of husband and wife.

Real Life Example

Carl Larsson publishes Ett hem (A Home)
1899 · Sundborn, Dalarna, Sweden
In 1889 the painter Carl Larsson and his wife Karin, also an artist, were given a small cottage at Sundborn, which they enlarged and transformed into their family home, with Karin designing and weaving many of its textiles. From 1894 Carl painted everyday life there in a series of watercolours, published in 1899 as the book Ett hem (A Home). The pictures of the family's self-made home became a model for Swedish interior decoration, and the house is now a museum.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The book turned a modest, hand-made family home into a national ideal of comfort and warmth.
The kural compares the beloved's embrace to the contentment of living in one's own home and eating what one has earned after sharing it, and the Larssons' Sundborn home, built and furnished by their own hands and shared with the world through Carl's pictures, shows that contentment in real life.
