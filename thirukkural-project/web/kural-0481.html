Kural 481

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

பகல்வெல்லும் கூகையைக் காக்கை இகல்வெல்லும் வேந்தர்க்கு வேண்டும் பொழுது.
Pakalvellum Kookaiyaik Kaakkai Ikalvellum Vendharkku Ventum Pozhudhu.

Meaning

In the daytime even a crow can beat the stronger owl. In the same way, a king who wants to beat his enemies must wait for the right time.

Elementary

An owl is bigger and fiercer than a crow, but owls can hardly see in bright daylight. So in the daytime a crow can chase an owl away. If you pick the moment when you are at your best and the other side is at its weakest, even a small player can win.

University

The opening couplet grounds the chapter in natural observation. The kookai (owl) is stronger than the kaakkai (crow), but it is day-blind, so pakal (daytime) reverses their strength. Parimelazhagar draws the lesson for the vendhar (king): an ikal (hostile contest) is won not by strength alone but by the pozhudhu (hour) that suits one’s side. The image has a close Sanskrit parallel in the Panchatantra’s third book, the war of the crows and owls (Kākolūkīya), where the crows burn the owls’ cave by daylight. Both editions consulted (Thirukkural.net and Project Madurai) give the same text, apart from a sandhi spacing in ikalvellum.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Book III of the Panchatantra, the war of the crows and the owls, the owl-king’s army attacks the crows at night and slaughters them. The crow minister Sthirajivin later wins the owls’ trust and lives in their cave. Then, in daylight, when the owls are blind and helpless, he and the crows block the entrance and set it on fire, destroying the whole colony.
— Panchatantra, Book III (Kākolūkīya, ‘Crows and Owls’), tr. Arthur W. Ryder [3]
The weaker crows cannot win by night, which is the owls’ hour. They win completely by choosing the day, when the owls cannot see.
This is the Kural’s crow-and-owl image worked out as a full political fable.
Christianity
“Then Jesus said unto them, My time is not yet come: but your time is alway ready.”
— John 7:6 (KJV) [4]
Jesus’ brothers urge him to show himself publicly in Judea at the feast. He refuses because the moment (kairos) is not yet right for him, though any time suits them.
Like the Kural’s king, Jesus acts not when pressed to but when the hour favours his purpose.
Islam
At the Battle of the Trench (al-Khandaq, 627 CE), a much larger confederate army besieged Medina. The Muslims held their defensive line rather than meet it in open field. After weeks, a bitter cold wind at night wrecked the besiegers’ camp and they withdrew. The Qur’an recalls this in Sura 33:9, where God sent a wind and unseen forces against them.
— Qur’an 33:9–10 and the sira accounts of the Battle of the Trench [5]
The weaker side avoided the stronger side’s favoured ground and waited until conditions turned against the besiegers.
As with the crow by day, victory came to the weaker side when circumstances were in its favour, not through raw strength.
Judaism
“And of the children of Issachar, which were men that had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do.”
— 1 Chronicles 12:32 (KJV) [4]
When David was gathering support for his kingship, the Chronicler singles out the men of Issachar for one skill: they understood the times and so knew what Israel should do.
The verse makes reading the moment a qualification of royal counsel, just as the Kural says kings need the right time.
Buddhism
In the Dhammaññu Sutta the Buddha lists seven qualities that make a monk worthy of gifts and respect. One of them is being kālaññū, ‘one who knows the right time’: the time for study, questioning, effort and seclusion.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 7.64 (Dhammaññu Sutta), tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [6]
Knowing the proper time is counted as a distinct virtue, next to knowing the teaching, its meaning, oneself, moderation, assemblies and persons.
Kālaññutā is almost a Pali name for this chapter’s title, kaalam arithal (‘knowing the time’).
Jainism
“This mendicant who knows the time, the strength (of himself), the measure (of all things), the practice, the occasion (for begging, &c.), the conduct, the religious precepts, the true condition (of the donor or hearer), … he proceeds securely (on the road to final liberation) after having cut off both (love and hate).”
— Acaranga Sutra I.2.5.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Acaranga describes the wise mendicant as one who knows the right time, his own strength and the occasion, and who therefore moves securely toward liberation.
Valluvar's crow beats the owl because it knows its hour; the Acaranga likewise pairs knowing the time with knowing one's own strength as the mark of the wise, though its goal is liberation rather than victory.
Sikhism
“He could appoint a worm as king, and reduce an army to ashes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 144, Raag Maajh, Majh Ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Majh Ki Vaar Guru Nanak says that God can raise the weakest creature to a throne and bring the mightiest army to nothing.
The Kural shows the weaker crow beating the stronger owl when the hour favours it; Guru Nanak also sees the weak overturning the strong, though by God's will rather than timing.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad Book 10, Odysseus and Diomedes scout the Trojan lines by night, when the Trojans and their new allies are asleep. They kill the Thracian king Rhesus and his men in their sleep and take his famous horses, which Achilles’ absence had left the Greeks unable to win in daylight battle.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 10 (the Doloneia), tr. Samuel Butler [9]
Having been beaten by day, the Greeks switch to the hour that favours stealth, and two men do what an army could not.
This is the Kural’s point in reverse lighting. Each side has its hour, and the wise fighter strikes in his own.
Philosophy
“There is surely no greater wisdom, than well to time the beginnings, and onsets, of things.”
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Delays’ (1625) [10]
In his essay on delays, Bacon calls the timing of beginnings the height of practical wisdom. He warns both against missing the moment and against acting before it is ripe.
Bacon states as a maxim what the Kural shows with the crow: the hour chosen decides the contest.

Real Life Example

Finnish ski troops defeat Soviet divisions at Suomussalmi
December 1939 - January 1940 · Suomussalmi, Finland
In the Winter War, the Soviet Union attacked Finland with forces many times larger than the Finnish army. At Suomussalmi, Soviet motorised divisions, many drawn from the milder Ukrainian military district and not equipped for the northern winter, were tied to narrow forest roads, while lightly armed Finnish troops moved quickly on skis. The Finns cut the road-bound columns into isolated pockets and destroyed two Soviet divisions, though Finland was later forced to make peace and give up territory.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A much smaller force won by fighting in the season and conditions that favoured it and crippled its stronger enemy.
The kural says even a crow beats the stronger owl by day, so the weaker side must wait for the time that favours it, and the northern winter was the Finns' daylight and the Soviets' night.
