Kural 1212

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

கயலுண்கண் யானிரப்பத் துஞ்சிற் கலந்தார்க்கு உயலுண்மை சாற்றுவேன் மன்.
Kayalunkan yaanirappath thunjir kalandhaarkku uyalunmai saatruvaen man.

Meaning

If my fish-shaped eyes would only fall asleep when I beg them, I could tell my love, when he comes in my dream, how hard it has been to stay alive.

Elementary

Have you ever had so much to tell someone that you lay awake and couldn’t sleep? This girl wants to fall asleep so she can meet her faraway husband in a dream and tell him how hard it has been, but the worry keeps her eyes wide open.

University

Kayal uṇ kaṇ, ‘carp-like eyes lined with collyrium’, is a stock Sangam image of feminine beauty, here made pathetic: the eyes that once drew the lover now refuse the sleep (tuñcil, ‘if they sleep’) that would let her meet him. Yāṉ irappa means ‘as I beg’, casting the heroine as a supplicant to her own eyes. Kalantārkku, ‘to him who united with me’, names the husband by their past union; uyal uṇmai is ‘the truth of my survival’, i.e. how she has scraped through the separation, and cāṟṟuvēṉ ‘I would declare’. The closing particle maṉ marks a wish that cannot be fulfilled, which Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan render as ‘I would tell him, if only’. Pope: ‘If my dark, carp-like eye will close in sleep, as I implore, / The tale of my long-suffering life I’ll tell my loved one o’er.’ Both base sources print the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Captive in Lanka, Sita entrusts Hanuman with a message for Rama telling him how she has endured, and warns that she can hold on to life only a little longer unless he comes.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, Sita’s message to Rama through Hanuman (paraphrase) [3]
The Sundara Kanda turns a separated wife’s account of her own survival into the message she most needs her husband to hear.
Sita’s message is what the Kural’s heroine longs to deliver, the ‘truth of how I have survived’, if only sleep would let her meet her husband.
Christianity
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Solomon 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song of Solomon lies awake at night seeking the beloved who is absent.
Like the Kural’s sleepless heroine, she spends the night on her bed searching for a meeting that will not come.
Islam
“I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know.”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob, long separated from Joseph, will pour out his grief only before God.
The Kural’s heroine also has a tale of suffering she longs to pour out; she seeks a hearing from her beloved in sleep, where Jacob seeks it from God.
Judaism
“Thou holdest fast the lids of mine eyes; I am troubled, and cannot speak.”
— Psalms 77:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist lies awake in distress, his eyelids held open, unable to put his trouble into words.
This is the Kural’s predicament exactly reversed into lament: sleeplessness that keeps one from speaking one’s sorrow.
Buddhism
Asked whether he slept well on a cold night on leaves, the Buddha says he did, and explains that a householder in a fine bed may still lie awake, burned by fevers born of passion, while one who has let passion go sleeps at ease.
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.35, Hatthaka Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Hatthaka discourse ties sleeplessness directly to desire.
From a Buddhist angle, the Kural’s heroine cannot sleep for exactly this reason: her longing is the fever that keeps her eyes open.
Jainism
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that one who becomes attached to beautiful forms through the eye brings misery on himself, never finding satisfaction and suffering when the object is absent.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 32 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching traces suffering to the senses’ attachment to what they love to see.
The Kural’s heroine suffers precisely because her eyes are bound to a beloved they cannot see; Jainism would counsel loosening that attachment rather than seeking it in sleep.
Sikhism
“This night, I did not sleep with my Husband Lord, and now my body is suffering in pain. Go and ask the deserted bride, how she passes her night.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 30) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid speaks as a bride kept from her Husband Lord for a single night, and says only a deserted wife knows how such nights are survived.
The Kural's wife begs her eyes to sleep so that she can tell her beloved in a dream how barely she has lived through his absence; Farid's bride likewise wants someone to hear how the night of separation is endured.
Greek Mythology
A short lyric fragment traditionally attributed to Sappho says that the moon and the Pleiades have set, it is midnight, the hours pass, and the speaker lies alone.
— Fragment 168B Voigt, attributed to Sappho (paraphrase) [10]
Greek lyric captures the lover lying awake through the hours of night without the beloved.
The Kural’s heroine shares that midnight vigil, with the added ache that sleep itself would bring her love to her.
Philosophy
Aristotle explains sleep as a natural seizing of the primary sense-faculty that renders it unable to function, arising from bodily processes rather than from choice.
— Aristotle, On Sleep and Waking (De Somno), Parva Naturalia (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, sleep is not something the will can summon; it comes by nature.
The Kural’s heroine begs her eyes to sleep, and Aristotle explains why begging does not work: sleep is not under the will’s command.

Real Life Example

Mary Shelley's waking dream gives her Frankenstein
June 1816 · Villa Diodati, Lake Geneva, Switzerland
In June 1816 Mary Godwin, later Mary Shelley, Percy Bysshe Shelley, Lord Byron and their companions were kept indoors by bleak weather near Lake Geneva, and Byron proposed that each of them write a ghost story. Mary struggled to think of one until a waking dream showed her a pale student kneeling beside the figure he had put together and seeing it stir to life. That image grew into Frankenstein, published anonymously in 1818.
— Sources: [12] [13]
What she could not find by trying while awake came to her only in a dream-like state at night.
The kural's heroine pleads with her eyes to sleep so that a dream can give her what waking will not; Mary Shelley likewise found in a waking dream what days of effort had failed to give her.
