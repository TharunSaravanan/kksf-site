Kural 997

Courtesy · Panpudaimai · பண்புடைமை

அரம்போலும் கூர்மைய ரேனும் மரம்போல்வர் மக்கட்பண்பு இல்லா தவர்.
Arampolum koormaiya renum marampolvar makkatpanpu illaa thavar.

Meaning

People without good human qualities are no better than trees, even if their minds are as sharp as a file (a metal tool for sharpening).

Elementary

A pencil sharpener is very sharp, but it can't give you a hug or say sorry. Valluvar says a person can be super clever, but if they aren't kind they are like a tree trunk: standing there, but not really acting like a person.

University

The couplet plays on the rhyme aram (அரம், 'file, rasp') and maram (மரம், 'tree'): those who lack makkat-panbu (மக்கட்பண்பு, 'the quality proper to human beings') are 'like trees' even if they have a file's koormai (கூர்மை, 'sharpness'). Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah both gloss maram as ஓரறிவுயிர், 'a one-sensed being', invoking the classical Tamil (and Jain) grading of living beings by the number of their senses, so the insult is precise: an intellect without humanity sinks to the lowest rung of life. Pope renders 'Though sharp their wit as file, as blocks they must remain, / Whose souls are void of courtesy humane'. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads aram as a sharp cutting tool and makkat-panbu as 'humanitarianism'. The kural extends the argument of 993 from likeness to humanity itself. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A well-known verse in the introduction to the Hitopadesha says that eating, sleeping, fear and mating are common to humans and animals; dharma alone is what sets humans apart, and those lacking it are equal to beasts.
— Hitopadesha, Introduction (prastāvikā), verse on āhāra-nidrā-bhaya-maithuna [3]
The Sanskrit fable collection teaches that what makes a human being more than an animal is moral character, not bodily functions or cleverness.
Like the Kural, it says a person without the qualities proper to humans is reduced to a lower form of life.
Christianity
“And though I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries, and all knowledge; and though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that even the greatest knowledge and gifts count for nothing without love.
A mind sharp enough to know all mysteries but empty of love is the Kural's sharp file that is still only a tree.
Islam
“The example of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not take it on is like that of a donkey who carries volumes [of books].”
— Qur'an 62:5 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an likens those who carry knowledge without living by it to a donkey loaded with books.
The image of a beast carrying learning it does not embody parallels the Kural's sharp-witted person who is still like a tree.
Judaism
“He used to say: one whose wisdom exceeds his deeds, to what may he be compared? To a tree whose branches are numerous but whose roots are few, so that when the wind comes, it uproots it and overturns it.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:17 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah compares the person whose learning outstrips his conduct to a tree that topples in the wind.
By a striking coincidence of imagery, it too likens wisdom without goodness to a tree.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Dhammapada says that learning recited but not lived brings no real benefit.
Like the Kural, it treats knowledge cut off from good conduct as empty.
Jainism
“Acknowledging the truth about bondage and liberation, but talking only, not acting (in accordance with these tenets), they seek comfort for themselves in mighty words. Clever talking will not work salvation; how should philosophical instruction do it? Fools, though sinking lower and lower through their sins, believe themselves to be wise men.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.9–10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira rejects those who rely on clever talk and doctrine while failing to act rightly, calling them fools who think themselves wise.
This matches the Kural's sharp-minded person who lacks the conduct that makes one truly human.
Sikhism
“He acts like a beast, and does not understand anything. Practicing falsehood, he becomes false.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1132, Raag Bhairao, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das describes the manmukh, the self-willed person, as living at the level of an animal despite being human, because he lacks the understanding that makes human life worthwhile. Gurbani often uses the image of the beast for a human life emptied of its proper quality.
The Kural says those without humane qualities are no better than trees however sharp their minds, and Guru Amar Das likewise says that a person without true understanding sinks to the level of a beast.
Greek Mythology
Homer describes the Cyclopes as lawless and overbearing beings who hold no assemblies and have no laws, each ruling his own family without regard for others; Polyphemus later scorns the guest-right of Zeus.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.106–115 and 9.273–278 [10]
The Cyclopes are powerful and capable, yet lack the social and moral qualities of civilized humans.
They embody the Kural's being who has capacity but lacks the qualities that make one fully human.
Philosophy
“For man, when perfected, is the best of animals, but, when separated from law and justice, he is the worst of all; since armed injustice is the more dangerous, and he is equipped at birth with arms, meant to be used by intelligence and virtue, which he may use for the worst ends.”
— Aristotle, Politics I.2, 1253a (tr. Benjamin Jowett, Internet Classics Archive) [11]
Aristotle argues that human intelligence without virtue makes man not the best but the worst and most savage of animals.
Like the Kural, Aristotle says that a sharp intellect without humane virtue sinks a person below the human.

Real Life Example

Fritz Haber, Nobel chemist, directs the first chlorine gas attack
22 April 1915 · Ypres, Belgium
The German chemist Fritz Haber developed a practical method of synthesising ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen, the basis of modern nitrogen fertiliser, and received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. During the First World War he also led Germany's development of poison gas and supervised its first large-scale use, the chlorine attack at Ypres on 22 April 1915. After the war he was widely criticised, and in some circles shunned, for his part in chemical warfare.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Haber's career shows that outstanding intellectual brilliance and humane judgement are different things, and that the first does not guarantee the second.
The kural says that those without humane qualities are no better than trees even if their minds are as sharp as a file, and Haber's brilliance did not prevent him from turning science to the mass killing of soldiers.
