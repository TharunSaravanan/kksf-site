Kural 1325

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

தவறிலர் ஆயினும் தாம்வீழ்வார் மென்றோள் அகறலின் ஆங்கொன் றுடைத்து.
Thavarilar aayinum thaamveezhvaar mendroal Akaralin aangon rudaiththu.

Meaning

Even when a man has done nothing wrong, being kept for a while from the soft arms of the woman he loves has a joy of its own.

Elementary

Waiting for a birthday makes the party feel even better when it comes. A husband who is told ‘not now’ by his wife in a playful sulk, even though he did nothing wrong, finds a funny kind of sweetness in the waiting.

University

Here the voice shifts to the husband, or to a general maxim about men: taviṟilar āyiṉum (‘though they are without fault’), being separated from (akaṟal) the mel tōḷ (‘soft shoulders’, a standard metonymy for the beloved’s embrace) of those whom they love (tām vīḻvār) has āṅku oṉṟu (‘a certain something there’, i.e. a special pleasure). Solomon Pappaiah reads the husband as accepting the role of the guilty one in order to be sulked at, while Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan simply note that such separation contains a pleasure; Pope renders the thought as estrangement having ‘its own special charms’. The couplet is the same on both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the elided form āṅkoṉṟuṭaittu is split as āṅku oṉṟu uṭaittu.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Harivamsa, when Krishna gives a celestial parijata flower to Rukmini, Satyabhama withdraws in a jealous sulk; Krishna, though he meant no slight, patiently soothes her and promises to bring her the whole parijata tree from Indra’s heaven.
— Harivamsa, Vishnu Parva (Parijata-harana episode; paraphrase) [3]
The episode became a favourite theme in Indian art and drama for a faultless husband humbly winning back a sulking wife.
Like the Kural’s husband, Krishna is innocent, kept at a distance by his wife’s pique, and finds the wooing worth it.
Christianity
“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying, Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled: for my head is filled with dew, and my locks with the drops of the night.”
— Song of Solomon 5:2 (KJV) [4]
In the Song of Songs the beloved stands knocking at night, kept waiting outside the door though he has done nothing wrong.
He is the Kural’s faultless lover, kept for a while from his beloved, with the waiting adding to the longing.
Islam
“There was something between us and he got angry with me and went out. He did not sleep (midday nap) in the house.” … Allah’s Messenger started cleaning the dust from him saying: “Get up! O Aba Turab. Get up! O Aba Turab”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 441 [5]
After a small quarrel with Fatima, Ali went to sleep in the mosque; the Prophet found him covered in dust and affectionately nicknamed him ‘Father of Dust’.
A brief separation after a small domestic quarrel ends in tenderness, close to the Kural’s pleasant estrangement.
Judaism
“A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; A time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing;”
— Ecclesiastes 3:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Ecclesiastes says that holding close and holding back each have their proper season.
The Kural’s husband discovers that the time of refraining from embrace has its own pleasure.
Buddhism
In the Bhallatiya Jataka, a pair of kinnaras (forest spirits) are still weeping, hundreds of years later, over a single night when a flooded stream kept them apart, and they say they have never forgotten it.
— Jataka 504, Bhallatiya-Jataka (ed. E. B. Cowell; paraphrase) [7]
The story shows how much a single separation weighs on a loving couple, and the king who hears it learns to value his queen.
The Kural’s husband feels the same separation more lightly, as a short absence whose ache sweetens the reunion.
Jainism
The monk Sthulabhadra spent a rainy season in the house of Kosha, the courtesan he had once loved, and remained unmoved by every attempt to win him back; his self-control so impressed her that she became a devout laywoman.
— Hemachandra, Parishishtaparvan, life of Sthulabhadra (paraphrase) [8]
Jain narrative presents a former lover’s nearness, resisted, as a triumph of restraint.
The Kural’s husband also stays apart from his beloved’s arms, but he relishes the distance as part of love, which is the opposite of Sthulabhadra’s aim.
Sikhism
“Many talk of the pain and suffering of separation; O pain, you are the ruler of all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Salok Sheikh Fareed (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses the Gurus included in the Guru Granth Sahib, calls the pain of separation from the Beloved the sovereign of all feelings, because it keeps love alive.
The Kural says that being kept apart from the beloved, even without fault, holds a pleasure of its own; Farid likewise honours the ache of separation as a royal and precious part of love.
Greek Mythology
“My dear,” said he, “heaven has endowed you with a heart more unyielding than woman ever yet had. No other woman could bear to keep away from her husband when he had come back to her after twenty years of absence, and after having gone through so much.”
— Homer, Odyssey 23 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus, home at last and blameless towards Penelope, is kept at a distance while she tests him.
Like the Kural’s faultless husband, Odysseus is held back from his wife’s embrace for a time before the joyful reunion.
Philosophy
La Rochefoucauld observed that absence lessens moderate passions and increases great ones, as the wind blows out candles but fans a fire.
— La Rochefoucauld, Maxims 276 (paraphrase) [11]
Distance tests love: weak love dies of it, strong love grows.
The Kural’s husband feels a strong love grow during a short separation, which is why the separation has its own sweetness.

Real Life Example

Georgia O'Keeffe spends her first summer in New Mexico
1929 · Taos, New Mexico, United States
In the summer of 1929 the painter Georgia O'Keeffe left New York and her husband, the photographer Alfred Stieglitz, to spend time in northern New Mexico, staying first in Taos. Her letters from there were full of happiness, while Stieglitz wrote that he was broken by her absence. For the next two decades she spent most summers working in New Mexico, and their marriage, sustained by thousands of letters, lasted until Stieglitz died in 1946.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The O'Keeffe and Stieglitz letters are a well-known record of a couple who found that time apart could renew both their work and their bond.
The kural says that being kept apart for a while from one's beloved, even without fault, holds a pleasure of its own, and O'Keeffe's summers away brought her joy without ending her marriage.
