Kural 240

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

வசையொழிய வாழ்வாரே வாழ்வார் இசையொழிய வாழ்வாரே வாழா தவர்.
Vasaiyozhiya vaazhvaare vaazhvaar isaiyozhiya Vaazhvaare vaazhaa thavar.

Meaning

People who live without blame are the ones who truly live. People who live without a good name are not really living at all.

Elementary

Being alive is like being given a notebook. Some people fill it with kind, brave, helpful stories. Others leave it empty or scribble unkind things. Valluvar says only the first kind are really ‘living’; the others just have the notebook.

University

The chapter closes with a chiastic couplet built on repetition: வசையொழிய வாழ்வாரே வாழ்வார் / இசையொழிய வாழ்வாரே வாழாதவர். The halves pivot on a near-rhyme, வசை (blame) and இசை (renown), so that ‘living without blame’ and ‘living without renown’ sound almost identical yet lead to opposite verdicts. The emphatic particle ஏ marks exclusivity: only the blameless truly live; the renownless are precisely the not-living. Life is thus redefined qualitatively rather than biologically. Similar ideas appear in the Talmudic teaching that the wicked are called dead even in their lifetimes, the Dhammapada’s ‘the heedless are as if dead already’, the Gita’s verdict that the self-indulgent person ‘lives in vain’, and Revelation’s church that has ‘a name that thou livest, and art dead’. Valluvar’s distinctive move is to make renown, the social trace of virtue, the test of whether one has lived at all.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man who does not follow this prescribed Vedic system of sacrifice certainly leads a life of sin, for a person delighting only in the senses lives in vain.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.16 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna says the self-indulgent person who ignores the cycle of duty and sacrifice ‘lives in vain’ (moghaṁ jīvati).
Both texts deny that mere biological life counts as living; a life empty of worth is in vain.
Christianity
“I know thy works, that thou hast a name that thou livest, and art dead.”
— Revelation 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The risen Christ tells the church in Sardis that despite its reputation for being alive, it is spiritually dead.
Both separate true life from mere appearance of life; Revelation warns that a name must reflect real works.
Islam
“And is one who was dead and We gave him life and made for him light by which to walk among the people like one who is in darkness, never to emerge therefrom?”
— Qur’an 6:122 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the guided person as someone brought from death to life, contrasted with one who stays in darkness.
Like the Kural, it treats true life as a moral and spiritual state rather than mere breath.
Judaism
“These are the wicked, who even during their lives are called dead.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 18b (William Davidson/Koren edition) [6]
The Talmud contrasts the righteous, called living even after death, with the wicked, who are called dead while still alive.
Almost the Kural’s exact verdict: the blameless truly live, and those without worth are the ‘not-living’ even while alive.
Buddhism
“Heedfulness is the path to the Deathless. Heedlessness is the path to death. The heedful die not. The heedless are as if dead already.”
— Dhammapada 21 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that careless, unmindful people are effectively dead even while living, while the heedful do not die.
The same redefinition of life and death by quality of living that the Kural makes.
Jainism
“The day that goes by will never return; the days elapse without profit to him who acts contrary to the Law.” “The day that goes by will never return; the days elapse with much profit to him who acts up to the Law.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.24–25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The young sons of the priest Bhṛgu tell their father that every passing day is lost for good, and that its worth depends on whether it was lived righteously.
Valluvar separates those who truly live from those who merely stay alive. The Jain verses draw the same line, saying that only days lived by the Law bear fruit.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, no one else is truly alive; those who merely live shall depart in dishonor; everything they eat is impure.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 142, Raag Maajh, Maajh ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that only those in whom God dwells truly live; others merely exist and leave the world in dishonour.
The Kural closes by saying that those who live without renown, though alive, do not really live; Guru Nanak makes the same distinction between true life and mere living in dishonour.
Greek Mythology
“I would rather be a paid servant in a poor man's house and be above ground than king of kings among the dead.”
— Homer, Odyssey 11 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
In the underworld, Achilles, the very emblem of undying glory, tells Odysseus that even the humblest life on earth is better than all his fame among the dead.
A deliberate counterpoint: Homer lets his greatest hero question whether renown can compensate for life itself, the opposite of the Kural’s verdict.
Philosophy
“The life which is unexamined is not worth living.”
— Plato, Apology 38a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
At his trial, Socrates says a life without moral self-examination is not worth living.
Like Valluvar, Socrates sets a qualitative test for whether a life counts; the test is self-examination rather than renown.

Real Life Example

Socrates refuses to escape before his execution
399 BCE · Athens, Greece
In 399 BCE Socrates was tried in Athens on charges of impiety and corrupting the young, convicted and sentenced to death. While he awaited execution, his friend Crito urged him to escape into exile, which was a realistic option, but Socrates refused, arguing that escape would be a wrong against the laws he had lived under. He drank the poison, most likely hemlock, and died calmly among his friends.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Socrates chose a blameless death over a compromised life, and his example has been treated ever since as a model of integrity.
The kural says that only those who live free of reproach truly live, and Socrates judged that a life bought by doing wrong would not be worth living.
