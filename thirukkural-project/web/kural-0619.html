Kural 619

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

தெய்வத்தான் ஆகா தெனினும் முயற்சிதன் மெய்வருத்தக் கூலி தரும்.
Theyvaththaan aagaadhu eninum muyarchithan Meyvaruththak kooli tharum.

Meaning

Even if fate stops you from reaching your goal, your effort will still pay you back for all your hard work.

Elementary

Say you practise hard for a race and still don’t win because it rained and you slipped. You didn’t get the trophy, but your legs are stronger and you’re faster than before. Hard work always pays something, even when luck says no.

University

Parimelazhagar glosses தெய்வம் as ஊழ் (fate, the fruit of past deeds), so the protasis concedes that destiny may make the full aim unattainable (ஆகாது); even then முயற்சி pays தன் மெய்வருத்தக் கூலி, the wage owed for its own bodily strain. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah keep the fate reading, but Kalaignar takes தெய்வம் literally as God, turning the couplet into a rationalist contrast between calling on God and working, a reading the Tamil can bear but the commentarial tradition does not support. The couplet is a careful concession to chapter 38 (ஊழ்), where kural 380 asks what is stronger than fate: Valluvar grants fate’s power over outcomes while denying it power to cancel the return on labour. Both editions agree, thirukkural-thamizh.org printing தெய்வத்தா னாகா தெனினு முயற்சிதன்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The body, the doer (soul), the various senses, the many kinds of efforts, and Divine Providence—these are the five factors of action.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.14 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna lists effort (ceṣṭā) and providence (daiva) as distinct contributors to any action, so neither alone determines the result.
The Gita, like the Kural, gives effort its own share in outcomes even where fate also plays a part.
Christianity
“Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord.”
— 1 Corinthians 15:58 (KJV) [4]
Paul assures the Corinthians that faithful labour is never wasted, whatever happens.
‘Not in vain’ is the Kural’s promise that effort always earns its wage.
Islam
“And that his effort is going to be seen - Then he will be recompensed for it with the fullest recompense”
— Qur’an 53:40-41 (Sahih International) [5]
Following the verse that a person has only what he strives for, the Qur’an promises that every effort will be seen and fully repaid.
Effort is guaranteed its recompense, exactly the Kural’s ‘wage of toil’.
Judaism
“Ben He He said: According to the labor is the reward.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 5:23 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah’s terse Aramaic saying (lefum tza‘ara agra) ties reward to the pain of the labour.
‘According to the labour, the reward’ is the Kural’s ‘wage of the body’s toil’ almost word for word.
Buddhism
Shipwrecked on his way to recover his kingdom, Prince Mahajanaka swims for seven days with no shore in sight. When the sea-goddess Manimekhala asks why he struggles in vain, he replies that one who strives does his duty and is free of blame even if he fails; moved, she carries him to safety.
— Mahajanaka Jataka (Jataka No. 539) [7]
The Mahajanaka Jataka, one of the Ten Great Birth Stories, illustrates the perfection of energy (viriya).
Effort that seems doomed by fate still earns its reward, here literally, as the Kural promises.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra teaches that austerity (tapas) brings about both the stopping of new karma (saṃvara) and the shedding of karma already bound (nirjarā). For Jains, effort always bears fruit at the level of karma, even when an outward aim fails because of past karma that is ripening.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.3 (tapasā nirjarā ca); see Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is (1994) [8]
In Jain doctrine 'fate' is the ripening of one's own past karma; it may block a present goal, but sincere exertion is never wasted, because it wears away karma.
As the Kural says effort pays the wages of the body's toil even when fate denies the goal, Jainism holds that exertion always earns its return in shed karma.
Sikhism
“They receive the wages of their labor, begging at the Door of their Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 788, Raag Soohee (Soohee ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Soohee ki Vaar, Guru Nanak speaks of devotees who labour and receive the wages of that labour at the Lord's door.
The Kural says that even when fate blocks the goal, effort still pays the wages of the body's toil; Guru Nanak likewise speaks of labour that is paid its wage.
Greek Mythology
A carter whose wagon is stuck in the mud prays to Heracles for help; the god appears and tells him to put his shoulder to the wheel and goad his oxen, for the gods help only those who help themselves.
— Aesop, Hercules and the Wagoner (Perry 291; trans. G. F. Townsend) [10]
The Aesopic fable rebukes reliance on the gods in place of one’s own exertion.
Like the Kural, the fable says that effort, not appeal to heaven, gets results.
Philosophy
Kant writes that even if, through the special disfavour of fortune or the meagre provision of a stepmotherly nature, a good will wholly lacked the power to achieve its purpose, it would still shine like a jewel by its own light, as something having its full worth in itself.
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Section I [11]
For Kant the worth of a good will does not depend on whether fortune allows it to succeed.
Both thinkers hold that adverse fortune cannot cancel the value of honest effort.

Real Life Example

Dorando Pietri is disqualified from the Olympic marathon and honoured by the Queen
July 1908 · London, United Kingdom
In the marathon at the 1908 London Olympic Games, the Italian runner Dorando Pietri entered the stadium first but, exhausted, collapsed several times and went the wrong way, and officials helped him across the finish line. He was disqualified for receiving outside assistance, and the gold medal went to the American Johnny Hayes. The next day Queen Alexandra presented Pietri with a special silver-gilt cup, and his effort made him a popular hero.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Pietri was denied the victory he had all but reached, yet his extraordinary effort earned a unique royal honour and lasting fame.
The kural says that even when fate keeps the goal out of reach, effort still pays the wages of one's toil, and Pietri lost the gold but received a reward of his own for what his body had endured.
