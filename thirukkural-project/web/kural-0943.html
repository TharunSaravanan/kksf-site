Kural 943

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

அற்றால் அறவறிந்து உண்க அஃதுடம்பு பெற்றான் நெடிதுய்க்கும் ஆறு.
Atraal aravarindhu unga ahdhudambu petraan nedidhuykkum aaru.

Meaning

Once your last meal is digested, eat only the right amount. That is the way to keep the body you were given going for a long time.

Elementary

A car runs far if you fill the tank properly and do not overflow it. Valluvar says your body is a gift you have been given, and eating the right amount at the right time is how you make it last a long, long time.

University

Atraal (அற்றால், 'when it has been digested') picks up the condition of 942, and adds aravarindhu (அறவறிந்து, அற + அறிந்து): Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read it as knowing the needed quantity, Puliyur Kesikan simply as eating in measure. The second line names the payoff: udambu petraan (உடம்பு பெற்றான், 'he who has obtained a body') can nedidhu uykkum (நெடிது உய்க்கும், 'carry it on long'). Kesikan glosses the body as 'this human frame so hard to obtain' (பெறுவதற்கரியதான இந்த மானிட யாக்கை), an Indian commonplace that human birth is rare and should not be wasted through gluttony. Parimelazhagar's line of interpretation thus links diet to longevity, not merely to comfort. Some editions print the first line as அற்றால் அளவறிந்து; ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net both read அறவறிந்து.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who is temperate in his habits of eating, sleeping, working and recreation can mitigate all material pains by practicing the yoga system.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.17 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, As It Is, 1972) [3]
Krishna teaches that regulated eating (yuktahara), along with regulated sleep and work, is the ground of a pain-free yogic life.
The Kural's 'eat knowing the measure' is the Gita's yuktahara, here tied to long life rather than to yoga.
Christianity
“By surfeiting have many perished; but he that taketh heed prolongeth his life.”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 37:31 (KJV Apocrypha) [4]
Ben Sira warns that overeating has killed many, while the one who is careful about food lengthens his days.
It is a near paraphrase of the Kural: measured eating is the way to keep the body long.
Islam
“O children of Adam, take your adornment [i.e., wear your clothing] at every masjid, and eat and drink, but be not excessive. Indeed, He likes not those who commit excess.”
— Qur'an 7:31 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an permits eating and drinking but forbids going beyond measure.
Both texts allow food freely while insisting that its measure be known and kept.
Judaism
“One should not eat until his stomach is full. Rather, [he should stop when] he has eaten to close to three quarter's of full satisfaction.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:2 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides gives a concrete measure: stop eating at about three quarters of fullness.
This is the Kural's 'knowing the measure', given an actual number.
Buddhism
“When a man is always mindful, knowing moderation in eating, his discomfort fades, and he ages slowly, taking care of his life.”
— Donapaka Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 3.13 (tr. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha spoke this verse to King Pasenadi, who was panting after eating rice by the bucketful; the king had it recited at every meal and slimmed down.
Knowing the measure in eating so that one 'ages slowly, taking care of his life' is the Kural's promise of long life almost exactly.
Jainism
“He who takes less food than he usually does, in the extreme case but one mouthful, performs abstinence with regard to substance.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 30.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Avamodarika, eating less than one's full measure, is one of the six external austerities in Jain discipline.
Jainism, like the Kural, makes the measure of food a matter of deliberate knowledge, though it sets the measure below fullness to burn off karma rather than to prolong life.
Sikhism
“This human body has been given to you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa, So Purakh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In So Purakh, part of the evening prayer Rehras, Guru Arjan reminds the listener that a human body is a rare gift and the chance it offers should not be wasted.
The Kural's commentators gloss 'one who has obtained a body' as one granted this hard-won human frame, and urge measured eating so that it lasts; Gurbani shares the sense that the body is a gift to be cared for.
Greek Mythology
The maxim 'Nothing in excess' (meden agan) was inscribed at Apollo's temple at Delphi, which Plato says the sages dedicated as the first fruits of their wisdom.
— Plato, Protagoras 343b (tr. Benjamin Jowett), paraphrase [10]
The Delphic god's own precinct carried a warning against excess in all things.
The Kural applies the Delphic rule to the dinner plate.
Philosophy
“...both excessive and defective exercise destroys the strength, and similarly drink or food which is above or below a certain amount destroys the health, while that which is proportionate both produces and increases and preserves it.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.2 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle uses diet as his model for the mean: the right amount of food produces, increases and preserves health.
Proportionate food that 'preserves' health is the Kural's measured eating that keeps the body long.

Real Life Example

Alvise Cornaro writes his Treatise on the Sober Life
1558 · Padua, Republic of Venice (now Italy)
Alvise (Luigi) Cornaro, a businessman and patron of the arts in Padua, became critically ill around the age of forty after years of intemperate living and resolved to follow strict rules on the quantity and quality of what he ate and drank. He recovered, and in 1558 published his Treatise on the Sober Life, describing his measured diet as the source of his health and recommending moderation as the way to live to a great age. He revised and added to the work in the following years, lived into his eighties, and died in 1566; the book became an international success and was translated into English many times.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Cornaro's book is one of the earliest and most widely read personal accounts of measured eating as a path to long life.
The kural says that one who eats in the right measure after digestion keeps the body going for a long time, which is exactly the regimen and result Cornaro described.
