Kural 1292

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

உறாஅ தவர்க்கண்ட கண்ணும் அவரைச் செறாஅரெனச் சேறியென் நெஞ்சு.
Uraaa thavarkkanda kannum avaraich cheraa-arenach chaeriyen nenju.

Meaning

My heart, even after seeing that he does not care for me, you still go to him, thinking he will not be angry.

Elementary

Think of a puppy that keeps running to someone who ignores it, wagging its tail and hoping for a pat. The puppy does not stop to think. The wife says her heart is like that puppy: it runs to her husband even when he has not shown much love.

University

Uṟāatavar is ‘one who does not draw near’, i.e. who shows no love (uṟu, to approach, be attached); ceṟāar is ‘he will not be angry’, and cēṟi ‘you go’. Both uṟāa and ceṟāa show the long-vowel extension (aḷapeṭai) used for metre, also seen in 1295. The commentators agree on the sense: Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan have the heart go to the beloved ‘thinking he will not hate us’, and Solomon Pappaiah adds ‘thinking he will not be angry if we go’; Pope renders ‘Why go to him as though he were not enemy?’ The point within the chapter is that the heart’s eagerness undercuts the wife’s plan to sulk. Both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, Radha learns that Krishna is dancing with other cowherd women, yet in her loneliness her heart keeps recalling him and longing for him rather than turning away.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 2 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva shows Radha unable to stop loving Krishna even when he seems indifferent to her.
Like the Kural’s heart, Radha’s heart goes on seeking a beloved who, for now, does not seem to seek her.
Christianity
“Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as endlessly patient and willing to hope for the best.
The wife’s heart ‘believes all things’: it hopes he will not be angry even after seeing his coldness.
Islam
“But perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allah Knows, while you know not.”
— Qur’an 2:216 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that what the heart loves is not always what is good for it.
The wife’s heart loves one who does not love it back, against what she can plainly see.
Judaism
“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had turned away, and was gone. My soul failed me when he spoke. I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Songs 5:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman in the Song keeps seeking her beloved even after he has withdrawn and does not answer.
As in the Kural, love goes on reaching toward a beloved who seems to hold himself back.
Buddhism
“Wonderful, indeed, it is to subdue the mind, so difficult to subdue, ever swift, and seizing whatever it desires. A tamed mind brings happiness.”
— Dhammapada 35 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes the untrained mind as quick to grab at whatever it wants.
The wife’s heart rushes after what it desires without weighing what it has already seen.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of ‘colours,’ will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana warns that passionate attachment drives a being toward what will hurt it, like a moth to a flame.
The wife’s heart flies toward him just as blindly, though she has seen that he does not care.
Sikhism
“If You seat me near You, then I worship and adore You. Even if You beat me and drive me out, I will still meditate on You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 757, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says the devotee's love does not depend on how the Lord treats him; welcomed or driven away, he keeps turning to God.
Like the Kural's heart that goes to the beloved even when he shows no care, the devotee's heart keeps going to God whatever the treatment.
Greek Mythology
The nymph Echo falls in love with Narcissus and follows him through the woods even after he coldly rejects her, wasting away until only her voice remains.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.356-401 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid tells of a love that keeps pursuing a beloved who gives nothing back.
Echo’s heart, like the wife’s, goes after one who has shown no love, unable to stop itself.
Philosophy
Hume argues that reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions, and can never by itself oppose a passion in directing the will.
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 2.3.3 (paraphrase) [11]
For Hume, knowing a fact does not by itself stop desire; feeling drives action.
The wife’s heart knows he is cold, yet that knowledge cannot hold it back from going to him.

Real Life Example

Mary Wollstonecraft travels to Scandinavia on Gilbert Imlay’s business
June–September 1795 · Sweden, Norway and Denmark
In Paris during the French Revolution, Mary Wollstonecraft lived with the American adventurer Gilbert Imlay and in 1794 gave birth to their daughter, Fanny, but he soon drifted away from her. In 1795, after a Norwegian captain had made off with a valuable silver cargo from one of Imlay’s ships, Imlay asked her to go to Scandinavia to recover it, and she set out in June with her baby and a maid, spending more than three months negotiating with officials and travelling. She wrote letters to Imlay throughout, and on returning to London found that he had taken up with another woman; the letters became her book Letters Written During a Short Residence in Sweden, Norway, and Denmark (1796).
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Even after Imlay had made clear how little he valued her, Wollstonecraft went far out of her way for him and kept writing to him.
The kural’s wife tells her heart that even after seeing he does not care, it still goes to him, assuming he will not be angry; Wollstonecraft’s journey and letters show a heart that kept going toward a man who had stopped caring.
