Kural 133

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

ஒழுக்கம் உடைமை குடிமை இழுக்கம் இழிந்த பிறப்பாய் விடும்.
Ozhukkam udaimai kudimai izhukkam Izhindha pirappaai vidum.

Meaning

Having good behaviour is what makes a person’s family noble. Losing it turns their birth into a low one.

Elementary

Coming from a ‘good family’ is not about your last name. If you act kindly and honestly, you are from a good family. If you behave badly, it is as if you did not come from a good family at all.

University

Kudimai (lineage, the dignity of one's house) is redefined here in terms of behaviour. Possessing ozhukkam is itself nobility of birth, while izhukkam (lapse, slipping) ‘becomes’ (aai vidum) a base birth. The verb matters: a lapse does not just disgrace one's family, it converts one's birth into a low one after the fact. Commentators differ on scope. Parimelazhagar and Pappaiah tie conduct to the duties of one's station, a view that fits a varna framework, while Varadarasanar and Karunanidhi read the couplet as a universal critique of status by birth. Either way, it belongs to a long Indian debate, also voiced by the Buddha in the Vasala Sutta and by Yudhishthira in the Mahabharata, over whether birth or behaviour makes a person noble.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the boy Satyakama Jabala admits that his mother does not know who his father was, the sage Gautama accepts him as a Brahmin student. He reasons that only a true Brahmin would speak such an unflattering truth.
— Chandogya Upanishad 4.4 [3]
Satyakama's honesty, not his known lineage, proves his worth and earns him entry to Vedic study.
As in the Kural, conduct (here truthfulness) is what establishes noble standing.
Christianity
“And think not to say within yourselves, We have Abraham to our father: for I say unto you, that God is able of these stones to raise up children unto Abraham.”
— Matthew 3:9 (KJV) [4]
John the Baptist warns the religious elite that descent from Abraham means nothing without repentance and good fruit.
Both reject lineage as a substitute for conduct.
Islam
“Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allāh is the most righteous of you.”
— Qur'an 49:13 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an says tribes and lineages exist so that people may know one another, but true nobility is measured by righteousness.
This is the Kural's redefinition of nobility almost word for word.
Judaism
“a mamzer who is a Torah scholar precedes a High Priest who is an ignoramus”
— Mishnah Horayot 3:8 (Koren–Steinsaltz tr., Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah sets out an order of precedence by birth, then overturns it: a person of the most stigmatized birth who is a scholar outranks the highest-born priest who is ignorant.
Like the Kural, personal attainment overrides the rank one is born into.
Buddhism
“Not by birth is one an outcast; not by birth is one a brahman. By deed one becomes an outcast, by deed one becomes a brahman.”
— Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7, v. 21 (tr. Piyadassi Thera) [7]
Called an outcast by a Brahmin, the Buddha answers that high and low status are made by one's actions, not by birth.
This states both halves of the Kural: good conduct makes one noble, misconduct makes one low.
Jainism
“The value of penance has become visible, birth appears of no value! Look at the holy Harikêśa, the son of a Śvapâka, whose power is so great.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.1, 12.5 and 12.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Harikeśa-Bala, born among the Śvapākas (outcastes) and mocked by priests 'stuck up by pride of birth', is honoured by gods for his discipline, and the scene ends with this verdict.
Valluvar says conduct is what makes birth noble and its lack makes it low; the Jain canon dramatizes the same reversal, with an outcaste's conduct outranking the priests' high birth.
Sikhism
“God does not ask about social class or birth; you must find your true home. That is your social class and that is your status - the karma of what you have done.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1330, Raag Prabhati, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak rejects birth as the measure of a person and says one's real class and standing are the deeds one has done.
The Kural says right conduct is itself noble birth and a lapse makes one's birth low; Guru Nanak likewise makes deeds, not lineage, one's true jaat and pat.
Greek Mythology
The nobly born suitors of Ithaca eat up Odysseus' household with insolence and abuse the disguised king, while the slave swineherd Eumaeus shows him true hospitality. The suitors end in death and disgrace.
— Homer, Odyssey Books 14–22 [10]
In the Odyssey, the aristocrats behave basely and the lowly servant behaves nobly.
Conduct, not birth, shows who is truly noble, as the Kural claims.
Philosophy
Seneca argues that philosophy does not look at pedigree, and that a hall full of ancestral busts does not make a nobleman. Only the soul (animus) makes a person noble, and it can rise above any condition of birth.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 44 [11]
For Seneca, virtue is open to slave and aristocrat alike, and nobility belongs to the character, not the family tree.
This is the same relocation of nobility from lineage to conduct.

Real Life Example

Narayana Guru consecrates a Shiva lingam at Aruvippuram
1888 · Aruvippuram, near Neyyattinkara, Kerala, India
Narayana Guru, a sage and social reformer whose family belonged to the Ezhava community, then ranked low in the Hindu caste order, consecrated a stone taken from the Neyyar river as a Shiva lingam at Aruvippuram in 1888. At the time only Brahmin priests were allowed to install temple deities, so the act broke orthodox practice and was challenged by upper-caste critics who questioned his right to perform it. The shrine became the Aruvippuram Shiva temple, and Narayana Guru went on to found temples in southern India and Ceylon that welcomed people of all castes.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The consecration is regarded as a turning point in Kerala's social reform movement, because it asserted that spiritual authority comes from a person's life and learning rather than from the caste into which they are born.
On the reading of modern commentators who take the kural as a critique of status by birth, Narayana Guru's act embodies its claim that conduct, not birth, is what makes a person noble.
