Kural 827

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

சொல்வணக்கம் ஒன்னார்கண் கொள்ளற்க வில்வணக்கம் தீங்கு குறித்தமை யான்.
Solvanakkam onnaarkan kollar̲ka vilvanakkam theengu kur̲iththamai yaan.

Meaning

Do not trust an enemy’s polite, bowing words. A bow bends low only so it can shoot and do harm.

Elementary

When an archer pulls back a bow, it bends down low, almost like it is bowing politely. But that bend is what sends the arrow flying. Sometimes people who don’t like you act extra polite right before they try to hurt you.

University

The couplet turns on a pun in vaṇakkam (வணக்கம்), which means both ‘bowing, reverence’ and ‘bending’: sol-vaṇakkam is ‘humility in speech’, vil-vaṇakkam ‘the bending of a bow’. Onnaar (ஒன்னார்) are those who are not one with us, enemies. Since the bow’s bend ‘signals harm’ (theengu kuṟiththamaiyaan, தீங்கு குறித்தமையான்), a foe’s deferential words should be read the same way; Pappaiah and Kalaignar draw out the simile in this sense, and Pope translates ‘pliant speech’ and ‘pliant bow’. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Ravana, planning to abduct Sita, approaches her hermitage disguised as a gentle wandering ascetic, praising her with courteous and reverent words until she offers him hospitality; then he reveals his true form and carries her off.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda, Sargas 46-49 [3]
Humble, respectful speech is the opening move of an act of violence.
Ravana’s bowing words are the bent bow of the Kural, curved for harm.
Christianity
“Judas, betrayest thou the Son of man with a kiss?”
— Luke 22:48 (KJV) [4]
Judas greets Jesus with a disciple’s kiss of respect, the agreed signal for his arrest.
A gesture of reverence used as the trigger for harm is the Kural’s bending bow.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not take as intimates those other than yourselves, for they will not spare you [any] ruin. They wish you would have hardship. Hatred has already appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater.”
— Qur’an 3:118 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns against taking as intimates those whose words already betray hostility and whose hearts hide more.
Like the Kural, it tells believers not to accept hostile people’s outward speech as friendship.
Judaism
“Wounds by a loved one are long lasting; / The kisses of an enemy are profuse.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs contrasts a true friend’s honest blows with an enemy’s lavish shows of affection.
An enemy’s profuse kisses are the foe’s humble words that the Kural says not to accept.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who flatters be understood as a foe in the guise of a friend: (i) he approves of his friend's evil deeds, (ii) he disapproves his friend's good deeds, (iii) he praises him in his presence, (iv) he speaks ill of him in his absence.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The flatterer praises to your face and undermines you behind your back.
His praise is the respectful ‘bowing’ speech that the Kural says hides harm.
Jainism
“The absence of benevolence of disposition in him whose form is beautiful is like water mixed with milk, that is pleasant to drink. For those who are wise, to become companions of the wicked is like the nâgâ playing with the female cobra.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 24 ('Improper Friendship'), st. 10 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār's chapter on improper friendship says a pleasing exterior can hide a lack of goodwill, and that the wise who keep company with the wicked are like a snake playing with a deadly cobra.
Valluvar says a foe's humble, bowing speech is like the bend of a bow, which signals harm; the Nālaṭiyār similarly warns that a pleasant surface and playful closeness can hide venom.
Sikhism
“Everyone bows down to himself; no one bows down to another. … The sinner, like the deer hunter, bows down twice as much. But what can be achieved by bowing the head, when the heart is impure?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok 1 before pauri 14), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa Ki Vaar Guru Nanak observes that the hunter stalking a deer bends lowest of all, so deep bowing can serve harm, and bowing means nothing when the heart is impure.
The Kural says a foe's humble speech is like the bending of a bow, which bends only to harm; Guru Nanak's sinner who bows twice as much, like the hunter, makes the same point.
Greek Mythology
When Agamemnon returns from Troy, Clytemnestra greets him with an elaborate, flattering speech of welcome and has rich purple tapestries spread for him to walk on into the palace, where she then kills him.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon (the carpet scene) [10]
Extravagant reverence is the prelude to murder.
Clytemnestra’s bowing welcome is the Kural’s bent bow: deference that signals harm.
Philosophy
Sun Tzu advises that when the enemy’s envoys use humble words while the enemy steps up its preparations, it is a sign that he is about to advance.
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War, Chapter IX (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Strategic thought reads humility from a foe as a warning, not a reassurance.
Sun Tzu and Valluvar both read a foe’s humble speech the way one reads a drawn bow.

Real Life Example

Japan's envoys keep negotiating in Washington as the Pearl Harbor fleet sails
26 November - 7 December 1941 · Washington, D.C., and Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, United States
Through late 1941 Ambassador Kichisaburo Nomura and special envoy Saburo Kurusu held talks in Washington with Secretary of State Cordell Hull, holding out hope of a negotiated settlement. On 26 November 1941 a Japanese carrier fleet secretly left the Kuril Islands for Hawaii while the talks continued. On 7 December the envoys reached Hull's office with Japan's reply shortly after 2 p.m. Washington time, after the attack on Pearl Harbor had already begun.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Courteous diplomacy continued in Washington while a striking force was already on its way, so the talks gave no warning of what was coming.
The kural says a foe's humble, bowing speech should not be trusted, just as a bow bends only to do harm, and the polite negotiations ran side by side with the preparation of the attack.
