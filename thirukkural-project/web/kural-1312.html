Kural 1312

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

ஊடி இருந்தேமாத் தும்மினார் யாம்தம்மை நீடுவாழ் கென்பாக் கறிந்து.
Ooti irundhaemaath thumminaar yaamdhammai neetuvaazh kenpaak karindhu

Meaning

While I sat sulking, he sneezed. He knew I would have to break my silence and wish him a long life.

Elementary

Imagine you are not talking to your brother after a squabble, and he pretends to sneeze so that you will say ‘Bless you!’ without thinking. Now you have spoken, and the game is over. That is the clever trick the husband plays here.

University

This couplet is the wife’s speech (talaivi kūṟṟu): ūṭi iruntēmā means ‘as we were sulking’, and she reports that he sneezed (tummiṉār) knowing (aṟintu) that we would say nīṭu vāḻka (‘live long’) to him. It rests on the old Tamil custom of blessing a person who sneezes, which forces the sulking wife to speak and so end her silence. Solomon Pappaiah adds that he sneezed deliberately and that she, despite herself, did bless him; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it the same way. Pope renders it ‘One day we silent sulked; he sneezed: the reason well I knew; he thought that I, to speak well pleased, would say, “Long life to you!”’ The text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the ninth canto of the Gita Govinda, Radha’s friend urges her to drop her proud sulk (mana) toward Krishna, who is eager to make up, telling her not to be proud with Madhava.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 9 (paraphrase) [3]
Sanskrit love poetry treats the beloved’s sulk as something that must eventually be coaxed into giving way.
The husband’s sneeze is his own trick for making the sulking wife give way and speak.
Christianity
“Then he returned, and walked in the house to and fro; and went up, and stretched himself upon him: and the child sneezed seven times, and the child opened his eyes.”
— 2 Kings 4:35 (KJV) [4]
When Elisha revives the Shunammite’s son, the child’s sneezing is the first sign that life has returned.
The sneeze in the Kural also signals life, which is why custom answers it with a wish for long life.
Islam
“If anyone of you sneezes, he should say ‘Al-Hamduli l-lah’ (Praise be to Allah), and his (Muslim) brother or companion should say to him, ‘Yar-hamuka-l-lah’ (May Allah bestow his Mercy on you).”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6224 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet makes answering a sneeze with a blessing a duty of companionship.
The husband counts on just such a duty: a sneeze obliges the person beside you to bless you, even mid-quarrel.
Judaism
“The members of the house of Rabban Gamliel would not say good health when someone sneezed in the study hall, due to the fact that it would lead to suspension of study in the study hall.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 53a (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud takes for granted that people wish ‘good health’ (marpe) to someone who sneezes, noting only one setting where it was skipped.
The husband relies on the same reflex: a sneeze draws out a wish for health, here the Tamil ‘Live long!’.
Buddhism
In the Vinaya, when the Buddha sneezed while teaching, the monks called out ‘Long life to you, Lord!’; he asked whether such a wish could make anyone live or die and forbade monks to say it, but later allowed them to answer laypeople who wished them long life.
— Vinaya, Cullavagga V.33 (paraphrase) [7]
The story records the same custom of answering a sneeze with ‘long life’, and the Buddha’s cool view of it.
The Kural’s whole joke depends on this very custom of wishing long life to someone who sneezes.
Jainism
“As an impassioned deer allured (by a song) rushes into death, without being satisfied with the sound.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira’s teaching warns that the ear, like the eye, can be used to lure a being toward what it longs for.
The husband’s sneeze is a sound used as a lure, drawing his wife out of her silence before she can stop herself.
Sikhism
“Good omens and bad omens affect those who do not keep the Lord in the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 401, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan teaches that one who remembers God is not ruled by signs and omens.
The Kural's husband sneezes to force his sulking wife to speak the customary blessing; Gurbani sets aside such customs around signs, saying they bind only those who forget God.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, as Penelope speaks of the hoped-for return of Odysseus, Telemachus sneezes loudly so that the hall rings, and Penelope laughs and takes the sneeze as a good omen for her words.
— Homer, Odyssey 17.541-547 (paraphrase) [10]
For Homer’s Greeks, a sneeze at a meaningful moment was a sign worth answering.
In the Kural too, a well-timed sneeze carries meaning and demands a response from the woman who hears it.
Philosophy
The Aristotelian Problems asks why people regard a sneeze as sacred or divine while they do not treat a cough that way, and tries to explain the custom.
— [Aristotle], Problems 33.7 (paraphrase) [11]
Greek natural philosophy noticed that sneezes, unlike other bodily noises, were greeted with reverence and good wishes.
The husband exploits exactly this special status of the sneeze, which compels a blessing.

Real Life Example

Fred Ott sneezes for Edison's camera
January 1894 · Orange, New Jersey, United States
When a journalist from Harper's Weekly asked Thomas Edison's laboratory for someone to perform a sneeze for the Kinetograph camera, the mechanic Fred Ott agreed. W. K. L. Dickson and William Heise filmed his sneeze in the first week of January 1894, and the record was deposited for copyright at the Library of Congress on 9 January 1894 as Edison Kinetoscopic Record of a Sneeze. It became one of the most famous early motion pictures.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sneeze was performed on purpose, at a request, so that it would be seen and noticed, which made an ordinary bodily act into a famous piece of film history.
In the kural the husband sneezes on purpose, knowing it will force his sulking wife to speak, and Fred Ott's sneeze is a documented case of a sneeze made deliberately to get a reaction.
