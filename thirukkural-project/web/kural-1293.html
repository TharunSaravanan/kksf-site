Kural 1293

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

கெட்டார்க்கு நட்டார்இல் என்பதோ நெஞ்சேநீ பெட்டாங்கு அவர்பின் செலல்.
Kettaarkku nattaar-il enpadho nenjaenee pettaangu avarpin selal.

Meaning

My heart, is it because "people who are ruined have no friends" that you leave me and follow him as you please?

Elementary

There is an old saying that when someone is in trouble, their friends disappear. The wife feels sad and alone, and even her own heart has left her to go after her husband. So she asks her heart, ‘Did you leave me because I am having a hard time?’

University

The first line quotes a proverb: keṭṭārkku naṭṭār il, ‘the ruined have no friends’; the wife bitterly applies it to herself, worn down by separation. Peṭṭāṅku means ‘as you please, at your own desire’ and avar piṉ celal ‘going after him’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read keṭṭār as ‘those ruined by suffering’, and Kalaignar puts it as ‘those destroyed by sorrow have no friends to stand by them’; Pope renders ‘The ruined have no friends, they say; and so, my heart, / To follow him, at thy desire, from me thou dost depart.’ The rhetorical question (eṉpatō, ‘is it that…?’) carries the chapter’s tone of affectionate reproach. The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As a boat on the water is swept away by a strong wind, even one of the senses on which the mind focuses can carry away a man’s intelligence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.67 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna warns that the mind, once fixed on one object, can sweep a person’s judgement away with it.
The wife’s heart, fixed on her husband, is carried off after him, leaving her judgement behind.
Christianity
“For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”
— Matthew 6:21 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that the heart follows whatever a person values most.
The wife’s treasure is her husband, so her heart goes where he is, whatever she tells it.
Islam
“Have you seen the one who takes as his god his own desire? Then would you be responsible for him?”
— Qur’an 25:43 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes a person who simply follows wherever desire leads.
The wife’s heart goes peṭṭāṅku, ‘as it pleases’, following its desire rather than her will.
Judaism
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth; I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Songs 3:1 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman in the Song rises and goes out through the city in search of her beloved.
Her soul goes after the one it loves, just as the wife’s heart follows her husband.
Buddhism
“Dwelling in the cave (of the heart), the mind, without form, wanders far and alone. Those who subdue this mind are liberated from the bonds of Mara.”
— Dhammapada 37 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes the mind as roaming far from its owner on its own.
The wife’s heart has wandered far off after her husband and left her behind.
Jainism
When Prince Neminatha turned back from his own wedding procession and renounced the world, his bride Rajimati was left grief-stricken; yet her heart stayed fixed on him, and in time she too renounced and followed the path he had taken.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (Rathanemi) (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain tradition tells of a bride whose love for Nemi led her to follow him even after he left her.
Like the wife’s heart in the Kural, Rajimati’s heart goes after the one who has gone away from her.
Sikhism
“Emotional attachment to children and spouse is poison; in the end, no one will go along with you as your helper.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 41, Siree Raag, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das warns that worldly ties do not stay with us when we most need them.
The Kural quotes the proverb that the ruined have no friends and feels even her heart has left her; Gurbani makes the same point that no worldly companion stays to the end.
Greek Mythology
Clytie, abandoned by the sun god Helios, sits on the bare ground day after day, turning her face to follow his course across the sky until she becomes the heliotrope, the flower that always faces the sun.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.256-270 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid tells of a rejected lover whose whole being keeps turning after the one who left her.
Clytie’s gaze follows Helios just as the wife’s heart follows her husband.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes tells how humans were once double beings who were split in two, so that each half now longs for and pursues its missing other half.
— Plato, Symposium 189c-193d (paraphrase) [11]
The myth explains love as the pull of a part toward the whole it belongs to.
The wife’s heart behaves as if it belongs with him, and so it leaves her to go after its other half.

Real Life Example

Emma Hamilton dies in debt and exile at Calais
15 January 1815 · Calais, France
On the morning of the Battle of Trafalgar in 1805, Admiral Lord Nelson added a codicil to his will asking the British government to provide for Emma Hamilton, but the government, which gave money and honours to Nelson’s family, ignored her. Deep in debt, she had to sell Nelson’s house at Merton and her mementoes of him, and in 1813 she was arrested for debt and lived within the bounds of the King’s Bench debtors’ prison, cut off by many people she had once entertained lavishly. She fled abroad to escape her creditors and died at Calais on 15 January 1815.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Once Nelson was gone and her fortunes collapsed, the society and state that had celebrated her abandoned her.
The kural quotes the saying that the ruined have no friends, and Emma Hamilton’s last years are a textbook case of friends vanishing once misfortune struck.
