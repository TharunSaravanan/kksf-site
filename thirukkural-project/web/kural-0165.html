Kural 165

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அழுக்காறு உடையார்க்கு அதுசாலும் ஒன்னார் வழுக்காயும் கேடீன் பது.
Azhukkaaru udaiyaarkku adhusaalum onnaar Vazhukkaayum kedeenbadhu.

Meaning

For jealous people, jealousy alone is enough to ruin them. Even if their enemies fail to hurt them, their own jealousy will bring them down.

Elementary

Imagine someone who never needs a bully to hurt them, because they keep hurting themselves by feeling jealous all the time. Envy is like a bully that lives inside you.

University

The phrase அது சாலும் (“that is enough”) makes envy a self-sufficient engine of destruction. No external enemy (ஒன்னார்) is needed, and even if enemies falter (வழுக்காயும்), envy does not. The same insight appears across traditions: Proverbs calls envy “the rottenness of the bones,” Ovid pictures Envy gnawing herself, and the Dhammapada compares the depraved man to a creeper that strangles its own host tree. Aristotle's definition explains why: envy is pain at another's good that brings the envier no gain, only suffering.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“These six are always miserable, viz., the envious, the malicious, the discontented, the irascible, the ever-suspicious, and those depending upon the fortunes of others.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 33 (Vidura Niti), Ganguli translation [3]
Vidura tells King Dhritarashtra that the envious come first among those who are always unhappy, whatever their outward circumstances.
Both texts say envy produces its own misery; the envious need no enemy.
Christianity
“A sound heart is the life of the flesh: but envy the rottenness of the bones.”
— Proverbs 14:30 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says envy rots a person from the inside, while a calm heart gives life.
Envy destroys from within, needing no enemy, just as the Kural says.
Islam
“Avoid envy, for envy devours good deeds just as fire devours fuel or (he said) “grass.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4903 [5]
The hadith says envy consumes a person's own store of good deeds, as fire eats up wood.
Both texts describe envy as a self-consuming fire that destroys the envier.
Judaism
“Rabbi Elazar Ha-kappar said: envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah names envy (kin'ah) first among three passions that destroy a person.
Like the Kural, it treats envy as enough on its own to ruin someone.
Buddhism
“Just as a single creeper strangles the tree on which it grows, even so, a man who is exceedingly depraved harms himself as only an enemy might wish.”
— Dhammapada 162 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares a corrupt person to a tree choked by a creeper growing on it: he does to himself what an enemy would want to do.
This matches the Kural's point that the envious need no enemy because they are their own.
Jainism
“The god Saṅgamaka, hearing Śakra praise Mahāvīra's steadfastness, set out to break him: “His jealousy increased greatly when he saw Śrī Vīra standing so, undisturbed, a disinterested friend of the world.” After six months of failed torments he lamented, “how long have I wandered over the earth, alas! deceived by my own stupidity!”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X (Mahāvīracaritra), ch. IV, Part 4 “Attacks by Saṅgamaka” (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS) [8]
Hemacandra's account has the envious god throw every terror at Mahāvīra without disturbing his meditation, while Saṅgamaka himself breaks his vow, loses his radiance and is expelled from heaven.
Valluvar says envy alone suffices to ruin the envier even if his foes do him no harm, and Saṅgamaka's jealousy left its target untouched while destroying Saṅgamaka himself.
Sikhism
“In jealousy, the body is not at peace; without the Fear of God, multitudes are ruined.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 933, Raag Raamkalee, Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Dakhni Oankar Guru Nanak teaches that jealousy destroys a person's peace from within, and that those without reverence for God are brought to ruin.
The Kural says envy alone is enough to ruin a man even if his enemies fail; Guru Nanak likewise places the destruction inside the envious person himself.
Greek Mythology
“She finds men's successes disagreeable, and pines away at the sight. She gnaws and being gnawed is also her own punishment.”
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.760-786 (trans. A. S. Kline) [10]
Ovid personifies Envy (Invidia) as a figure who eats vipers' flesh and wastes away at others' success: she is her own torment.
Ovid pictures exactly what the Kural states: envy punishes the envier without any outside enemy.
Philosophy
“Envy is pain at the sight of such good fortune as consists of the good things already mentioned; we feel it towards our equals; not with the idea of getting something for ourselves, but because the other people have it.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.10 (trans. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle defines envy as pain at another's good that brings no gain to the envier.
If envy is pain with no gain, it harms only the envier, which is why the Kural calls it “enough” to ruin a person.

Real Life Example

The Bone Wars ruin Cope and Marsh
1870s-1890s · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; New Haven, Connecticut; and the American West, United States
The palaeontologists Edward Drinker Cope and Othniel Charles Marsh, once friends, spent decades in a bitter competition to find and name dinosaur fossils in the American West. In January 1890 Cope gave a journalist material accusing Marsh of plagiarism and misuse of federal funds, and the New York Herald ran the charges for weeks. Congress then cut the Geological Survey's budget, Marsh lost his paleontology post and had to hand over many fossils, while Cope, who had already lost his fortune, was left with little but his collection and died in 1897.
— Sources: [12] [13]
PBS and National Geographic describe the feud as ruining both men, who did each other far more damage than any outside opponent.
The kural says envy alone is enough to destroy the envious even if no enemy harms them, and Cope and Marsh's rivalry brought down both of them.
