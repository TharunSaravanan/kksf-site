Kural 578

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

கருமம் சிதையாமல் கண்ணோட வல்லார்க்கு உரிமை உடைத்திவ் வுலகு.
Karumam sidhaiyaamal kannoda vallaarkku Urimai udaiththiv vulagu.

Meaning

The world truly belongs to those who can show kindness without letting their duty be harmed.

Elementary

A good referee is friendly and kind to all the players, but still calls a foul when someone breaks the rules. Valluvar says the best leaders are like that: kind to everyone, but they never let kindness stop them from doing what is right.

University

கருமம் here means one’s proper task, and for a king Parimelazhagar specifies it as the administration of justice (the ytamizh English gloss adds ‘administration of justice’ in brackets); சிதையாமல் means ‘without letting it be spoiled’. The couplet thus sets a limit on kannottam: kindness toward familiar people must never become favouritism that ruins just government, which explains the அளவினால் (‘in due measure’) of 574. உரிமை உடைத்து இவ்வுலகு (‘this world has rightful ownership [for them]’) promises that such rulers are the world’s natural possessors. Modern commentators generalise the principle to anyone who is compassionate without neglecting their duties (Kalaignar: ‘first in not failing duty and in showering mercy’). Both texts agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi forms கருமஞ் சிதையாமற் … வல்லார்க் / குரிமை யுடைத்திவ்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya teaches that a king severe with the rod terrifies his subjects, one too mild with it is despised, and one who wields it justly is respected.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.4 [3]
The Arthashastra demands a balance: neither harshness nor softness, but punishment measured by justice.
This is the Kural’s balance of kindness that does not ruin duty, seen from the side of the king’s rod.
Christianity
“Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee? She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more.”
— John 8:10-11 (KJV) [4]
Jesus shows mercy to the woman accused of adultery while still clearly naming the wrong and calling her to change.
Mercy without abandoning the moral standard is exactly the Kural’s kindness that does not damage duty.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct and giving to relatives and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression.”
— Quran 16:90 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran joins ʿadl (justice) and iḥsān (excellence or kindness) in a single command, so that neither is to be sacrificed to the other.
Justice held together with kindness is the combination the Kural says wins the world.
Judaism
“Mercy and truth preserve the king: and his throne is upholden by mercy.”
— Proverbs 20:28 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that a king’s rule is secured by mercy (ḥesed) together with truth.
As the Kural says the world belongs to the ruler who is kind without neglecting duty, Proverbs says mercy with truth upholds the throne.
Buddhism
The Jatakas list ten royal virtues (dasa raja dhamma) for a righteous king, including generosity, gentleness (maddava), patience (khanti) and non-violence, alongside moral conduct and adherence to what is right.
— Jataka 534 (Mahahamsa Jataka) and related Jataka tradition of dasa raja dhamma [6]
The Buddhist ideal king combines gentleness and patience with steady righteousness.
This list of royal virtues pairs kindness with righteousness, just as the Kural does.
Jainism
“Virodhi Himsa is the kind of injury which is necessarily committed in defense of person and property, against thieves, robbers, dacoits, assailants and enemies, in meeting their aggression, and in causing the least possible injury, necessary in the circumstances, in which one may find oneself.”
— 'The Concept of Himsa', Jain philosophy pages on jainworld.com [7]
Jain lay ethics requires a householder to give up intentional violence but recognises that protecting people and property may require force, always kept to the least possible injury.
The Kural says the world belongs to those who are kind without letting their duty suffer; Jain ethics likewise lets the householder, or the ruler, do the duty of protection while keeping the injury done to a minimum.
Sikhism
“They are kind to all beings; they have overpowered the five thieves.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1357, Salok Sehskritee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes the holy as joining kindness to all beings with firm mastery over lust, anger, greed, attachment and pride.
The Kural says the world belongs to those who show kindly regard without letting their duty be spoiled; Guru Arjan likewise pairs kindness with a discipline that it is never allowed to weaken.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Eumenides, Athena founds the Areopagus court to try Orestes and then persuades the vengeful Furies to accept honour in Athens, turning them into the ‘Kindly Ones’ without abolishing justice.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides [9]
The myth shows justice kept intact while vengeance is softened into benevolence.
Athena achieves what the Kural praises: kindness that does not destroy the work of justice.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes equity (epieikeia) as a correction of the law where its generality makes it unjust, and the equitable person as one who takes less than his strict due though the law is on his side.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V.10 [10]
Equity lets humane judgement soften the law without abandoning justice itself.
Aristotle’s equity is the kind of measured mildness the Kural asks for: kind, yet never spoiling justice.

Real Life Example

George Washington's Newburgh Address to his discontented officers
15 March 1783 · Newburgh, New York, United States
Near the end of the American Revolutionary War, Continental Army officers who had gone months or years without pay or promised pensions circulated an anonymous address threatening to defy Congress. George Washington forbade the meeting they had called and summoned the officers himself on 15 March 1783, where he read a speech that sympathised with their grievances but condemned the methods being proposed. The next day the officers passed a unanimous resolution commending Washington, the threatened mutiny ended, and Washington kept pressing Congress until the officers were granted five years' full pay.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Washington showed real understanding of his officers' hardship without letting that sympathy override his duty to uphold civilian authority.
The kural says the world belongs to those who can show kindly regard without letting their duty be damaged, and at Newburgh Washington's sympathy for his men and his duty to the republic stood side by side.
