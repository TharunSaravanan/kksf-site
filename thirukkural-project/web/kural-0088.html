Kural 88

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

பரிந்தோம்பிப் பற்றற்றேம் என்பர் விருந்தோம்பி வேள்வி தலைப்படா தார்.
Parindhombip patratrem enbar virundhombi, Velvi thalaippadaa thaar.

Meaning

Those who never cared for guests will one day cry, ‘We worked hard and saved, but now we have nothing to hold on to.’

Elementary

Picture someone who spends their whole life putting food in a locked cupboard and never once shares it. One day they open the cupboard and it's empty, or they're too old to eat it — and they say, sadly, “What was all that for?” This kural says that's what happens when you save everything and give nothing.

University

The verse is structured as a reported lament: பற்றற்றேம், 'we have become without support/without anything to grasp,' is put directly in the mouths of the failed householders, a rhetorical device Valluvar uses sparingly and to great effect. பரிந்து ('with pain, with anxious care') and ஓம்பி ('guarding') together characterise hoarding as an exhausting labour, quietly contrasting with the same verb ஓம்பி used throughout the chapter for tending guests: the same effort, spent on goods rather than people, ends in destitution. The word வேள்வி again marks hospitality as sacrifice, and தலைப்படாதார் means those who never even embarked on it. The pathos of the verse lies in its temporality — it describes a realisation that arrives too late — placing it in the long tradition of wisdom literature about the rich fool, from Qohelet through the Gospels to the Stoic epistles.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But he who does not feed these five, the gods, his guests, those whom he is bound to maintain, the manes, and himself, lives not, though he breathes.”
— Manusmriti 3.72 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East) [4]
Manu's verdict on the householder who omits the five daily offerings is not that he is punished later but that he is already, in the present tense, not alive in any meaningful sense.
The same judgement as the Kural: a life of accumulation without the sacrifice of hospitality amounts to having nothing at all.
Christianity
“But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided?”
— Luke 12:20 (King James Version) [5]
The rich man in the parable has just resolved to pull down his barns and build bigger ones so he can store everything and take his ease; God interrupts the plan with the question of who will actually get the goods.
The parable stages the exact lament of the Kural — a lifetime's careful hoarding ending in the recognition that it secured nothing.
Islam
“Who collects wealth and [continuously] counts it. He thinks that his wealth will make him immortal.”
— Qur'an 104:2–3 (Sahih International) [6]
Surah al-Humazah opens with a curse on the scorner and mocker, and identifies his defining behaviour as the compulsive tallying of a fortune he believes will secure him permanently.
Names the same futile labour the Kural describes — anxious guarding of goods that in the end supports nothing.
Judaism
“There is a sore evil which I have seen under the sun, namely, riches kept for the owners thereof to their hurt.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:13 (King James Version) [5]
Qohelet observes that hoarded wealth does not merely fail to benefit its owner but actively harms him, and goes on to describe such riches perishing through bad venture and leaving nothing for his son.
Identical diagnosis to the Kural: wealth guarded rather than shared turns into the source of the owner's own destitution.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The verse was spoken, according to the commentary, in connection with a famously unrivalled act of almsgiving, and contrasts two ministers — one who rejoiced at the giving and one who could only see the waste.
Draws the same line the Kural draws: the one who never gave ends up with nothing on the far side of his own effort.
Jainism
“He who in this life has done no good actions and has not practised the Law, repents of it in the next world when he has become a prey to Death.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 13.21 (cf. 13.24) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Citra warns his rich former brother, King Brahmadatta, that wealth and kin are left behind at death and only karma goes with a person. Whoever neglects good deeds will regret it too late.
Valluvar has hoarders who never took up hospitality lament too late that they have nothing to hold on to, and the Jain sage gives the same warning of a regret that comes only after death.
Sikhism
“For the sake of this wealth, so many were ruined; because of this wealth, so many have been disgraced. It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In an Aasaa hymn, Guru Nanak reflects on hoarded wealth that brings its owners ruin and cannot be carried beyond death.
It echoes the late lament of the Kural's hoarders, who toiled to guard their goods and are left with nothing to hold.
Greek Mythology
Midas, granted his wish that everything he touched turn to gold, finds that his bread hardens in his hands and his wine solidifies in his throat; surrounded by unimaginable riches he is starving, and begs the god to take the gift back.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85–145 (Midas) [10]
Ovid's telling makes the point with almost mathematical cruelty — the more perfectly wealth is accumulated, the less it can be used or shared.
Midas starving amid his gold is the visual form of the Kural's lament: everything hoarded, nothing to hold on to.
Philosophy
Epictetus instructs the student never to say of anything that he has lost it, but rather that he has given it back — since nothing we hold, including property and the people in our household, was ever ours to begin with.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 11 [11]
The Stoic point is that the language of loss presupposes a false claim of ownership, and that the pain of the lament is generated by that false claim rather than by the event.
Diagnoses the very cry the Kural puts in the hoarder's mouth — 'we have nothing left to hold' — as the predictable end of a life spent gripping.

Real Life Example

The fall of Heshen, the Qianlong emperor's favourite
1799 · Beijing, China
Heshen rose from imperial bodyguard to the highest offices of the Qing state under the Qianlong emperor and amassed an enormous private fortune through corruption. When Qianlong died in February 1799, his son the Jiaqing emperor had Heshen arrested, stripped of all his posts and condemned to death, and his vast estate was confiscated. Official inventories of the seized wealth, possibly exaggerated by his enemies, listed huge quantities of silver, pawnshops, furs and gold.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A lifetime spent gathering and guarding wealth for himself ended with Heshen losing all of it at a single stroke.
The kural says those who never took up the sacrifice of hospitality will one day lament that they toiled and saved and have nothing to hold on to, and Heshen's hoard was taken from him entirely.
