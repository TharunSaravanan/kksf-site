Kural 580

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

பெயக்கண்டும் நஞ்சுண் டமைவர் நயத்தக்க நாகரிகம் வேண்டு பவர்.
Peyakkandum nanjundu amaivar nayaththakka Naagarigam vendu pavar.

Meaning

People who want the kind of politeness everyone admires will even drink poison they watched a friend pour for them, and still stay friends.

Elementary

Suppose a friend gives you a cookie that tastes awful, and you know they didn’t make it right. A very gracious person might eat it and still smile, because they care more about the friend than the cookie. Valluvar tells this as an extreme story to show how far true kindness can go.

University

The couplet is deliberately hyperbolic: பெயக் கண்டும் (‘even having seen it poured’) and நஞ்சு உண்டு அமைவர் (‘they drink the poison and remain calm/content’) describe people who knowingly accept harm from someone close rather than break the bond. நாகரிகம் here does not mean modern ‘civilisation’; Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar take it as the refined kannottam, the urbane courtesy that is நயத்தக்க (‘worthy of being desired’ by all). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah specify that the poison is offered by those one has long associated with, and the Sangam anthology Natrinai (poem 355) is frequently cited for the closely related idea that the truly refined will drink even poison offered by friends. Commentators treat the couplet as illustrating the extreme limit of kannottam rather than a literal instruction. Both texts agree word for word.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the gods and demons churn the ocean of milk, the deadly poison halahala rises first and threatens all the worlds; out of compassion Shiva drinks it and holds it in his throat, which turns blue, earning him the name Nilakantha.
— Bhagavata Purana 8.7 [3]
Shiva knowingly takes poison upon himself for the sake of others, showing the highest kind of grace.
Like the refined person of the Kural, Shiva drinks poison he has seen, calmly, out of care for others.
Christianity
“He it is, to whom I shall give a sop, when I have dipped it. And when he had dipped the sop, he gave it to Judas Iscariot, the son of Simon.”
— John 13:26 (KJV) [4]
At the Last Supper Jesus, knowing Judas will betray him, still shares food with him as a sign of fellowship.
Keeping table fellowship with one known to be preparing harm is the Kural’s courtesy that drinks the poison a companion pours.
Islam
“A Jewess brought a poisoned (cooked) sheep for the Prophet (ﷺ) who ate from it. She was brought to the Prophet and he was asked, ‘Shall we kill her?’ He said, ‘No.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2617 [5]
The Prophet was served poisoned meat, suffered its effects, and still refused to have the woman who served it killed.
Enduring poison served by a host and refusing revenge is a striking historical parallel to the Kural’s image.
Judaism
“But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive. Now therefore fear ye not: I will nourish you, and your little ones. And he comforted them, and spake kindly unto them.”
— Genesis 50:20-21 (KJV) [4]
Joseph, once sold into slavery by his own brothers, forgives them and promises to provide for them.
Joseph swallowed the ‘poison’ his kin gave him and still kept the bond, which is the Kural’s gracious courtesy.
Buddhism
At Pava the smith Cunda serves the Buddha a dish (sukara-maddava) after which he falls gravely ill; the Buddha eats it, then asks Ananda to reassure Cunda that the last meal given to a Buddha brings great merit, so that no one blames him.
— Digha Nikaya 16, Maha-parinibbana Sutta [6]
Even as the meal leads to his final illness, the Buddha’s concern is to protect his host from remorse and blame.
Accepting a harmful dish from a devoted host while guarding the bond is close to the Kural’s image of drinking a friend’s poison calmly.
Jainism
The monk Dharmaruchi, breaking a month-long fast, was served a dish of bitter gourd that his teacher recognised as deadly; sent to dispose of it, he saw ants die after touching a drop, and rather than let more creatures die he ate the whole poisonous dish himself and died calmly.
— Dharmaruchi Muni story (Jnatadharmakatha tradition), as retold on jainworld.com [7]
This story, from the Jnatadharmakatha tradition, shows a monk knowingly swallowing poison he has seen served to him rather than cause harm.
The Kural praises those who calmly drink poison they have seen poured; Dharmaruchi does this literally, though his motive is compassion for small beings rather than courtesy toward a friend.
Sikhism
“Your Will seems so sweet to me; whatever You do, is pleasing to me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 713, Raag Todee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan prays to accept whatever God sends, bitter or sweet, as pleasing; Sikh tradition remembers him for keeping this acceptance through his torture and martyrdom in 1606.
The Kural praises those who, for the sake of courtesy, will calmly drink poison poured for them by a friend; Guru Arjan's acceptance of whatever comes as sweet is the Sikh counterpart of such calm endurance.
Greek Mythology
The wise centaur Chiron, teacher and friend of heroes, is accidentally struck by one of Heracles’ arrows dipped in the Hydra’s poison; unable to heal, he bears it without enmity toward Heracles and finally gives up his immortality.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5.4; Ovid, Fasti 5.379-414 [9]
Chiron endures a poison that came from a friend’s hand without turning against him.
Chiron bearing poison from a friend without resentment echoes the Kural’s refined person who drinks poison from a companion.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedo, the servant of the Eleven who brings Socrates the hemlock weeps as he says farewell, and Socrates speaks warmly of the man’s kindness before calmly drinking the cup.
— Plato, Phaedo 116b-117c [10]
Socrates meets the bearer of his poison with graciousness and drinks it with complete composure.
His calm, courteous acceptance of the cup is a classical image of the Kural’s refinement that drinks poison and stays at peace.

Real Life Example

John Adams and Thomas Jefferson renew their friendship
1812 · Quincy, Massachusetts, and Monticello, Virginia, United States
John Adams and Thomas Jefferson, partners in the American Revolution, became bitter political opponents, and after Jefferson defeated Adams in the 1800 presidential election the two men stopped writing to each other. Their mutual friend Benjamin Rush spent years urging them to make peace, and in 1812 Adams wrote to Jefferson and Jefferson replied. They then corresponded until both died on 4 July 1826, the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Two men who had wounded each other deeply in public life chose to set the injuries aside and value the friendship above them.
The kural praises those who, for the sake of courtesy and friendship, will swallow even a bitter injury from a friend and stay at peace with him, as Adams and Jefferson did for the last fourteen years of their lives.
