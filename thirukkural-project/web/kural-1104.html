Kural 1104

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

நீங்கின் தெறூஉம் குறுகுங்கால் தண்ணென்னும் தீயாண்டுப் பெற்றாள் இவள்.
Neengin theruum kurugungaal thannennum theeyaandup petraal ival.

Meaning

If I move away it burns me, and if I come near it cools me. Where did she get such a strange fire?

Elementary

Have you ever felt grumpy and hot inside when you had to leave someone you love, and calm again as soon as you were back together? The lover says she must own a magic fire: it burns when you go away and feels cool and gentle when you come close. Real fire does the opposite!

University

The conceit rests on reversing the nature of fire: ordinary fire burns those who come near and leaves those who move away, but this fire teṟūum (தெறூஉம், ‘burns’, with the vowel lengthening called uyiraḷapeṭai for metre) when he leaves (nīṅkiṉ) and taṇ eṉṉum (‘turns cool’) when he draws near (kuṟukuṅkāl). The question tī yāṇṭup peṟṟāḷ (‘from where did she get the fire?’) is wonder rather than inquiry, and every gloss on both source sites (Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan, Kalaignar) calls it a putumaiyāṉa tī, a ‘new, unheard-of fire’; Solomon Pappaiah adds that she has lit it in his heart. The couplet anticipates the separation chapters of the kaṟpiyal, where absence is described as burning. Pope: ‘Withdraw, it burns; approach, it soothes the pain; / Whence did the maid this wondrous fire obtain?’ Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“’Twas love that caused the burning pain; / ’Tis love that eases it again; / As when, upon a sultry day, / Rain breaks, and washes grief away.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
Overhearing that Shakuntala’s fever comes from love for him, the king says the love that burns is the same love that brings relief like rain.
This is the Kural’s strange fire: love burns and love cools, depending on whether the lovers are apart or together.
Christianity
“But if they cannot contain, let them marry: for it is better to marry than to burn.”
— 1 Corinthians 7:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul advises those who burn with desire to marry, so that union quiets the burning.
As in the Kural, love burns when lovers are apart and is cooled by union.
Islam
Rumi opens the Masnavi with the reed flute that wails because it was cut from its reed-bed, and says that its cry is the fire of love, not mere wind.
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book I, opening verses (tr. R. A. Nicholson) [5]
The reed’s burning lament comes from separation from its source, and only reunion would quiet it.
Rumi’s fire of separation is the Kural’s fire that burns when the lover moves away.
Judaism
“For love is strong as death, Jealousy is cruel as the grave; The flashes thereof are flashes of fire, A very flame of the LORD. Many waters cannot quench love.”
— Song of Songs 8:6-7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs calls love a fire so fierce that no flood can put it out.
The Kural too calls love a fire, though its fire has the strange gift of turning cool at the beloved’s side.
Buddhism
“The eye is aflame. Forms are aflame... Aflame with what? Aflame with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, the fire of delusion.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 35.28, Adittapariyaya Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Fire Sermon the Buddha teaches that every sense burns with passion, and that only disenchantment puts the fire out.
Buddhism agrees that passion is fire, but it cools that fire by letting go, not by drawing near to the beloved.
Jainism
“As in a forest, full of fuel, a fire fanned by the wind cannot be extinguished, so the fire (as it were) of the senses of him who eats as he lists; it does not benefit any chaste man.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira likens the senses of an unrestrained person to a forest fire that cannot be put out.
Jainism also calls desire a fire, but holds that indulgence only feeds it, while the Kural’s lover finds it cooled by nearness.
Sikhism
“The month of Aasaarh seems burning hot, to those who are not close to their Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 134, Raag Maajh, Barah Maha, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha (song of the twelve months), Guru Arjan says the scorching summer month burns those who are far from their Husband Lord, while those near Him find peace.
The Kural's fire burns when the lover moves away and cools when he comes near; Guru Arjan likewise says separation from the Beloved burns and nearness to Him cools.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls Eros glukupikron, ‘sweet-bitter’, the loosener of limbs, an irresistible creature that both delights and pains the lover.
— Sappho, fragment 130 (Lobel-Page / Voigt numbering) [10]
For Sappho love is a single power that gives sweetness and bitterness at once.
The Kural’s fire that both scorches and cools is the same double nature of love.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus Socrates describes the lover’s soul as throbbing and burning with pain when apart from the beloved, and finding relief from its pangs and a sweet release as soon as it sees the beloved again.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251c-e (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato the lover’s soul suffers when the beloved is absent and is soothed in the beloved’s presence.
This is exactly the Kural’s fire that burns at a distance and cools close by.

Real Life Example

June Carter co-writes Ring of Fire
1963 · United States
June Carter, of the Carter Family of country musicians, co-wrote the song Ring of Fire with Merle Kilgore, picturing love as a burning ring of fire; she wrote it out of her feelings for Johnny Cash and her worries about his troubled life. Her sister Anita Carter recorded it first, and Cash's own version, recorded in March 1963 with Mexican-style horns, reached number one on the country chart and stayed there for seven weeks. June Carter and Johnny Cash married in 1968.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The song became one of the best-known love songs in American popular music, and its writer later married the man it was about.
The kural marvels at a strange fire that burns when the lover moves away and grows cool when he comes near; June Carter's song likewise pictures love itself as a fire, one that burned through the years before she and Cash were finally together.
