Kural 553

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

நாடொறும் நாடி முறைசெய்யா மன்னவன் நாடொறும் நாடு கெடும்.
Naadorum naadi muraiseyyaa mannavan Naadorum naadu kedum.

Meaning

A king who does not look into wrongs every day and give fair judgments will watch his country grow weaker every day.

Elementary

If you never look after a garden, weeds creep in a little each day until the flowers are gone. A king who doesn’t check every day for unfair things happening, and fix them, lets his whole country slowly go to ruin in the same way.

University

The couplet plays on நாள் / நாடு: நாடொறும் (daily, from நாள் + தொறும்) and நாடி (examining, from நாடு-தல்), then நாடு (country). Parimelazhagar glosses: the king who does not daily examine the wrongs occurring in his land and render fitting justice will daily ‘lose’ his country. He explains that since the country is a limb (உறுப்பு) of the king, the verb ‘perish’ is attributed to him, and that ‘losing’ here means failing to enjoy its fruits (பயன் எய்தாமை). He records an alternative construal, ‘the king’s country perishes daily’ (மன்னவன் நாடு நாள்தொறும் கெடும்), which Manakkudavar and Kalaignar adopt. For the first word, thirukkural.com prints the unassimilated form நாள்தொறும், while ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net give the sandhi form நாடொறும்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya divides the king’s day into eighths and assigns him times to hear the petitions of citizens and country people. He warns that a king who makes petitioners wait at his door, or who is hard to reach, causes confusion in the state and disaffection among the people.
— Kautilya, Arthaśāstra 1.19 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [4]
For the Arthashastra, attending daily to the people’s complaints is the core routine of kingship.
It sets out as a timetable the daily enquiry that Valluvar says keeps the kingdom from decaying.
Christianity
Jesus tells of an unjust judge who for a long time ignored a widow pleading for justice, and gave way only because she kept coming back.
— Luke 18:1-8 (KJV) [5]
The parable assumes that a judge who neglects the cries of the weak is unjust.
The judge is the negligent ruler of the Kural, who does not look into wrongs day by day.
Islam
The Prophet said that any ruler given charge of people who does not look after them with sincere care will not smell the fragrance of Paradise.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7150 [6]
The hadith makes neglect of one’s subjects a grave moral failure, not just bad administration.
It condemns the same inattentive ruler whom the Kural says will slowly lose his realm.
Judaism
“O house of David, thus saith the LORD: Execute justice in the morning, And deliver the spoiled out of the hand of the oppressor, Lest My fury go forth like fire…”
— Jeremiah 21:12 (JPS 1917) [7]
Jeremiah tells the royal house to do justice every morning and to rescue the robbed.
‘Justice in the morning’ is the Kural’s daily enquiry and justice, with ruin as the price of neglect.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sīhanāda Sutta a king fails to give wealth to the needy; poverty spreads, then theft, then weapons, killing and lying, and the lifespan and beauty of the people decline generation after generation.
— Dīgha Nikāya 26, Cakkavatti Sīhanāda Sutta [8]
The sutta traces how one royal neglect grows step by step into a society’s collapse.
It is a long-scale version of the Kural’s ‘day by day the country perishes’.
Jainism
“The day that goes by will never return; the days elapse without profit to him who acts contrary to the Law.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The two Brahmin youths tell their father that every day lived without dharma is lost for good, so dharma cannot be put off.
The Kural warns that a king who fails to do justice every day sees his country decline every day, and the Jain verse makes the same day-by-day reckoning of what is lost when dharma is neglected.
Sikhism
“They accept bribes, and block justice.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 951, Raag Raamkalee (Raamkalee ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak condemns the man who sits in the seat of judgment but takes bribes and so denies people what is rightfully theirs.
The Kural warns that a king who does not examine wrongs and render justice every day loses a little of his country each day; Guru Nanak points to the same failure at the seat of justice.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ play, Thebes is struck by blight and plague because the murder of King Laius was never investigated; Creon admits the Sphinx made the city set aside the unsolved crime.
— Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 22-30, 128-131 [11]
The city suffers because a wrong was never looked into and put right.
It dramatizes the Kural’s warning that unexamined wrongs slowly destroy the land.
Philosophy
Aristotle calls the ruler the guardian of justice, and says that for this reason a true ruler does not take more than his share and labours for others.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V.6, 1134b (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Rule, for Aristotle, is justified only by constant attention to what is just.
A ruler who fails at this guardianship is the one the Kural says wears his country away.

Real Life Example

Emperor Huizong's neglect of government and the fall of Kaifeng
1100-1127 · Kaifeng, Song China
Emperor Huizong of the Song dynasty, who reigned from 1100, is remembered as a gifted painter, calligrapher and patron of the arts, and Britannica records that he sought escape from affairs of state in the pleasures of arts and letters. When the Jurchen invaded he abdicated in favour of his son in 1125-26. The Jurchen then captured the capital, Kaifeng, and took both Huizong and his son into captivity, ending the Northern Song dynasty in 1127.
— Sources: [13] [14]
An emperor absorbed in the arts rather than the daily business of ruling saw his realm decline and finally fall.
The kural warns that a king who does not look into wrongs every day and give justice will see his country decline day by day, as Huizong's did.
