Kural 1181

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

நயந்தவர்க்கு நல்காமை நேர்ந்தேன் பசந்தவென் பண்பியார்க்கு உரைக்கோ பிற.
nayandhavarkku nalkaamai naerndhaen pasandhaven panpiyaarkku uraikko pira

Meaning

I myself agreed to let my loving husband go away. Now that my body has turned pale with longing, who else can I complain to?

Elementary

Imagine you told your best friend, ‘It’s okay, go on the trip without me,’ and then you missed them so much you felt sick. You can’t really complain to anyone, because you said yes! The wife in this couplet feels exactly that way.

University

Chapter 119 of the karpiyal (married love) section turns on pasalai or pasappu (பசலை / பசப்பு), the sallow, pallid tint that classical Tamil poetics treats as the visible sign of a wife’s grief in separation; both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net title the chapter பசப்புறு பருவரல் (pasappuṟu paruvaral, ‘the distress of having become pallid’), while this edition’s chapter list spells it பசப்பறு. In this opening couplet the heroine admits that she nērntēn (நேர்ந்தேன், ‘consented’) to nalkāmai (நல்காமை, literally ‘not showing grace’, which the modern commentators Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss as the husband’s separation); having agreed, she has forfeited the right to complain, hence yārkku uraikkō piṟa, ‘to whom else shall I tell it?’. In the traditional commentary associated with Parimelazhagar the chapter is voiced by the heroine to her confidante (tōḻi), and the irony of complaining while saying one cannot complain is the point. Pope renders: ‘I willed my lover absent should remain; / Of pining’s sickly hue to whom shall I complain?’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After Dushyanta leaves the hermitage to return to his capital, Shakuntala, who has accepted his going, sits so lost in thoughts of him that she fails even to notice the sage Durvasas at the door, and her friends must keep her sorrow to themselves.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 4 (prelude) (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa shows a young wife who agreed to her husband’s departure and is then consumed by an absence she cannot openly lament.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Shakuntala has consented to the parting and is left with a private grief she has no one to tell.
Christianity
“I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love.”
— Song of Solomon 5:8 (KJV) [4]
The bride, separated from her beloved, has no way to reach him and must ask others to carry word of her love-sickness.
The Kural’s heroine asks the same question in reverse: she is sick with love and has no one she can rightly tell.
Islam
“I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allāh, and I know from Allāh that which you do not know.”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob, grieving for the absent Joseph and rebuked by his sons for his endless sorrow, says he will bring his complaint to God alone.
The Kural’s heroine also has no human listener for her grief; Jacob’s answer is that the only fitting hearer is God.
Judaism
“The heart knoweth its own bitterness; And with its joy no stranger can intermeddle.”
— Proverbs 14:10 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that the deepest sorrows of the heart are its own and cannot really be shared with another.
The heroine’s pallor is exactly such a bitterness known only to her own heart, with no one to whom she can hand it over.
Buddhism
“So don’t make anything dear, for it’s dreadful to be far from what’s dear.”
— Dhammapada 211 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha teaches that holding someone dear makes distance from them a source of dread and pain.
The heroine has discovered just this: being far from the one she loves has turned her body pale with grief.
Jainism
“When the daughter of the king heard of the ordination of the Gina, laughter and gaiety forsook her, and she was overwhelmed with affliction.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati, betrothed to Arishtanemi, learns that he has turned away from the wedding to become a monk, and her joy vanishes.
As in the Kural, a woman is left to bear the sorrow of a beloved’s departure, though Rajimati soon turns it into her own renunciation.
Sikhism
“By the actions we have committed, we are separated from You. Please show Your Mercy, and unite us with Yourself, Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 133, Raag Maajh, Barah Maha, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha (the song of the twelve months), Guru Arjan has the separated soul-bride admit that her separation comes from her own deeds, so she can only ask the Lord for mercy and reunion.
The Kural's wife admits she herself agreed to her husband's going and so has no one to complain to; the soul-bride in Gurbani likewise owns that the separation is her own doing and turns only to the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
Penelope recalls that when Odysseus set out for Troy he took her by the wrist, entrusted the household to her care and told her what to do if he did not return; she accepted, and for twenty years she weeps for him in private while the suitors crowd her hall.
— Homer, Odyssey 18.257-270 (paraphrase) [10]
Penelope agreed to her husband’s going and is left to grieve with no one in the house she can trust with her sorrow.
Like the Kural’s wife, Penelope consented to the parting and now pines with no proper listener.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that people in grief are comforted when friends share their sorrow, as if the burden were lightened by being divided.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.11 (1171a) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, the presence of a sympathetic friend is one of the main reliefs of sorrow.
The Kural’s heroine lacks exactly that relief: she has no one to share her grief with, because she agreed to its cause.

Real Life Example

Eva Nansen at home while the Fram drifts through the Arctic
June 1893 - September 1896 · Christiania (Oslo), Norway
The singer Eva Sars married the polar explorer Fridtjof Nansen on 6 September 1889, and he was often away on expeditions. In June 1893 Nansen sailed north on the Fram, which was frozen into the Arctic ice and drifted for about three years before returning to Christiania in September 1896. During his absences Eva continued to sing at concerts and kept a warm home for the family.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Eva Nansen's life shows the long, quiet side of a famous expedition: the spouse who stays behind for years while the journey goes on.
The kural's wife says she herself let her husband go and so has no one else to blame for her pining, and Eva Nansen lived through three years of a separation that came with marrying an explorer.
