Kural 195

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

சீர்மை சிறப்பொடு நீங்கும் பயனில நீர்மை யுடையார் சொலின்.
Seermai sirappodu neengum payanila, Neermai yudaiyaar solin.

Meaning

If even good people speak empty, useless words, they lose both their good name and the respect it earned them.

Elementary

Imagine a famous, respected captain of a sports team. If she starts saying silly, pointless things all the time, the team slowly stops looking up to her. This kural says that even good people lose their respect if they fill the air with useless words.

University

This kural turns from the habitual babbler to the virtuous person who lapses. நீர்மையுடையார் (neermaiyudaiyaar) means those who have fine nature, literally a ‘watery’ or gentle disposition. The couplet warns that they are not immune: their சீர்மை (seermai, ‘excellence, eminence’) departs together with its சிறப்பு (sirappu, ‘honour, distinction’). Solomon Pappaiah adds ‘அப்பொழுதே’ (‘at that very moment’), stressing how suddenly the loss comes. The point is that reputation is a fragile social credit, and vain talk spends it faster than virtue earns it. Ecclesiastes’ image of dead flies spoiling the perfumer’s ointment is perhaps the closest cross-cultural parallel.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the Pandavas’ Maya Sabha, Duryodhana stumbles in the illusory hall and is laughed at, and the humiliation feeds the enmity behind the dice game and the war. (Popular retellings give Draupadi a cutting jibe, but this line is not found in the critical text.)
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Duryodhana’s account of the Maya Sabha; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The Pandavas were exemplary figures, yet a moment of careless mockery by them had consequences out of all proportion.
As in the Kural, the noble are not exempt: a few idle, mocking words can undo the standing that virtue has built.
Christianity
“Dead flies cause the ointment of the apothecary to send forth a stinking savour: so doth a little folly him that is in reputation for wisdom and honour.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:1 (KJV) [4]
A tiny contaminant spoils a costly perfume, and a little folly in the same way spoils the name of a respected sage.
This is almost the Kural’s exact thought: a person reputed for wisdom and honour loses both through a small lapse.
Islam
“May your mother grieve your loss O Mu’adh! Are the people tossed into the Fire upon their faces, or upon their noses, except because of what their tongues have wrought.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2616 (graded hasan, sunnah.com) [5]
Mu‘ādh ibn Jabal, one of the most learned Companions, is surprised to learn that people are held accountable for speech. The Prophet grasps his own tongue and says ‘Restrain this’.
Even a righteous, eminent believer is warned that the tongue can undo everything else, just as the Kural warns even people of good character.
Judaism
“Silence is fitting for the wise, and a fortiori the same is true for fools.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 99a (William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz trans., Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud cites this saying of the Sages, followed by Proverbs 17:28 (‘even a fool, when he holds his peace, is considered wise’).
Both texts begin with the wise. The Kural warns that the good lose standing through idle talk, and the Talmud says restraint suits even the wise.
Buddhism
“Surely, when a person is born, an axe is born in his mouth, with which he cuts himself— the fool saying a bad statement.”
— Sutta Nipata 3.10, Kokālika Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The monk Kokālika, despite having gone forth, keeps speaking against Sāriputta and Moggallāna and suffers a terrible fall.
A man in a revered position destroys himself with his own words, much as the Kural’s person of good character loses excellence and honour.
Jainism
“When a monk quarrels and uses very bad language, he will suffer great spiritual loss; therefore a wise man should not quarrel.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.2.2.19 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga warns that even a monk who has taken the vows loses much of his spiritual gain through careless, bad speech.
Valluvar warns that even people of fine character lose their excellence and honour if they speak empty words, and the Jain verse likewise says a monk's own words can cost him great spiritual loss.
Sikhism
“The insipid person is discarded in the Court of the Lord, and the insipid one's face is spat upon.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa (Asa ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Continuing the same salok of Asa ki Vaar, Guru Nanak says the person given to fikaa speech is cast out of the divine Court and disgraced.
The Kural warns that even people of fine character lose their excellence and honour when they speak empty words; Guru Nanak likewise says such speech costs a person all honour, before God as well as people.
Greek Mythology
“Thamyris “boasted that he would surpass even the Muses, daughters of aegis-bearing Jove, if they should sing against him; whereon they were angry, and maimed him. They robbed him of his divine power of song.”
— Homer, Iliad 2.594-600 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
A gifted singer loses the very excellence he was famous for because of a vain, boastful claim.
As in the Kural, a person of real excellence loses that excellence and its fame through empty words.
Philosophy
“Avoid, likewise, an endeavor to excite laughter. For this is a slippery point, which may throw you into vulgar manners, and, besides, may be apt to lessen you in the esteem of your acquaintance.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus advises the aspiring philosopher that trivial, attention-seeking talk costs dignity.
Epictetus says it directly: frivolous talk will ‘lessen you in the esteem’ of others, even if you are a serious person. That is the Kural’s point.

Real Life Example

Neville Chamberlain's return from Munich
30 September 1938 · London, England, United Kingdom
Returning from Munich, where Britain, France, Germany and Italy had agreed that Czechoslovakia must give up the Sudetenland to Germany, Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain displayed an Anglo-German declaration signed by himself and Hitler and told the crowd at Downing Street that he believed he had brought back peace for our time. Germany went on to occupy the rest of Czech lands in March 1939 and invaded Poland in September 1939, and Britain declared war. Chamberlain's phrase became a lasting byword for political naivety.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Chamberlain was a respected, hard-working statesman, yet one confident public pronouncement that proved empty did lasting damage to how he is remembered.
The kural warns that when even people of good character speak empty words, their eminence and honour slip away, and Chamberlain's reputation has never fully recovered from his promise of peace.
