Kural 192

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

பயனில பல்லார்முன் சொல்லல் நயனில நட்டார்கண் செய்தலிற் றீது.
Payanila pallaarmun sollal nayanila, Nattaarkan seydhalir reedhu.

Meaning

Talking about pointless things in front of many people is worse than being unkind to your own friends.

Elementary

If you are rude to one friend, that hurts one person. But if you stand up in front of the whole school and waste everyone’s time with silly talk, you hurt everyone’s time at once. This kural says doing that in front of many people is even worse than being unkind to a friend.

University

The couplet works through a startling comparison. Doing நயனில (nayanila, “what is devoid of goodness/propriety”) to நட்டார் (nattaar, “friends”), which is a real ethical fault, is ranked below speaking பயனில (payanila, “what is devoid of benefit”) before பல்லார் (“the many”). The two phrases rhyme (nayanila / payanila), and the parallel sound makes the ranking stick in the ear. The argument depends on scale and publicity: an unkind act toward a friend stays in a private relationship that can be repaired, while empty talk before an assembly wastes the attention of many people at once and fixes one’s public character. Modern commentators (Mu. Varadarasan, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar) all keep this ‘worse than’ reading, which shows how seriously Valluvar takes the public squandering of speech.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrihari’s Nītiśataka calls silence the ornament that the unlearned can wear in an assembly of the wise, a covering for their lack of knowledge.
— Bhartrihari, Nītiśataka (verse on silence as the ornament of the unlearned; numbering varies by edition) [3]
The gnomic poet advises that in a learned gathering the unqualified are better off saying nothing than exposing themselves.
Both texts see the assembly as the place where empty talk does the most damage, and both advise silence there.
Christianity
“But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment.”
— Matthew 12:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that even ‘idle’ (argos, workless) words are weighed at the final judgment.
Both texts insist that careless words weigh more than people assume. The Kural ranks them above unkind deeds, and Jesus makes them answerable before God.
Islam
“And [they are] those who do not testify to falsehood, and when they pass near ill speech, they pass by with dignity.”
— Qur’an 25:72 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes God’s true servants as refusing to join in laghw (vain, idle speech) when they come across it.
Both texts link dignity with keeping out of vain talk when others are present.
Judaism
“If two sit together and there are no words of Torah [spoken] between them, then this is a session of scorners.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:2 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Hananiah ben Teradion says that a gathering filled only with empty talk turns into a ‘seat of the scornful’.
Both texts judge talk by its social setting: empty words in company harm the whole gathering.
Buddhism
“It isn’t right, monks, that sons of good families, on having gone forth out of faith from home to the homeless life, should get engaged in such topics of conversation.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 10.69, Kathavatthu Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha finds monks gathered in the assembly hall chatting about kings, food, gossip and so on, and tells them to discuss ten wholesome topics instead.
Here idle talk in an assembly is corrected at once, which reflects the Kural’s concern about pointless words before many.
Jainism
“A monk should not tell stories, nor ask idle questions, nor gossip. But, knowing the highest Law, he should perform his religious duties, and regard nothing his own.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.2.2.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga tells the monk to give up story-telling, idle questions and gossip and to spend himself on his religious duties instead.
Valluvar ranks pointless talk before many people as worse than unkindness to friends, and the Jain rule likewise treats idle talk and gossip as a real fault to be given up, not a harmless habit.
Sikhism
“To speak too much and babble is useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 661, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn in Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Nanak dismisses endless talk and babbling as worthless.
The Kural ranks pointless talk before many people as worse than wronging one's friends; Guru Nanak shares the low estimate of babble, though he makes no such comparison.
Greek Mythology
Tantalus, the mortal favoured with a seat at the gods’ table, falls by an ‘unbridled tongue’ in Euripides’ telling: he betrays the confidences of his divine hosts by talking.
— Euripides, Orestes 4-10 (the ‘unbridled tongue’ of Tantalus); cf. Pindar, Olympian 1 [10]
Several versions put Tantalus’s crime in his mouth (leaking the gods’ secrets) rather than in a deed, and he suffers eternal punishment.
The Tantalus tradition, like the Kural, rates reckless speech as worse than a wrong done by hand to one’s friends.
Philosophy
Theophrastus’s sketch of ‘The Garrulous Man’ shows a man who sits beside strangers and pours out his dreams, dinners and trivial opinions to anyone within earshot.
— Theophrastus, Characters, ‘The Garrulous Man’ (Adoleschia) [11]
Aristotle’s successor catalogues the chatterer as a recognisable social type whose talk burdens every company he enters.
Theophrastus shows how public idle talk imposes on many people at once, which is why the Kural judges it so harshly.

Real Life Example

Khrushchev's intemperate speech at the farewell reception for Gomulka
November 1956 (reported by TIME on 26 November 1956) · Moscow, Soviet Union (now Russia)
At a Kremlin-attended farewell reception in Moscow for the visiting Polish leader Wladyslaw Gomulka, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev turned to the Western diplomats present and delivered a long, boastful, off-the-cuff harangue about the capitalist countries, Hungary and the Suez crisis. TIME reported that by the time he finished, the Western diplomats had left the room. One line from the speech, rendered in English as a declaration that the Soviets would bury the West, became one of the most widely remembered remarks of the Cold War.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Unplanned words spoken before a large gathering of foreign envoys did lasting damage to the speaker's image abroad, far beyond the occasion itself.
The kural warns that pointless talk before many people does more harm than an unkind act among friends, and Khrushchev's rambling remarks before the diplomatic corps were repeated around the world for decades.
