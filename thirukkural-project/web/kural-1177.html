Kural 1177

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

உழந்துழந் துள்நீர் அறுக விழைந்திழைந்து வேண்டி அவர்க்கண்ட கண்.
Uzhandhuzhan thulneer aruka vizhaindhizhaindhu vaenti avarkkanda kan.

Meaning

My eyes once looked at him with melting, eager longing. Now let them ache and ache until every tear in them is gone.

Elementary

Sometimes when we feel very sad, we get a little cross with ourselves too. The lady is so sad that she tells her own eyes, ‘You wanted to look at him so much, so now you can cry until you have no tears left!’ It is her way of showing how much she misses him.

University

The couplet is built on two reduplicated verbs: uḻantu uḻantu, ‘suffering, suffering’, and viḻaintu iḻaintu, ‘desiring, melting with tenderness’, so that the present agony mirrors the past yearning sound for sound. Uḷ nīr aṟuka is an optative, ‘may the water within be cut off’, a mock-curse on the eyes that ‘longed and saw him’ (vēṇṭi avark kaṇṭa kaṇ). Commentators differ on whether the curse is sleeplessness plus tears (Mu. Varadarasanar) or an address directly to the eyes (Kalaignar: ‘O eyes… suffer!’); Puliyur Kesikan reads viḻaintu as part of the past delight. Pope: ‘Aching, aching, let those exhaust their stream, / That melting, melting, that day gazed on him.’ No variants were found between ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Held captive in Ravana’s Ashoka grove, Sita, separated from Rama, weeps continually, grows thin and pale, and longs only to see him again.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, Hanuman finds Sita in the Ashoka grove (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki’s Sundara Kanda shows Sita wasted by tears and longing for her absent husband.
Sita’s endless weeping is what the Kural’s heroine wishes on her eyes: tears until they are spent.
Christianity
“Oh that my head were waters, and mine eyes a fountain of tears, that I might weep day and night for the slain of the daughter of my people!”
— Jeremiah 9:1 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah wishes his eyes could become an unending fountain so that he could weep enough for his grief.
Jeremiah and the Kural both speak to the eyes as wells of tears, though Jeremiah longs for more and the heroine for them to run dry.
Islam
“The eyes are shedding tears and the heart is grieved, and we will not say except what pleases our Lord, O Ibrahim! Indeed we are grieved by your separation.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1303 (Book of Funerals) [5]
At the death of his infant son, the Prophet weeps openly and names the tears of the eyes as the honest sign of the heart’s grief.
In both texts the eyes carry the whole weight of grief at separation from a beloved.
Judaism
“For these things I weep; Mine eye, mine eye runneth down with water; Because the comforter is far from me…”
— Lamentations 1:16 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Lamentations repeats ‘mine eye, mine eye’ as it pours out tears because the one who could comfort is far away.
The doubled ‘mine eye, mine eye’ echoes the Kural’s doubled uḻantu uḻantu, both for eyes weeping because the beloved comforter is absent.
Buddhism
“Ill done is that action of doing which one repents later, and the fruit of which one, weeping, reaps with tears.”
— Dhammapada 67 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada describes an action whose fruit is harvested in tears and regret.
The eyes’ eager gaze was such an action; the heroine now bids them reap its fruit in tears until none remain.
Jainism
“How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that the eye’s passion brings misery at every stage, including when the thing desired is lost and missed.
The eyes that once ‘longed and melted’ at the sight of him are now in the ‘missing’ stage the sutra describes, and the heroine says they must bear it.
Sikhism
“I cherish and remember that Friend; the eyes of this soul-bride are filled with tears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1111, Raag Tukhaari, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's soul-bride keeps remembering her divine Friend, and her eyes fill with tears as she does.
The Kural wishes that the eyes which once melted with longing as they gazed on him may ache until their tears run dry; Guru Nanak's bride remembers her Beloved with eyes that are full of tears.
Greek Mythology
After the loss of all her children, Niobe weeps until she is turned to stone, and even as a rock on a mountain she goes on shedding tears.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.301-312 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Niobe is the image of grief that drains a person of everything but tears.
The Kural’s heroine wishes such unending weeping on her eyes, though she imagines the tears finally running dry.
Philosophy
Plato tells of Leontius, who wanted to look at corpses lying by the wall, struggled against the desire, and finally ran to them with his eyes wide open, crying to his eyes, ‘Look, you wretches, take your fill of the fine sight!’
— Plato, Republic 4.439e-440a (paraphrase) [11]
Plato uses the story to show that a part of the soul can be angry with its own desires, even speaking to the eyes as separate culprits.
The heroine curses her eyes in the same way, telling them to suffer their fill for the looking they insisted on.

Real Life Example

Rembrandt draws Saskia days after their betrothal
8 June 1633 · Dutch Republic (now the Netherlands); drawing now in Berlin, Germany
On 6 June 1633 Rembrandt became engaged to Saskia van Uylenburgh, the cousin of his business partner Hendrick Uylenburgh. On 8 June he made a silverpoint drawing of her, inscribed in Dutch that it was drawn of his wife when she was 21 years old, on the third day after their betrothal; it is now in the Kupferstichkabinett in Berlin. After three children who did not survive infancy, Saskia gave birth to their son Titus, and Rembrandt's later decline in productivity followed her death.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The tender drawing preserves the moment when Rembrandt gazed on his bride-to-be with delight, before the losses that later came to the marriage.
The kural speaks of eyes that once gazed on the beloved with melting, eager longing and must now ache, and Rembrandt's betrothal drawing is a record of such a loving gaze that later turned to grief.
