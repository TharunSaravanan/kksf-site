Kural 1

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

அகர முதல எழுத்தெல்லாம் ஆதி பகவன் முதற்றே உலகு.
Agara mudhala ezhuthu ellaam aadhi, Pakavan mudhatre ulagu.

Meaning

Just as the letter A comes first among all the letters, God comes first in the whole world, as the beginning of everything.

Elementary

The alphabet always starts with the letter A, and everything else is built from it. This kural says God is like that letter A for the whole world — the very first thing, the starting point everything else comes from.

University

Valluvar opens his work not with an invocation to a specific deity but with a logical analogy drawn from language itself: just as அ (a) is structurally primary in the Tamil alphabet — the vowel from which the entire phonetic system is generated — so too is “Pahavan” (rendered here “God”/“the Primal Being”) ontologically primary to the cosmos. The term is deliberately non-sectarian, part of why the Thirukkural has been embraced across Saiva, Vaishnava, Jain, and secular-humanist readings alike. The appeal to a “first principle” or arche is one of the oldest moves in both theology and philosophy — found equally in Vedic cosmology, biblical self-declaration, and Aristotelian metaphysics.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I am the Self, seated in the hearts of all creatures. I am the beginning, the middle, and the end of all beings.”
— Bhagavad Gita 10.20 [4]
Krishna tells Arjuna he isn’t one god among many, but the very point every being’s existence starts, continues, and ends from.
Same claim as the Kural: the divine is the literal starting point that everything else comes from.
Christianity
“I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end, the first and the last.”
— Revelation 22:13 (KJV) [5]
Christ names himself using the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet, meaning he contains and precedes everything from start to finish.
Nearly identical device to the Kural’s own move — using ‘the first letter’ as a stand-in for God being the origin of all things.
Islam
“He is the First and the Last, the Ascendant and the Intimate, and He is, of all things, Knowing.”
— Qur'an 57:3, Al-Hadid (Sahih International) [6]
This verse pairs opposites — first/last, hidden/manifest — to say nothing exists before God and nothing escapes His awareness.
Matches the Kural’s basic claim that God precedes and grounds everything else that exists.
Judaism
“I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God.”
— Isaiah 44:6 (Tanakh / KJV) [5]
Spoken through the prophet Isaiah, this is God’s own claim that no deity came before Him and none will follow — He alone spans all of existence.
Same ‘first’ claim the Kural opens with, used here to establish that the divine has no rival or predecessor.
Buddhism
The Adi-Buddha (‘Primordial Buddha’) is described as self-existent (svayambhu), without beginning or end, and the source from which the other Buddhas and the universe itself emanate.
— Adi-Buddha, Kalachakra Tantra (Vajrayana Buddhism) [7]
In this much later strand of Buddhism, a single primordial awareness is described as the ultimate ground from which everything, including the historical Buddhas, arises.
The nearest thing Buddhism offers to the Kural’s ‘God is first’ claim — though it comes from one specific tantric school, not the earlier or mainstream tradition.
Jainism
“During his reign he taught, for the benefit of the people, the seventy-two sciences, of which writing is the first, arithmetic the most important, and the knowledge of omens the last”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Rishabha §§210–211 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
The Kalpa Sutra names Rishabha, the first Tirthankara, 'First King, First Mendicant, First Jina, and First Tirthakara', and credits him with giving humanity its arts, beginning with writing itself.
Jain readers have long taken the Kural's 'Adi Bhagavan' (the Primal Lord) as Rishabhanatha, and this passage gives that reading a striking fit: the first Jina is also the one who taught the first of all sciences, letters.
Sikhism
“Through these fifty-two letters, the three worlds and all things are described. These letters shall perish; they cannot describe the Imperishable Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 340, Raag Gauri Poorbi, Bavan Akhri, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir opens his acrostic on the alphabet by saying every letter points beyond itself to the One who precedes all speech; the same composition goes on, “I know only the One, the Universal Creator, the Primal Being.” The Guru Granth Sahib itself begins with the numeral 1 in Ik Onkar, putting the One before every word that follows.
Like Valluvar, Kabir uses the alphabet to say that God is first and is the source of everything the letters go on to describe.
Greek Mythology
“Verily at the first Chaos came to be.”
— Hesiod, Theogony, lines 116–117 [10]
Hesiod’s creation account opens by naming Chaos — a vast, formless void — as the very first thing to exist, before any god or ordered world.
Same structural claim as the Kural — something has to be ‘first’ before a world can exist — though Chaos is an impersonal void, not a conscious, virtuous being like the Kural’s God.
Philosophy
“It is necessary that there should be an eternal unmovable substance... substances are the first of existing things.”
— Aristotle, Metaphysics, Book XII (Lambda) [11]
Aristotle argues that all motion and change must ultimately trace back to something itself unmoved and eternal — his famous ‘Prime Mover.’
Independently arrives at the same conclusion as the Kural: there must be a ‘first,’ uncaused reality behind everything else.

Real Life Example

Apollo 8 astronauts read the opening of Genesis from lunar orbit
24 December 1968 · Lunar orbit (broadcast to Earth by NASA, United States)
Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and Bill Anders of Apollo 8 became the first humans to orbit the Moon, entering lunar orbit on Christmas Eve 1968. During a live television broadcast from lunar orbit, the three astronauts took turns reading the opening verses of the Book of Genesis, which describe the creation of the heavens and the earth, before wishing everyone on Earth well. The broadcast reached one of the largest audiences in history up to that time.
— Sources: [12] [13]
At the moment humans first left Earth's orbit, the crew chose to mark it with an ancient text about the origin of everything, treating the question of what comes first as the fitting thing to say from space.
Just as the kural places the Primal Being first, the way the letter A begins the alphabet, the Apollo 8 crew answered humanity's first look back at Earth with words about the beginning from which the world came.
