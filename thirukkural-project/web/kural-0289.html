Kural 289

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

அளவல்ல செய்தாங்கே வீவர் களவல்ல மற்றைய தேற்றா தவர்.
Alavalla seydhaange veevar kalavalla, Matraiya thetraa thavar.

Meaning

People who know nothing but stealing will be destroyed right there, while doing things that go beyond proper limits.

Elementary

If the only trick you know is cheating, it’s like a player who only knows how to foul — sooner or later, right in the middle of the game, they get caught and sent off.

University

The couplet says the fraudster’s ruin is not deferred but comes āṅkē — “right there,” in the act itself. The phrase kaḷavu alla maṟṟaiya tēṟṟātavar (“those who do not understand anything other than fraud”) describes a person whose repertoire has narrowed to one method, which completes the habituation argument of 288. Plato gives a striking parallel: a completely unjust person could not act at all, because cooperation depends on some remnant of justice. Acts 5’s account of Ananias, struck down while misrepresenting a sale, is the most literal illustration of perishing ‘right there.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But he who enjoys such gifts without offering them to the demigods in return is certainly a thief.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.12 [5]
Krishna calls anyone who only takes and never gives back a thief, cut off from the cycle of reciprocity that sustains life.
The person who knows nothing but taking has broken the order that sustains him, which parallels the Kural’s self-destroying fraudster.
Christianity
“Ananias and Sapphira sold a property, secretly kept back part of the price, and presented the rest as the whole. When Peter confronted him, “Ananias hearing these words fell down, and gave up the ghost”.”
— Acts 5:1-5 (KJV) [6]
A deception about the price of a sale is followed at once by death.
The most literal parallel to āṅkē vīvar — the fraudster perishes ‘right there,’ in the act.
Islam
“And O my people, give full measure and weight in justice and do not deprive the people of their due and do not commit abuse on the earth, spreading corruption.”
— Qur’an 11:85 (Sahih International); destruction narrated at 11:94 [7]
The prophet Shu’ayb warns the people of Madyan, notorious for short-weighting, to stop; the Qur’an goes on to tell how they persisted and were destroyed (11:94).
A community that knew nothing but fraud in measure perished in its measureless deeds, as the Kural warns.
Judaism
“The getting of treasures by a lying tongue Is a vapour driven to and fro; they [that seek them] seek death.”
— Proverbs 21:6 (JPS 1917) [8]
Proverbs says treasure gained by lies is a passing vapour, and those who pursue it are in effect pursuing their own death.
Matches the Kural: those committed to deceitful gain are destroyed by the very pursuit.
Buddhism
“One who destroys life, utters lies, takes what is not given, goes to another man’s wife, and is addicted to intoxicating drinks — such a man digs up his own root even in this world.”
— Dhammapada 246-247 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Dhammapada says that one who takes what is not given destroys his own foundations in this very life.
‘Digs up his own root even in this world’ matches the Kural’s ruin āṅkē — here and now.
Jainism
“As the burglar caught in the breach of the wall perishes by the work the sinner himself had executed, thus people in this life and the next cannot escape the effect of their own actions.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.3, with Jacobi's note citing Devendra's commentary (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
Jacobi's note gives the commentator Devendra's story. A burglar is caught by the feet in the hole he dug, and as his companion pulls him from the other side, the wall falls and crushes him: he dies in the very breach he made.
Valluvar says those who know nothing but fraud are ruined 'right there' (āṅkē), in the act itself. The Jain image of the burglar killed in his own breach is almost exactly that picture.
Sikhism
“O my mind, do not practice deception, even inadvertently. In the end, your own soul shall have to answer for its account.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 656, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In a hymn in Raag Sorath, Bhagat Kabir describes a man who brings home others' wealth through many deceptions. He warns that the deceiver's own soul must render the account, and none of those he fed will help him.
The Kural says those who know no way of living but fraud are destroyed by their own measureless deeds. Kabir likewise makes the deceiver's ruin the direct reckoning of his own deception.
Greek Mythology
Sisyphus, remembered as the craftiest of men for his many deceptions (including cheating death), is seen by Odysseus in the underworld endlessly pushing a huge stone uphill, only for it to roll back each time.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.593-600 [12]
A life built entirely on trickery ends in labour that achieves nothing and never ends.
The Kural’s ‘those who know nothing but fraud’ find their cleverness turned into futile, self-defeating action.
Philosophy
“for had they been whole villains, and utterly unjust, they would have been utterly incapable of action.”
— Plato, Republic I, 352c (tr. B. Jowett) [13]
Socrates argues that even a band of thieves needs some justice among its members to act; total injustice would make action impossible.
A philosophical form of the Kural’s claim: someone who knows only fraud undermines the very conditions for successful action and fails in the act.

Real Life Example

The Mona Lisa thief is caught trying to sell it
11 December 1913 · Florence, Italy
Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian former worker at the Louvre, took the Mona Lisa from the museum in August 1911 and hid it in his Paris room for more than two years. In late 1913 he wrote to Florentine art dealer Alfredo Geri offering to sell it, and Geri, together with Uffizi director Giovanni Poggi, examined the painting and alerted the police. Peruggia was arrested at his hotel in Florence on 11 December 1913 and the painting was returned to the Louvre.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
After two years of perfect concealment, the theft unravelled at the very moment the thief tried to profit from it.
The kural says those who know no way but fraud are destroyed in the very act, and Peruggia was undone in the act of trying to cash in on his theft.
