Kural 853

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இகலென்னும் எவ்வநோய் நீக்கின் தவலில்லாத் தாவில் விளக்கம் தரும்.
Ikalennum evvanoi neekkin thavalillaath thaavil vilakkam tharum.

Meaning

If you get rid of the painful disease called hostility, you will win a spotless fame that never fades.

Elementary

Imagine carrying a heavy, smelly bag of grudges everywhere you go. When you finally drop it, you feel lighter, and people start to remember you as the kind one. The poet says letting go of hostility makes you shine in a way that lasts.

University

Evvanoi (எவ்வநோய்) is a compound of evvam (எவ்வம், distress, affliction) and noi (disease): hostility is a sickness that torments its host. Its removal yields vilakkam (விளக்கம், literally ‘light, clarity’, here ‘renown, distinction’) qualified twice: thaval illaa (தவல் இல்லா, undiminishing) and thaavil (தாவில், flawless or unfailing). Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read the outcome as imperishable fame (அழியாத புகழ்), Kalaignar as ‘stainless, lasting fame’, while Sivayogi Sivakumar takes vilakkam more literally as inner clarity. The image of illness and cure continues from 851, so the chapter treats hostility medically: diagnosis (851), restraint (852), cure and its fruit (853). Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Among the divine qualities (daivī sampad) Krishna lists in Gita 16.1-3 are freedom from anger, freedom from malice (adroha) and forgiveness, which he says lead toward liberation, while the demonic qualities bind.
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-5 [3]
The Gita treats freedom from hostility as part of the radiant, divine nature.
The Kural promises a lasting radiance (vilakkam) to those who remove hostility.
Christianity
“A sound heart is the life of the flesh: but envy the rottenness of the bones.”
— Proverbs 14:30 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs contrasts a healthy heart with envy that rots a person from within.
Both texts picture ill will as a disease and its absence as health and life.
Islam
“Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good.”
— Qur’an 3:134 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who swallow their anger and forgive, calling them beloved of God.
Like the Kural, it promises honour to those who rid themselves of hostile feeling.
Judaism
“What man is he that desireth life, and loveth many days, that he may see good? ... Depart from evil, and do good; seek peace, and pursue it.”
— Psalm 34:12-14 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
The Psalm promises long life and good to the one who leaves evil and actively pursues peace.
The Kural similarly promises an unfading reward to those who put away hostility.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha declares that only the removal of hatred ends hatred, as a timeless law.
Both texts present the removal of hostility as the one cure, with a lasting result.
Jainism
In Hemacandra's life of Pārśvanātha, Kamaṭha nurses a hatred for his brother Marubhūti that lasts through many births. In the last of them, as the god Meghamālin, he attacks the meditating Pārśva with storm and flood, while Pārśva never answers with hostility. The Kalpa Sūtra gives Pārśva the lasting epithet 'the people's favourite' (puruṣādānīya).
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book IX (Pārśvanāthacaritra), tr. Helen M. Johnson; Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Pārśva (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [7]
Jain tradition contrasts the enemy whose rage drags him through birth after birth with the Tīrthaṅkara who, free of enmity, becomes the most beloved and honoured of teachers.
The Kural promises unfading renown to whoever rids himself of the disease of hostility, and Pārśva, who never returned Kamaṭha's enmity, is remembered with a lasting title of affection.
Sikhism
“Farid, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger. Your body shall not suffer from any disease, and you shall obtain everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381-1382, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 78), Bhagat Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Farid promises that one who returns good for evil and keeps anger out of the mind will be free of disease and will gain everything.
The Kural calls hostility a painful disease whose removal brings undying renown; Farid likewise ties the giving up of anger to freedom from disease and a full reward.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Eumenides, Athena persuades the vengeful Furies to give up their wrath against Athens; they become the Eumenides, ‘Kindly Ones’, and receive an honoured, permanent home and worship in the city.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides (final scenes) [9]
Spirits of hostility, once they lay aside their anger, gain lasting honour.
This mirrors the Kural’s promise of unfading distinction to whoever casts out hostility.
Philosophy
Seneca closes On Anger by urging that, since life is short, we should spend it not in hostility but in cultivating humanity, so that no one fears us and we leave a gentle memory.
— Seneca, De Ira III.42-43 [10]
Seneca argues that freedom from anger is the path to a calm life and a good name.
The Kural likewise links ending hostility with lasting good repute.

Real Life Example

Robert Schuman proposes pooling French and German coal and steel
9 May 1950 · Paris, France
On 9 May 1950 French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman proposed that French and German coal and steel production be placed under a common High Authority open to other European countries, so that war between France and Germany would become not merely unthinkable but materially impossible. The proposal led to the Treaty of Paris of 1951, which created the European Coal and Steel Community of six countries. In 1985 the date of the declaration was made Europe Day.
— Sources: [11] [12]
After three wars between France and Germany in seventy years, the declaration began the reconciliation that grew into the European Union, and Schuman is honoured as one of its founders.
The kural promises unfading renown to those who rid themselves of the disease of hostility, and Schuman's turn away from Franco-German enmity is still commemorated every year on Europe Day.
