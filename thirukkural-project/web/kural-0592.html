Kural 592

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

உள்ளம் உடைமை உடைமை பொருளுடைமை நில்லாது நீங்கி விடும்.
Ullam udaimai udaimai poruludaimai Nillaadhu neengi vidum.

Meaning

A strong, eager mind is the thing you own that lasts. Money and things do not stay. They slip away.

Elementary

A sandcastle can be washed away by the next wave, but the skill and excitement that built it stay with you, so you can build another one tomorrow. Money and things can disappear, but a strong, eager mind stays yours.

University

The couplet sets two kinds of udaimai (property) against each other: ullam udaimai, possession of ‘mind’, which Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar gloss as ookkam, and porul udaimai, possession of goods. The triple repetition ‘udaimai udaimai … udaimai’ is a deliberate sound-play (sol-pin-varunilai) that forces the reader to ask which ‘having’ deserves the name. The verbs ‘nillaadhu neengi vidum’ (does not stay, departs) echo the Aram-section chapter on impermanence (Nilaiyaamai, ch. 34), but here the lesson is practical rather than renunciatory: since wealth is fugitive, the ruler’s real capital is the energy that can regain it. Solomon Pappaiah renders ullam as mana uruthi (firmness of mind), a slight shift from zeal to resolve. The text agrees verbatim in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Yama offers Nachiketas cattle, gold, horses and long-lived sons in place of the secret of death, the boy refuses, answering that a man cannot be satisfied by wealth and that such things wear away the vigour of the senses.
— Katha Upanishad 1.1.23-27 [3]
Nachiketas turns down every treasure Death offers because riches are temporary and cannot give lasting satisfaction.
Like the Kural, the Upanishad ranks an inner possession above material goods that ‘do not stay’.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal:”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that earthly riches decay or are stolen, and urges people to invest in the lasting treasure of the spirit.
The Kural makes the same contrast between perishable wealth and an inner possession that abides.
Islam
“Wealth is not in having many possessions, but rather (true) wealth is feeling sufficiency in the soul.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6446 [5]
The Prophet teaches that true richness is an inner state of the soul, not a large pile of goods.
Both sayings redefine real wealth as something held within the self.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot…”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines riches as an inner disposition toward one’s portion, not the size of one’s holdings.
Like the Kural, the Mishnah locates true possession in the mind rather than in property.
Buddhism
The Buddha lists seven ‘noble treasures’ (faith, virtue, conscience, concern, learning, generosity and discernment) and says that, unlike gold, they cannot be taken by kings, thieves, fire or flood.
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.6 (Dhana Sutta) [7]
The Buddha contrasts qualities of mind that no disaster can seize with material riches that can be lost at any time.
The Kural too says wealth ‘departs’ while a possession of the mind remains.
Jainism
“Wealth will not protect a careless man in this world and the next. Though he had seen the right way, he does not see it, even as one in the dark whose lamp has suddenly been put out.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on carelessness opens by urging exertion and warns that wealth gives no lasting protection to one who lacks vigilance.
Like the Kural's contrast between energy, which endures, and wealth, which slips away, this verse sets the unreliability of riches against the inner alertness that alone keeps a person on the right road.
Sikhism
“Your wealth shall not go with you; why do you cling to it, you fool?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 288, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan reminds the listener that riches are left behind and are no fit object of attachment.
The Kural sets the lasting possession of an energetic mind against wealth that slips away; Guru Arjan shares the verdict on wealth, though what he sets against it is the Name rather than drive.
Greek Mythology
King Midas is granted the golden touch, but his food and drink turn to gold, and he begs Dionysus to take the gift away, washing it off in the river Pactolus.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 [10]
Midas learns that a mountain of gold is worthless and even harmful when it cannot sustain life.
The story shows the Kural’s point that material wealth is not a true or lasting possession.
Philosophy
Lady Philosophy tells the imprisoned Boethius that Fortune’s gifts of wealth and rank were never truly his, since what can be taken away does not belong to a person; only goods of the mind are one’s own.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2 [11]
Boethius, stripped of wealth and office, is taught that external goods are only on loan from Fortune.
This mirrors the Kural’s claim that property ‘does not stay but departs’ while the mind’s possession endures.

Real Life Example

Thomas Carlyle rewrites The French Revolution after the only manuscript is burned
1835-1837 · London, England
After spending months in 1834 on the first volume of his history of the French Revolution, Thomas Carlyle lent his only draft to the philosopher John Stuart Mill, in whose house it was accidentally burned. When Mill confessed, Carlyle responded generously and began to reconstruct the work at once. The completed three-volume The French Revolution was published in 1837 and established his reputation; a small burnt fragment is kept at Carlyle's House in Chelsea.
— Sources: [12] [13]
What Carlyle owned, a finished manuscript, vanished overnight, but the power of mind that had produced it remained and produced it again.
The kural says energy of mind is the possession that lasts while other possessions slip away, and Carlyle's lost manuscript and rewritten book are a vivid example.
