Kural 17

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

நெடுங்கடலும் தன்நீர்மை குன்றும் தடிந்தெழிலி தான்நல்கா தாகி விடின்.
Nedungadalum than neermai kundrum thadinthezhili, Thaan nalkaadhaagi vidin.

Meaning

Even the huge sea will lose its fullness if the clouds, which draw water up from it, stop giving that water back as rain.

Elementary

Imagine a piggy bank you keep taking coins out of but never put any coins back into. Sooner or later it is empty. This kural says even the huge ocean is like that piggy bank: the clouds take water from it, and if they never return it as rain, even the sea runs low.

University

எழிலி (ezhili) is the rain-cloud and தடிந்து means "having cut off, drawn up," so the line describes evaporation as an act of extraction from the sea. The phrase தன் நீர்மை (than neermai) is a fine pun: நீர்மை means both "wateriness, fullness of water" and "one's own proper nature or quality," so the sea loses its water and its character at once. What makes the couplet remarkable is that it states a closed evaporation-and-precipitation cycle — cloud takes from ocean, must return to ocean — many centuries before that cycle was formalised in European science. Commentators from Parimelazhagar onward also read a social allegory here, developed vigorously by Kalaignar Karunanidhi: those who rise out of a community and do not give back diminish the very source that raised them.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Uniform, with the passing days, this water mounts and fails again. The tempest-clouds give life to earth, and fires re-animate the heaven.”
— Rigveda 1.164.51 (Griffith translation) [4]
This riddle-verse from the Rigveda's great cosmological hymn describes water as a single quantity that rises and descends in an unbroken rhythm.
Both texts describe rain as the return leg of a circuit rather than a one-way gift.
Christianity
“For he maketh small the drops of water: they pour down rain according to the vapour thereof: which the clouds do drop and distil upon man abundantly.”
— Job 36:27–28 (KJV) [5]
Elihu describes rain as vapour drawn up and then condensed back into drops — an explicit two-stage account of the water cycle.
It supplies the mechanism the Kural relies on: the cloud must give back what it first drew up.
Islam
“And We have sent down rain from the sky in a measured amount and settled it in the earth. And indeed, We are Able to take it away.”
— Qur'an 23:18, Al-Mu'minun (Sahih International) [6]
The verse presents rainfall as a measured quantity deposited in the earth, with the explicit reminder that the same power can withdraw it.
It shares the Kural's accounting sense of water as a finite stock that can be drawn down as well as replenished.
Judaism
“All the rivers run into the sea; yet the sea is not full; unto the place from whence the rivers come, thither they return again.”
— Ecclesiastes (Kohelet) 1:7 (KJV, rendering the Hebrew Tanakh) [5]
Kohelet observes the circulation of water between sea and river as his opening example of nature's endless, unaccumulating repetition.
It describes the same closed circuit the Kural describes, viewed from the rivers' side rather than the cloud's.
Buddhism
“Mendicants, when the ocean surges it makes the rivers surge. When the rivers surge they make the streams surge. When the streams surge they make the lakes surge … When the ocean recedes it makes the rivers recede.”
— Upayanti Sutta, Saṁyutta Nikāya 12.69 (Bhikkhu Sujato translation) [7]
The Buddha uses the linked rise and fall of ocean, rivers, streams and ponds as an image for how the twelve links of dependent origination swell and subside together.
It shares the Kural's picture of ocean and the waters above it as one interdependent system that rises or falls as a whole.
Jainism
“The function of the souls (jīva) is to help one another.”
— Tattvartha Sutra 5.21 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [8]
Umasvati's sutra, parasparopagraho jivanam, became the motto of Jainism: living beings exist in a web of mutual support and mutual dependence.
The Kural's sea that shrinks when the cloud stops giving back illustrates the same principle of reciprocity: what is taken must be returned, or even the greatest reserve declines.
Sikhism
“Whatever he takes, he does not give back. Seeking to earn more and more, he is troubled and uneasy.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaran te Vadheek, Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes the man who prospers by taking without ever giving back. Sikh ethics answers this with vand chhakna, sharing what one has earned.
The Kural's cloud diminishes the sea if it takes water and never returns it. Commentators read that as a warning about people who take from their community without giving back, which is exactly the person this salok condemns.
Greek Mythology
“deep-flowing Ocean, from whom all rivers flow and every sea, and all the springs and deep wells”
— Homer, Iliad 21.195–197 (A. T. Murray translation) [10]
Achilles names Okeanos as the single ultimate reservoir from which every river, sea, spring and well derives its water.
Both texts make the ocean the master account from which all other water is drawn, which is why its depletion would matter.
Philosophy
“When the sun is near, the stream of vapour flows upwards; when it recedes, the stream of water flows down, and the order of sequence, at all events, in this process always remains the same.”
— Aristotle, Meteorologica II.2 / I.9, 346b (E. W. Webster translation) [11]
Aristotle sets out the first systematic Greek account of the hydrological cycle: solar heat lifts water as vapour, which cools and falls again, endlessly.
It is the scientific statement of the circuit the Kural assumes when it warns that the cloud must give back what it took.

Real Life Example

Pierre Perrault measures rain and river flow in the Seine basin
1674 · Seine basin, France
In his book De l'origine des fontaines, published in 1674, Pierre Perrault made quantitative estimates of rainfall and of river discharge in part of the Seine basin. He showed that rainfall was more than enough to sustain the flow of rivers and springs, refuting older theories that rivers were fed by seawater returning underground. His work, followed by that of Edme Mariotte and Edmond Halley, marked the start of quantitative study of the water cycle.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Perrault helped establish that the world's waters move in a closed cycle in which rain is the essential link.
The kural describes the cloud drawing water from the sea and having to give it back, and Perrault's measurements began the scientific proof that rain is the link that keeps that cycle full.
