Kural 937

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

பழகிய செல்வமும் பண்பும் கெடுக்கும் கழகத்துக் காலை புகின்.
Pazhagiya selvamum panbum kedukkum kazhagaththuk kaalai pugin.

Meaning

If a person spends their time in the gambling hall, it destroys both the riches they were born into and their good character.

Elementary

Your grandparents may have saved a treasure for your family and taught you good manners. Valluvar says that hanging around a gambling house can waste both: the treasure is lost, and the good manners slowly disappear too.

University

Pazhagiya selvam (பழகிய செல்வம்) is 'long-familiar wealth', which all commentators take as ancestral or inherited property (Kalaignar: மூதாதையர் தேடிவைத்த சொத்து). Panbu (பண்பு) is good character or natural virtue. The second line contains an interpretive crux: kaalai (காலை) is read by Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah, Kalaignar and Kesikan as kaalam, 'time' ('if one's time is passed in the gaming hall'), and Kesikan adds 'the time meant for doing good'. Sivayogi Sivakumar on ytamizh reads it literally as 'morning' ('if one goes to the gaming hall in the morning'), the hour that should belong to work. Kazhagam (கழகம்) also appears in 935 as the gaming place. The couplet is identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Drinking, dice, women, and hunting, these four (which have been enumerated) in succession, he must know to be the most pernicious in the set that springs from love of pleasure.”
— Manusmriti 7.50 (trans. Georg Bühler) [3]
Manu tells the king that dice are among the four most destructive vices born of love of pleasure.
Manu's grouping (drink, dice, women) is the same as the Kural's sequence of chapters 92 to 94, and both warn that these destroy a ruler's estate and character.
Christianity
“And not many days after the younger son gathered all together, and took his journey into a far country, and there wasted his substance with riotous living.”
— Luke 15:13 (KJV) [4]
The prodigal son takes his share of the family inheritance and wastes it in dissolute living.
A squandered inheritance and a corrupted life are exactly what the Kural says the gaming hall produces.
Islam
“Satan only wants to cause between you animosity and hatred through intoxicants and gambling and to avert you from the remembrance of Allah and from prayer. So will you not desist?”
— Qur'an 5:91 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says gambling turns people away from prayer and remembrance of God, and sows hatred.
Time taken by the gaming hall is time stolen from virtue, as the Kural's kaalai reading suggests.
Judaism
“Wealth may dwindle to less than nothing, / But one who gathers little by little increases it.”
— Proverbs 13:11 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, on Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs contrasts wealth that dwindles away with wealth that grows through steady care.
The Kural's ancestral fortune, lost in the gaming hall, is wealth dwindling to nothing.
Buddhism
“What are the six channels for dissipating wealth which he does not pursue? ... (d) indulgence in gambling which causes heedlessness...”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), trans. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha lists gambling among the six channels through which a householder's wealth drains away.
Both texts present gambling as a leak that empties a family's fortune and its virtue.
Jainism
“Those endowed with excellent knowledge live many nayutas of years; so great a loss suffer the fools in a life of less than a hundred years!”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana laments how much fools throw away when they spend their brief human life on passing pleasures.
Valluvar says time passed in the gaming hall destroys inherited wealth and good character; the Jain verse likewise grieves over the great loss that comes from wasting a short human life on trifles.
Sikhism
“Like a gambler he loses and wastes his precious life.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 37, Pauri 26 (English translation as published on BaniDB/SikhiToTheMax and searchgurbani.com) [9]
In a pauri headed ‘Wasting the life by the gambler’, Bhai Gurdas compares the person who throws away the priceless human birth to a gambler who loses everything at play.
The Kural says days spent in the gaming hall destroy both inherited wealth and good character, and Bhai Gurdas makes the stake even larger: the gambler loses life itself.
Greek Mythology
Penelope's suitors pass their days in Odysseus' hall playing board games and feasting, eating up the estate of his house until they are destroyed.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 1 [10]
Day after day spent in idle play and feasting swallows a great household's inherited wealth.
This mirrors the Kural's warning about time spent in the gaming hall wasting ancestral wealth.
Philosophy
“Not that there is in fact any happiness in them, or that men imagine true bliss to consist in money won at play, or in the hare which they hunt; we would not take these as a gift.”
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées §139 (trans. W. F. Trotter) [11]
Pascal says people go to gaming not for the money but to fill and escape their time.
The Kural's focus on time spent in the gaming hall picks up the same insight: the real cost is the life poured away.

Real Life Example

Young Leo Tolstoy's gambling debts cost him the house where he was born
1854 · Yasnaya Polyana, Tula Province, Russia
Leo Tolstoy inherited the family estate of Yasnaya Polyana in 1847, but as a young man he gambled heavily, and by the time he married in 1862 he had sold half his land and the estate's main house to pay gambling debts. The large manor house in which he had been born was sold in 1854 while he was away in the army, taken apart and carried off to the village of Dolgoye, about 40 kilometres away, where it stood until it was demolished in 1913. Tolstoy lived the rest of his life in one of the two smaller wings, and a stone marks the spot where the house once stood.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The ancestral home built by his grandfather and father was lost to the card tables before Tolstoy had written his great novels.
The kural says that time spent in the gaming hall destroys a person's inherited wealth, and Tolstoy's gambling literally carried off the family house he had inherited.
