Kural 216

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

பயன்மரம் உள்ளூர்ப் பழுத்தற்றால் செல்வம் நயனுடை யான்கண் படின்.
Payanmaram ulloorp pazhuththatraal selvam Nayanudai yaankan padin.

Meaning

When riches come to a kind, generous person, it is like a fruit tree ripening in the middle of the village, where everyone can pick its fruit.

Elementary

A fruit tree deep in a forest helps almost no one, but a mango tree in the middle of town feeds everyone who walks by. A kind person's money is like that town tree.

University

The emphasis falls on ul-oor, ‘inside the village’: the same fruit tree in a distant forest would be of little use, so location stands for accessibility. Parimelazhagar explains nayan (courtesy, kindly propriety) as oppuravu itself, ‘since among the many forms of worldly justice oppuravu is pre-eminent,’ and says the point is that such wealth yields benefit easily to all. Manakkudavar reads nayanudaiyaan as ‘one who is loved by others’ and adds that such wealth benefits even those who dislike him. The couplet moves beyond the tank of 215 to an image of organic, seasonal abundance, suggesting that generosity in the right person is as natural as ripening.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Bounteous is he who gives unto the beggar who comes to him in want of food and feeble. Success attends him in the shout of battle. He makes a friend of him in future troubles.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.3 (trans. R. T. H. Griffith) [4]
The Vedic hymn praises the giver who feeds the needy, saying such generosity creates lasting bonds.
Like the village fruit tree, the bountiful person's wealth is open to whoever comes in need.
Christianity
“As we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith.”
— Galatians 6:10 (KJV) [5]
Paul urges believers to do good to everyone whenever they get the chance.
Shares the Kural's ideal that a good person's resources should be open to all.
Islam
“There is none amongst the Muslims who plants a tree or sows seeds, and then a bird, or a person or an animal eats from it, but is regarded as a charitable gift for him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2320 [6]
The Prophet says that whenever anyone — even an animal — eats from a tree you planted, it counts as charity from you.
Uses the same image of a fruit tree whose produce benefits all comers.
Judaism
“That man himself found a world full of carob trees. Just as my ancestors planted for me, I too am planting for my descendants.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Taanit 23a (Sefaria, William Davidson ed.) [7]
A man planting a carob tree that will take seventy years to bear fruit tells Honi he is planting for future generations, as others once did for him.
Presents the fruit tree as a shared gift to the community across time, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
“One who gives, who is a master of giving, is dear & charming to people at large.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.34, Siha Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha tells General Siha that one visible fruit of generosity is being loved by people in general.
Matches Manakkudavar's reading of nayanudaiyaan as ‘one loved by others’ — the generous person is the village's beloved tree.
Jainism
“Those who give are like the female palm tree surrounded by the terrace in the midst of the village, they live beloved by many. Men who eat without giving to others, though their family be flourishing, are like the male palm in a burning-ground.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 10 ('Liberality'), st. 6 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Nālaṭiyār contrasts the generous person, like a fruit-bearing palm in the middle of the village, with the miser, like a barren palm in a cremation ground.
Valluvar likens the generous man's wealth to a fruit tree ripening in the middle of the village; the Nālaṭiyār uses almost the same image of a fruitful tree at the village centre.
Sikhism
The Sikh langar is a communal kitchen kept in every gurdwara, where free food is served to all who come. Under Guru Amar Das everyone ate together in the casteless langar, and in it all sit in one straight line so that no one claims higher or lower status.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, langar (and linked coverage in Guru Amar Das and Sikhism: The rejection of caste) [10]
The langar puts shared resources where anyone can reach them, regardless of caste, creed or rank.
The Kural pictures the generous person's wealth as a fruit tree ripening in the middle of the village, where everyone can pick its fruit; the langar is that kind of open, central provision for all.
Greek Mythology
In the contest for Athens, Athena plants the first olive tree on the Acropolis as her gift to the city, and the city is awarded to her.
— Apollodorus, Library 3.14.1 [11]
The olive, giving fruit, oil, and wood to the whole city, was remembered as Athena's great public gift.
A fruit tree planted at the heart of the city for everyone's benefit — close to the Kural's image.
Philosophy
“he is like a vine which has produced grapes, and seeks for nothing more after it has once produced its proper fruit ... so a man when he has done a good act, does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act, as a vine goes on to produce again the grapes in season.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.6 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius compares the good person to a vine that simply bears fruit, season after season, without seeking credit.
Same fruit-bearing image for the good person whose benefits flow naturally to others.

Real Life Example

George Peabody founds the Peabody Donation Fund for London's poor
March 1862; first estate opened 1864 · London, England, United Kingdom
George Peabody, an American-born banker who had made his fortune in London, wrote to The Times in March 1862 announcing a gift of 150,000 pounds, later increased, to improve the condition of the poor of London. The trustees of the Peabody Donation Fund used it to build model dwellings for working people, with running water and decent sanitation at affordable rents, and the first block opened in Spitalfields in 1864. The trust he founded still exists today as one of London's oldest and largest housing associations.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A private fortune was set up as a lasting public resource in the heart of the city, where the people who needed it could reach it.
The kural compares the wealth of a generous person to a fruit tree ripening in the middle of the village, where everyone can pick its fruit, and Peabody placed his wealth in the midst of London's working poor.
