Kural 883

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

உட்பகை அஞ்சித்தற் காக்க உலைவிடத்து மட்பகையின் மாணத் தெறும்.
Utpakai anjiththar kaakka ulaivitaththu matpakaiyin maanath therum.

Meaning

Fear the enemy within your own circle and protect yourself. When you are weak, it will cut you down as surely as a potter’s tool cuts clay.

Elementary

A potter uses a thin wire or blade to slice through soft clay in one easy stroke. Hidden enemies wait for a time when you are tired or in trouble, and then they can hurt you just as easily, so it is wise to watch out for them early.

University

The verse pairs a command, utpakai anji thar kaakka (உட்பகை அஞ்சித் தற்காக்க, 'fearing internal enmity, guard yourself'), with a warning about timing: ulaividaththu (உலைவிடத்து), 'at the time of tottering' or decline. The image matpakai (மட்பகை), literally 'clay-enemy', is glossed by Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar as the potter's cutting tool that slices clay vessels, so that maana therum (மாணத் தெறும்) means 'will destroy thoroughly'. Pope's 'potter's knife' follows the same reading. Sivayogi Sivakumar instead reads the second line as setting a cooking pot on an unfired clay vessel, an interpretive variant rather than a textual one. The Tamil text is identical in ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Protect thyself, O king, from all fears that arise from within. Fears, however, that arise from outside are of little consequence. The first kind of fear, O king, may cut thy roots in a single day.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section CVII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Bhishma tells Yudhishthira that internal dissension is the real danger to a ruler, able to destroy him suddenly.
This matches the Kural almost point for point: guard against the enemy within, which strikes all at once.
Christianity
In Gethsemane, while Jesus' disciples sleep, Judas, one of the Twelve, arrives with an armed crowd and betrays him with a kiss.
— Matthew 26:47-50 [4]
The betrayal comes from inside the inner circle and at the hour of greatest vulnerability.
As the Kural warns, the internal enemy strikes when one is weakest.
Islam
“They are the enemy, so beware of them.”
— Qur'an 63:4 (Sahih International) [5]
Describing the hypocrites inside the Medinan community, whose appearance and speech are pleasing, the Qur'an names them the real enemy.
Both texts urge vigilance against the foe who lives within one's own community.
Judaism
Ahithophel, David's trusted counsellor, joins Absalom's rebellion; David prays that his counsel be turned to foolishness, and Ahithophel urges an immediate strike while David is weary and weak-handed.
— 2 Samuel 15:12, 31; 17:1-2 [4]
The trusted insider knows exactly when the king is most vulnerable.
This illustrates the Kural's warning that an internal enemy strikes in a time of weakness.
Buddhism
“Whatever harm an enemy may do to an enemy, or a hater to a hater, an ill-directed mind inflicts on oneself a greater harm.”
— Dhammapada 42 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada teaches that the most damaging enemy is the one inside oneself.
The Kural speaks of a hostile insider, the Dhammapada of a hostile mind, but both rank the inner foe above the outer one.
Jainism
“Self is the one invincible foe, (together with the four) cardinal passions, (viz. anger, pride, deceit, and greed, they are five) and the (five) senses (make ten). These (foes), O great sage, I have regularly vanquished.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Asked by Keśi how he withstands the thousands of foes that attack him, Gautama names the true enemies as the unsubdued self, the four passions and the five senses, all of them inside the person.
The Kural urges a man to fear the enemy within and guard himself, and Gautama's answer turns the same vigilance on the foes that live inside one's own camp.
Sikhism
“Within this body dwell the five thieves: sexual desire, anger, greed, emotional attachment and egotism.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 600, Raag Sorath, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das locates the most dangerous enemies inside the person, as thieves already living within the house of the body who plunder its true wealth.
The Kural urges guarding oneself against the enemy within before it strikes at a moment of weakness, and Gurbani names that inner enemy as the five thieves.
Greek Mythology
In the underworld, Agamemnon's shade tells Odysseus how he survived the Trojan War only to be killed at his own homecoming feast by his wife Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.405-434 [9]
The victor of a ten-year war dies in his own house, at the moment he lets down his guard.
The enemy within strikes when defences are down, just as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
In his 1838 Lyceum Address, Abraham Lincoln argued that danger to the American republic would not come from abroad but would spring up from among its own people.
— Abraham Lincoln, Address before the Young Men's Lyceum of Springfield (1838) [10]
Lincoln held that no external power could crush the nation, and that only internal disorder could destroy it.
Like the Kural, he identifies the internal enemy as the one to fear and guard against.

Real Life Example

Kobayakawa Hideaki changes sides at Sekigahara
21 October 1600 · Sekigahara, Mino Province, Japan
At the Battle of Sekigahara the western coalition led by Ishida Mitsunari faced the eastern army of Tokugawa Ieyasu. Kobayakawa Hideaki, whose troops were posted on Mount Matsuo as part of the western army, had secretly promised to support Ieyasu; during the battle he led his forces down the mountain against his nominal ally Otani Yoshitsugu, and other commanders switched sides with him. The collapse that followed destroyed the western army and opened the way for Tokugawa rule.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The western coalition was undone not by the enemy facing it but by a commander standing within its own lines.
The kural warns that the enemy within strikes at the moment of weakness and cuts as surely as the potter's tool, as Kobayakawa's turn did at the battle's turning point.
