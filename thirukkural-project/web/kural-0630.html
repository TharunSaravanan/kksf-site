Kural 630

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

இன்னாமை இன்பம் எனக்கொளின் ஆகுந்தன் ஒன்னார் விழையுஞ் சிறப்பு.
Innaamai inpam enakkolin aakundhan Onnaar vizhaiyunj chirappu.

Meaning

If a person can take hard times as if they were good times, they will win a kind of greatness that even their enemies admire and wish they had.

Elementary

Imagine a runner who smiles even while climbing the steepest hill in a race. Even the runners from the other team end up cheering for her. The Kural says if you can treat hard work and hard times as something good, even people who don’t like you will respect you.

University

Iṉṉāmai, ‘the unpleasant, pain’, is the negative of iṉṉā (sweet), so the couplet literally asks one to count the un-sweet as sweet (iṉpam eṉakkoḷiṉ). The reward is ciṟappu, eminence or distinction, qualified as oṉṉār viḻaiyum, ‘which even foes (oṉṉār, those not at one with us) desire or esteem’; ākuntaṉ is ākum taṉ with the final m assimilated, and viḻaiyuñ likewise before ciṟappu. Solomon Pappaiah specifies the hardship as that met while carrying out an undertaking, keeping the chapter’s Parimelazhagar-style link to the king’s or doer’s enterprise, while Mu. Varadarasanar reads it generally. The two sources agree on the text, and the couplet closes the chapter by turning the whole theme outward: inner mastery of trouble becomes public honour.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.37 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that the highest kind of happiness often tastes bitter at first and only later proves sweet.
The Gita’s poison-that-becomes-nectar is the Kural’s pain accepted as pleasure, which in the end yields true distinction.
Christianity
“Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ’s sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong.”
— 2 Corinthians 12:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul declares that he actually takes pleasure in hardships, because through them he finds real strength.
Paul does what the Kural commends, treating pain as pleasure, and finds in it a strength others cannot take away.
Islam
“But perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allāh knows, while you know not.”
— Qur’an 2:216 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that what feels painful may in fact be good, since God sees its outcome.
The Kural asks us to count the painful as pleasant; the Qur’an explains why this can be wise, since the disliked may be the good.
Judaism
“And as for you, ye meant evil against me; but God meant it for good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive.”
— Genesis 50:20 (JPS 1917) [6]
Joseph, once sold into slavery by his brothers, now rules Egypt and tells them the suffering they caused became the means of great good.
Joseph treats his years of pain as a gift, and the brothers who once hated him now bow before his greatness, as the Kural predicts.
Buddhism
“Though one may conquer a thousand times a thousand men in battle, yet he indeed is the noblest victor who conquers himself.”
— Dhammapada 103 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says the greatest victory and honour belong to the one who masters his own mind.
The Kural’s person who can turn pain into pleasure has won this inner victory, and it earns an honour that outshines conquest over enemies.
Jainism
“For more than twelve years the Venerable Ascetic Mahâvira neglected his body and abandoned the care of it; he with equanimity bore, underwent, and suffered all pleasant or unpleasant occurrences arising from divine powers, men, or animals.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §117 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Kalpa Sutra says Mahavira bore all hardships, from gods, men and animals, with equanimity for more than twelve years before attaining omniscience (kevala), after which gods and men honoured him.
The Kural promises distinction even enemies admire to one who takes hardship as if it were pleasure; Mahavira's equal acceptance of pleasant and unpleasant ordeals is the Jain model of that, and led to his supreme standing.
Sikhism
“If You will bless me with happiness, then I will worship and adore You. Even in pain, I will meditate on You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 757, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das vows to turn to God in exactly the same way in pain as in happiness.
The Kural asks us to take hardship as if it were pleasure; Guru Ram Das treats pain and happiness alike as occasions to remember God.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads, the young Heracles is approached by two women, Vice offering an easy life of pleasure and Virtue offering a hard road of toil; he chooses Virtue’s arduous path, which leads to lasting glory.
— Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 (Prodicus’ ‘Choice of Heracles’) [10]
Prodicus’ fable, retold by Xenophon, makes the willing embrace of hardship the way to true renown.
Heracles chooses toil as the Kural’s person counts pain as pleasure, and both win a fame the world must acknowledge.
Philosophy
Nietzsche resolves to learn more and more to see what is necessary in things as beautiful, calling this amor fati, love of fate, and hoping one day to be only a Yes-sayer.
— Friedrich Nietzsche, The Gay Science §276 [11]
Nietzsche’s ideal is not merely to endure hardship but to affirm and love it as part of one’s life.
Amor fati is a modern echo of the Kural’s counsel to take pain itself as pleasure.

Real Life Example

Abdelkader, after defeat and captivity, wins the esteem of Europe
1847-1860 · Algeria, France and Damascus, Syria
Abdelkader, who led Algerian resistance against French conquest, surrendered in December 1847 on the promise of passage to the East but was instead held in France for about four years until Louis-Napoleon allowed his release in 1852. He settled in Damascus, and during the communal violence of July 1860 he and his followers sheltered and protected thousands of Christians at great personal risk. As a result he was highly commended by several European governments.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A defeated enemy who had endured years of broken promises and captivity emerged with a moral standing that the European powers themselves honoured.
The kural says one who can take hardship as though it were sweet will win a distinction even enemies admire, and Abdelkader, after defeat and captivity, won praise from the very European world whose armies he had fought.
