Kural 1260

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

நிணந்தீயில் இட்டன்ன நெஞ்சினார்க்கு உண்டோ புணர்ந்தூடி நிற்பேம் எனல்.
Ninandheeyil ittanna nenjinaarkku undoa punarndhoodi niRpaem enal.

Meaning

Can those whose hearts melt like fat thrown into a fire ever say, "We will be close to him and still keep up our sulking"?

Elementary

Put a pat of butter in a hot pan and it melts almost at once. The heroine says her heart is like that butter: the moment her husband is near, she cannot stay cross with him.

University

The chapter closes with a vivid simile: niṇam tīyil iṭṭaṉṉa neñciṉār, ‘those whose hearts are like fat (niṇam) put into fire’, cannot say puṇarntu ūṭi niṟpēm, ‘we will unite and yet stand firm in ūṭal (sulking)’. Commentators differ over the construction: Mu. Varadarasanar reads ‘can those like me, with melting hearts, keep up a sulk once they have agreed to it’, Kalaignar takes it as ‘can they hold firm in sulking after having been together’, and Solomon Pappaiah expands it into a general statement about women whose hearts melt at the sight of their lovers. The couplet answers 1259 in general form and closes the chapter’s argument: with a heart like this, no niṟai can last. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the musical setting by P.A. Muthukrishnan reproduced on thirukkural.net paraphrases the image as butter in the flame (தழலிடை வெண்ணெய்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A devotee whose speech is sometimes choked up, whose heart melts, who cries continually and sometimes laughs, who feels ashamed and cries out loudly and then dances — a devotee thus fixed in loving service to Me purifies the entire universe.”
— Bhagavata Purana 11.14.24 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Srimad-Bhagavatam) [3]
Krishna describes a love so strong that the devotee’s heart melts and outward composure disappears.
The Kural’s heroine has the same melting heart (dravate cittam), which leaves no room for holding back.
Christianity
“I am poured out like water, and all my bones are out of joint: my heart is like wax; it is melted in the midst of my bowels.”
— Psalms 22:14 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes a heart that has melted like wax, all strength gone.
The Kural uses almost the same image, fat melting in fire, for a heart that cannot hold firm.
Islam
“The skins shiver therefrom of those who fear their Lord; then their skins and their hearts relax at the remembrance of Allah.”
— Qur’an 39:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes hearts that soften and yield at the remembrance of God.
The Kural’s heroine has a heart that softens and yields the moment her beloved is near.
Judaism
“And Joseph made haste; for his heart yearned toward his brother; and he sought where to weep; and he entered into his chamber, and wept there. And he washed his face, and came out; and he refrained himself…”
— Genesis 43:30-31 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Joseph’s heart melts at the sight of Benjamin, and he has to hurry away to weep before he can compose himself again.
The Kural asks whether a melting heart can hold firm; Joseph barely manages it, and only by leaving the room.
Buddhism
“There is no fire like lust; there is no grip like hatred; there is no net like delusion; there is no river like craving.”
— Dhammapada 251 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha calls passion the fiercest of fires.
The Kural’s heroine shows that fire at work: her heart melts in it like fat, unable to keep any resolve.
Jainism
“A pot filled with lac thrown into the fire melts quickly and is destroyed; so monks are lost through intercourse with women.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.27 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga uses the image of lac melting in fire for a monk whose resolve dissolves in the presence of desire.
This is almost the Kural’s simile, fat melting in fire, applied to the same dissolving of firmness.
Sikhism
“As metal melts into metal again, so does love melt into love.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 725, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak uses the image of molten metal flowing into metal to show how love naturally merges with the one it loves.
The Kural asks whether those whose hearts melt like fat in the fire could ever hold firm in a sulk once they are together; Guru Nanak likewise pictures love as something that melts and merges on contact.
Greek Mythology
Ovid says Narcissus, consumed by love, wastes away as yellow wax melts before a gentle flame or as morning frost melts in the sun.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.487-490 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid uses a melting image for a lover dissolved by passion.
The Kural’s heart like fat in the fire is the same picture of a lover who cannot hold firm.
Philosophy
Spinoza holds that an emotion can be restrained or removed only by an opposite and stronger emotion, not by knowledge or resolution alone.
— Spinoza, Ethics IV, Proposition 7 (paraphrase) [11]
Spinoza argues that a resolution cannot hold against a strong feeling unless a stronger feeling opposes it.
The heroine’s plan to sulk is too weak against her melting love, just as Spinoza would predict.

Real Life Example

Charles Darwin weighs marriage on paper, then proposes
1838 · London, England
In 1838 Charles Darwin wrote two notes weighing the case for and against marriage, listing companionship, a home and children against lost time, money and freedom. The second note, probably written in July 1838, came down on the side of marrying. On 11 November 1838 he proposed to his cousin Emma Wedgwood, who accepted, and they married on 29 January 1839; the notes are kept at Cambridge University Library.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Even one of history's most methodical minds could not keep his feelings in the cool columns of a list; the case for love won.
The kural asks whether hearts that melt like fat in fire can ever hold firm; Darwin's careful balance sheet tipped toward marriage, showing calculation giving way to feeling.
