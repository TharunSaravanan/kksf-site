Kural 990

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

சான்றவர் சான்றாண்மை குன்றின் இருநிலந்தான் தாங்காது மன்னோ பொறை.
Saandravar saandraanmai kundrin irunilandhaan thaangaadhu manno porai.

Meaning

If good people ever lost their goodness, even this great earth could not carry its own weight.

Elementary

Imagine a big table held up by a few strong legs. If those legs get weak, everything on the table comes crashing down. Valluvar says good people are like those legs for the whole world: if they stop being good, the world cannot hold itself up.

University

The closing couplet extends saandraanmai from personal virtue to cosmic support. If the saandravar (சான்றவர், 'the perfected') should decline (kundrin, குன்றின்) in their saandraanmai, the 'great earth' (irunilam, இருநிலம்) itself could not bear (thaangaadhu, தாங்காது) its porai (பொறை, 'burden'); manno (மன்னோ) is an emphatic particle. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kesikan read porai as the earth's burden, so that the noble become, with the five pillars of Kural 983, the hidden supports of the world; Karunanidhi instead takes porai as 'patience', reading that the world would not patiently tolerate such a decline. Sivayogi Sivakumar's reading of irunilam as the 'two states' of inner and outer life is idiosyncratic. Pope: 'The mighty earth its burthen to sustain must cease, / If perfect virtue of the perfect men decrease.' The idea that the righteous sustain the world appears in many traditions, and here completes the chapter's arc from personal duty (981) to the stability of the world. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If I did not perform prescribed duties, all these worlds would be put to ruination. I would be the cause of creating unwanted population, and I would thereby destroy the peace of all living beings.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.24 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna says that if he, the exemplar, ceased doing his duty, the worlds would fall into ruin, since others follow the example of the great.
Like the Kural, it ties the stability of the world to the continued right conduct of its noblest figures.
Christianity
“Ye are the salt of the earth: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be salted? it is thenceforth good for nothing, but to be cast out, and to be trodden under foot of men.”
— Matthew 5:13 (KJV) [4]
Jesus calls his disciples the salt that preserves the earth and warns of what happens if they lose their quality.
Like the Kural, it makes the good the preservers of the world and warns of the consequences if their goodness fails.
Islam
“And your Lord would not have destroyed the cities unjustly while their people were reformers.”
— Qur'an 11:117 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says that God does not destroy communities while they contain people who work for good.
Like the Kural, it suggests that the presence of the righteous keeps the world from ruin.
Judaism
“And he said, “Let not my Sovereign be angry if I speak but this last time: What if ten should be found there?” “I will not destroy, for the sake of the ten,” came the reply.”
— Genesis 18:32 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Abraham bargains with God over Sodom, and God agrees to spare the city for the sake of just ten righteous people.
Like the Kural, it shows the fate of a whole community resting on a few righteous people.
Buddhism
“Monks, these two bright qualities guard the world. Which two? Conscience & concern. If these two bright qualities did not guard the world, there would be no recognition of 'mother' here, no recognition of 'mother's sister,' 'uncle's wife,' 'teacher's wife,' or 'wife of those who deserve respect.' ...”
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.9, Lokapala Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha teaches that conscience and concern (hiri and ottappa) are the guardians of the world, without which social order would collapse.
Like the Kural, it says the moral qualities of people are what hold the world together.
Jainism
“The most excellent twice-born men who possess these good qualities, are able to save themselves and others.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.35 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
At the end of his account of the true Brahmana, the monk Jayaghosha says that only those with real virtues can save both themselves and others.
Like the Kural, it makes the welfare of others depend on the virtue of the perfected few.
Sikhism
“The mythical bull is Dharma, the son of compassion; this is what patiently holds the earth in its place. One who understands this becomes truthful. What a great load there is on the bull!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib, Pauri 16, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak takes the old Indian image of a bull holding up the earth and reinterprets it: what really bears the world is dharma, born of compassion. The weight of creation rests on righteousness, not on a literal animal.
The Kural says the earth could not bear its burden if the noble fell short, and Japji likewise makes moral righteousness the support that keeps the earth in place.
Greek Mythology
In Works and Days, Hesiod contrasts the city of the just, where peace, plenty and healthy children flourish under Zeus' favour, with the city of the unjust, on which Zeus sends famine, plague and ruin, so that even one wicked man can bring suffering on a whole city.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 225-247 (trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod teaches that the prosperity or ruin of a community follows the justice of its people.
Like the Kural, it ties the well-being of the world to the virtue of those in it.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates argues that until philosophers rule, or rulers become genuine lovers of wisdom, cities and the human race will have no rest from their evils.
— Plato, Republic V, 473c-e (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato holds that the well-being of human society depends on the wise and good being at its centre.
Like the Kural, it makes the fate of the world hinge on the presence of perfected people.

Real Life Example

Stanislav Petrov judges a missile alert to be a false alarm
26 September 1983 · Early-warning command centre near Moscow, Soviet Union
In the early hours of 26 September 1983, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov was on duty at a Soviet early-warning centre when the satellite system signalled that the United States had launched intercontinental ballistic missiles. Doubting the alert, partly because only a few missiles were shown, he judged it a false alarm rather than reporting an attack, and no missiles arrived. Later investigation found that sunlight reflecting off clouds had fooled the satellites; the incident stayed secret for years.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The steadiness of one officer at a single moment may have prevented a catastrophe for the whole world.
The kural says the great earth could not bear its burden if the noble fell short, and Petrov's sound judgement helped keep the world from a disaster it could not have borne.
