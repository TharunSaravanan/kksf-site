Kural 1008

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

நச்சப் படாதவன் செல்வம் நடுவூருள் நச்சு மரம்பழுத் தற்று.
Nachchap Pataadhavan Selvam Natuvoorul Nachchu Marampazhuth Thatru

Meaning

The riches of a person whom no one loves are like a poison tree bearing fruit in the middle of town.

Elementary

Imagine a tree in the middle of the playground covered in shiny fruit, but the fruit is poisonous, so nobody can eat it. Valluvar says a selfish rich person's money is like that tree: right there in front of everyone, but no use to anyone.

University

The couplet is built on a pun: nachchap padaadhavan (நச்சப் படாதவன், 'one not loved', from நச்சு- 'to desire') and nachchu maram (நச்சு மரம், 'poison tree'). Pope's explanation identifies it as the etty tree (எட்டி, the nux-vomica, Strychnos nux-vomica), and Sivayogi Sivakumar quotes the proverb எட்டி பழுத்தால் என்ன, ஈயாதான் வாழ்ந்தால் என்ன ('what if the etty ripens, what if the non-giver prospers'). The setting naduvooruL (நடுவூருள், 'in the middle of the town') heightens the irony: the fruit is in full view and within reach of all, yet useless. Mu. Varadarasanar supplies the reason the man is unloved, that he helps no one. The image is the exact inverse of Kural 216, where the wealth of the generous is a fruit-bearing tree in the midst of the village, so Valluvar deliberately pairs the two chapters on Oppuravu and Nandriyil Selvam. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“No friend is he who to his friend and comrade who comes imploring food, will offer nothing. / Let him depart-no home is that to rest in-, and rather seek a stranger to support him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.4 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith, sacred-texts.com) [3]
The Vedic poet says the one who offers nothing to those who come to him is no friend, and his house is no refuge.
The Kural's unloved rich man is just such a 'house that is no home': present in the town but of no use to anyone.
Christianity
Proverbs says that the people curse the one who withholds grain, but blessing rests on the head of the one who sells it.
— Proverbs 11:26 [4]
The hoarder of what others need earns the community's hatred.
The Kural's unloved man whose wealth benefits no one is the hoarder the people curse.
Islam
A hadith in Jami' at-Tirmidhi says the generous person is near to God, near to people and near to Paradise, while the miser is far from God, far from people and far from Paradise.
— Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1961 [5]
Miserliness is pictured as distance from everyone, divine and human.
It matches the Kural's miser whom no one loves and whose wealth no one can approach.
Judaism
The Talmud says the people of Sodom grew arrogant through their abundance and resolved to stop travellers coming among them, since visitors would only diminish their wealth.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 109a [6]
Sodom's riches, sealed off from strangers and the poor, became a byword for wickedness.
Like the Kural's poisonous tree in the town, Sodom's plenty stood in full view yet was deliberately made useless to others.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells General Siha that a giver, a master of giving, is dear and charming to people at large, and that good people associate with him.
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.34, Siha Sutta [7]
Generosity makes a person beloved; its absence leaves one unloved.
The Kural describes the reverse case: the non-giver whom no one loves and whose wealth no one enjoys.
Jainism
“The bat will not go to the rough-stemmed wood-apple tree, though near and fruitful. So the riches of those who, though they be very near to one, have no greatness of soul, have not the excellence of being considered as profitable.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 27 ('Riches without goodness'), st. 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain poet compares the riches of small-souled men to a fruitful tree near at hand that no creature will approach.
This is the Kural's own tree image: wealth close by and laden with fruit, yet of no benefit to anyone.
Sikhism
“The Simmal tree is straight as an arrow; it is very tall, and very thick. But those birds which visit it hopefully, depart disappointed. Its fruits are tasteless, its flowers are nauseating, and its leaves are useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak uses the tall silk-cotton tree as an image of imposing greatness that offers nothing to those who come to it in hope.
The Kural compares the unloved man's wealth to a poison tree fruiting in the middle of town; Guru Nanak's towering tree likewise stands in full view and sends everyone away empty.
Greek Mythology
In Aristophanes' comedy, Ploutos, the god of Wealth, is blind and so visits the wicked and the stingy instead of the good; when his sight is restored, he leaves the unworthy rich and enriches the deserving.
— Aristophanes, Plutus (Wealth) [10]
The play mocks wealth that sits with the undeserving where it does no good.
The Kural's unloved rich man is precisely the undeserving holder of wealth that benefits no one.
Philosophy
Boethius has Lady Philosophy argue that money shows its worth in being spent rather than hoarded, and that avarice makes men hated while generosity makes them famous.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy II, prose 5 [11]
Hoarding not only wastes money but makes its owner odious.
The Kural's unloved rich man is hated for the same avarice that makes his wealth useless.

Real Life Example

Ceausescu clears a Bucharest district for the House of the People
1984-1989 · Bucharest, Romania
From 1984 the Romanian leader Nicolae Ceausescu had an enormous palace built in the centre of Bucharest as a new political and administrative centre, on the site of the old Uranus district, a hillside neighbourhood of small cobbled streets and older houses that was demolished for the project. The construction required clearing large areas of the historic downtown. The building was still unfinished inside when Ceausescu fell in December 1989, and since 1991 it has housed Romania's Parliament.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The colossal palace stood in the heart of the capital as a monument to the ruler rather than a benefit to the people whose homes it replaced.
The kural likens the wealth of a man whom nobody loves to a poisonous tree bearing fruit in the middle of the town, and Ceausescu's vast palace rose in the centre of Bucharest offering nothing to the citizens around it.
