Kural 824

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

முகத்தின் இனிய நகாஅ அகத்தின்னா வஞ்சரை அஞ்சப் படும்.
Mugaththin iniya nagaaa agaththinnaa vanjarai anjap padum.

Meaning

Be afraid of two-faced people who smile sweetly with their faces while their hearts mean you harm.

Elementary

A shiny red apple can still have a worm inside. A big smile on the outside does not always mean kindness on the inside, so it is wise to watch what people do, not just how they smile.

University

The couplet sets mugam (முகம், face) against agam (அகம், the inner self), a pairing that will return in Kural 830. Nagaaa (நகாஅ, ‘smiling’) shows alapeḍai, the metrical lengthening of a vowel, and akaththinnaa (அகத்து இன்னா) means ‘bitter or harmful within’. Vanjar (வஞ்சர்) are deceivers, and anjappaḍum (அஞ்சப்படும்) is the impersonal ‘is to be feared’; Varadarasanar reads it as fearing to make friends with them, Kalaignar as fearing them and keeping away. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kamsa sends the demoness Putana to kill the infant Krishna. She comes as a beautiful, affectionate woman, takes the child lovingly and offers him her breast, which she has smeared with poison; Krishna draws out her life instead.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.6 [3]
A face of tenderness hides a murderous purpose.
Putana is the Kural’s sweet face over a bitter heart, in its most vivid form.
Christianity
“Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravening wolves.”
— Matthew 7:15 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns of people whose gentle appearance hides a predatory heart.
‘Beware’ of the gentle outside and the wolf inside is exactly the Kural’s ‘fear those who smile but are bitter within’.
Islam
“And of the people is he whose speech pleases you in worldly life, and he calls Allah to witness as to what is in his heart, yet he is the fiercest of opponents.”
— Qur’an 2:204 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes a man whose pleasant words and pious oaths cover deep hostility.
Pleasing on the surface, the fiercest opponent beneath: the Kural’s smiling deceiver.
Judaism
“with talk smoother than butter, / yet with a mind set on war; / with words that were softer than oil, / yet in fact were drawn swords.”
— Psalms 55:22 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria; KJV 55:21) [6]
The psalmist describes a former companion whose soft words hid a mind bent on war.
Smooth words outside, swords within: the same split the Kural tells us to fear.
Buddhism
“What is the use of your matted hair, O witless man? What of your garment of antelope's hide? Within you is the tangle (of passion); only outwardly do you cleanse yourself.”
— Dhammapada 394 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha rebukes a man whose outward ascetic appearance hides inner corruption.
Both texts warn that a pleasing outside can conceal what is tangled within.
Jainism
“Anger destroys love, pride destroys modesty, deceit destroys friendship; greed is destructive of everything.”
— Saman Suttam, v. 135 (from the Daśavaikālika Sūtra 8.38), English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit, via jainworld.com [8]
Jain teaching names deceit (māyā) as one of the four passions (kaṣāya) that bind karma, and says that deceit in particular destroys friendship.
Valluvar tells us to fear those who smile with the face while bitter within; the Jain verse explains why: such deceit is exactly the passion that destroys friendship.
Sikhism
“Farid, you wear your prayer shawl on your shoulders and the robes of a Sufi; your words are sweet, but there is a dagger in your heart. Outwardly, you look bright, but your heart is dark as night.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1380, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 50) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid exposes the person whose outward look and sweet words hide a dagger within.
The Kural says to fear deceivers who smile with the face while the heart is bitter; Farid's sweet words with a dagger in the heart are the same split between face and heart.
Greek Mythology
Circe welcomes Odysseus’s men graciously into her hall and serves them a pleasant mixture of cheese, barley, honey and wine, into which she has put drugs; then she strikes them with her wand and turns them into swine.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 10 [10]
Warm hospitality is the vehicle of the harm.
Circe’s gracious welcome over a harmful purpose is the sweet face and bitter heart of the Kural.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Republic, Glaucon argues that the height of injustice is to seem just without being just, so that the unjust man wears a reputation for goodness while doing harm.
— Plato, Republic, Book II (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato poses the problem of the person whose appearance of virtue is a mask.
The Kural’s deceiver is the person who seems good to our face but is not good to us.

Real Life Example

An Lushan, the emperor's favourite, rebels against the Tang
755-756 · Northeastern China, Luoyang and Chang'an
An Lushan, a general of Sogdian and Turkish origin, rose in the 740s to command all three northeastern military regions, with about 160,000 troops, and on his frequent visits to the capital became a personal favourite of Emperor Xuanzong and his consort Yang Guifei. Toward the end of 755, claiming a secret order from the emperor to remove the chief minister Yang Guozhong, he marched his army on the capital. He took Luoyang, proclaimed himself emperor in 756 and captured Chang'an, forcing Xuanzong to flee to Sichuan.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The general who had won the emperor's warm personal favour became the leader of a rebellion that shook the Tang dynasty for eight years.
The kural warns against deceivers whose faces smile sweetly while their hearts are bitter, and An Lushan rose as a court favourite before turning his army against the emperor who had trusted him.
