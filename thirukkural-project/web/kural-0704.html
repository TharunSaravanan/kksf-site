Kural 704

The Knowledge of Signals · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

குறித்தது கூறாமைக் கொள்வாரோ டேனை உறுப்போ ரனையரால் வேறு.
Kuriththadhu kooraamaik kolvaarodu enai Uruppor anaiyaraal veru.

Meaning

People who understand what others mean without being told, and people who cannot, may look the same, but they are very different.

Elementary

Two phones can look exactly the same on the outside, but one has a smart app that understands your voice and the other does not. People are like that too: two people can look alike, but one understands what you mean without words and the other does not.

University

Kuriththadhu is ‘what (someone) has in mind’, and kooraamaik kolvaar ‘those who take it in without its being spoken’. Enai = the others (ஏனை), and uruppu or anaiyar (உறுப்பு ஓர் அனையர்) means ‘alike in limbs’, i.e., in bodily form; aal (ஆல்) is concessive (‘though’), and veru ‘they are different’. Parimelazhagar supplies the implied contrast: they are different in understanding (arivu), so the bodily likeness is misleading. The couplet quietly shifts uruppu from 703’s political ‘limbs of the kingdom’ to the body’s limbs. Commentators link it with the Kural’s repeated claim that the unperceptive are human only in form (compare 993 and the chapter on folly), and with the Sanskrit maxim that intelligence shows itself in grasping what is unsaid. The sandhi text reads ‘யுறுப்போ ரனையரால்’; there are no variants of meaning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He that understandeth quickly, listeneth patiently, pursueth his objects with judgment and not from desire and spendeth not his breath on the affairs of others without being asked, is said to possess the foremost mark of wisdom”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 33 (Vidura Niti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Vidura tells King Dhritarashtra that quick understanding is the first sign that separates the wise (pandita) from the rest.
Vidura, like Valluvar, uses quickness of understanding to divide people who otherwise look the same.
Christianity
“But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil.”
— Hebrews 5:14 (KJV) [4]
The letter contrasts spiritual infants with mature people whose perception has been trained by practice.
Like the Kural, it divides people not by appearance but by trained powers of discernment.
Islam
“And if We willed, We could show them to you, and you would know them by their mark; but you will surely know them by the tone of [their] speech.”
— Qur’an 47:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Prophet is told he will recognise the hypocrites, who look like everyone else, from subtle signs in how they speak.
People who look identical are told apart by the perceptive, which is exactly the Kural’s contrast.
Judaism
The Mishnah lists seven traits that mark a golem (an uncultivated person) and seven that mark a wise person, such as not interrupting, answering to the point, and knowing what one has not understood.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 5:7 [6]
Wisdom is defined by how a person listens and takes in what others say, not by status or appearance.
Both texts say that the real difference between people lies in how they understand others, not in how they look.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that a person’s virtue, purity, endurance and discernment can be known only through long association, and only by one who is attentive and discerning, not by one who is not.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.192, Thana Sutta [7]
The ability to see what others are really like belongs to the discerning person and not to others.
The sutta, like the Kural, draws a line between those who perceive what lies beneath and those who cannot.
Jainism
“An excellent pupil needs no express directions, or he is (at least) quickly directed; he always carries out his duties as he is told.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.44 (cf. 1.2–3) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana sets the pupil who understands without being told against the 'ill-behaved' one who stays insubordinate and inattentive even when given an order (1.3).
Both texts divide people who look alike into two different kinds: those who grasp what is wanted without being told, and those who do not.
Sikhism
Sikh tradition tells how a jug fell into mud and Guru Nanak asked his sons to pick it up; they refused the dirty, menial task, but Bhai Lehna lifted it out, washed it clean and brought it back full of water. Guru Nanak later named Lehna, renamed Angad, as his successor instead of his own sons.
— Wikipedia, 'Guru Angad' (summarising W. Owen Cole and Piara Singh Sambhi, The Sikhs: Their Religious Beliefs and Practices, 1978, p. 18) [9]
The sons and the disciple stood equally close to the Guru, yet only Lehna understood and carried out what the Guru wanted; the story is one of several told to explain his choice as Guru Angad.
The Kural says those who grasp a master's intent unbidden and those who do not may look alike in body yet are different kinds of people; the Guru's sons and Lehna show exactly this.
Greek Mythology
“so true is it that the gods do not grace all men alike in speech, person, and understanding.”
— Homer, Odyssey 8 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus rebukes the handsome but tactless Euryalus, saying one man may look plain yet be gifted in mind, while another looks like a god but lacks sense.
Odysseus makes the Kural’s point: outward form is no guide to the powers of the mind.
Philosophy
Aristotle separates understanding (synesis) and considerate judgement (gnome), the capacity to grasp and fairly judge what others say and mean, from other intellectual virtues, noting that people are praised as ‘understanding’ for it.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.10-11 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
He treats the grasp of another’s meaning as a distinct excellence that some people have and others lack.
Aristotle, like Valluvar, names the faculty that makes people who look alike very different.

Real Life Example

Dr Joseph Bell's powers of observation inspire Sherlock Holmes
1870s-1892 · Edinburgh, Scotland, United Kingdom
Joseph Bell, a surgeon who taught at the University of Edinburgh and the Royal Infirmary, taught that keen observation of small details was the key to accurate diagnosis, and reportedly demonstrated this by working out strangers' backgrounds and habits in front of his classes from their clothing and manner. The medical student Arthur Conan Doyle became Bell's assistant at the Infirmary, and when he created Sherlock Holmes in A Study in Scarlet (1887) the detective's powers were widely said to be modelled on Bell's. In a letter of 7 May 1892, now held by the Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh, Doyle told Bell that he owed Sherlock Holmes to him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bell showed that, among people who look alike, the one who notices what others miss is in effect a different kind of person.
The kural says those who grasp what is unspoken and those who cannot may look alike in body yet are different kinds of people, the very contrast Bell drew between himself and his students, and Doyle between Holmes and Watson.
