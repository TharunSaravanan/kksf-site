Kural 923

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

ஈன்றாள் முகத்தேயும் இன்னாதால் என்மற்றுச் சான்றோர் முகத்துக் களி.
Eendraal mugaththeyum innaadhaal enmatruch chaandror mugaththuk kali.

Meaning

Being drunk is painful to see even for the mother who gave birth to you. How much worse must it look to good and wise people?

Elementary

Your mom loves you no matter what, even after you spill juice on the carpet. But some behaviour makes even a loving mom sad and embarrassed. If something upsets the person who forgives you most, imagine how it looks to a strict teacher or a respected elder.

University

The couplet argues from the lesser to the greater. Eendraal (ஈன்றாள், 'she who bore') is the most forgiving witness a person has; Puliyur Kesikan glosses her as the one who rejoices whatever her child does. Even 'in her face' (mugaththeyum, முகத்தேயும்) kali (களி, intoxication) is innaadhu (இன்னாது, painful). The question en matru (என் மற்று, 'what, then?') carries the argument to the saandror (சான்றோர்), whom Mu. Varadarasanar describes as having the nature of rebuking faults (குற்றம் கடியும் இயல்பு). The repeated mugam (முகம், face) presents shame as something seen. Kalaignar reads the subject as the son his mother cannot bear to look at. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Chandogya Upanishad names the one who drinks liquor among four great sinners who ‘fall’, along with the thief of gold, the violator of the teacher's bed and the slayer of a Brahmin, and adds as a fifth whoever keeps company with them.
— Chandogya Upanishad 5.10.9 [3]
The Upanishad treats drinking as a fall so grave that it stains even those around the drinker.
As in the Kural, drink brings grief and disgrace not only to the drinker but to those near him.
Christianity
“And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brethren without. And Shem and Japheth took a garment, … and covered the nakedness of their father; and their faces were backward, and they saw not their father's nakedness.”
— Genesis 9:22-23 (KJV) [4]
After Noah gets drunk, his own sons cannot bear to look at him and cover his shame.
As with the mother in the Kural, it is the closest family who find the drunkard painful to see.
Islam
“Hamza was drunk and his eyes were red. … and then said, ‘You are not but my father's slaves.’ When the Prophet (ﷺ) understood that Hamza was drunk, he retreated, walking backwards went out and we left with him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 4003 [5]
Before Islam forbade wine, the Prophet's own uncle Hamza, drunk, insults the Prophet, who quietly withdraws.
Drunkenness in the presence of the most honoured person turns into a painful scene of dishonour, as the Kural says.
Judaism
“his father and mother shall take hold of him and bring him out to the elders of his town at the public place of his community. They shall say to the elders of his town, ‘This son of ours is disloyal and defiant; he does not heed us. He is a glutton and a drunkard.’”
— Deuteronomy 21:19-20 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The law of the wayward son has the parents themselves bring their drunkard son before the town's elders.
The pain begins with the mother and father and ends before the elders, the same movement as the Kural.
Buddhism
“In five ways, young householder, the parents thus ministered to as the East by their children, show their compassion: (i) they restrain them from evil, (ii) they encourage them to do good, …”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (Narada Thera trans.) [7]
In the Sigalovada the parents' love shows itself in holding their children back from wrong, intoxicants included, which the same sutta lists as a ruinous habit.
A parent's grief at a child's drinking comes from this protective love, which the Kural appeals to.
Jainism
“An ignorant man kills, lies, deceives, calumniates, dissembles, drinks liquor, and eats meat, thinking that this is the right thing to do.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 5.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahāvīra's lecture on 'death against one's will' lists drinking liquor among the habits of the ignorant man (bāla), the opposite of the wise, who brings himself to grief.
Valluvar says that if even a mother cannot bear the sight of her drunken son, the wise will find it far worse; the Uttarādhyayana likewise puts the drinker firmly among the ignorant whom the wise reject.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, those mortals who consume marijuana, fish and wine”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1377, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir’s salok goes on to say that whatever pilgrimages, fasts and rituals such people keep, they are lost. For him piety does not excuse intoxication; the wise judge the drinker strictly.
The Kural argues that if drunkenness pains even a forgiving mother, the wise who excuse no fault will see it as worse still, and Kabir is that unforgiving wise judge.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides' Alcestis, Heracles drinks and sings in King Admetus' house without knowing it is in mourning; a household servant is appalled, and Heracles is ashamed once he learns the truth.
— Euripides, Alcestis [10]
The revelry of a hero who is loved and honoured becomes painful to the house that welcomed him.
Even friends and household are hurt by the sight of someone in drink, as the Kural says of a mother.
Philosophy
“Show how base it is to pour down more liquor than one can carry, and not to know the capacity of one's own stomach; show how often the drunkard does things which make him blush when he is sober; state that drunkenness is nothing but a condition of insanity purposely assumed.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 83.18-19 (tr. Richard Mott Gummere) [11]
Seneca sets aside Zeno's clever syllogisms against drink and says the honest case against drunkenness is simply to show how shameful it is. He adds that it strips away the sense of shame that otherwise holds men back from wrong.
Like the Kural, Seneca makes the drunkard's shame, seen through the eyes of others and of his own sober self, the main charge against drink.

Real Life Example

The Women's Crusade against the saloons begins at Hillsboro, Ohio
December 1873 · Hillsboro, Ohio, United States
After the lecturer Dio Lewis told audiences how his own mother had prayed with other wives of drinking men until a saloon keeper closed his business, women in Hillsboro, Ohio, formed a committee led by Eliza Jane Thompson and from 26 December 1873 went into saloons and drug stores to pray, sing and ask sellers to sign pledges to stop selling liquor. Similar bands spread across the Midwest and Northeast in the winter of 1873-74, drawing tens of thousands of women, most of whom had until then confined themselves to the roles of wives and mothers. The movement led directly to the founding of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union in Cleveland in November 1874.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Crusade showed how deeply drunkenness wounded the women closest to drinkers, enough to bring wives and mothers out of their homes into public protest for the first time on such a scale.
The kural says that drunkenness is painful to see even for a mother, the most forgiving of witnesses, and the Women's Crusade was born from the grief of mothers and wives watching the men of their families drink.
