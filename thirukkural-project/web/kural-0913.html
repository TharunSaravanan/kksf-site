Kural 913

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

பொருட்பெண்டிர் பொய்ம்மை முயக்கம் இருட்டறையில் ஏதில் பிணந்தழீஇ அற்று.
Porutpendir poimmai muyakkam iruttaraiyil edhil pinandhazheei atru.

Meaning

The fake hug of a woman who wants only money is like hugging a stranger's dead body in a dark room.

Elementary

Think of hugging a big cardboard cut-out of a friend in the dark. It is shaped like a person, but nobody is really there to hug you back. Valluvar says that affection bought with money is like that: it looks like love, but there is no real heart inside it.

University

Kural 913 is the chapter’s most startling simile. Porut-pendir (பொருட்பெண்டிர், ‘women of wealth’, i.e. women who are for wealth) offer a poimmai muyakkam (பொய்ம்மை முயக்கம், false embrace), compared to clasping an edhil pinam (ஏதில் பிணம், a stranger’s corpse) in an iruttarai (இருட்டறை, dark room). Parimelazhagar’s gloss, followed by Puliyur Kesigan and by the bracketed ‘(hired men)’ in the standard English explanation, explains the image: it is the hired corpse-bearer who handles an unknown body in the dark for pay, without any bond, so the transaction is equally loveless on both sides. Kalaignar reads it as acting out love purely for money. The form thazheei (தழீஇ) is an alapedai (lengthened vowel) form of thazhuvi, ‘having embraced’. Both base sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The pleasures that arise from contact with the sense objects, though appearing as enjoyable to worldly-minded people, are verily a source of misery... so the wise do not delight in them.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.22 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna teaches that pleasures born only of sense contact are empty and end in sorrow.
Kural 913 shows the same emptiness in its most vivid form: an embrace with no living heart in it.
Christianity
“Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death.”
— James 1:15 (KJV) [4]
James traces a path from desire to sin to death.
Valluvar puts death at the very center of the embrace itself, which is cold as a corpse.
Islam
“But those who disbelieved - their deeds are like a mirage in a lowland which a thirsty one thinks is water until, when he comes to it, he finds it is nothing...”
— Qur’an 24:39 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an compares empty deeds to a mirage that promises water and gives nothing.
The false embrace in Kural 913 also looks like love up close and turns out to hold nothing.
Judaism
“Her house is the way to the nether-world, Going down to the chambers of death.”
— Proverbs 7:27 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs ends its warning about the enticing woman by linking her house to death.
Kural 913 also ties this intimacy to death, through the corpse in the dark room.
Buddhism
“Behold this body — a painted image, a mass of heaped up sores, infirm, full of hankering — of which nothing is lasting or stable!”
— Dhammapada 147 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada asks the reader to see the attractive body as a painted surface over decay.
Valluvar likewise strips the purchased embrace down to what it really is: a lifeless body.
Jainism
Jain monks and devout lay people meditate on twelve reflections (anupreksha). The sixth is asuchi, the impurity of the body, which Sangave renders as the body being impure and dirty.
— Asuchi anupreksha, as described in V.A. Sangave, 'Jaina ethics' (jainworld.com); cf. Tattvartha Sutra ch. 9 on the twelve reflections [8]
Jainism weakens physical desire by meditating on the body as perishable and impure, not as a source of real joy.
Kural 913 likens the false embrace of a woman who wants only money to clasping a stranger's corpse in a dark room, a shock image of the same kind as the Jain reflection on the body's impurity.
Sikhism
“Beholding an illusion, you are deceived, and on your bed, you enjoy a false lover.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 460, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan pictures the deluded soul as a bride taken in by a mirage, lying on her bed with a lover who is not real. The false lover is Maya: an embrace that gives nothing true back.
The Kural’s stranger’s corpse in a dark room and Guru Arjan’s false lover on the bed both describe an embrace with no living love in it.
Greek Mythology
In the Underworld, Odysseus tries three times to embrace the shade of his mother Anticleia, and three times she slips through his arms like a shadow or a dream.
— Homer, Odyssey 11 [10]
Homer’s scene shows an embrace that holds nothing living.
Kural 913’s embrace of a corpse is also contact without any real presence, though Homer’s scene is tender rather than mercenary.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that self-indulgence in the pleasures of touch is especially open to reproach because it belongs to us not as human beings but as animals.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.10 [11]
For Aristotle, pleasure cut off from reason and relationship is the least human kind.
Valluvar’s corpse simile makes the same point more harshly: a purely bought embrace has nothing human in it.

Real Life Example

Madame du Barry becomes Louis XV's official favourite
1769-1774 · Versailles, France
Jeanne Bécu, a Paris shop assistant, became the companion of Jean du Barry, a nobleman who had made a fortune as a war contractor and who introduced her into high society. Because the position of official royal favourite required a noble husband, a nominal marriage was arranged to his brother, and in 1769 she was presented at the court of Louis XV. When the king died in May 1774, his successor Louis XVI had her sent away from Versailles to a convent.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her rise was arranged through a marriage of convenience and ended the moment the king who had favoured her was gone.
The kural compares a relationship sought for wealth and position to an embrace without feeling, and the purely formal arrangements that brought du Barry to court show how little of genuine bond such a connection could hold.
