Kural 169

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அவ்விய நெஞ்சத்தான் ஆக்கமும் செவ்வியான் கேடும் நினைக்கப் படும்.
Avviya nenjathaan aakkamum sevviyaan Kedum ninaikkap padum.

Meaning

When a jealous person grows rich and a good, honest person falls into hard times, it is something strange that we should stop and think about.

Elementary

Sometimes a mean, jealous kid seems to get everything, while a kind kid has a hard time. That looks unfair, and this kural says: stop and think about it. Things may not stay that way, and there is a deeper reason behind it.

University

Valluvar recognizes the classic problem of theodicy: the wicked sometimes prosper and the good sometimes suffer. He does not deny it. He calls these cases நினைக்கப்படும், “to be reflected upon.” Parimelazhagar resolves the anomaly through karma: the envious man's prosperity comes from past merit now being used up, and the good man's suffering from past deeds now being paid off, so neither lasts. The same wrestling appears in Psalm 73, in Rabbi Yannai's admission in Pirkei Avot, in the Dhammapada's teaching that evil “ripens” late, and in Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic, which makes this one of the most widely shared questions in world ethics.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In exile in the forest, Draupadi laments to Yudhishthira that the righteous king suffers while the wicked Duryodhana prospers, and questions divine justice; Yudhishthira answers that one practices dharma for its own sake and that its fruits come in time.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (Draupadi–Yudhishthira dialogue), Ganguli translation [3]
The Mahabharata stages a full debate over why the virtuous suffer and the envious thrive.
This is exactly the anomaly the Kural says must be pondered: the envious prosper and the upright suffer.
Christianity
“For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked. ... Until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end.”
— Psalm 73:3, 17 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist is troubled that the wicked prosper, until reflection in God's sanctuary shows him their final end.
This is the Kural's point: the prosperity of the wicked is a puzzle to be pondered until one sees how it ends.
Islam
“And let not those who disbelieve ever think that [because] We extend their time [of enjoyment] it is better for them. We only extend it for them so that they may increase in sin, and for them is a humiliating punishment.”
— Qur'an 3:178 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an explains that the apparent prosperity of wrongdoers is a respite that will end in punishment, not a sign of favor.
Both texts ask the reader to think past the surface when the undeserving prosper.
Judaism
“Rabbi Yannai said: it is not in our hands [to explain the reason] either of the security of the wicked, or even of the afflictions of the righteous.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:15 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah admits that the ease of the wicked and the suffering of the righteous are beyond full human explanation.
It names the same two anomalies as the Kural: the wicked's prosperity and the upright's suffering.
Buddhism
“It may be well with the evil-doer as long as the evil ripens not. But when it does ripen, then the evil-doer sees (the painful results of) his evil deeds. It may be ill with the doer of good as long as the good ripens not. But when it does ripen, then the doer of good sees (the pleasant results of) his good deeds.”
— Dhammapada 119-120 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha explains that results of deeds ripen with a delay, so the wicked may prosper and the good suffer for a while.
This is the karmic resolution that Parimelazhagar gives for the Kural's anomaly.
Jainism
“When Mahāvīra broke his fast at the proud, wealthy Abhinava's house, a stream of treasure fell there, while the devout but ruined Jinadatta, who had waited in vain, gained nothing visible; an omniscient disciple of Pārśva then explained that Jinadatta had earned rebirth in the Acyuta heaven, and that “the stream of treasure, fruit in this world of the Arhat’s fast-breaking was obtained by Sheth Abhinava, who is devoid of pure character.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book X, ch. IV, Part 4 (episode of Jīrṇaśreṣṭhin and Abhinava) (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS) [8]
The episode answers why a man of poor character can prosper while a good man stays poor: visible wealth is only a this-worldly fruit, and the real merit lies with the pure-minded, to bear fruit in time.
Valluvar says the prosperity of the envious and the poverty of the upright must be pondered, and this Jain story ponders exactly that case and resolves it through karma and inner disposition.
Sikhism
“By His Command, some are high and some are low; by His Written Command, pain and pleasure are obtained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Pauri 2, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the second pauri of Japji, Guru Nanak teaches that rise and fall, joy and suffering all happen within hukam, the divine order, which is beyond human calculation.
The Kural asks us to ponder why the envious sometimes prosper and the upright suffer; Japji answers that such reversals of fortune lie within God's hukam.
Greek Mythology
When the wealthy King Croesus asks Solon who is the happiest of men, Solon refuses to call Croesus happy and says no one should be called fortunate until one has seen how his life ends; Croesus later loses his kingdom.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.30-33, 1.86 [10]
Solon warns that visible prosperity is no sure sign of lasting fortune.
Like the Kural, the story invites reflection on why fortune seems to go to the wrong people, and on how it ends.
Philosophy
In Book II of the Republic, Glaucon challenges Socrates with the case of the perfectly unjust man who enjoys wealth and reputation and the perfectly just man who is thought unjust and suffers torture. Socrates spends the rest of the dialogue showing that justice is still better.
— Plato, Republic II, 360e-362c [11]
Plato builds his central ethical argument around the anomaly of the prosperous wicked and the suffering righteous.
The Kural says the same anomaly must be pondered; the Republic ponders it at length.

Real Life Example

Aristides the Just is ostracised and then recalled
482-479 BCE · Athens, Salamis and Plataea, Greece
Aristides, an Athenian statesman renowned for his honesty and known as the Just, was ostracised in 482 BCE after political manoeuvring by his rival Themistocles and accusations of sympathy with Persia. When Xerxes invaded, the Athenians recalled the ostracised exiles in 480 BCE, and Aristides led a hoplite force at the battle of Salamis. In 479 BCE he was one of the Athenian commanders at the victory of Plataea.
— Sources: [12] [13]
An upright man suffered exile while his rival prevailed, yet within two years his city recalled him and gave him high command.
The kural invites reflection on the puzzle of the upright suffering misfortune, and Aristides' exile, soon reversed, shows such misfortune need not last.
