Kural 519

Selection and Employment · Therindhu Vinaiyaadal · தெரிந்து வினையாடல்

வினைக்கண் வினையுடையான் கேண்மைவே றாக நினைப்பானை நீங்கும் திரு.
Vinaikkan vinaiyudaiyaan kenmaive raaga Ninaippaanai neengum thiru.

Meaning

Good fortune leaves a ruler who wrongly doubts the loyalty of a worker who is truly devoted to the work.

Elementary

Imagine a kid who works really hard on a group project and is friendly with the teacher, and the teacher starts thinking “he must be up to something.” If you treat your best helpers that way, you lose them, and everything goes wrong.

University

The couplet warns against a failure of trust after appointment. வினைக்கண் வினையுடையான் is the man wholly absorbed in his assigned work; கேண்மை வேறாக நினைத்தல் is to read his familiarity or friendship with the ruler as something else, such as presumption or disloyalty. Pope renders it as failing to bear the informal friendly ways of such servants, and Pappaiah adds the courtly setting of slanderers who poison the ruler’s mind against capable officers. The penalty is that திரு (prosperity, the goddess of fortune) departs, because a ruler who drives away his best workers destroys his own wealth. Saul’s jealousy of David is the classic Hebrew example.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Dhritarashtra repeatedly suspects and rebukes his loyal half-brother and minister Vidura, at one point driving him from court, while listening to Duryodhana and his allies; the kingdom ends in ruin.
— Mahabharata, Sabha and Udyoga Parvas (Ganguli trans.) [3]
A king who distrusts his most faithful counsellor loses everything.
Suspecting the devoted servant drives away fortune, as the Kural warns.
Christianity
“Who then is a faithful and wise servant, whom his lord hath made ruler over his household, to give them meat in due season? Blessed is that servant, whom his lord when he cometh shall find so doing. Verily I say unto you, That he shall make him ruler over all his goods.”
— Matthew 24:45-47 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says a wise master rewards the servant he finds faithfully at work by giving him even more.
The positive side of the Kural: honour the devoted worker, don’t suspect him.
Islam
“O you who have believed, if there comes to you a disobedient one with information, investigate, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become, over what you have done, regretful.”
— Qur’an 49:6 (Sahih International) [5]
Believers are told to check reports from unreliable people before acting, or they may harm the innocent and regret it.
This matches Pappaiah’s reading: a ruler must not turn on a loyal worker because of slanderers.
Judaism
“And David had great success in all his ways; and the LORD was with him. And when Saul saw that he had great success, he stood in awe of him.”
— 1 Samuel 18:14-15 (JPS 1917) [6]
King Saul grows fearful and suspicious of David, his most successful servant, and tries to kill him; the kingdom passes from Saul’s house.
A ruler who turns on his devoted, effective servant loses his fortune, the Kural’s exact scenario.
Buddhism
According to the Dhammapada commentary, King Pasenadi of Kosala, swayed by jealous courtiers’ slander, had his loyal commander Bandhula and his sons killed; he later learned Bandhula was innocent, and in the end lost his throne with the help of Bandhula’s aggrieved nephew.
— Dhammapada Commentary, story of Vidudabha (Burlingame, Buddhist Legends) [7]
A king who distrusts his faithful commander on the word of slanderers brings about his own downfall.
Fortune leaves the ruler who misreads a loyal worker, as Kural 519 says.
Jainism
“(A bad pupil thinks:) 'I get but knocks and boxes on the ear, hard words and blows;' and he believes a teacher who instructs him well, to be a malevolent man.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on discipline describes the bad pupil who misreads the well-meant care of a devoted teacher as hostility.
The kural warns the ruler who misreads a devoted worker's closeness and grows suspicious of him; the Jain verse shows the same error of turning genuine devotion into imagined ill will, though here it runs from pupil to teacher.
Sikhism
“Slandering the Saints, all happiness vanishes. … Slandering the Saints, all one's influence vanishes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib (ashtapadi 13), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan lists the losses that come to one who speaks ill of the devoted: peace, standing and influence all depart.
The Kural says fortune leaves the ruler who grows suspicious of a servant devoted to his work; Guru Arjan says influence and happiness leave the one who turns against the devoted.
Greek Mythology
Theseus believes Phaedra’s false accusation against his son Hippolytus without looking into it and curses him; Hippolytus dies, and only then does Theseus learn the truth.
— Euripides, Hippolytus [10]
A ruler who misjudges a loyal, upright member of his household on false report brings ruin on himself.
This mirrors the Kural’s warning against thinking otherwise of the faithful.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes how tyrants keep power by cutting down anyone who stands out, distrusting good and able men, and preferring flatterers, and he treats these as marks of the worst kind of rule.
— Aristotle, Politics V.11, 1313a-1314a [11]
Suspicion of excellent subordinates is a defining vice of tyranny.
The Kural counts the same suspicion among the things that drive fortune away.

Real Life Example

Sultan Muhammad III has his minister Mahmud Gawan executed
1481 · Bidar, Bahmani Sultanate (now Karnataka, India)
Mahmud Gawan served the Bahmani sultans for many years as chief minister, and under him the sultanate reached its greatest size and degree of central control. Rival nobles falsified evidence, including a forged letter, to make him appear a traitor, and Sultan Muhammad III ordered his execution in 1481. Many nobles then withdrew from the court, and the effective power of the Bahmani state rapidly dissolved.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A ruler who let others turn him against his most devoted minister lost the support that held his kingdom together.
The kural warns that fortune leaves the ruler who grows suspicious of a servant devoted to his work, and the Bahmani kingdom's collapse after Gawan's death shows it.
