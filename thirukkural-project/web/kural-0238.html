Kural 238

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

வசையென்ப வையத்தார்க் கெல்லாம் இசையென்னும் எச்சம் பெறாஅ விடின்.
Vasaiyenba vaiyaththaarkku ellaam isaiyennum Echcham peraaa vidin.

Meaning

Wise people say that for anyone on earth, it is a shame not to leave behind a good name.

Elementary

A good camper leaves the campsite better than they found it, maybe with a helpful sign for the next visitors. Life is similar: it’s a shame to leave without leaving behind something good that people remember you by.

University

The pivotal word is எச்சம்: ‘remainder, residue, what is left over’, and by extension ‘offspring’ or ‘legacy’. Renown (இசை) is defined as the proper residue of a life; to fail to obtain it (பெறாஅ, with the poetic vowel-lengthening called அளபெடை) is வசை, reproach, and the plural subject வையத்தார்க் கெல்லாம் makes the standard universal. Pope captured the offspring sense: ‘Fame is virtue’s child, they say; if, then, / You childless live, you live the scorn of men.’ The idea that a person’s true progeny is their good name has a remarkable parallel in Isaiah 56:5, where the childless are promised ‘a name better than of sons and of daughters’, and in Diotima’s speech in Plato’s Symposium, where the ‘children’ of Homer and Lycurgus are their works. The Islamic teaching on what continues after death (ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, a righteous child) offers a devotional analogue: what a person leaves behind continues to count.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Your enemies will describe you in many unkind words and scorn your ability. What could be more painful for you?”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.36 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna warns Arjuna that abandoning his duty would leave him an object of public scorn, the most painful outcome of all.
Both texts treat the world’s reproach, for failing to achieve what earns honour, as a real and bitter disgrace.
Christianity
“By faith Abel offered unto God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain, by which he obtained witness that he was righteous, God testifying of his gifts: and by it he being dead yet speaketh.”
— Hebrews 11:4 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews says that Abel’s righteous offering still ‘speaks’ long after his death.
A good deed as the part of a person that remains and keeps speaking: the Kural’s எச்சம் of renown.
Islam
“When a man dies, his acts come to an end, but three, recurring charity, or knowledge (by which people) benefit, or a pious child, who prays for him (for the deceased).”
— Sahih Muslim 1631 [5]
The Prophet names three things that keep benefiting a person after death: ongoing charity (sadaqah jariyah), useful knowledge, and a righteous child.
An exact counterpart to எச்சம், the ‘remainder’ a life leaves behind, even including the offspring sense of the word.
Judaism
“Even unto them will I give in mine house and within my walls a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters: I will give them an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off.”
— Isaiah 56:5 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
God promises faithful eunuchs, who can have no children, an everlasting name that is better than descendants.
Precisely Pope’s reading of the Kural: renown is the offspring that outlasts a life, better than sons and daughters.
Buddhism
“As from a great heap of flowers many garlands can be made, even so should many good deeds be done by one born a mortal.”
— Dhammapada 53 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada urges every mortal to make the most of the material of life by producing many good deeds.
Both treat a human life as something that ought to leave a worthy product behind.
Jainism
“Neither his kinsmen, nor his friends, nor his sons, nor his relations will share his suffering, he alone has to bear it; for the Karman follows the doer.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 13.23 (cf. 13.24) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The monk Citra tells King Brahmadatta that at death nothing goes with a person except the karma they have earned.
Valluvar calls renown the proper legacy (eccam) that a life leaves behind. The Jain answer is that what remains of a life is the karma it carries forward, not a name left among others.
Sikhism
“Those who lack the Naam wander around lost, with their noses cut off; without the Name, they sit and cry.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1414, Salok Vaaran te Vadheek, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das says that those without the Name live in disgrace, using the Punjabi idiom of a cut-off nose for public shame.
The Kural says it is a disgrace to leave no legacy of renown; Guru Amar Das likewise calls the life that lacks its true treasure a life of shame.
Greek Mythology
“Let me not then die ingloriously and without a struggle, but let me first do some great thing that shall be told among men hereafter.”
— Homer, Iliad 22 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Realising he is doomed before Achilles, Hector resolves to leave behind at least a deed that will be told in the future.
Hector’s last wish is the Kural’s எச்சம்: not to leave the world without a remainder of renown.
Philosophy
“Who, when he thinks of Homer and Hesiod and other great poets, would not rather have their children than ordinary human ones?”
— Plato, Symposium 209d (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Diotima argues that great works and laws are ‘children of the soul’ that bring their parents everlasting glory, more precious than physical offspring.
Exactly the Kural’s ‘offspring called renown’: a legacy of fame as a truer child than any bodily heir.

Real Life Example

James Smithson's bequest founds the Smithsonian Institution
1829-1846 · London, England, and Washington, D.C., United States
James Smithson, a British scientist and the illegitimate son of Hugh Smithson, 1st Duke of Northumberland, never visited the United States and had no children. His will of 1826 provided that if his nephew died without heirs, his estate should go to the United States to found at Washington an institution bearing his name for the increase and diffusion of knowledge. The nephew died childless in 1835, Congress accepted the gift in 1836, and the Smithsonian Institution was established by an Act signed on 10 August 1846.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A childless man denied his father's name left a legacy that made his own name one of the best known in the world of learning.
The kural calls renown the legacy every life should leave behind, and Smithson, with no children, left a name that has outlived any line of descendants.
