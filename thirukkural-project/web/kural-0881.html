Kural 881

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

நிழல்நீரும் இன்னாத இன்னா தமர்நீரும் இன்னாவாம் இன்னா செயின்.
Nizhalneerum innaadha innaa thamarneerum innaavaam innaa seyin.

Meaning

Shade and water are pleasant, but they are bad when they make you sick. In the same way, your own relatives become painful when they do you harm.

Elementary

A shady tree and a cool drink are lovely on a hot day, but if the water is dirty it can make you sick. Family and friends are meant to be like shade and water, so when someone close to us starts hurting us, it feels even worse than a stranger doing it.

University

The chapter opens with a paired simile: nizhal-neer (நிழல்நீர், shade and water), the archetypal comforts of a hot land, are innaadha (இன்னாத, painful) when innaa (இன்னா, harmful, i.e. disease-bearing); so too thamar-neer (தமர்நீர், the 'nature' or disposition of one's kin) becomes innaavaam when it does harm. The wordplay on neer, meaning both 'water' and 'nature/quality', is deliberate and is reflected in Mu. Varadarasanar's and Kalaignar's glosses, which read thamar-neer as the qualities of kinsfolk. Solomon Pappaiah extends thamar to one's own party or faction, showing how modern commentators read utpakai (உட்பகை, enmity from within) politically as well as domestically. In the traditional reading framed by Parimelazhagar, the Porul section addresses a ruler, so the 'kin' include courtiers and relations around the throne. Text is identical in ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, Queen Kaikeyi, a member of Dasharatha's own household and once his favourite, uses two old boons to force Rama's exile, breaking the king's heart.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (Kaikeyi's boons) [3]
The person closest to the king, whose presence should have been a comfort, becomes the source of the household's greatest grief.
Like shade and water turned harmful, a beloved relative turned hostile brings pain precisely because she was trusted.
Christianity
“Yea, mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me.”
— Psalm 41:9 (KJV); cf. John 13:18 [4]
The psalmist laments betrayal by an intimate who shared his table, a verse the Gospel of John later applies to Judas.
The Kural likewise says that harm from those who should sustain us, like food and shade, is uniquely bitter.
Islam
“O you who have believed, indeed, among your wives and your children are enemies to you, so beware of them. But if you pardon and overlook and forgive - then indeed, Allah is Forgiving and Merciful.”
— Qur'an 64:14 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an warns that even the nearest family can, at times, work against one's good, while still urging forgiveness.
Both texts recognize that those meant to be our comfort can become a source of harm.
Judaism
Absalom, King David's own son, wins the hearts of the people at the city gate and then raises a rebellion that drives his father out of Jerusalem.
— 2 Samuel 15:1-14 [4]
The Hebrew Bible presents the revolt as a tragedy precisely because it comes from David's own child.
As the Kural warns, kin who turn to harm become a deeper misery than strangers.
Buddhism
Devadatta, the Buddha's own cousin and a monk in his order, plotted against the Buddha's life and tried to split the Sangha.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Cullavagga VII (Devadatta) [6]
Buddhist tradition remembers Devadatta as the danger that arose from inside the Buddha's own family and community.
He embodies the Kural's point that a relative who turns harmful is like refreshment that has turned to sickness.
Jainism
“What is this evil whim of warriors for persistence in heroism in which there is such a quarrel between brothers ending in destruction? If my elder brother does not live, enough of life for me.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 1 (Ādīśvara-caritra), ch. 5, part 11 'The fight between Bharata and Bāhubali' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS, 1931; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In Hemacandra's telling, Bāhubali has just felled his elder brother Bharata in their duel over the kingdom, and he fans the unconscious man with his garment, weeping, appalled at what the quarrel has made of two sons of Ṛṣabha.
The Kural warns that kin, like shade and water, are a comfort that turns to pain once they turn harmful, and the duel of Bharata and Bāhubali is the classic Jain case of brothers whose closeness became the source of their deepest injury.
Sikhism
When Guru Ram Das installed his youngest son Arjan as the fifth Guru in 1581, his eldest son Prithi Chand resented being passed over, and the succession caused lasting discord among the brothers.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Why was Guru Arjan's installation as Guru disputed?' (Britannica Editors) [8]
The Guru's own household, which should have been his chief support, became a source of rivalry and grief, and Prithi Chand's opposition dogged Guru Arjan's early years.
As the Kural says that shade and water turn harmful when they breed sickness, the closeness of kin became painful here precisely because the hostility came from within the family.
Greek Mythology
Deianira sends her husband Heracles a robe smeared with what she believes is a love charm from the centaur Nessus; it is poison, and it burns him to death.
— Sophocles, Women of Trachis [9]
A gift meant to bind and comfort becomes deadly, delivered by the hero's own wife.
This mirrors the Kural's image of comforts that turn harmful and of harm arriving through one's own household.
Philosophy
Paracelsus argued that all things can be poison and that only the dose decides whether a thing heals or harms.
— Paracelsus, Seven Defensiones, Third Defence (1538) [10]
The principle holds that even beneficial substances become harmful under the wrong conditions.
It echoes the first half of the Kural, where shade and water, ordinarily good, become harmful.

Real Life Example

Aurangzeb confines his father Shah Jahan in Agra Fort
1658-1666 · Agra, India
After the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657, his sons fought a war of succession, and his son Aurangzeb emerged victorious and took the throne in 1658. Aurangzeb had his father confined in Agra Fort, where Shah Jahan remained for about eight years until his death in January 1666. He was buried in the Taj Mahal beside his wife Mumtaz Mahal.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The emperor who had built the most famous monument of devotion to family spent his last years a prisoner of his own son.
The kural says the closeness of one's own people, like shade and water, becomes painful when it turns harmful, as Shah Jahan found when his son became his jailer.
