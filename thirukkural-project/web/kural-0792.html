Kural 792

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

ஆய்ந்தாய்ந்து கொள்ளாதான் கேண்மை கடைமுறை தான்சாம் துயரம் தரும்.
Aaindhaaindhu kollaadhaan kenmai kadaimurai thaansaam thuyaram tharum.

Meaning

If you make a friend without checking that person again and again, the friendship will bring you pain so bitter it could kill you in the end.

Elementary

If you eat a mushroom from the woods without checking it, it might look fine today and make you very sick tomorrow. Friends are like that too. Look carefully, and then look again, before you let someone become your close friend.

University

The doubling aaindhu-aaindhu (ஆய்ந்தாய்ந்து, ‘having examined and examined’) insists that a single check is not enough; Parimelazhagar understands it as examining a person by many means and on many occasions. Kenmai (கேண்மை) is intimate friendship, and kadaimurai (கடைமுறை) means ‘at the last’ or ‘in the final course’, so the harm is delayed, which is why it is so easily ignored. Thaan saam thuyaram (தான்சாம் துயரம்) is ‘a sorrow from which he himself dies’; most modern commentators, including Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar, take this as hyperbole for grief that can bring a person to death. The sandhi-joined text at thirukkural-thamizh.org reads தான்சாந் துயரந் தரும், a purely orthographic difference from the ytamizh and thirukkural.net texts.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Hitopadesha’s opening book, a deer befriends a smooth-talking jackal against the warning of his old friend the crow, who objects that one should not trust a newcomer whose nature is unknown; the jackal later lures the deer into a hunter’s snare hoping to feast on him, and only the crow’s cleverness saves the deer.
— Hitopadesha, Book I (Mitralabha), the deer, the crow and the jackal [4]
The fable, part of the Mitralabha (‘Winning of Friends’), turns on a friendship accepted on a stranger’s pleasant words without any examination.
It dramatises the Kural’s warning that an unexamined friendship ends in mortal danger.
Christianity
“Make no friendship with an angry man; and with a furious man thou shalt not go: Lest thou learn his ways, and get a snare to thy soul.”
— Proverbs 22:24-25 (KJV) [5]
The proverb warns against close friendship with a hot-tempered person because his ways become a trap for those who stay with him.
Both texts see a poorly chosen friendship as a trap whose damage arrives later.
Islam
“Oh, woe to me! I wish I had not taken that one as a friend. He led me away from the remembrance after it had come to me. And ever is Satan, to man, a deserter.”
— Qur’an 25:28-29 (Sahih International) [6]
On the Day of Judgment, the wrongdoer bites his hands and laments the friend who led him astray.
The verse paints exactly the Kural’s ‘grief at the last’: bitter regret over a friendship that should never have been formed.
Judaism
Amnon, son of King David, has a friend named Jonadab, described as a very shrewd man, who devises the scheme by which Amnon wrongs his half-sister Tamar; two years later Absalom has Amnon killed in revenge.
— 2 Samuel 13 (Tanakh) [7]
The narrative in 2 Samuel 13 traces Amnon’s ruin back to the counsel of a clever but unscrupulous friend.
It is a biblical case of the Kural’s point: a friend not judged by character leads at last to death.
Buddhism
“The man who has evil comrades and friends is given to evil ways, to ruin does he fall in both worlds — here and the next.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
The Buddha tells the young householder Sigala that bad companions drag a person into ruin in this life and the next.
The verse matches the Kural’s picture of a bad friendship ending in destruction.
Jainism
According to the Bhagavatī Sūtra, the wandering ascetic Gośāla attached himself to Mahāvīra and travelled with him as a disciple for about six years. He then broke away, founded the rival Ājīvika sect, and years later came to denounce Mahāvīra, killing two of his monks with a blast of fiery power (tejoleśyā) and hurling it at Mahāvīra himself, though it turned back on Gośāla.
— Bhagavatī Sūtra (Vyākhyāprajñapti) 15, as retold in 'Association with Gosala' (jainworld.com) [9]
Jain tradition remembers Gośāla as the companion whose long association ended in bitter enmity and death, a cautionary tale about close bonds with someone of unsound character.
The Kural warns that intimacy with someone who was not examined again and again brings deadly grief in the end, and the Gośāla episode is Jainism's best-known story of a close companionship that turned lethal.
Sikhism
“Kabir! I have been ruined and destroyed by bad company, like the banana plant near the thorn bush. The thorn bush waves in the wind, and pierces the banana plant; see this, and do not associate with the faithless cynics.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir 88 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir pictures a soft banana plant growing beside a thorny beri bush: every gust of wind drives the thorns into it, so the damage of bad company comes slowly, again and again, until it destroys.
The Kural says friendship formed without repeated examination brings, in the end, grief that can kill; Kabir's banana plant is that slow, fatal wounding by a companion who was never tested.
Greek Mythology
Pirithous, king of the Lapiths, meets Theseus and the two swear friendship on the spot. Bound by that oath, Theseus joins Pirithous’ reckless plan to carry off Persephone from the underworld; both are trapped in Hades, and while Heracles later frees Theseus, Pirithous is left there for ever.
— Apollodorus, Library, Epitome 1.23-24; Plutarch, Life of Theseus 30-35 [11]
The myth, told by Apollodorus and others, is the classic Greek case of a friendship sworn at first meeting and kept at ruinous cost.
It shows how a friendship formed without scrutiny ends in deadly sorrow.
Philosophy
“Further, such friendship requires time and familiarity; as the proverb says, men cannot know each other till they have ‘eaten salt together’ … for a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle says the best kind of friendship takes long acquaintance, because only time shows whether each person really deserves the other’s trust.
Aristotle’s ‘eating salt together’ is the long, repeated testing that the Kural’s aaindhu-aaindhu demands.

Real Life Example

Pompey seeks refuge with the court of Ptolemy XIII
48 BC · Pelusium, Egypt
After his defeat by Julius Caesar at the Battle of Pharsalus in 48 BC, Pompey the Great fled to Egypt, seeking refuge with the young king Ptolemy XIII, whose family had long-standing ties to him. The king's courtiers, wishing to win the favour of the victorious Caesar, decided to be rid of their guest, and Pompey was killed as he came ashore by men sent to meet him.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Pompey relied on an old connection without weighing the men who now held power at the Egyptian court, and that misplaced trust cost him his life.
The kural warns that a friendship not examined again and again can bring, in the end, sorrow enough to kill, and Pompey's untested reliance on his Egyptian ties did exactly that.
