Kural 745

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

கொளற்கரிதாய்க் கொண்டகூழ்த் தாகி அகத்தார் நிலைக்கெளிதாம் நீரது அரண்.
Kolarkarithaaik kondakoozhth thaaki akaththaar nilaikkelithaam neerathu aran.

Meaning

A fort should be hard to capture, full of stored food, and easy for the people inside to defend.

Elementary

If you are playing a long game of capture-the-flag, your base needs three things. It should be hard for the other team to get into, it should have snacks and water so you don’t have to leave, and it should be easy for your own team to move around in. Valluvar says a real fort needs the same three things.

University

The couplet names three conditions. Kolarku arithaai means ‘hard to take’; Pappaiah adds ‘even under a siege of many days’. Konda koozhthu aagi means ‘holding provisions’. Koozh (literally gruel or food) is the same word that names the Koozhiyal (the section on wealth and resources) in some divisions of the Porutpaal, and here it means stored food. Akaththaar nilaikku elithaam means ‘easy for those within to hold their position’. Varadarasanar reads this as easy for the defenders to stay in, while Pappaiah and Kalaignar read it as convenient for the defenders to fight from. The final neerathu (‘of such a nature’) marks the three conditions as the fort’s defining nature. The ytamizh text reads ‘நிலைக்கெளிதாம் நீரது’, and the sandhi-joined text on thirukkural-thamizh.org reads ‘நிலைக்கெளிதா நீர தரண்’. These are orthographic variants of the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let that (fort) be well supplied with weapons, money, grain and beasts of burden, with Brahmanas, with artisans, with engines, with fodder, and with water.”
— Manusmriti 7.75 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu lists everything a royal fort must hold, from grain and water to weapons and skilled people.
Manu’s well-supplied fort is exactly the Kural’s fort ‘holding provisions’.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?”
— Luke 14:28 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says that anyone planning to build a tower must first make sure he has the resources to finish it.
Like the Kural, it treats a stronghold as useless without enough resources behind it.
Islam
“[Joseph] said, ‘You will plant for seven years consecutively; and what you harvest leave in its spikes, except a little from which you will eat.’”
— Qur’an 12:47 (Saheeh International) [5]
Joseph advises Egypt to store most of seven years’ harvest in preparation for the famine to come.
Stored grain against hard times is the Kural’s ‘konda koozh’, the fort’s food reserve.
Judaism
“He strengthened the fortified towns and put commanders in them, along with stores of food, oil, and wine, and shields and spears in every town.”
— 2 Chronicles 11:11-12 (JPS Tanakh 1985, via Sefaria) [6]
King Rehoboam fortified the towns of Judah and stocked each one with food, oil, wine and weapons.
A strong fort stocked with food is exactly what this couplet asks for.
Buddhism
“Furthermore, it has large stores of rice & barley for the delight, convenience, & comfort of those within, and to ward off those without.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha’s ideal fortress keeps large grain stores so that those inside are comfortable and can resist those outside.
This joins the Kural’s ideas of stored food and a fort that is ‘easy for those within’.
Jainism
“Making Faith his fortress, Penance and Self-control the bolt (of its gate), Patience its strong wall, so that guarded in three ways it is impregnable …”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.20–22, first half (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Nami replies to Indra that a sage's true fortress is built from faith, austerity, self-control and patience, and that, guarded by the three guptis of mind, speech and body, it cannot be taken.
The Kural asks for a fortress that is hard to capture and easy to hold; the Jain sage describes an inner citadel that is impregnable because its gate is barred by self-control and its wall is patience.
Sikhism
When the Rajput hill chiefs and imperial Mughal contingents laid siege to Guru Gobind Singh's fort at Anandpur, the Guru and his Sikhs withstood their successive assaults over many months despite dire scarcity of food caused by the prolonged blockade, until the besieged were reduced to desperate straits.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entry Gobind Singh, Guru [9]
The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism shows that Anandpur held firm against assault, and that the blockade's real weapon was hunger.
The Kural says a fortress must be hard to take and well stocked with food; Anandpur proved the first, and its ordeal shows why the second matters.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus reports that when Cyrus besieged Babylon, the Babylonians had stored provisions for many years in advance and did not worry about the siege. Cyrus took the city only by diverting the Euphrates and entering along the riverbed.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.190-191 [10]
Babylon’s stores made it almost impossible to starve out, so the attacker had to use a trick instead.
Babylon illustrates the Kural’s provisioned fort and hints at its warning, in the next couplets, about stratagem.
Philosophy
“With a view to the latter it should afford easy egress to the citizens, and at the same time be inaccessible and difficult of capture to enemies.”
— Aristotle, Politics VII.11 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle says that, for war, a city’s site should make movement easy for its own citizens and approach hard for enemies.
This is almost word for word the Kural’s ‘hard to capture’ yet ‘easy for those within’.

Real Life Example

Hunger forces the surrender of Vicksburg
4 July 1863 · Vicksburg, Mississippi, United States
The Confederate army under General John Pemberton had spent more than a year fortifying Vicksburg with a ring of earthen forts and over 170 cannon, and two Union assaults ordered by General Ulysses S. Grant on 19 and 22 May 1863 failed to break through. Grant then laid siege to the city and cut it off from supplies, and the defenders' daily rations shrank to tiny portions of bacon, flour, sugar, peas and molasses. After 47 days of siege, Pemberton's army surrendered on 4 July 1863.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Vicksburg showed that earthworks and guns could repel direct assault but not hunger; once its food ran short, the stronghold that controlled the Mississippi had to yield.
The kural asks that a fort be hard to capture, well stocked with food and easy for its defenders to hold; Vicksburg had the first and third qualities but not the second, and fell for that lack.
