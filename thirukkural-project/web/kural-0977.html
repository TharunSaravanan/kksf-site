Kural 977

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

இறப்பே புரிந்த தொழிற்றாம் சிறப்புந்தான் சீரல் லவர்கண் படின்.
Irappe purindha thozhitraam sirappundhaan seeral lavarkan padin.

Meaning

When honor and high position come to people of poor character, it only makes them act in ways that go beyond all limits.

Elementary

Give a bossy child the job of class leader and he might start ordering everyone around and showing off. The badge was meant for helping, but in the wrong hands it turns into bullying.

University

Sirappu (சிறப்பு, 'eminence, distinction') here covers whatever raises a person; Solomon Pappaiah lists money, learning and office, and Pope's explanation 'nobility of birth, wealth and learning' reflects the traditional commentarial list. When it reaches seeral lavar (சீரல்லவர், 'those without worth'), it becomes irappe purindha thozhitru (இறப்பே புரிந்த தொழிற்று), 'of the nature of acting only in excess': irappu (இறப்பு) is taken by Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar as 'overstepping the bounds' (வரம்பு மீறிய செயல்) and by Kesikan as being driven into arrogance. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads irappu as 'destruction', a minority gloss. The couplet is the negative counterpart to Kural 975: the same eminence that lets the great do rare deeds makes the small insolent. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Self-complacent and always impudent, deluded by wealth and false prestige, they sometimes proudly perform sacrifices in name only, without following any rules or regulations.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.17 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna describes the demoniac as people whom wealth and prestige make impudent and lawless.
Like the Kural, it shows advantages turning into insolence and disregard of every limit in unworthy hands.
Christianity
“For three things the earth is disquieted, and for four which it cannot bear: For a servant when he reigneth; and a fool when he is filled with meat;”
— Proverbs 30:21-22 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs names the unfit person suddenly raised to power as something the world cannot bear.
This is the Kural's distinction falling to the unworthy and turning into excess.
Islam
“No! [But] indeed, man transgresses Because he sees himself self-sufficient.”
— Qur'an 96:6-7 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says a person oversteps bounds once he feels he needs no one.
The Kural's 'deeds that overstep the limit' arise in the same way when the unworthy gain distinction.
Judaism
“When you have eaten your fill, and have built fine houses to live in, and your herds and flocks have multiplied, and your silver and gold have increased, and everything you own has prospered, beware lest your heart grow haughty and you forget the Eternal your God…”
— Deuteronomy 8:12-14 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Moses warns Israel that prosperity can make the heart haughty.
Like the Kural, it warns that advantages can turn into arrogance.
Buddhism
“To his own ruin the fool gains knowledge, for it cleaves his head and destroys his innate goodness.”
— Dhammapada 72 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that in a fool even knowledge becomes destructive.
This is the Kural's point: a distinction such as learning, held by the unworthy, turns to harm.
Jainism
Samantabhadra's householder manual lists eight kinds of pride (mada) to be given up: pride in knowledge, honour, family, caste, power, attainments, austerity and bodily beauty, warning that one who looks down on others through such pride offends against the faith itself.
— Ratnakaranda Shravakachara of Samantabhadra, vv. 25-26 (tr. Champat Rai Jain) [8]
Jain ethics treats every advantage of birth, learning, power or beauty as a possible source of corrupting pride.
The eight madas are exactly the Kural's distinctions that, in the unworthy, breed overbearing conduct.
Sikhism
“Royal power, wealth, beauty, social status and youth are the five thieves. These thieves have plundered the world; no one’s honor has been spared.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1288, Raag Malaar, Vaar Malaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak lists the very things that raise a person in the world (power, wealth, beauty, status, youth) and calls them thieves. Unless a person is anchored in the Guru, they lead to ruin, and the next line says only those who fall at the Guru's feet rob these robbers.
The Kural warns that distinction falling to the unworthy turns into excess, and Guru Nanak names the same gifts of eminence as forces that plunder anyone who is not inwardly fit to hold them.
Greek Mythology
Ixion, after being purified by Zeus and honoured with a place among the gods on Olympus, repaid the favour by trying to seduce Hera, and was bound for ever to a fiery wheel.
— Pindar, Pythian Ode 2 (Ixion myth) [10]
Pindar tells the story as a warning about a man who could not bear his good fortune.
Ixion is the Kural's unworthy man whose distinction turns at once into outrageous excess.
Philosophy
In the Rhetoric Aristotle sketches the characters that wealth and power produce, observing that the newly rich in particular become insolent and arrogant, behaving as though they possessed every good.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.16 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle analyses how fortune tends to warp the character of those who receive it.
Like the Kural, he notes that distinction in unfit hands turns into insolence.

Real Life Example

Mobutu Sese Seko builds his palace city at Gbadolite
During Mobutu's rule, 1965-1997 · Gbadolite, Zaire (now Democratic Republic of the Congo)
Mobutu Sese Seko seized power in Zaire in 1965 and, while corruption and mismanagement led the country into decline, amassed one of the largest personal fortunes in the world. In his home region of Gbadolite he built three palaces lined with Italian marble, guest houses for the world's rich and famous, Chinese pagodas, a private hydroelectric plant and an airstrip for the Concorde airliner he frequently used, a complex nicknamed the Versailles of the Jungle. After he was overthrown in 1997 the palaces were looted and now stand in ruins in one of the poorest countries on earth.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The highest office in a vast nation became, in Mobutu's hands, a means to lavish excess while its people grew poorer.
The kural says that distinction falling to the unworthy turns into deeds that overstep every limit, and supreme office in Mobutu's hands produced the boundless extravagance of Gbadolite.
