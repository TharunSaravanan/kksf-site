Kural 406

Ignorance · Kallaamai · கல்லாமை

உளரென்னும் மாத்திரையர் அல்லால் பயவாக் களரனையர் கல்லா தவர்.
Ularennum maaththiraiyar allaal payavaak Kalaranaiyar kallaa thavar.

Meaning

Uneducated people can only be said to exist. Beyond that, they are like salty land where nothing grows.

Elementary

Some land is so salty that nothing grows on it, no matter how much rain falls. Valluvar says a person who never learns is like that land: they are there, alive, but nothing useful grows from them for themselves or anyone else.

University

Ularennum maaththiraiyar — ‘mere measure of existing’ — reduces the unlearned to bare presence. Kalar is saline or alkaline soil, proverbial in Tamil for barrenness; payavaa (‘unyielding’) insists on the absence of any fruit. The agricultural image makes learning the cultivation that turns land into field. Striking parallels exist: the Buddha’s Desana Sutta (SN 42.7) speaks of a field that is ‘salty’ and poor as the worst kind of soil; the hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari 79 compares recipients of knowledge to types of land, including barren land that neither holds water nor grows anything; Jeremiah 17:6 speaks of ‘a salt land and not inhabited’; Hesiod calls the man who neither thinks nor learns ‘unprofitable’; and Seneca’s ‘leisure without study is death… a tomb for the living man’ matches the Kural’s reduction of such a life to mere existence.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Hitopadesha asks what use is a son who is born but is neither learned nor virtuous — like a blind eye, which brings only pain.
— Hitopadesha, Prastavika (Introduction), verse on the unlearned son [4]
A life without learning, even in a family, is compared to an organ that is present but useless.
Both reduce the unlearned person to mere presence without benefit — existing, but yielding nothing.
Christianity
“Behold, these three years I come seeking fruit on this fig tree, and find none: cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?”
— Luke 13:7 (KJV) [5]
In Jesus’s parable, a fig tree that bears no fruit year after year is judged to be merely taking up space.
The Kural’s unlearned person likewise merely occupies the ground, yielding nothing.
Islam
The Prophet compared the guidance and knowledge sent through him to abundant rain falling on land: some fertile soil absorbs it and grows vegetation, some holds water for others, and “a portion of it was barren which could neither hold the water nor bring forth vegetation.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 79 (tr. Muhsin Khan) [6]
People respond to knowledge like different kinds of land; some benefit themselves and others, while some remain barren.
An almost identical image: the one who takes in no knowledge is barren land that yields nothing.
Judaism
“For he shall be like the heath in the desert, and shall not see when good cometh; but shall inhabit the parched places in the wilderness, in a salt land and not inhabited.”
— Jeremiah 17:6 (Hebrew Bible; KJV wording) [5]
Jeremiah compares a spiritually cut-off person to a shrub in salty, barren ground where nothing good comes.
The same ‘salt land’ image the Kural uses for the unlearned.
Buddhism
In the Desana Sutta the Buddha compares three fields — excellent, middling, and one that is poor, hard, salty, with bad soil — to three kinds of listeners to his teaching.
— Samyutta Nikaya 42.7 (Desana Sutta) [7]
The same seed does very differently depending on the ground; the salty, poor field yields least.
The Kural uses the same image of saline, unproductive soil for the person who has not taken in learning.
Jainism
“Though great stones do not understand the speech of men, yet since they do what is required of them, as standing, sitting, lying down, or moving, they are far more useful than a fool (as it is impossible to induce a fool to act as we wish him).”
— Naladiyar, ch. 34 'Ignorance', stanza 4 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar's chapter on ignorance ranks the fool below even dead stones. Stones are at least of some use, while the fool yields nothing.
Valluvar says the unlearned merely exist, like barren saline land that bears nothing. The Naladiyar similarly calls the fool more useless than lifeless stone.
Sikhism
“The evil-minded ones plant their seed in the barren soil; such is the sign of those who have no Guru.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1275, Raag Malaar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Malaar Guru Nanak uses the image of seed sown in barren, saline soil for the effort of those without the Guru's guidance.
The Kural compares the unlearned to barren saline land that yields nothing; Guru Nanak uses the same image of barren soil (kalar) for a life without the Guru's teaching.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says the best man thinks things through for himself, the next best listens to good advice, but the man who neither thinks for himself nor takes to heart what another tells him is an unprofitable man.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 293–297 [10]
The person who neither reasons nor learns from others is of no use.
Both call the unlearned person ‘unyielding’ or ‘unprofitable’ — present but without fruit.
Philosophy
“Leisure without study is death; it is a tomb for the living man.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 82.3 (tr. R. M. Gummere) [11]
Seneca says a life with free time but no study is not truly living — it is a kind of burial while still alive.
Just as the Kural says the unlearned merely ‘exist’, Seneca says a life without learning is death in life.

Real Life Example

Japan's Education System Order aims for no illiterate household
1872 · Japan
In 1872 the Meiji government issued the Gakusei, Japan's first comprehensive national education system, which divided the country into school districts and in principle required all children to attend elementary school regardless of sex, family occupation or social status. Its preamble declared that learning was the key to success in life and set the goal that no community should contain an illiterate family and no family an illiterate member. Lack of money, teachers and facilities meant the plan was only partly realised, and it was replaced by a new Education Order in 1879.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The order treated universal learning as the soil from which productive lives and a modern nation would grow.
The kural likens the unlearned to barren saline land that yields nothing, and the Gakusei rested on the same conviction that only learning makes a person's life fruitful.
