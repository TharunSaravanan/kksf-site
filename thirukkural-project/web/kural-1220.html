Kural 1220

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

நனவினால் நம்நீத்தார் என்பர் கனவினால் காணார்கொல் இவ்வூ ரவர்.
Nanavinaal namneeththaar enpar kanavinaal kaanaarkol ivvoo ravar.

Meaning

The people of this town say he left me in waking life. Do they not see him coming to me in my dreams?

Elementary

Imagine the neighbours saying, ‘Poor girl, her friend never visits.’ But every night her friend calls her on a secret walkie-talkie. She thinks, ‘They just can’t hear what I hear!’ That is how this woman feels about the town’s gossip.

University

The chapter closes by setting the heroine’s private knowledge against public opinion (alar, the town’s gossip that runs through the Kāmattuppāl). Nam nīttār eṉpar, ‘they say he has forsaken us’, reports the townsfolk’s verdict; ivvūravar, ‘the people of this town’, are the critics already glimpsed in 1219. The particle kol makes kāṇārkol a wondering question, ‘do they not see?’, which can be read two ways: Varadarasanar and Pappaiah take it as ‘do they not know that he visits me in dreams?’, whereas Kalaignar turns it back on the townsfolk, ‘do they never see their own departed lovers in dreams?’ Either way the dream, invisible to others, is her proof of his faithfulness. Pope: ‘They say, that he in waking hours has left me lone; / In dreams they surely see him not,- these people of the town.’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After Sita’s return to Ayodhya, the townspeople whisper doubts about her, unaware of the fidelity she kept through captivity, and Rama, hearing of the talk, bows to public opinion.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda, the townspeople’s gossip about Sita (paraphrase) [3]
The Ramayana’s final book dramatises the cruelty of town gossip that cannot see a wife’s inner truth.
The Kural’s townsfolk likewise pronounce judgement on a marriage whose real life, here the nightly dream-visits, they cannot see.
Christianity
“What is thy beloved more than another beloved, O thou fairest among women? what is thy beloved more than another beloved, that thou dost so charge us?”
— Song of Solomon 5:9 (KJV) [4]
The daughters of Jerusalem, not sharing the bride’s love, question why she prizes an absent beloved so highly.
They are the Kural’s ‘people of this town’: outsiders puzzled by a devotion whose grounds they cannot see.
Islam
“Indeed, I find the smell of Joseph [and would say that he was alive] if you did not think me weakened in mind.” They said, “By Allah, indeed you are in your [same] old error.”
— Qur’an 12:94-95 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob senses his long-lost son is near, and those around him dismiss his inner certainty as an old delusion.
The Kural’s heroine faces the same scepticism: she knows her beloved has not truly left her, while the town sees only his absence.
Judaism
“And they said one to another: ‘Behold, this dreamer cometh.’”
— Genesis 37:19 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Joseph’s brothers mock him as ‘the master of dreams’, scorning visions they do not share.
The Kural’s townsfolk, likewise dismissing what is seen only in dreams, are blind to the truth the dreamer holds.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells of blind men who each touch one part of an elephant and then quarrel, each sure the whole elephant is like the part he felt.
— Udana 6.4, Tittha Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The parable warns against confident judgements formed from a partial view.
The Kural’s townsfolk see only the waking half of the marriage and confidently declare the husband gone, missing the nightly half.
Jainism
Jain epistemology teaches that reality is known through valid knowledge and partial standpoints (nayas), and that any single standpoint, taken as the whole truth, misleads.
— Tattvartha Sutra 1.6 and 1.33 on pramana and naya (paraphrase; cf. tr. Nathmal Tatia) [8]
Anekantavada warns that judgements formed from one angle grasp only part of a many-sided reality.
The townsfolk judge the marriage from the single standpoint of waking life; the Kural’s heroine knows a second standpoint that changes the verdict.
Sikhism
“The miracle of a dream is known to the one who has seen it. No one else can see it. Then how can anyone else know about it?”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabit Savaiye, Kabit 305 (English translation as given on BaniDB) [9]
Bhai Gurdas uses the dream as an image for the Sikh's private meeting with the Guru, which outsiders cannot see or judge.
The Kural's wife answers the town's gossip that her husband has abandoned her by asking whether they cannot see him coming to her in dreams; Bhai Gurdas agrees that what happens in a dream is known only to the one who sees it.
Greek Mythology
Laodamia, whose husband Protesilaus fell at Troy, keeps a likeness of him in her chamber and embraces it; a servant who glimpses this reports that she has a lover, and her father, misunderstanding, has the image burned.
— Hyginus, Fabulae 104 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth shows outsiders misreading a wife’s private communion with her absent husband.
Like Laodamia’s household, the Kural’s townsfolk see only the outside and misjudge a love whose real life is hidden from them.
Philosophy
Heraclitus is reported as saying that the waking share one common world, whereas each sleeper turns aside into a private world of his own.
— Heraclitus, fragment B89 Diels-Kranz, reported by Plutarch (paraphrase) [11]
The fragment marks the dream as a world others cannot enter or verify.
The Kural’s townsfolk live only in the common waking world; the heroine’s husband visits her in the private world Heraclitus describes, which they cannot see.

Real Life Example

Lady Jane Franklin refuses to give up on her husband
1848–1859 · London, England, and the Canadian Arctic
Sir John Franklin's Arctic expedition, which sailed in 1845, was never heard from again. His wife Jane supported the searches from 1848, offered rewards and paid for ships of her own; after the government ended its searches, she bought and equipped the yacht Fox under Leopold McClintock. In 1859 the Fox expedition found a written record near Point Victory on King William Island confirming that the ships had been abandoned and Franklin had died.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
While official opinion gave the expedition up, Jane Franklin kept faith with her husband's memory and finally brought home the truth of his fate.
The kural's heroine says the townsfolk claim her husband has abandoned her, but they do not see him with her in her dreams; Jane Franklin likewise held to her own conviction about her husband while the authorities gave him up.
