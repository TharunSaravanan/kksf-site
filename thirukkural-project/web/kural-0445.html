Kural 445

Seeking the Aid of Great Men · Periyaaraith Thunaikkodal · பெரியாரைத் துணைக்கோடல்

சூழ்வார்கண் ணாக ஒழுகலான் மன்னவன் சூழ்வாரைச் சூழ்ந்து கொளல்.
Soozhvaarkan naaka ozhukalaan mannavan Soozhvaaraich soozhndhu kolal.

Meaning

A king must use his advisers as his eyes, so he should choose them only after thinking very carefully.

Elementary

A king can’t see everything himself, so his advisers are like his eyes. If your eyes are bad, you can’t see clearly. So this kural says a leader must think very carefully before choosing advisers.

University

The couplet plays on the root ‘soozh’ (to deliberate, plan): ‘soozhvaar’ are those who deliberate, and the king must ‘soozhndhu’ (deliberate) in choosing them. The wordplay enacts the argument — deliberation is needed to choose the deliberators. ‘Kan aaka’ (as eyes) is the governing metaphor: a king perceives his realm largely through ministers’ reports and judgments, so their quality determines the accuracy of his vision. The later chapter on ministers (Amaichu, ch. 64) builds on this principle.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Sovereignty (rájatva) is possible only with assistance. A single wheel can never move. Hence he shall employ ministers and hear their opinion.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.7 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [4]
Kautilya holds that no ruler can govern alone; he must appoint ministers and listen to them.
Same premise: the ruler depends on counselors and must choose and heed them.
Christianity
“Where no counsel is, the people fall: but in the multitude of counsellors there is safety.”
— Proverbs 11:14 (KJV) [5]
A people whose leaders lack good counsel collapse; many sound advisers bring security.
Affirms the ruler’s dependence on counselors that the Kural expresses.
Islam
“… and whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves.”
— Quran 42:38 (Sahih International); cf. 3:159 [6]
The Quran praises believers who settle their affairs by mutual consultation (shura); elsewhere (3:159) it tells the Prophet himself to consult his companions.
Consultation as a pillar of rule parallels the Kural’s ruler who sees through his counselors.
Judaism
Jethro sees Moses judging the people alone from morning to evening and counsels him to choose able men, God-fearing, truthful and hating bribes, as judges over the people; Moses heeds him.
— Exodus 18:13–26 [5]
Moses accepts that a leader cannot see and judge everything himself and must select capable, upright helpers.
Both the need for counselors and the careful criteria for choosing them echo the Kural.
Buddhism
Among the conditions of welfare the Buddha gives for the Vajjian republic: they meet often in assembly, and they honor, respect, and revere their elders and deem them worthy of being listened to.
— Mahaparinibbana Sutta, DN 16 [7]
A polity remains strong as long as it deliberates together and heeds its elders.
Governance through heeded counsel, as in the Kural.
Jainism
“So a novice who has not mastered the Law, does not know the Law, not being awakened; but afterwards he knows it well through the words of the Ginas, as with his eye (the wanderer sees the way) after sunrise.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.14.12–13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga says the untrained novice is like a guide in the dark night, and that the teaching of the Jinas becomes his eye, letting him see the road as if the sun had risen.
Valluvar says a king sees through his counsellors as his eyes and so must choose them with care. The Jain text likewise treats wise instruction as the eye by which one who cannot see finds the way.
Sikhism
“The Guru has applied the true ointment of spiritual wisdom to my eyes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 124, Raag Maajh, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das describes the Guru as the one who gives true sight, anointing the eyes with spiritual wisdom (gian anjan).
The Kural says a king sees through his counsellors as his eyes and must choose them carefully; in Gurbani the Guru is the one who gives the eyes true vision.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad 10, Diomedes, asked to pick a companion for a night raid, chooses Odysseus, observing that when two go together, one sees before the other how profit may be gained.
— Homer, Iliad 10.222–247 [10]
Diomedes deliberately chooses the shrewdest partner as a second pair of eyes.
Almost literally the Kural’s counselor-as-eyes chosen with care.
Philosophy
Machiavelli writes that the first opinion one forms of a prince and of his understanding comes from observing the men he has around him; if they are capable and faithful, he may be reckoned wise.
— Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 22 (tr. W. K. Marriott) [11]
A ruler’s own wisdom is judged by the quality of the ministers he chooses.
Shares the Kural’s view that selecting counselors is itself the test of a ruler.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth I chooses William Cecil as her chief minister
November 1558 · England, United Kingdom
On her accession to the English throne in November 1558, Elizabeth I appointed William Cecil, an experienced administrator, as her principal secretary of state. Cecil served her for about 40 years until his death in 1598, first as secretary and from 1572 as Lord Treasurer, having been made Baron Burghley in 1571. He handled almost all of the queen's formal correspondence and was her most trusted adviser through most of her reign.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Elizabeth's first and most lasting decision as queen was the choice of the minister through whose reports and judgement she would see her kingdom.
The kural says a king must treat his counsellors as his eyes and therefore choose them with great care, and Elizabeth's careful choice of Cecil served her for four decades.
