Kural 841

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

அறிவின்மை இன்மையுள் இன்மை பிறிதின்மை இன்மையா வையா துலகு.
Arivinmai inmaiyul inmai piridhinmai inmaiyaa vaiyaa thulagu.

Meaning

Of all the things a person can lack, lacking good sense is the real poverty. The world does not count lacking other things, like money, as real poverty.

Elementary

Imagine two kids: one has an empty piggy bank but knows how to read, count and solve problems; the other has a full piggy bank but can’t figure anything out. The first one can earn coins again, but the second one will keep losing them. That is why the poet says not knowing is the emptiest pocket of all.

University

The chapter opens with a paradox built on the repeated word inmai (இன்மை, lack, poverty): arivinmai (அறிவின்மை, absence of understanding) is ‘the poverty among poverties’, while piridhinmai (other lacks, chiefly of wealth) the world will not reckon as poverty at all. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar render it as ‘the famine of knowledge is the cruelest famine’, and Solomon Pappaiah shifts the evaluator from ‘the world’ (ulagu) to ‘the great ones’ (periyor), reflecting the classical commentary habit of reading ulagu as ‘the wise of the world’. Puliyur Kesigan adds that material poverty is not regarded as lasting poverty, since it can be remedied, whereas folly cannot. Placed in the Natpiyal after Pedhaimai (ch. 84), Pullarivaanmai shifts the focus from simple folly to the conceited, perverse ignorance (pull-arivu, ‘petty knowledge’) of one who thinks himself wise. Text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the second line is printed in its traditional sandhi-split form ‘inmaiyaa vaiyaa thulagu’ (for vaiyaadhu ulagu).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“In this world, there is nothing so sublime and pure as transcendental knowledge.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.38 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna ranks knowledge above every other purifying possession or practice.
If knowledge is the greatest possession, its absence is the greatest poverty, exactly Valluvar’s claim.
Christianity
“How much better is it to get wisdom than gold! and to get understanding rather to be chosen than silver!”
— Proverbs 16:16 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs weighs wisdom against money and finds money the lesser treasure.
Lacking gold is a small want; lacking wisdom is the real one.
Islam
“Say, "Are those who know equal to those who do not know?" Only they will remember [who are] people of understanding.”
— Qur’an 39:9 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an poses a rhetorical question whose answer is plainly no: knowledge separates people more than anything else.
Both texts make the lack of knowledge the decisive deficiency in a person.
Judaism
“If he acquired this, what else is lacking? If he has not acquired this, what has he acquired?”
— Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 41a (William Davidson/Koren translation) [6]
In the same passage Abaye cites a tradition that a poor person is only one lacking in intelligence, and the saying from the West follows: whoever has understanding has everything.
Almost Valluvar’s exact thought: the only true poverty is poverty of understanding.
Buddhism
“A worse taint than these is ignorance, the worst of all taints.”
— Dhammapada 243 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
After listing many stains on a person’s life, the Buddha names ignorance as the deepest of them.
Both rank ignorance as the worst among a whole list of deficiencies.
Jainism
“All men who are ignorant of the Truth are subject to pain; in the endless Samsâra they suffer in many ways.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on the false ascetic opens by making ignorance of the truth the root of every kind of suffering across endless rebirth, and goes on to urge the wise to search for the truth themselves.
Valluvar calls lack of understanding the real poverty among all poverties, and Mahavira's sermon likewise treats ignorance, not any lack of goods, as the want that causes all other suffering.
Sikhism
“No one respects the poor man. … Says Kabir, he alone is poor, who does not have the Naam, the Name of the Lord, in his heart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1159, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir starts from the world's contempt for the poor and turns it round: the only real poverty is lacking the Name in one's heart.
The Kural says the lack of understanding is the real poverty and the world should not count other lacks as want; Kabir likewise moves true poverty from money to the inner life.
Greek Mythology
King Midas, already rich, asks Bacchus that everything he touches turn to gold; his food and drink harden into metal and he begs to be freed from the gift, and later he earns ass’s ears for his poor judgment in the contest between Pan and Apollo.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11 [10]
Midas has endless wealth but no sense, and the wealth does him no good.
Gold could not cure his real poverty, which was a poverty of judgment.
Philosophy
Diogenes Laertius reports Socrates as saying that there is only one good, knowledge, and only one evil, ignorance.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 2.31 [11]
Socrates reduces all good and evil to the presence or absence of knowledge.
Valluvar likewise treats the absence of knowledge as the only lack that truly matters.

Real Life Example

Paulo Freire's forty-hour literacy programme at Angicos
1963 · Angicos, Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil
In 1963 the educator Paulo Freire led a government-funded pilot programme in the small town of Angicos in north-eastern Brazil, in which nearly 300 poor rural workers and adolescents were taught to read and write in about forty hours of instruction. Teachers built lessons around words and images drawn from the learners' own lives, aiming at political awareness as well as literacy. The pilot was meant to lead to a national literacy programme, but that plan was halted after the military coup of 1964 and Freire left Brazil.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Angicos became the founding example of Freire's view that illiteracy, more than lack of money, kept the poor powerless, and his methods were later used in literacy campaigns around the world.
The kural says the lack of understanding is the real poverty, and Freire's programme treated the workers' want of literacy, rather than their want of wealth, as the deprivation to be overcome first.
