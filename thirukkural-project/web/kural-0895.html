Kural 895

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

யாண்டுச்சென்று யாண்டும் உளராகார் வெந்துப்பின் வேந்து செறப்பட் டவர்.
Yaantuchchendru yaantum ularaakaar vendhuppin vendhu serappat tavar.

Meaning

People who make a fierce and powerful king angry will not survive, wherever they run to.

Elementary

Playing hide-and-seek with someone who can see everywhere is no fun, because there is nowhere to hide. If you make a very powerful ruler angry, no hiding place is safe.

University

Here the great one is a vendhu (வேந்து, king) of vem-thuppu (வெந்துப்பு, fierce strength); those seRappaTTavar (செறப்பட்டவர், who have incurred his wrath, from seRu, to be angry) will be uLar aakaar (உளர் ஆகார், will not exist, will not live) yaaNDu senRu yaaNDum (wherever they go, anywhere). Solomon Pappaiah and Mu. Varadarasanar agree that the offender cannot live anywhere; Sivayogi Sivakumar reads uLar aakaar more psychologically, as being unable to settle. Pope’s rendering notes they will not prosper wherever they go. The two sources agree on the text; thirukkural.net’s transliteration shows the metrical division வெந்துப்பின் / வேந்து, with the split செறப்பட் டவர் in both.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Sita recalls how a crow (Kakasura) that wounded her was pursued by a grass blade Rama charged as a missile; the crow fled through the worlds, but no god would shelter it, until it returned to Rama himself for refuge.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (the crow episode recounted by Sita) [3]
No place in any world offered protection from the wrath of the one it had offended.
This is the Kural’s point exactly: wherever the offender flees, there is no refuge from a mighty ruler’s anger.
Christianity
“And the kings of the earth, and the great men, and the rich men, and the chief captains, and the mighty men, and every bondman, and every free man, hid themselves in the dens and in the rocks of the mountains; And said to the mountains and rocks, Fall on us, and hide us from the face of him that sitteth on the throne, and from the wrath of the Lamb.”
— Revelation 6:15-16 (KJV) [4]
John’s vision shows all ranks of people trying in vain to hide from divine wrath.
As in the Kural, there is no hiding place from the anger of the supreme ruler.
Islam
“Man will say on that Day, ‘Where is the [place of] escape?’ No! There is no refuge.”
— Qur’an 75:10-11 (Saheeh International) [5]
On the Day of Judgment people search for an escape and find none.
The Kural likewise says the one who angers the mighty finds no place to survive.
Judaism
Jonah tries to flee from God’s command by sailing to Tarshish, but a storm and a great fish bring him back.
— Book of Jonah 1-2 [4]
The prophet learns that there is no running away from the Sovereign he tried to evade.
Like the Kural’s offender, Jonah found that flight to distant places does not escape a mighty ruler.
Buddhism
“Neither in the sky nor in mid-ocean, nor by entering into mountain clefts, nowhere in the world is there a place where one may escape from the results of evil deeds.”
— Dhammapada 127 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that no place in the world shelters one from the fruit of one’s evil acts.
The Kural applies the same no-escape logic to the wrath of a mighty king.
Jainism
“Like as the golden-coloured serpent trembles, though in Patala, if he hear the sound of the fierce anger of the thunder in the heavens, so enemies, though they have shut themselves up in a fort difficult of access, will not be able to escape when the great are angry.”
— Nāladiyār 17.4 (English translation serialised in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In the Nāladiyār's chapter against reproaching the great, a serpent deep in the underworld still trembles at thunder, and so enemies cannot hide even in a strong fort once the great are angry.
The Kural says those who incur a mighty king's anger will not survive wherever they flee, and the quatrain makes the same point that no refuge holds when the great are roused.
Sikhism
“The slanderer of the Saint has no place of rest.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 280, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan says that one who sets himself against the holy finds no refuge anywhere.
The Kural says those who incur a mighty king's anger will not survive wherever they flee, and Gurbani describes the same homelessness for the enemy of the Saint.
Greek Mythology
After killing his mother, Orestes is hounded from land to land by the Furies and finds no rest until he reaches Athens.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides [9]
The avenging powers pursue him wherever he goes.
This echoes the Kural’s image of one who can find no place to live after angering a great power.
Philosophy
Boethius, once a high official, fell foul of King Theodoric, was imprisoned and condemned, and wrote The Consolation of Philosophy while awaiting execution.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book I (author’s circumstances) [10]
Even a man of great standing could not escape once he had lost the favour of a powerful king.
His fate illustrates the Kural’s warning about incurring a mighty ruler’s anger, though Boethius protested his innocence.

Real Life Example

Rome pursues Hannibal into exile
c. 195-183 BCE · Carthage, Ephesus and Bithynia (present-day Tunisia and Turkey)
After his defeat at Zama, Hannibal was denounced to Rome by rivals in Carthage and fled in 195 BCE to the court of Antiochus III. When Rome defeated Antiochus, one of its peace terms was that Hannibal be surrendered, and he fled again, eventually taking refuge with King Prusias of Bithynia. When the Romans demanded him there too, he took poison at Libyssa around 183 BCE rather than be handed over.
— Sources: [11] [12]
For more than a decade Rome's enmity followed Hannibal from one kingdom to the next until no refuge remained.
The kural says those who earn the wrath of a fiercely strong power will not survive wherever they flee, which matches Hannibal's last years.
