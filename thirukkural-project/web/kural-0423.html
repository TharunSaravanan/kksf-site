Kural 423

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

எப்பொருள் யார்யார்வாய்க் கேட்பினும் அப்பொருள் மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்ப தறிவு.
Epporul yaaryaarvaaik ketpinum apporul Meipporul kaanpa tharivu.

Meaning

Whatever you hear, and whoever you hear it from, wisdom is finding the real truth of it.

Elementary

Sometimes people believe something just because a famous or important person said it. Sometimes they ignore something true because someone they don't like said it. Valluvar says a wise person looks at what was said and asks, 'Is this actually true?', no matter who said it.

University

This is among the most frequently quoted Kurals, often cited as an early statement of critical thinking. The repetition யார்யார் ('whoever, whoever') stresses that the speaker's status, reputation, or hostility is irrelevant. The contrast between பொருள் (porul, the thing said or its surface content) and மெய்ப்பொருள் (meipporul, its true substance) separates what is asserted from what is real. Two errors are ruled out. One is deference to authority, accepting a claim because of who said it. The other is the ad hominem, rejecting it for the same reason. The Kural is less radical than modern empiricism, because it assumes the truth can be discerned through judgment. Still, its location of arivu in the evaluation of claims, not in the collecting of them, anticipates the Buddhist Kalama Sutta and Aristotle's 'truth above friends'. For a king in Porutpal, this is also advice about handling the reports of ministers, spies, and flatterers.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Rig Veda prays, 'Let noble thoughts come to us from every side' (ā no bhadrāḥ kratavo yantu viśvataḥ).
— Rig Veda 1.89.1 [4]
This Vedic invocation is widely cited in India as a charter for openness: good insight is welcome whatever its source.
The Kural shares the Vedic openness to wisdom from any quarter, and adds the requirement that it be tested for real truth.
Christianity
“Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.”
— 1 Thessalonians 5:21 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells the Thessalonians not to reject teachings outright or accept them uncritically, but to test everything and keep what holds true.
This is the same method as the Kural: examine whatever you hear and hold onto its true substance.
Islam
“Who listen to speech and follow the best of it. Those are the ones Allah has guided, and those are people of understanding.”
— Qur'an 39:18 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an praises those who hear what is said, weigh it, and follow the best of it, and calls them ulu al-albab, 'people of understanding'.
Both texts define understanding as sifting what one hears to find what is truly best.
Judaism
Ben Zoma asks, 'Who is wise?' and answers, 'One who learns from every person.'
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 [7]
The Mishnah defines the wise person as someone willing to receive truth from anyone, whatever their standing.
This is almost a direct echo of the Kural's 'from whomever you hear it'.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells the Kalamas not to accept teachings merely because of report, tradition, scripture, logic, or the speaker's seeming competence, or because 'this contemplative is our teacher'. They should accept a teaching only when they know for themselves that it is skillful and leads to welfare.
— Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 [8]
The Kalama Sutta is the classic Buddhist charter for evaluating claims on their merits rather than on the authority of the speaker.
It makes the same point as the Kural: a claim's truth is not settled by who says it; wisdom sees its real substance.
Jainism
“Wisdom recognises the truth of the Law and the ascertainment of true things.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The followers of Parshva and of Mahavira were troubled because the two teachers' rules differed (four vows against five, clothing against none). Gautama's answer is that wisdom looks past the outward differences to what is actually true, and Kesi, satisfied, accepts it.
The Kural asks the wise to find the real substance (meipporul) of whatever is said by whoever says it; Gautama likewise tells the disputing disciples to judge by the truth of the matter, not by which master's label a rule carries.
Sikhism
“From the True Guru, I have obtained a discriminating intellect; the Guru has revealed the spiritual wisdom of God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 711, Raag Todee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Raag Todee Guru Ram Das calls the discriminating intellect (bibek budh), the power to tell true from false and good from bad, a gift of the Guru.
The Kural defines wisdom as seeing the truth of whatever is said, whoever says it; Guru Ram Das prizes the same discernment as bibek budh.
Greek Mythology
Cassandra, cursed by Apollo so that no one would believe her, foretells the fall of Troy and Agamemnon's murder. Her hearers dismiss her true words because of who she is.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1072-1330 [11]
The Trojans and the Argive chorus judge the prophecies by the speaker (a 'mad' captive) and not by their content, and ruin follows.
This is the failure the Kural warns against: failing to see the truth of a claim because of the person saying it.
Philosophy
“For, while both are dear, piety requires us to honour truth above our friends.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.6, 1096a (tr. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle criticises the Platonic Form of the Good even though Plato was his teacher and friend. He insists that truth outranks loyalty to any person.
Both texts put the truth of a claim above the identity or status of the person who holds it.

Real Life Example

Ibn al-Haytham tests the authorities on light and vision
early 11th century · Cairo, Fatimid Egypt
Working in Cairo in the early eleventh century, the scholar Ibn al-Haytham wrote the Book of Optics, which used experiment and mathematics to show that vision results from light entering the eye, rejecting the view of Euclid and Ptolemy that the eye sends out rays. He also wrote Doubts Concerning Ptolemy, a critique of inconsistencies in the astronomy and optics of the most respected scientific authority of his age. Translated into Latin, his Optics influenced European scholars for centuries, and UNESCO marked its thousandth anniversary during the International Year of Light in 2015.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Ibn al-Haytham is celebrated as a pioneer of experimental method because he insisted on testing claims against evidence, whatever the fame of their author.
The kural says wisdom is seeing the true substance of whatever is heard, whoever says it, and Ibn al-Haytham refused to accept even Ptolemy's teaching without checking whether it was true.
