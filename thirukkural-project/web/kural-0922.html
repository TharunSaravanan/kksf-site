Kural 922

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

உண்ணற்க கள்ளை உணில்உண்க சான்றோரான் எண்ணப் படவேண்டா தார்.
Unnarka kallai unilunka saandroraan ennap padavendaa thaar.

Meaning

Do not drink alcohol. If anyone wants to drink, let it be only those who do not care whether good and wise people respect them.

Elementary

Suppose a teacher says, ‘Don't cheat on the test. Well, if you really want to cheat, go ahead, but only if you don't care whether anyone ever trusts you again.’ The teacher isn't giving permission. She is showing how much it costs. The Kural speaks about drinking the same way.

University

The first clause is a flat prohibition, unnarka kallai (உண்ணற்க கள்ளை, 'drink not toddy'). The second, unil unka (உணில் உண்க, 'if you would drink, drink'), is an ironic concession, a figure the commentators call igazhchchi (இகழ்ச்சி, scorn). It limits the 'permission' to those who are saandroraan ennappada vendaathaar (சான்றோரான் எண்ணப்பட வேண்டாதார்), people who do not wish to be esteemed by the saandror (சான்றோர், the noble and learned). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all keep this ironic reading. Puliyur Kesikan adds that the first clause is addressed to arivudaiyor (the wise), since toddy clouds the mind. The structure foreshadows Kural 923, where the saandror again sit in judgment. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The wretched Brahmana who from this day, unable to resist the temptation, will drink wine shall be regarded as having lost his virtue, shall be reckoned to have committed the sin of slaying a Brahmana, shall be hated both in this and the other worlds.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section 76 (K. M. Ganguli trans.) [3]
After unknowingly drinking his disciple Kacha's ashes mixed in his wine, the sage Shukra bans drink for Brahmanas.
Shukra's decree states the Kural's price outright: whoever drinks gives up the esteem of the virtuous.
Christianity
“And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit;”
— Ephesians 5:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to refuse drunkenness and seek a better kind of fullness.
Like the Kural's opening clause, it is a direct command not to give oneself to drink.
Islam
“If a large amount of anything causes intoxication, a small amount of it is prohibited.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 3681 [5]
The Prophet closes the loophole of drinking ‘only a little’.
The Kural's mock permission and this hadith both refuse any respectable middle ground for drinking.
Judaism
“Drink no wine or other intoxicant, you or your sons, when you enter the Tent of Meeting, that you may not die. This is a law for all time throughout the ages, for you must distinguish between the sacred and the profane, and between the impure and the pure;”
— Leviticus 10:9-10 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Priests must be sober to keep the clear judgment their sacred role requires.
Those who hope to be counted among the worthy must abstain, which is the Kural's implied ideal.
Buddhism
“One who destroys life, utters lies, takes what is not given, goes to another man's wife, and is addicted to intoxicating drinks — such a man digs up his own root even in this world.”
— Dhammapada 246-247 (Buddharakkhita trans.) [7]
The Dhammapada puts addiction to drink alongside the gravest moral failings.
Choosing drink means choosing to stand outside the company of the good, just as the Kural implies.
Jainism
“The Most Excellent saints call abstinence from the consumption of wine (madya), flesh (māñsa) and honey (madhu), together with the observance of five small vows (aṇuvrata), as the eight fundamental virtues (mūlaguṇa) of the householder.”
— Samantabhadra, Ratnakaraṇḍaka-śrāvakācāra v. 66 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018 per wisdomlib.org) [8]
Samantabhadra counts giving up wine among the eight root virtues (mūlaguṇa) that every Jain householder must keep, alongside the five small vows.
Valluvar's flat command 'drink not toddy' has a direct Jain counterpart in the householder's basic vows, where abstaining from wine is a foundation of a respectable life and not an optional refinement.
Sikhism
“Do not drink the false wine at all, if it is in your power.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 554, Raag Bihaagraa, Bihaagre ki Vaar, Salok, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
After describing how wine drives out sense and makes a man forget his Lord, Guru Amar Das gives a flat command not to drink it at all. He then says that, by the True Guru’s grace, one receives the true wine of the Lord’s love instead.
The Kural’s opening command, drink not, matches Guru Amar Das’s, though the Guru gives no ironic permission to those who do not care about the good.
Greek Mythology
The Scythian sage Anacharsis is reported to have said that the vine bears three clusters: the first of pleasure, the second of drunkenness, the third of disgust.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 1.103 (Hicks trans.) [10]
Anacharsis traces how drinking moves from pleasure to drunkenness to disgust.
Like the Kural, he points to where drinking ends: in the contempt of others.
Philosophy
Plato's Socrates says the guardians of the ideal city must avoid drunkenness above all, since a guardian is the last person who should be drunk and not know where he is.
— Plato, Republic III (c. 403e) [11]
Those trusted with the city must keep sober judgment.
Plato, like the Kural, makes sobriety the condition for being counted among the worthy.

Real Life Example

Babur publicly renounces wine before the Battle of Khanwa
February 1527 (battle on 16-17 March 1527) · Near Sikri and Khanwa, northern India
As the Rajput confederacy under Rana Sanga of Mewar advanced against him, and his men were losing heart after early setbacks, the Mughal founder Babur publicly forswore wine, which had been one of the great pleasures of his life. About 300 of his nobles and household joined him in the vow, and he also declared the coming fight a holy war, with his men swearing on the Quran not to turn back. Babur then defeated Rana Sanga's much larger army at Khanwa in March 1527, a victory that secured Mughal rule in northern India.
— Sources: [12] [13]
At the moment when he most needed the trust and respect of his followers, Babur chose to give up drink openly, and the gesture was part of what restored his army's morale.
The kural bids people not to drink, leaving liquor only to those who do not care for the esteem of the worthy, and Babur gave up wine precisely when he needed the respect of the men who would follow him into battle.
