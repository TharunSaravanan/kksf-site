Kural 694

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

செவிச்சொல்லும் சேர்ந்த நகையும் அவித்தொழுகல் ஆன்ற பெரியா ரகத்து.
Sevichchollum serndha nakaiyum aviththozhukal Aandra periyaar akaththu.

Meaning

When you are with great and powerful people, do not whisper in someone's ear or share a secret smile with anyone.

Elementary

Imagine you are speaking to the class and two children in the front row start whispering and giggling. You would wonder whether they were laughing at you. That is why, around important people, you should not whisper or trade secret smiles.

University

Sevichchol is literally ‘ear-speech’, words whispered into another person’s ear, and serndha nakai is ‘joined laughter’, a smile or glance shared with someone else. Solomon Pappaiah glosses it as winking and smiling while looking at another’s face. The verb avi (to extinguish or suppress) asks the courtier to put out these impulses altogether. Aandra periyaar are ‘the great ones who are complete in power’, which Parimelazhagar takes as kings. The rule rests on the suspicion theme of the previous couplet: private signals between courtiers look to a ruler like conspiracy or mockery. Kautilya’s Arthashastra 5.4 lists almost the same prohibitions, secret talk with another in the assembly and loud laughter without cause. The older text reads “செவிச்சொல்லுஞ் சேர்ந்த நகையு மவித்தொழுக / லான்ற பெரியா ரகத்து.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“In the presence of even laughable objects, a man should not break out into loud laughter, like a maniac; nor should one show (unreasonable) gravity by containing himself, to the utmost. One should smile modestly, to show his interest.”
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva, Section 4 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Dhaumya teaches the Pandavas to control their laughter at court: neither laughing wildly nor standing stiff, but smiling modestly.
Both texts treat uncontrolled laughter in the ruler’s presence as a breach of the courtier’s discipline.
Christianity
“Being filled with all unrighteousness, fornication, wickedness, covetousness, maliciousness; full of envy, murder, debate, deceit, malignity; whisperers,”
— Romans 1:29 (KJV) [4]
Paul lists ‘whisperers’, people who spread talk behind others’ backs, among the vices of a corrupt society.
The Kural bans ear-whispering before great persons, and Paul counts whispering itself as a vice.
Islam
“O you who have believed, when you converse privately, do not converse about sin and aggression and disobedience to the Messenger but converse about righteousness and piety.”
— Quran 58:9 (Saheeh International) [5]
Surah al-Mujadila addresses the private conversations (najwa) that some people held in the Prophet’s community, which hurt others and bred suspicion.
Like the Kural, the Quran treats private whispering in the leader’s circle as a source of harm and mistrust.
Judaism
“Curse not the king, no not in thy thought; and curse not the rich in thy bedchamber: for a bird of the air shall carry the voice, and that which hath wings shall tell the matter.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:20 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet warns that even private talk about the king has a way of reaching him.
The Kural forbids whispers in the king’s presence because whispers always look suspicious and seldom stay secret.
Buddhism
“Let him not laugh too much, nor keep always silent; he should utter, when the due season comes, a concise and measured speech.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 163 [6]
Vidhura teaches that a courtier should keep his laughter and speech measured.
Both texts ask the servant to rein in laughter and loose talk before the king.
Jainism
“One should always be meek, and not be talkative in the presence of the wise; one should acquire valuable knowledge, and avoid what is worthless.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The sutra asks for meek, restrained behaviour and little talk in the presence of superiors; Uttaradhyayana 11.4 also lists 'not to be fond of mirth' as part of discipline.
The Kural forbids whispering and shared laughter in the presence of the great; the Jain rule asks for quiet, restrained conduct before one's superiors.
Sikhism
“It is not good to slander anyone, but the foolish, self-willed manmukhs still do it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 755, Raag Soohee, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das condemns slander and talk behind others' backs as the habit of the self-willed (manmukh).
The Kural forbids whispering in another's ear or sharing private smiles in the presence of the great; Gurbani's ban on backbiting covers the moral side of such whispering, though not its court etiquette.
Greek Mythology
King Midas’s barber discovers that Apollo has given the king ass’s ears. Unable to keep the secret, he whispers it into a hole in the ground, but the reeds that grow there murmur it to everyone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.172-193 (Midas’s barber) [9]
Ovid’s story shows that whispered words about a king do not stay hidden.
The Kural forbids ear-whispering at court. Midas’s barber shows why: whispers about the powerful always travel.
Philosophy
“He shall also avoid talking to another in secret, mutual conversation with another in the assembly (of ministers) ... nor loud laughter with no cause for jest.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 5.4, The Conduct of a Courtier (trans. R. Shamasastry) [10]
Kautilya lists behaviours a courtier must avoid in the king’s assembly, including whispered side-conversations and laughter without cause.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s pair: whispering to another and laughing with another before the king.

Real Life Example

Lord Chesterfield warns his son against audible laughter in polite company
9 March 1748 · Bath, England, United Kingdom
Philip Stanhope, 4th Earl of Chesterfield, a British statesman and diplomat who served as secretary of state from 1746 to 1748, wrote a long series of letters of advice on manners and worldly success to his son Philip. In a letter dated Bath, 9 March 1748, he warned his son that he hoped he would often be seen to smile but never heard to laugh, calling audible laughter ill-bred and a mark of folly. The letters, not written for publication, later appeared as Letters to His Son and became famous as guides to the art of pleasing.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A seasoned courtier passed on the rule that in polished society the face may show pleasure but must not break into the private, noisy mirth that offends others.
Like the kural, which tells the courtier to suppress shared smiles and whispers before the great, Chesterfield counselled restraint of laughter in the company one must please.
