Kural 946

Medicine · Marundhu · மருந்து

இழிவறிந்து உண்பான்கண் இன்பம்போல் நிற்கும் கழிபேர் இரையான்கண் நோய்.
Izhivarindhu unbaankan inbampol nirkum kazhiper iraiyaankan noi.

Meaning

Good health stays with a person who knows to eat a little less. In the same way, sickness stays with a person who eats far too much.

Elementary

Two friends go to a party. One eats until he is nicely full and goes home happy. The other stuffs himself and spends the night with a stomach ache. Valluvar says that is how it goes every time.

University

The couplet is a balanced antithesis. Izhivu (இழிவு) here means 'lessness': Varadarasanar glosses izhivarindhu unbaan as one who knows what the lesser measure is, and Kesikan as one who eats a little below the full measure; Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it more morally as one who knows that taking more than needed is base, exploiting the word's other sense of 'meanness'. Inbam (இன்பம்) is well-being or pleasure, which nirkum (நிற்கும், 'stays, abides'). Kazhiper iraiyaan (கழிபேர் இரையான்) is a striking phrase: irai (இரை) is the word for an animal's prey or fodder, so the glutton is pictured as a beast gulping feed, and kazhiper (கழிபேர்) intensifies it to 'exceedingly great'. Disease abides with him as surely as health with the moderate eater. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There is no possibility of one's becoming a yogī, O Arjuna, if one eats too much, or eats too little, sleeps too much or does not sleep enough.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.16 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, As It Is, 1972) [3]
Krishna makes moderation in eating a precondition of the disciplined, well-ordered life.
Like the Kural, the Gita treats the over-eater as one who has forfeited the good state that the moderate keep.
Christianity
“Sound sleep cometh of moderate eating: he riseth early, and his wits are with him: but the pain of watching, and choler, and pangs of the belly, are with an unsatiable man.”
— Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 31:20 (KJV Apocrypha) [4]
Ben Sira sets the rested, clear-headed moderate eater against the sleepless, bilious, cramped glutton.
It is the same two-sided picture as the Kural: well-being stays with the moderate and suffering with the insatiable.
Islam
“A believer eats in one intestine (is satisfied with a little food), and a kafir (unbeliever) eats in seven intestines (eats much food).”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5393 [5]
The Prophet contrasts the believer, content with little, with one who eats voraciously.
The Kural draws the same contrast between the one who eats little and the one who devours, measured by health rather than faith.
Judaism
“As long as one exercises, exerts himself greatly, does not eat to the point of satiation and has loose bowels, he will not suffer sickness and he will grow in strength.”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot De'ot 4:14 (tr. Eliyahu Touger) [6]
Maimonides promises freedom from sickness and growing strength to one who stops short of satiety.
Eating short of fullness and keeping well is the first half of the Kural's antithesis.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that Mara overthrows one who lives fixed on pleasant things, with senses unguarded and immoderate in eating, as wind topples a weak tree, but cannot shake one who is moderate in eating and restrained, as wind cannot shake a rocky mountain.
— Dhammapada 7-8, paraphrase [7]
The paired verses set the immoderate eater, easily overthrown, against the moderate one who stands firm.
The Kural's twin portrait of the moderate eater and the glutton has the same two-sided form.
Jainism
“Pleasant food should not be enjoyed with preference, for it generally makes men over-strong; and desires rush upon the strong, like birds upon a tree with sweet fruits.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana warns that rich eating breeds an excess that attracts the passions as fruit attracts birds.
Where the Kural sees disease settling on the glutton, the Jain text sees desires swarming on him.
Sikhism
“Fareed, my bread is made of wood, and hunger is my appetizer. Those who eat buttered bread, will suffer in terrible pain.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 28) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses the Gurus included in the Granth, contrasts his plain dry bread, made tasty only by hunger, with the rich food of those who indulge and later suffer for it.
The Kural sets the well-being of the moderate eater against the disease of the glutton; Farid sets plain food eaten in hunger against rich food that brings pain.
Greek Mythology
Homer introduces the beggar Irus as notorious for his insatiable belly, always eating and drinking, big to look at but with no strength or force in him; Odysseus fells him with a single blow.
— Homer, Odyssey 18.1-7 (tr. Samuel Butler), paraphrase [10]
The glutton's bulk hides weakness, and he is easily brought down.
Irus embodies the Kural's kazhiper iraiyaan, the over-eater with whom weakness abides.
Philosophy
Seneca observes that simple food once kept people healthy, and that the multiplying of rich dishes has multiplied diseases, so that one who wonders at the countless diseases should count the cooks.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 95.15-23 (tr. R. M. Gummere), paraphrase [11]
The Stoic ties the growth of disease directly to luxurious overeating.
Seneca's cooks and diseases are the Kural's glutton with whom illness stays.

Real Life Example

Kaibara Ekiken publishes the Yojokun
1713 · Fukuoka Domain, Chikuzen Province, Japan
Kaibara Ekiken, a scholar of the Fukuoka Domain who had also studied medicine, wrote the Yojokun (Lessons on Nourishing Life) at the age of 83, and it was published in 1713. The handbook advised balanced, moderate eating and stopping at about eight-tenths full, alongside exercise, sound sleep and a calm mind. Written in plain Japanese rather than classical Chinese, it became one of the most widely read health books in Japan and is still reprinted today; Kaibara himself lived to 86.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Yojokun spread the principle of stopping before one is full, still known in Japan as hara hachi bu, to generations of ordinary readers.
The kural says well-being stays with one who knows to eat a little less, which is the very rule of eating to eight-tenths that Kaibara taught.
