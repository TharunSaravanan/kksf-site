Kural 976

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

சிறியார் உணர்ச்சியுள் இல்லை பெரியாரைப் பேணிக்கொள் வேமென்னும் நோக்கு.
Siriyaar unarchchiyul illai periyaaraip penikkol vemennum nokku.

Meaning

Small-minded people never have the thought, 'Let us respect great people and follow their ways.'

Elementary

When a great teacher comes to school, some children run to listen and learn, hoping to become like her. Others just shrug and keep playing. Valluvar says wanting to learn from great people is itself a sign of a big heart.

University

Siriyaar (சிறியார், 'the small') is the counter-term to periyaar (பெரியார், 'the great') throughout this chapter and the next. The key phrase is peNikkoLvem (பேணிக்கொள்வேம், 'we will cherish and take to ourselves'): Puliyur Kesikan reads it as 'we will serve the great and attain their nature ourselves' (அவரியல்பை நாமும் அடைவோம்), Solomon Pappaiah as following them and preserving their tradition, and Mu. Varadarasanar as honouring them, which the small cannot do because they do not perceive the greatness at all. Nokku (நோக்கு, 'aim, outlook') and unarchchi (உணர்ச்சி, 'feeling, awareness') make the point psychological: the lack is in perception, not merely in will. Pope renders 'As votaries of the truly great we will ourselves enroll'. The text matches on both sites, with ytamizh omitting the final full stop.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized souls can impart knowledge unto you because they have seen the truth.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna urges Arjuna to approach the wise, serve them and learn from them.
This is the 'aim to cherish the great' that the Kural says small minds lack.
Christianity
“He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed.”
— Proverbs 13:20 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says that seeking the company of the wise makes one wise.
The Kural's great-hearted seek the great; the small do not, and stay small.
Islam
“Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you on [the condition] that you teach me from what you have been taught of sound judgement?’”
— Qur'an 18:66 (Saheeh International) [5]
Even the prophet Moses humbly asks to follow and learn from a wiser servant of God.
He shows the outlook the Kural finds lacking in small minds: the wish to follow the great and absorb their wisdom.
Judaism
“Let thy house be a house of meeting for the Sages and sit in the very dust of their feet, and drink in their words with thirst.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:4 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Yose ben Yoezer urges eager, humble attachment to the wise.
This thirst to cherish the great is exactly what the Kural says the small never feel.
Buddhism
“Though all his life a fool associates with a wise man, he no more comprehends the Truth than a spoon tastes the flavor of the soup.”
— Dhammapada 64 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says a fool can sit beside the wise for a lifetime and absorb nothing.
This is the Kural's point exactly: the small have no inner capacity to take on the nature of the great.
Jainism
The first lecture of the Uttaradhyayana, 'On Discipline' (vinaya), contrasts the well-behaved disciple, who stays near his teacher, carries out his instructions and understands his wishes, with the ill-behaved disciple who neglects the teacher and so gains nothing.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 1, 'On Discipline' (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain monastic teaching treats reverent attachment to the teacher as the gateway to knowledge.
The ill-behaved disciple is the Kural's small person who has no wish to cherish the great.
Sikhism
Macauliffe recounts that when Guru Nanak's end drew near he appointed his devoted disciple Angad (Bhai Lehna), who had served him wholeheartedly, as his successor, because the Guru's own sons had not obeyed him: their minds were insincere and they had rebelled and deserted him.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter XVII; corroborated by Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Angad [9]
In the Sikh account Lehna's readiness to serve the Guru and make the Guru's way his own is what made him Guru Angad, while the Guru's sons, though close to him by birth, never sought that service. Britannica likewise notes that Angad was known for his loyalty to the first Guru.
The Kural says the resolve to cherish the great and take on their way never arises in small minds, and the story sets Lehna, who had that resolve, against the sons, who lacked it.
Greek Mythology
Urged by Athena, the young Telemachus sails to Pylos and Sparta to seek out the aged Nestor and King Menelaus, learning from them about his father and about how great men act.
— Homer, Odyssey 3-4 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Telemachus' coming of age begins with going out to learn from the great.
He shows the outlook the Kural says the small lack: wanting to learn from great men and become like them.
Philosophy
In Plato's allegory of the cave, the prisoners who have only ever seen shadows mock the one who returns from the light and would even kill anyone who tried to lead them upward.
— Plato, Republic VII, 514a-517a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato portrays people who cannot recognise or welcome a mind greater than their own.
The prisoners are the Kural's small people, for whom honouring the great is simply not part of their outlook.

Real Life Example

James Boswell seeks out Samuel Johnson and becomes his biographer
16 May 1763 (Life of Johnson published 1791) · Thomas Davies's bookshop, Russell Street, Covent Garden, London, England
The young Scottish lawyer James Boswell, who had admired Samuel Johnson's writings for years, spent months in London trying to meet him and finally did so on 16 May 1763 in the back parlour of the bookseller Thomas Davies, where Johnson's sharp replies left him embarrassed. Undeterred, Boswell called on Johnson at his lodgings a week later and cultivated the friendship for twenty-one years, recording Johnson's conversation in remarkable detail in his journals. His Life of Samuel Johnson, published in 1791, is regarded as one of the greatest biographies in English.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Boswell's determination to learn from a great man and preserve his words gave the world its fullest portrait of Johnson.
The kural says that the resolve to cherish the great and make their way one's own never arises in small minds, and Boswell's persistent pursuit of Johnson is the very resolve the kural says the small lack.
