Kural 1249

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

உள்ளத்தார் காத லவரால் உள்ளிநீ யாருழைச் சேறியென் நெஞ்சு.
Ullaththaar kaadha lavaraal ulli-nee yaaruzhaich cheriyaen nenju.

Meaning

My heart, the one we love lives right here inside you. So when you think of him, where are you going to look for him?

Elementary

Imagine searching the whole house for your favourite toy while holding it in your hand the whole time. The Kural says the same to the heart: “The person you miss is already inside you, why go looking for them outside?”

University

The heroine catches her heart in a paradox: kātalavar uḷḷattār āl (‘the beloved is in the heart’, with āl as an emphatic particle), so when nī uḷḷi (‘you, thinking of him’) set off, yār uḻai cēṟi (‘to whom will you go?’; uḻai = ‘to the side of, to the place of’). Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read it as ‘to whom do you go searching, when he is within?’; Pope gives ‘Roaming, whom dost thou think to find?’. The line is a rare moment of consolation in the chapter, and has often been read devotionally, the beloved dwelling in the heart being a common image in Tamil bhakti. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (ytamizh prints a spaced kāta lavarāl).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Supreme Lord is situated in everyone’s heart, O Arjuna, and is directing the wanderings of all living entities, who are seated as on a machine, made of the material energy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.61 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna teaches that the One whom beings seek already dwells within their own hearts.
The Kural’s heroine realises the same of her beloved: he lives inside her heart, so there is nowhere outside to search.
Christianity
“Neither shall they say, Lo here! or, lo there! for, behold, the kingdom of God is within you.”
— Luke 17:21 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his hearers not to look for the kingdom here or there, for it is already within them.
The Kural similarly tells the heart not to go searching outside for the one who already lives inside it.
Islam
“And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein”
— Qur’an 50:16 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an says God is nearer to a person than their own life-vein, so there is no need to seek Him far away.
The Kural’s heroine discovers that her beloved, too, is nearer than anywhere her heart might wander to find him.
Judaism
“Set me as a seal upon thy heart, As a seal upon thine arm; For love is strong as death, Jealousy is cruel as the grave; The flashes thereof are flashes of fire, A very flame of the LORD.”
— Song of Songs 8:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved asks to be pressed into the heart like a seal, a permanent mark within.
The Kural’s beloved already is such a seal: he dwells in the heroine’s heart, so the heart need not seek him elsewhere.
Buddhism
“Dwelling in the cave (of the heart), the mind, without form, wanders far and alone. Those who subdue this mind are liberated from the bonds of Mara.”
— Dhammapada 37 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha describes the mind as dwelling in the heart yet constantly wandering far away.
The Kural’s heart behaves just so: its beloved lives inside it, yet it wanders off to search for him.
Jainism
Kundakunda teaches that the pure soul is to be found and known within oneself, by turning inward, and that seeking it among outward things is a delusion.
— Kundakunda, Samayasara (general teaching; paraphrase) [8]
Digambara mysticism insists that what one truly seeks is found within, not in external objects.
The Kural tells the heart a similar truth about its beloved, who already dwells within, though Jainism would point to the self rather than a lover.
Sikhism
“Fareed, why do you wander from jungle to jungle, crashing through the thorny trees? The Lord abides in the heart; why are you looking for Him in the jungle?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 19) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid tells the seeker that the Beloved already lives in the heart, so roaming outside to find Him is pointless.
The Kural's wife asks her heart where it is going to look for the beloved who already dwells inside it; Farid asks the very same question of the soul searching the jungles for a Lord who abides within.
Greek Mythology
Laodamia, whose husband Protesilaus was killed at Troy, kept a likeness of him in her chamber and devoted herself to it as if he were still present.
— Hyginus, Fabulae 104; cf. Ovid, Heroides 13 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth shows a wife keeping her absent husband present to herself through an image she holds close.
The Kural’s heroine keeps her husband present in her heart, which makes any outward search unnecessary.
Philosophy
“Too late did I love You, O Fairness, so ancient, and yet so new! Too late did I love You! For behold, You were within, and I without, and there did I seek You”
— Augustine, Confessions 10.27 (tr. J.G. Pilkington, NPNF) [11]
Augustine confesses that he searched outside himself for the One he loved, who was all the while within him.
The Kural’s heroine catches her heart making the same mistake: running outside to seek a beloved who lives within.

Real Life Example

Rumi's Divan-e Shams after Shams of Tabriz disappears
1240s · Konya, Anatolia (now Turkey)
In Konya in the 1240s the scholar and preacher Jalal al-Din Rumi met the wandering dervish Shams al-Din of Tabriz, and the two became close companions who were rarely apart. Shams later disappeared and was never seen again; Rumi turned to poetry, music and dance, and his great collection of lyric poems, the Divan-e Shams, is named after his lost companion and expresses his love and longing for him.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Rumi's grief for a vanished companion produced some of the most widely read poetry in the world.
The kural reminds the heart that the beloved lives within it, so there is no one outside to search for, and Rumi, having lost Shams, came to speak in his name in poems that carried Shams within them.
