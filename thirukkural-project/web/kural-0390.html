Kural 390

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

கொடையளி செங்கோல் குடியோம்பல் நான்கும் உடையானாம் வேந்தர்க் கொளி.
Kodaiyali sengol kudiyombal naangum Udaiyaanaam vendhark koli.

Meaning

A king who has these four things, generosity, kindness, fair rule and care for his people, is the light among kings.

Elementary

In a dark room, a lamp helps everyone see. This kural says the best king is like a lamp for all other kings, because he gives generously, is kind, rules fairly, and takes care of his people.

University

The chapter closes with a second set of four, this time outward-facing: kodai (gifts), ali (grace or compassion), sengol (the ‘straight sceptre’, i.e., just rule), and kudi ombal (nurturing the people). Where 382 described the king’s inner capacities, this kural describes their effects on subjects, and it crowns the one who has them as oli — the lamp or radiance — of kings, a model by which other rulers are measured. The sengol (straight sceptre) is a central symbol of Tamil political thought, contrasted with the kodungol (crooked sceptre) of the tyrant in later chapters. The image of the just ruler as light recurs in David’s last words in 2 Samuel, and the Hebrew Bible’s ‘sceptre of righteousness’ offers a remarkably close verbal parallel.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa says King Dilipa was the true father of his subjects, because he educated, protected, and sustained them, while their own fathers were merely the cause of their birth.
— Kalidasa, Raghuvamsha 1.24 [3]
A Sanskrit poem praises a king who cared for his people so completely that he was more of a father to them than their real fathers.
Embodies kudi ombal (nurturing the people) and ali (compassion), the heart of the Kural’s ‘light of kings’.
Christianity
“But unto the Son he saith, Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever: a sceptre of righteousness is the sceptre of thy kingdom.”
— Hebrews 1:8 (KJV) [4]
The Letter to the Hebrews describes Christ’s kingdom as ruled by a ‘sceptre of righteousness’.
‘Sceptre of righteousness’ is almost a word-for-word equivalent of the Kural’s sengol (the straight sceptre).
Islam
“The Imam (ruler) is the guardian of his subjects and is responsible for them.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 893 [5]
The Prophet taught that a ruler is like a shepherd who must answer for how he cared for his people.
Matches kudi ombal — the ruler’s duty to nurture and protect the people.
Judaism
“He that ruleth over men must be just, ruling in the fear of God. And he shall be as the light of the morning, when the sun riseth, even a morning without clouds;”
— 2 Samuel 23:3-4 (KJV) [4]
In his last words, King David says a just ruler is like the light of a clear sunrise.
Just rule producing radiance — the same image as the Kural’s ‘light of kings’.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta, the wheel-turning monarch is told to rely on the Dhamma, provide protection for all his people, allow no crime in his realm, and give wealth to the poor; a later king’s failure to give to the needy sets off a spiral of theft and violence (DN 26).
— Digha Nikaya 26 (Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta) [6]
The Buddha tells of an ideal king who protects everyone and gives to the poor; when a later king stops giving, crime spreads and society collapses.
Combines generosity, just rule, and protection of the people — the Kural’s four qualities of the model king.
Jainism
Jain tradition remembers Rishabhanatha, the first Tirthankara, as the first king. Before he renounced, he ruled from Vinita (Ayodhya) and taught his people six means of livelihood: asi (arms for protection), masi (writing), krishi (agriculture), vidya (knowledge), vanijya (trade) and shilpa (crafts).
— Jain tradition on Rishabhanatha (Adipurana and Svetambara accounts), as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Rishabhanatha' [7]
In the Jain account, the ideal first ruler protects his people and also equips them to live: he gives them arts, livelihoods and order. Only after that does he give up the throne for the ascetic path.
Valluvar's 'light among kings' has generosity, compassion, a just sceptre and care for his people. Rishabha as first king is the Jain picture of exactly this kind of nurturing, protecting ruler.
Sikhism
“Now, the Merciful Lord has issued His Command. Let no one chase after and attack anyone else. Let all abide in peace, under this Benevolent Rule.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 74, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes halemi raj, a gentle and benevolent rule under the merciful Lord's command, in which no one harasses another and all live in peace.
The Kural calls the ruler who joins generosity, compassion, just rule and care for the people the light among kings; Guru Arjan's halemi raj is Sikhism's picture of such compassionate, peaceful rule.
Greek Mythology
“...like that of some blameless king, who upholds righteousness, as the monarch over a great and valiant nation: the earth yields its wheat and barley, the trees are loaded with fruit, the ewes bring forth lambs, and the sea abounds with fish by reason of his virtues, and his people do good deeds under him.”
— Homer, Odyssey 19.109-114 (tr. Samuel Butler) [9]
Odysseus compares Penelope’s fame to that of a just king, under whose rule the land flourishes and the people do good.
The just king whose virtue nourishes his whole people is the Greek image of the Kural’s ‘light of kings’.
Philosophy
“...there is no one in any rule who, in so far as he is a ruler, considers or enjoins what is for his own interest, but always what is for the interest of his subject or suitable to his art;”
— Plato, Republic 1.342e (tr. Jowett) [10]
Socrates argues that a true ruler always acts for the good of those he rules, not for himself.
Supports the Kural’s ideal of the king defined by care for his people (kudi ombal).

Real Life Example

King Sejong creates the Korean alphabet for his people
1443-1446 · Hanyang (Seoul), Joseon Korea
King Sejong, the fourth ruler of Korea's Joseon dynasty, oversaw the creation of a new phonetic alphabet, completed around 1443, so that ordinary people who could not master Chinese characters could learn to read and write. In 1446 he promulgated it as Hunminjeongeum, the Proper Sounds to Instruct the People, with an explanatory text. That 1446 text is now inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register, and the alphabet, Hangul, is used throughout Korea today.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Sejong used royal power not to glorify himself but to give literacy to common people, and his reign is remembered as a golden age of Korean culture and science.
The kural crowns as the light among kings the ruler who combines generosity, compassion, just rule and care for his people, and Sejong's alphabet was a gift of care to all his subjects.
