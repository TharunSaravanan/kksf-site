Kural 823

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

பலநல்ல கற்றக் கடைத்து மனநல்லர் ஆகுதல் மாணார்க் கரிது.
Palanalla kat̲rak kadaiththu mananallar aaguthal maanaark karithu.

Meaning

Even after studying many good books, people with a bad nature find it hard to become good at heart.

Elementary

You can read every book about kindness and still be unkind. Knowing the rules of a game does not make you a fair player. A person who doesn’t care about you can learn lots of nice words without their heart changing.

University

Pala nalla katrak kadaiththu (பலநல்ல கற்றக் கடைத்து) means ‘even when it happens that they have learned many good things’; mana-nallar (மனநல்லர்) are the good-hearted. Maanaar (மாணார்) literally means ‘those who are not excellent’; Varadarasanar glosses it as those who have not attained greatness through inner affection, while Pappaiah and Kalaignar read it as those hostile at heart (பகையுணர்வு படைத்தோர்), and Pope’s gloss ‘enemies’ follows the latter. The couplet thus warns against assuming that a learned enemy has been softened by learning: knowledge of virtue is not virtue. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Vasishtha Dharmasutra teaches that the Vedas, even learned in full with their auxiliary sciences, do not purify a person who lacks good conduct.
— Vasishtha Dharmasutra 6.3 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East vol. 14) [3]
The dharma tradition holds that sacred learning is no substitute for character.
Same claim as the Kural: much learning does not make a bad heart good.
Christianity
“And though I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries, and all knowledge; and though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul insists that knowledge without love has no worth.
Both texts separate knowing much from being good at heart.
Islam
“The example of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not take it on is like that of a donkey who carries volumes [of books].”
— Qur’an 62:5 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an compares those who carry scripture without living by it to a donkey loaded with books.
Learning carried but not taken to heart is the Kural’s learned man who stays unkind.
Judaism
“one whose wisdom exceeds his deeds, to what may he be compared? To a tree whose branches are numerous but whose roots are few, so that when the wind comes, it uproots it and overturns it”
— Pirkei Avot 3:17 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah teaches that wisdom without matching deeds has no roots.
The Kural’s learned but ill-hearted person is a tree of many branches with no root of goodness.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that reciting scripture without practising it leaves a person outside its benefits.
Same insight: many good texts studied do not make a good heart.
Jainism
“Though they wash it with milk and put it to dry many days, charcoal has not the property of becoming white. Though they strike with a stick, and thrust too, understanding will not enter into the body void of virtue.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 26 ('The want of understanding'), st. 8 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār says that someone whose nature lacks goodness is not changed by instruction, just as charcoal stays black however often it is washed in milk.
Valluvar warns that a hostile person who has studied many good books still does not become good at heart; the Nālaṭiyār's charcoal that milk cannot whiten makes the same point, that learning poured over a bad nature does not change it.
Sikhism
“That scholar who is full of greed, arrogant pride and egotism, is known to be a fool.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 140, Raag Majh, Majh Ki Vaar (pauri 6), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Majh Ki Vaar Guru Nanak denies that learning by itself makes a person good: a scholar ruled by greed and ego is still counted a fool.
The Kural warns that those hostile at heart are not made good-hearted by much study; Guru Nanak likewise says learning does not cure a corrupt heart.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus, the most gifted craftsman of Athens, grew jealous of his nephew and pupil Perdix (also called Talos), who had invented the saw, and threw him from the Acropolis.
— Apollodorus, Library 3.15.8; Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 [10]
Supreme skill and knowledge did not prevent envy from ruling Daedalus’s heart.
Like the Kural’s learned enemy, great learning left the heart unchanged.
Philosophy
Heraclitus says that much learning does not teach understanding, otherwise it would have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, Xenophanes and Hecataeus.
— Heraclitus, fragment DK 22 B40 (preserved in Diogenes Laertius 9.1) [11]
Heraclitus mocks famous learned men for amassing information without insight.
Valluvar likewise denies that wide reading produces a sound heart.

Real Life Example

Seneca's teaching fails to make Nero good
49-65 CE · Rome, Italy
The Stoic philosopher Seneca became tutor to the young Nero in 49 CE and, after Nero became emperor in 54, served as one of his chief advisers. Nero relied less and less on Seneca's counsel, and around 62 Seneca withdrew from public life and devoted himself to writing philosophy. In 65, after Seneca's enemies accused him of a part in the Pisonian conspiracy against Nero, the emperor ordered his death.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Years of instruction by one of Rome's leading moral philosophers did not make Nero a good ruler, and in the end he turned against his own teacher.
The kural says that even after studying many good books, those who lack inner nobility rarely become truly good-hearted toward you, and Nero, schooled by Seneca himself, ended by condemning him.
