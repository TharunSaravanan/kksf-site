Kural 1233

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

தணந்தமை சால அறிவிப்ப போலும் மணந்தநாள் வீங்கிய தோள்.
Thanandhamai saala arivippa poalum manandha-naal veengiya thoal.

Meaning

On our wedding day my shoulders were full and happy. Now they have grown thin, and they seem to tell everyone loudly that we are apart.

Elementary

Think of a balloon at a birthday party, round and full, and then the same balloon a week later, small and wrinkled. The girl’s arms were full and strong when she was happy with the person she loves; now that he is away they have become thin, and anyone can see that she misses him.

University

The contrast is temporal and physical: maṇanta nāḷ (‘the day of union’, which may mean the wedding day or any day of togetherness) set against taṇantamai (‘the having-parted’, from taṇa, ‘to separate’). Vīṅkiya tōḷ (‘shoulders that swelled’) uses the Sangam convention that a woman’s tōḷ, shoulders or upper arms, grow plump (pūrittal) in happiness and thin in grief, so they become public evidence; cāla aṟivippa pōlum means ‘they seem to proclaim it fully’. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all add that the thinning announces the separation ‘to others’, continuing the motif of 1232 in which the body exposes private grief. Pope: ‘These withered arms, desertion’s pangs abundantly display, / That swelled with joy on that glad nuptial day.’ Pope transliterates the compound as ‘manandha-naal’; ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree on the Tamil text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Nala story, Damayanti, longing for Nala after the swan’s message, grows pale and thin and loses interest in everything, until her companions report to King Bhima that his daughter is unwell, and he resolves to hold her svayamvara.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
The Mahabharata shows a young woman’s love-sick wasting being noticed by others and reported to her father.
As in the Kural, the beloved’s absence is announced to the world by a body visibly growing thin.
Christianity
“By reason of the voice of my groaning my bones cleave to my skin.”
— Psalms 102:5 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist’s sorrow has wasted him until his body shows nothing but skin and bone.
The Kural’s shoulders, once full, similarly shrink and make grief visible.
Islam
“They said, ‘By Allah, you will not cease remembering Joseph until you become fatally ill or become of those who perish.’”
— Qur’an 12:85 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s sons warn him that his unending remembrance of Joseph is wasting him away.
Like Jacob, the Kural’s heroine is being worn down by constant remembrance of the absent one, and onlookers can see it.
Judaism
“Her princes were purer than snow, they were whiter than milk… Their visage is blacker than coal; they are not known in the streets; their skin is shrivelled upon their bones; it is withered, it is become like a stick.”
— Lamentations 4:7-8 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Lamentations sets the former radiance of Jerusalem’s nobles against their present shrunken, unrecognisable bodies.
The Kural makes the same ‘then and now’ contrast between shoulders that swelled on the day of union and shoulders now wasted.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada observes that even splendid royal chariots wear out, and the body too grows old, while only the Dhamma of the good does not decay.
— Dhammapada 151 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhist teaching treats the fading of bodily beauty as an instance of universal impermanence (anicca).
The Kural’s shoulders, full on the day of union and thin today, show that same impermanence, hastened by grief.
Jainism
In Hemachandra’s account of the emperor Sanatkumara, gods come to see his famed beauty; when he proudly receives them again in royal dress, they tell him that disease has already entered his body and his beauty has faded, and he renounces the world.
— Hemachandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 4, life of Sanatkumara (paraphrase; cf. tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
The Jain story makes the sudden loss of bodily beauty a lesson in the impermanence of the body.
The Kural, too, contrasts a body glowing on one day with the same body visibly diminished later.
Sikhism
“Fareed, my withered body has become a skeleton; the crows are pecking at my palms. Even now, God has not come to help me; behold, this is the fate of all mortal beings.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 90) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid describes a body wasted to bone while the Beloved he longs for has still not come.
The Kural's wife says her shoulders, once full on the day of union, have grown thin and loudly announce the separation; Farid's withered body tells the same story of waiting in vain.
Greek Mythology
Spurned by Narcissus, the nymph Echo hides in the woods; her love clings to her and grows with grief, her body wastes with wakeful care, her skin shrivels, her moisture dries into the air, until only her voice and bones remain, and the bones turn to stone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.393-399 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid tells of a lover literally wasting away from unreturned love.
The Kural’s shrunken shoulders are an early stage of what Ovid carries to its end in Echo.
Philosophy
Lucretius warns that lovers waste their strength and are worn out by their labour, their life spent at another’s beck, their wealth slipping away and their good name sickening.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1121-1124 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.H.D. Rouse) [11]
The Epicurean poet counts physical wasting among the costs of romantic passion.
The Kural records the same toll on the body, though it treats the heroine with sympathy rather than as a warning.

Real Life Example

Shah Jahan lives as a mourner after the death of Mumtaz Mahal
1631 · Mughal Empire, India
Mumtaz Mahal, the wife of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, died in 1631. For about two years afterwards the emperor lived as a mourner, going without music, fine clothing and the lavish display of his court.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The change in the emperor's dress and court life made his private grief visible to everyone around him.
The kural's shoulders, once full with the joy of union, grow thin and proclaim the separation to all, and Shah Jahan's plain dress and silent court likewise announced his loss to the whole empire.
