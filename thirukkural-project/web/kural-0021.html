Kural 21

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

ஒழுக்கத்து நீத்தார் பெருமை விழுப்பத்து வேண்டும் பனுவல் துணிவு.
Ozhukkaththu neeththaar perumai vizhuppaththu, Vendum panuval thunivu.

Meaning

All the good books agree that people who live by strict self-control and have given up their desires deserve more praise than anyone else.

Elementary

Imagine every good book in the world lined up on one shelf, and you ask them all the same question: “Who is the greatest kind of person?” This kural says they would all give the same answer — the person who keeps to good habits and stops grabbing for things they want.

University

Valluvar grounds the authority of asceticism not in revelation but in textual consensus: “panuval thunivu” means literally “the settled resolution (thunivu) of learned works (panuval).” His renunciant is “neeththaar,” “those who have let go” — a participle from nee- (to leave, to separate) rather than a sectarian title such as sannyāsin or bhikkhu, so the ascetic is defined by an act rather than by membership in an order. Crucially he binds renunciation to “ozhukkam,” disciplined conduct, refusing to let letting-go collapse into licence: greatness belongs only to those who stand “ozhukkaththu,” inside conduct. The word “vizhuppam” (pre-eminence, the choicest thing) makes the claim comparative rather than merely approving — not that renunciation is good, but that it is the highest good the books know. This opening move is characteristic of Tamil didactic poetry, which establishes authority by appeal to accumulated learned opinion, and it lets the chapter exalt renunciants without endorsing any one religion's account of why they matter.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A yogī is greater than the ascetic, greater than the empiricist and greater than the fruitive worker. Therefore, O Arjuna, in all circumstances, be a yogī.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.46 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [4]
Krishna closes the chapter on meditation by ranking the person of disciplined inner practice above the body-mortifying ascetic, above the scholar, and above the ritual performer.
Exactly the Kural's move: a canonical text explicitly ranking the disciplined renunciant at the top of every other class of person.
Christianity
“(Of whom the world was not worthy:) they wandered in deserts, and in mountains, and in dens and caves of the earth.”
— Hebrews 11:38 (KJV) [5]
The author of Hebrews ends a long roll-call of the faithful by saying that the world did not deserve those who gave up home and comfort to wander destitute in the wilderness.
Scripture here does precisely what the Kural says scripture does — it declares the homeless, self-denying figures to be worth more than the world itself.
Islam
“And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily, and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace, And those who spend [part of] the night to their Lord prostrating and standing [in prayer]”
— Qur'an 25:63-64, Al-Furqan (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an's portrait of the ʿibād ar-Raḥmān, the servants of the Merciful, describes people marked by humility, gentleness under provocation, and night-long vigil rather than by worldly appetite.
Like the Kural, revealed scripture singles out the self-disciplined, desire-restraining person for its highest praise.
Judaism
“Such is the way [of a life] of Torah: you shall eat bread with salt, and rationed water shall you drink; you shall sleep on the ground, your life will be one of privation, and in Torah shall you labor.”
— Pirkei Avot 6:4 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp) [7]
The closing chapter of Pirkei Avot lays out deliberate material deprivation as the proper setting for a life devoted to Torah, and promises that such a life is blessed in this world and the next.
A revered Jewish text setting austerity and discipline at the top of its scale of value, which is the Kural's exact claim about what books conclude.
Buddhism
“Though month after month for a hundred years one should offer sacrifices by the thousands, yet if only for a moment one should worship those of perfected minds that honor is indeed better than a century of sacrifice.”
— Dhammapada 106, Sahassavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada measures a lifetime of ritual offerings against a single instant of reverence for the perfected and finds the ritual wanting.
This is the Kural's thesis in Buddhist form: the settled verdict of the text is that honouring the renounced outranks every other meritorious thing.
Jainism
“Obeisance to all the Saints in the world.” … “This five-fold obeisance is destructive of all sins and is the foremost amongst all the auspicious.”
— Namokar Mantra as Saman Suttam vv. 1-2, Mangalasutra (tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; text via jainworld.com) [9]
The Namokar (Navkar) Mantra, the first formula every Jain learns and the opening of the Saman Suttam anthology, ends its five salutations by bowing to every renunciant in the world, and the verse that follows declares this homage the foremost of all auspicious things.
Where the Kural says the settled verdict of all worthy books is to praise renunciants above every other excellence, Jainism's own first and most-recited formula makes reverence for the world's renunciants, alongside the Jinas, the highest auspicious act.
Sikhism
“The God-conscious being is always unattached, as the lotus in the water remains detached.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 272, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 8, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sukhmani's praise of the brahm giani (God-conscious being) begins with detachment. Unlike the Kural's ascetic, though, the Sikh ideal stays in household life and is detached in the midst of it, like a lotus that sits in water without getting wet.
Both texts rank highest the person who has let go of desire. Sikhism places that person inside worldly life rather than in renunciation.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Zeus turns his eyes away from the fighting at Troy to the northern peoples, among them the milk-drinking Hippemolgoi and the Abioi, whom Homer calls the most righteous of men — a people imagined as living without wealth, war or luxury.
— Homer, Iliad 13.5-6 [11]
Greek epic occasionally idealises distant peoples whose material simplicity is taken as proof of moral superiority to the acquisitive Greek world.
It is the nearest thing in Greek epic to the Kural's claim that the texts reserve their highest praise for those who own and want least.
Philosophy
Epictetus devotes a whole discourse to the Cynic vocation, describing the true Cynic as a man with no house, no property, no wife and nothing but his own body, whom Zeus sends into the world as a messenger and a scout to show other people where good and evil actually lie.
— Epictetus, Discourses 3.22 (On the Cynic Calling) [12]
For Epictetus the propertyless wanderer is not a social failure but the highest human office, because only someone who wants nothing can testify credibly about what is worth wanting.
Like Valluvar, a teaching tradition places the disciplined renunciant at the summit of its own scale of human excellence.

Real Life Example

Francis of Assisi renounces his inheritance
1206 · Assisi, Italy
In early 1206 Francis's father, the wealthy cloth merchant Pietro di Bernardone, brought him before the bishop of Assisi and demanded that he renounce all claims and return his goods. Francis gave up his inheritance and handed back even the clothes he wore, and the bishop covered him with his own cloak. Francis went on to live in poverty and founded the Franciscan order, which has honoured him ever since.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Francis's public act of letting go of wealth became one of the most celebrated renunciations in European history, and he is still revered across the world eight centuries later.
The kural says the settled verdict of all good books is to praise those who have let go of desire, and Francis's renunciation is among the most praised such acts in the Christian tradition.
