Kural 574

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

உளபோல் முகத்தெவன் செய்யும் அளவினால் கண்ணோட்டம் இல்லாத கண்.
Ulapol mugaththevan seyyum alavinaal Kannottam illaadha kan.

Meaning

Eyes that do not show the right amount of kindness may sit in a face, but what good do they really do?

Elementary

A doll can have beautiful painted eyes, but they never notice when you are sad. They are just there for decoration. Valluvar says eyes that never look kindly at anyone are like doll’s eyes: they are in the face, but they are not really doing an eye’s job.

University

உளபோல் (‘as if they exist’) makes the eyes a mere appearance on the face (முகத்து), and எவன் செய்யும் asks what use they serve. The key qualifier is அளவினால் (‘in due measure’): Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read the kindness required as kannottam within proper limits, i.e., kindness that does not override duty, which anticipates kural 578. Solomon Pappaiah likewise speaks of ‘kannottam within bounds’ (வரம்பிற்கு உட்பட்ட), while Kalaignar reads it as the eyes failing to stir love and pity in the heart. The couplet shares its second line verbatim with 573 (கண்ணோட்டம் இல்லாத கண்), a deliberate repetition that ties the two images together. thirukkural-thamizh.org prints உளபோன் … மளவினாற் (sandhi forms) for ytamizh’s உளபோல் … அளவினால், with no change in wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But you cannot see Me with your present eyes. Therefore I give you divine eyes. Behold My mystic opulence!”
— Bhagavad Gita 11.8 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that his physical eyes, though working, cannot see what truly matters until he is given a higher kind of sight.
Both texts say that physical eyes alone are not enough; the eye has worth only when it sees in the right way.
Christianity
“The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light. But if thine eye be evil, thy whole body shall be full of darkness.”
— Matthew 6:22-23 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that the eye’s moral quality (a ‘single’ or generous eye versus an ‘evil’ or grudging eye) determines whether a person is full of light or darkness.
Like Valluvar, Jesus judges the eye by the disposition it carries, not by its mere presence.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth but He looks to your heart and to your deeds.”
— Sahih Muslim 2564c [5]
The Prophet teaches that outward features count for nothing before God; what matters is the heart and what it produces.
Eyes that merely sit in the face without kindness are exactly the outward form this hadith says has no value.
Judaism
“Rabbi Eliezer said, a good eye; Rabbi Joshua said, a good companion; Rabbi Yose said, a good neighbor; Rabbi Shimon said, foresight. Rabbi Elazar said, a good heart.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:9 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Asked for the right way to cleave to, Rabban Yohanan’s disciples name the ‘good eye’ (ayin tovah), a generous and benevolent way of looking at others, and its opposite, the evil eye, as the way to shun.
The rabbis, like Valluvar, make the moral quality of one’s gaze a central measure of character.
Buddhism
The Buddha reminds the over-strenuous monk Sona, a former lute player, that a lute sounds well only when its strings are neither too tight nor too loose, and tells him to set his effort in the same balance.
— Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, Sona Sutta [7]
Right practice, like right tuning, depends on measure rather than excess in either direction.
Valluvar’s அளவினால் (‘in due measure’) asks for kindness tuned in exactly this balanced way.
Jainism
“Though he may possess all five organs of sense, still it is a rare chance to be instructed in the best Law; for people follow heretical teachers”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira tells Gautama that having complete senses is not enough unless they lead a person to the true Law.
The Kural says eyes without kindly regard only seem to be in the face; the Jain verse likewise says faculties in themselves count for little until they serve something higher.
Sikhism
“O my eyes, the Lord has infused His Light into you; do not look upon any other than the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 922, Raag Raamkalee, Anand Sahib (pauri 36), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Anand Sahib Guru Amar Das tells his eyes that their power of sight is a divine light given for a purpose, and must be used for that purpose.
The Kural says eyes without kindly regard only seem to be in the face and do no good; Guru Amar Das likewise holds that eyes are worth only what they are used to see.
Greek Mythology
Argus Panoptes, the giant with a hundred eyes, is set by Hera to watch Io and never lets all his eyes sleep, yet uses them only for guarding and surveillance, never for pity.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.622-723 [10]
The many-eyed watchman sees everything but feels nothing for the captive he guards, and is finally lulled to sleep and slain by Hermes.
Argus shows that eyes, however many, are useless as eyes of a person when they carry no kindness.
Philosophy
Emmanuel Levinas holds that the face of another person makes an ethical demand on me before anything else; to see a face truly is already to be called to responsibility for the other.
— Emmanuel Levinas, Totality and Infinity (1961), Section III [11]
For Levinas, ethics begins in the face-to-face encounter, where looking at someone properly means responding to their vulnerability.
Valluvar’s eyes that merely sit in the face without responding to others fail exactly the encounter Levinas places at the root of ethics.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth Fry visits the women prisoners of Newgate
1813 · London, England
Early in 1813 a friend urged Elizabeth Fry, a Quaker minister, to visit the women's side of Newgate Prison, and she went in although the jailer warned her that the prisoners were dangerous. She found several hundred women and their children crowded into a few rooms, some sleeping on the floor. Fry kept returning, started a school for the prisoners' children, and with the prisoners and authorities introduced a matron, classification, education and paid sewing work, founding a Ladies' Association for Newgate in 1817.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Many officials had looked at Newgate for years; Fry was among the first to look at its women with real concern, and her measured, practical kindness changed prison practice in Britain.
The kural says eyes without kindly regard in due measure only seem to be present; the officials' eyes had seen Newgate without effect, while Fry's regard, combined with order and discipline, actually did good.
