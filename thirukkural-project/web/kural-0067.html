Kural 67

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

தந்தை மகற்காற்றும் நன்றி அவையத்து முந்தி இருப்பச் செயல்.
Thandhai Makarkaatrum Nandri Avaiyaththu, Mundhi Iruppach Cheyal.

Meaning

The best thing a father can do for his son is to make him good enough to sit in the front row among learned people.

Elementary

A dad can give his child lots of things — toys, money, a bike. This kural says the best gift is different: teach your child so well that when clever grown-ups sit down to talk, your child is the one they want sitting at the front.

University

The couplet defines the father's duty with unusual precision. நன்றி (naṉṟi) here means the benefit or good office a father performs, and the content of that benefit is not property but standing: to be able to sit முந்தி (muntī, "foremost, ahead") in the அவை (avai), the learned assembly. The avai was a real institution in the Tamil and broader Indic world — the court or scholarly council where reputation was won by demonstrated learning and skill in speech — and the Kural devotes two later chapters, அவையறிதல் and அவையஞ்சாமை, to how one conducts oneself there. Parimēlaḻakar draws the sharpest possible conclusion from the wording: making a son wealthy, he says, cannot count as நன்றி at all, because wealth brings suffering; only education does. The couplet thus quietly converts the entire chapter's language of "wealth" into a claim about cultural capital, and it is this verse, with 68, that makes Chapter 7 one of the most-cited passages in modern Tamil arguments for universal education.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Uddālaka Āruṇi tells his son Śvetaketu, at twelve years old, to go and live as a student, on the grounds that in their family no one has ever remained unlearned — no one has been a brahmin merely by birth; the boy returns at twenty-four full of learning and conceited with it, whereupon his father begins the teaching that culminates in tat tvam asi.
— Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.1.1–6.1.7 [5]
The father's obligation in this foundational Upaniṣadic scene is specifically educational: he will not permit his son to hold a status he has not earned by study.
It is the Kural's rule enacted — the father's gift to the son is the learning that lets him stand among the learned in his own right.
Christianity
“And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.”
— Ephesians 6:4 (King James Version) [6]
Paul addresses fathers directly and makes formation — instruction and discipline — the specific content of the paternal role, over against mere authority.
Both verses single out the father and define his duty as the shaping of the child rather than the provision of goods.
Islam
“No father has given a greater gift to his children than good moral training.”
— Jāmiʿ at-Tirmidhī 1952, Chapters on Righteousness [7]
The hadith ranks the cultivation of a child's character above every material provision a father might make.
Both texts define the father's best gift as formation rather than inheritance.
Judaism
“A father is obligated with regard to his son to circumcise him, and to redeem him if he is a firstborn son who must be redeemed by payment to a priest, and to teach him Torah, and to marry him to a woman, and to teach him a trade.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 29a (Sefaria/Koren translation) [8]
The Talmud lists the father's legal duties as a closed set, and two of the five are educational — Torah study and a marketable craft.
Like the Kural, it makes the father's obligation to his son concrete, enumerable, and centred on equipping him to hold his own place in the community.
Buddhism
In the Sigālovāda Sutta the Buddha lists the five ways parents show compassion to their children: they restrain them from evil, encourage them to do good, have them trained in a profession, arrange a suitable marriage, and in due time hand over their inheritance.
— Dīgha Nikāya 31, Sigālovāda Sutta (the six directions: parents) [9]
This is the fullest statement of lay parental duty in the Pali canon, and professional training sits at its centre.
Both texts define what a parent owes a child as a duty of equipping them for a public role, not simply of loving or feeding them.
Jainism
“He studied the seventy-two arts, and acquired knowledge of the world; he was in the bloom of youth, and had a fine figure and good looks.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 21.5–6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
Samudrapâla, son of the Jain layman Pâlita, was raised in his father's house to master the seventy-two arts before he went on to renounce the world and reach liberation.
A devout Jain father seeing his son fully educated in all the recognised arts is close to Valluvar's idea of a father's duty to make his son pre-eminent among the learned.
Sikhism
Sikh tradition credits Guru Angad, the second Guru, with standardizing the Gurmukhi script and setting up schools to teach the young to read and write in their own Punjabi language rather than in classical Sanskrit.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Angad' (Britannica Editors) [11]
Britannica reports these traditional attributions, which are central to Sikh memory of Guru Angad, while noting that historical documentation for some of his attributed institutions is limited.
The Kural says the best thing a father can give his son is learning that wins him a place in the assembly of the wise; Sikh tradition remembers its second Guru as the one who opened learning to the community's children.
Greek Mythology
“For this reason he sent me to instruct you in all these things, to be both a speaker of words and a doer of deeds.”
— Homer, Iliad 9.442–443 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb Classical Library, 1924) [12]
The old tutor Phoenix reminds Achilles that Peleus sent him along precisely because the boy was untaught in war and in the assemblies where men win distinction — so Phoenix was to make him excellent at both speech and action.
The Homeric father's stated aim for his son is the Kural's word for word: to hold the first place in the assembly.
Philosophy
“As soon as one of them grasps what is said to him, the nurse, the mother, the tutor, and the father himself strive hard that the child may excel, and as each act and word occurs they teach and impress upon him that this is just, and that unjust, one thing noble, another base, one holy, another unholy, and that he is to do this, and not do that.”
— Plato, Protagoras 325c–d (trans. W. R. M. Lamb, Loeb Classical Library) [13]
Protagoras is arguing that virtue is taught, and his evidence is the sheer relentlessness with which Athenian households instruct their children from the first moment they can understand language.
It supplies the argumentative backing for the Kural's assumption that a child's eventual standing among the learned is something a father actively makes.

Real Life Example

Philip II of Macedon engages Aristotle as tutor to Alexander
343 or 342 BCE · Macedonia (northern Greece)
King Philip II of Macedon summoned the philosopher Aristotle to act as tutor to his 13-year-old son Alexander. Aristotle taught the prince for about three years, from roughly age 13 to 16. Ancient tradition, reported by Plutarch, places the lessons at Mieza, where archaeologists have excavated a complex believed to be the school.
— Sources: [14] [15]
Philip, already a powerful king, judged that the best thing he could give his heir was the finest teacher in the Greek world.
The kural defines a father's duty as making his son fit to sit in the front rank of the learned, and Philip secured for Alexander the most learned tutor of the age.
