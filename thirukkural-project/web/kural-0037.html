Kural 37

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

அறத்தாறு இதுவென வேண்டா சிவிகை பொறுத்தானோடு ஊர்ந்தான் இடை.
Araththaaru ithuvena ventaa sivikai, Poruththaanotu oorndhaan itai.

Meaning

No one needs to explain what doing good brings. Just look at the difference between the man carrying a palanquin (a covered chair carried on poles) and the man riding in it.

Elementary

Some things you do not need explained — you just look. Two people are on the same road: one is carrying the chair, one is sitting in it. This kural says the difference between doing good and not doing good shows up like that, plain to see.

University

This is the Kural's most striking piece of epistemology. Parimelazhagar reads the verse in the technical vocabulary of Indian logic: there is no need for ஆகம அளவை (scriptural testimony, verbal authority) because காட்சி அளவை (pratyakṣa, direct perception) settles the matter. Valluvar is arguing that karmic consequence is empirically visible in the unequal fortunes of the palanquin-bearer and the palanquin-rider, an argument for virtue that requires no revelation. The verse has also been the chapter's most contested: modern readers, including Kalaignar Karunanidhi in his widely read commentary, have resisted the implication that social inequality is morally deserved, and reinterpret the pair as contrasting inner ease with inner burden rather than birth-station. Either way the rhetorical claim stands — the strength of virtue is a matter of observation, not argument.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Among them, those who did good work in this world [in their past life] attain a good birth accordingly. They are born as a brāhmin, a kṣatriya, or a vaiśya. But those who did bad work in this world [in their past life] attain a bad birth accordingly, being born as a dog, a pig, or as a casteless person.”
— Chandogya Upanishad 5.10.7, trans. Swami Lokeswarananda [3]
The Chandogya's teaching of the two paths explains the visible inequality of births as the direct ripening of conduct in a previous life.
It supplies precisely the doctrine that makes the Kural's palanquin image legible — present station reads off past conduct.
Christianity
“Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles? Even so every good tree bringeth forth good fruit; but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit.”
— Matthew 7:16-17 (KJV) [4]
Jesus offers an empirical test for distinguishing true teachers from false: do not weigh their claims, observe what their lives produce.
Same epistemic move as the Kural — do not expound the fruit of virtue in words, point at what is visible.
Islam
“They will each be assigned ranks according to their deeds. And your Lord is not unaware of what they do.”
— Qur'an 6:132, Al-An'am (Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The verse establishes that differences of rank (darajāt) among created beings correspond exactly to what each has done.
It gives the Kural's contrast its principle: the gap between bearer and rider is a difference of rank proportioned to deeds.
Judaism
“With the measure that a person measures, he is measured with it.”
— Mishnah Sotah 1:7 (William Davidson Talmud, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah states the principle of middah k'neged middah, measure for measure, and then reads a series of visible outcomes as exactly proportioned to the conduct that produced them.
Both texts read observable difference in fortune as a legible record of prior conduct.
Buddhism
King Ajatasattu asks the Buddha whether there is any fruit of the contemplative life that can actually be seen here and now, in the way a trainer of elephants or a cook can point to the visible benefit of his craft. The Buddha answers by asking the king what he would do if his own slave were to go forth and live virtuously — would he order him back to servitude? The king admits he would rise and offer him a seat; and the Buddha says: that is a fruit of the contemplative life, visible here and now.
— Digha Nikaya 2, Samaññaphala Sutta (The Fruits of the Contemplative Life) [7]
The whole sutta is constructed around the demand for sandiṭṭhika — a result that can be pointed at rather than merely asserted — and the Buddha meets it with an everyday social observation.
It is the Kural's argument staged as a dialogue, complete with a contrast between the man who serves and the man who is served.
Jainism
“Every good deed will bear its fruit to men; there is no escape from the effect of one's actions.” … “You are now a king of great power and prosperity, enjoying the reward of your good actions”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.1-20, esp. 13.10 and 13.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The brothers Citta and Sambhuta, once born together as outcaste Svapakas, meet in a later life, one as the universal monarch Brahmadatta and the other as a monk. They explain their very different stations as the visible fruit of past karma.
The Kural points to the gap between the palanquin-bearer and the rider as visible proof of virtue's fruit. The Jain story makes the same point, reading a king's splendour and an outcaste's misery as the open results of past deeds.
Sikhism
“By His Command, some are high and some are low; by His Written Command, pain and pleasure are obtained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Pauri 2, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the second stanza of the Japji, Guru Nanak traces every difference of high and low, pain and pleasure, to hukam, the divine order, and says that one who understands it no longer speaks in ego.
Both texts read the visible gap between the fortunate and the burdened as the working of a moral order, though Gurbani assigns it to God's Command rather than to one's own past virtue alone.
Greek Mythology
Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as travellers, are turned away from a thousand doors in Phrygia until the poor old couple Baucis and Philemon take them in and share what little they have. The gods lead them up the hillside, and from there the couple watch the whole inhospitable district sink beneath a marsh while their own cottage is transformed into a gleaming temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 (Baucis and Philemon) [10]
The reward and the ruin are made simultaneously visible from one vantage point, so the moral difference between the couple and their neighbours becomes a difference anyone can see in the landscape.
Like the Kural's roadside scene, it asks the observer simply to look at two conditions side by side and read the fruit of virtue off them.
Philosophy
In Republic Book IX Socrates sets the just and the tyrannical lives side by side and argues that anyone who could see inside the tyrant would find the least free and most wretched of men, despite his apparent mastery — and he invites Glaucon to judge the ranking of lives by inspection rather than by argument.
— Plato, Republic IX.576b-580c [11]
The argument turns on the claim that the true condition of a life is discoverable by looking at it whole, and that appearances of mastery and servitude can be exactly inverted.
It is the same comparative method as the Kural's palanquin, with the added twist that the one who appears to ride may be the one who is carried.

Real Life Example

Charles Booth maps London's wealth and poverty street by street
1889 · London, England, United Kingdom
Charles Booth, a successful businessman who doubted a claim that a quarter of Londoners lived in extreme poverty, funded and led a street-by-street inquiry into living conditions from 1886. In 1889 he published the first of his Descriptive Maps of London Poverty, colouring every residential street from yellow for the wealthy to black for the poorest, so that rich and poor streets could be seen side by side. His survey found that about 30.7 percent of Londoners lived in poverty, more than he had expected.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Booth's maps made the gulf between fortunes in one city visible at a glance, and they became a founding example of social mapping.
The kural says no argument is needed because the difference in fortunes can simply be seen, as between palanquin-bearer and rider, and Booth's coloured streets let anyone see such differences for themselves.
