Kural 1020

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

நாண்அகத் தில்லார் இயக்கம் மரப்பாவை நாணால் உயிர்மருட்டி அற்று.
Naanagath thillaar iyakkam marappaavai naanaal uyirmarutti atru.

Meaning

The actions of people with no shame in their hearts are like a wooden puppet moved by strings, which only fools people into thinking it is alive.

Elementary

Have you seen a puppet show? The puppet dances and waves, but it isn't really alive; someone is pulling strings. Valluvar says a person with no shame inside is like that: moving around, but with nothing real inside.

University

The chapter closes with a pun on naan (நாண்), which means both ‘shame’ and ‘cord, string’ (as in a bowstring). Those who lack naan agaththu (நாண் அகத்து, 'shame within') move like a marappaavai (மரப்பாவை, 'wooden doll, puppet') that, by naanaal (நாணால், 'by means of strings'), uyir maruttum (உயிர்மருட்டும், 'deceives people into thinking it has life'). Kesikan glosses the strings as the yantra cords of a mechanical doll, and Varadarasanar and Pappaiah describe a doll worked by rope to look alive. So the shameless are alive in appearance only, moved by outside pulls rather than by an inner moral life. The wordplay (shame in the first line, string in the second) is often singled out as one of Valluvar's finest. Pope: ‘'Tis as with strings a wooden puppet apes life's functions.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Supreme Lord is situated in everyone's heart, O Arjuna, and is directing the wanderings of all living entities, who are seated as on a machine, made of the material energy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.61 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna describes embodied beings as mounted on a mechanism (yantra), moved about by māyā.
It shares the Kural's image of a being moved like a mechanical figure, though the Gita applies it to all embodied life, not to the shameless.
Christianity
“I know thy works, that thou hast a name that thou livest, and art dead.”
— Revelation 3:1 (KJV) [4]
The risen Christ tells the church at Sardis that it looks alive but is spiritually dead.
This is the Kural's puppet: an appearance of life with no living core.
Islam
“And when you see them, their forms please you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech. [They are] as if they were pieces of wood propped up”
— Qur'an 63:4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the hypocrites as impressive in appearance but hollow, like propped-up pieces of timber.
The Qur'an and the Kural reach for the same image: wooden figures that only look alive, standing for people empty within.
Judaism
The Psalmist mocks idols of silver and gold: they have mouths but do not speak, eyes but do not see, feet but do not walk. Those who make them, and trust in them, become like them.
— Psalms 115:4-8 [6]
Psalm 115 contrasts lifeless crafted figures with the living God, and warns that their devotees grow equally lifeless.
Like the Kural, it treats a person without a living inner core as no better than a crafted figure that only mimics life.
Buddhism
“Behold this body — a painted image, a mass of heaped up sores, infirm, full of hankering — of which nothing is lasting or stable!”
— Dhammapada 147 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha calls the body a ‘painted image’ (cittakata bimba), a dressed-up figure without lasting substance.
It shares the Kural's image of a decorated figure that only looks alive and lasting.
Jainism
Jain doctrine sharply separates jiva, the conscious soul whose mark is awareness, from ajiva, non-sentient matter. The Uttaradhyayana's final lecture sets out this division, and karma is pictured as ajiva matter that binds the jiva and drives its movements through samsara.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 36 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45); Tattvartha Sutra 2.8 (consciousness as the mark of the soul) [8]
For Jainism, movement driven by karmic matter rather than by the awakened soul is not true living.
This loosely parallels the Kural's shameless puppet, whose motion comes from strings rather than an inner moral life.
Sikhism
“How does the puppet of clay dance?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 487, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas, whose hymns are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, marvels at the human body, a puppet of clay, dancing about as if under its own power while it is pulled by worldly attachment.
The Kural compares the shameless to a wooden puppet on strings that only seems alive; Ravidas uses the same image of a puppet that dances while having no life of its own.
Greek Mythology
Hephaestus is attended by handmaidens made of gold that look like living young women. They move, speak and help him as if they had minds of their own, though they are his crafted works.
— Homer, Iliad 18.417-421 [10]
Homer's golden automata are the classic Greek image of a made thing that imitates life.
They are the Kural's marappaavai in myth: figures that fool the eye into seeing life.
Philosophy
Plato's Athenian imagines each of us as a puppet of the gods, pulled by inner cords of pleasure, pain and fear. We should follow only the one soft golden cord of reason and law, and resist the hard iron ones.
— Plato, Laws I, 644d-645b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato, a person who is jerked about by appetites rather than guided by the golden cord lives a puppet's life.
This is the Kural's puppet image applied to ethics: without an inner guiding cord (for Valluvar, naan), a person is moved only by strings.

Real Life Example

Puyi is installed as head of Japanese-controlled Manchukuo
9 March 1932 · Changchun (Xinjing), Manchuria, China
After the Japanese army occupied Manchuria in 1931, Japan created the state of Manchukuo in March 1932 and brought Puyi, the last Qing emperor, from Tianjin to serve as its chief executive; he was enthroned as emperor in 1934. Although Manchukuo was proclaimed independent, its government was controlled and supervised by the Japanese, and the League of Nations refused to recognise it.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Manchukuo became the classic example of a puppet state, with a figurehead ruler moved by others.
The kural likens those without inner shame to a wooden puppet worked by strings to look alive, and Puyi in Manchukuo was a ruler in appearance only, moved by outside hands.
