Kural 526

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

பெருங்கொடையான் பேணான் வெகுளி அவனின் மருங்குடையார் மாநிலத்து இல்.
Perungotaiyaan penaan vekuli avanin Marungutaiyaar maanilaththu il.

Meaning

No one on this wide earth has more relatives around them than a person who gives generously and does not hold on to anger.

Elementary

Picture two kids. One shares a lot but gets angry and shouts all the time. The other shares and stays calm and kind. Everyone flocks to the second kid. Giving plus a calm heart brings the biggest family circle.

University

Parimelazhagar glosses பெருங்கொடை as ‘great liberality’ of a kind that never itself leads to want. He takes பேணான் வெகுளி (‘does not cherish anger’) to mean that the king does not indulge anger beyond measure, since he grants that a ruler may sometimes need to show it. மருங்கு (‘side’) here means kin (கிளை), so மருங்குடையார் are ‘those who have people at their side’. This kural adds a negative condition to the positive means of 525: generosity will not keep kin if it comes with an unpredictable temper, a point that looks ahead to Chapter 31 (வெகுளாமை) and to 306, where anger is said to burn the raft of one’s own people (இனம்). The text is stable, and all the main commentators agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O king, anger is a kind of bitter, pungent, acrid, and hot drink, painful in its consequences: ... Do thou, O king, swallow it up and obtain peace.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 36 (Vidura’s counsel; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura urges Dhritarashtra to master his anger so that peace can be restored within the Kuru family.
Restraining anger keeps kin together, which is the second condition in the Kural.
Christianity
“Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another,”
— Ephesians 4:31-32 (KJV) [4]
Paul pairs putting away anger with active kindness toward others.
Dropping anger and practising generous kindness is the combination the Kural says wins the widest circle of kin.
Islam
“So by mercy from Allah, [O Muhammad], you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude [in speech] and harsh in heart, they would have disbanded from about you.”
— Qur’an 3:159 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an credits the Prophet’s gentleness for keeping his followers around him, and says harshness would have scattered them.
The Kural says the same: a leader free of anger keeps his people close.
Judaism
Proverbs teaches that a soft answer turns away wrath while a grievous word stirs up anger.
— Proverbs 15:1 [6]
The proverb contrasts calm speech that defuses conflict with harsh words that inflame it.
Setting anger aside keeps relationships whole, which is the Kural’s second condition.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that one should overcome the angry person by non-anger, the wicked by goodness, the miser by generosity and the liar by truth.
— Dhammapada 223 [7]
The verse pairs non-anger and generosity as ways of winning people over.
Non-anger and liberality are exactly the two qualities the Kural names.
Jainism
“If he is frequently angry; if he perseveres in his wrath; if he spurns friendly advice”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
These open the list of faults that make a monk ill-behaved, a list that also includes being angry even with friends and not sharing with others.
The Kural says no one has more people around him than the generous man who does not nurse anger; the Jain text condemns exactly the nursed anger ('perseveres in his wrath') and the refusal to share.
Sikhism
“Fareed, answer evil with goodness; do not fill your mind with anger.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, tells the listener to repay wrong with good and never let anger settle in the mind.
The Kural says no one has more people at his side than the generous man who does not nurse anger; Farid joins the same two things, doing good and refusing to harbour anger.
Greek Mythology
Phoenix tells Achilles the story of Meleager, who withdrew from battle in anger at his mother and refused the pleas and gifts of elders, priests, friends and even his own family, until his city was nearly lost.
— Homer, Iliad 9.527-599 [10]
Meleager’s nursed anger cuts him off from his kin and nearly destroys his people.
This is the reverse of the Kural: cherished anger drives kin away.
Philosophy
Plutarch’s dialogue on controlling anger argues that anger, more than any other passion, makes a man hateful to those around him and drives away household and friends, while mildness makes him loved and approachable.
— Plutarch, Moralia, De Cohibenda Ira (On the Control of Anger) [11]
Plutarch treats mastery of anger as the key to keeping one’s household and friends loyal.
The Kural too makes freedom from anger a condition for having kin around one.

Real Life Example

Cyrus the Great lets deported peoples return home
539 BCE · Babylon (present-day Iraq)
After the Persian king Cyrus the Great captured Babylon in 539 BCE, a proclamation inscribed in his name on a clay cylinder recorded how he restored shrines and allowed deported peoples to return to their homes, reversing the policy of earlier Assyrian and Babylonian kings. It is thought to have been at this time that the Judaeans returned to Jerusalem to rebuild their Temple. About a century later the Greek historian Herodotus recorded that the Persians called Cyrus a father, because he was gentle and devised good things for them, while they called his son Cambyses a master.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Instead of keeping the peoples his predecessors had uprooted in captivity, Cyrus gave them back their homes and gods, and his own people remembered him as a father rather than a master.
The kural says no one on earth has more people at his side than one who gives freely and does not nurse anger, and Cyrus won lasting loyalty through generosity rather than vengeance.
