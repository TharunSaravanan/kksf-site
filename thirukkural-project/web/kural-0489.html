Kural 489

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

எய்தற் கரியது இயைந்தக்கால் அந்நிலையே செய்தற் கரிய செயல்.
Eydhar Kariyadhu Iyaindhakkaal Annilaiye Seydhar Kariya Seyal.

Meaning

When a rare chance comes, grab it at once and do the thing that would be too hard to do at any other time.

Elementary

Imagine a gate that opens only for a moment. If you run through right away, you get in. If you wait to think it over, it shuts. When a rare chance comes, use it straight away.

University

The couplet uses a chiasmus of ‘rare’ things: eydhar kariyadhu (what is hard to obtain, the rare opportunity) and seydhar kariya seyal (deeds hard to do). The link is annilaiye, ‘in that very state, at that very moment’. Iyaindhakkaal means ‘when it has come together’ or ‘when it has fallen into place’. Parimelazhagar stresses immediacy: the opportunity will not return, so the difficult act must be done while it lasts. The previous couplets counselled patience, and this one completes them by insisting that patience ends in decisive action. The Greek figure of Kairos, bald behind so that he cannot be grabbed once he has passed, is the classic Western emblem of the same idea. Text identical in both sources; ‘eydhar kariyadhu’ and ‘seydhar kariya’ are metrical splits of eydharkariyadhu and seydharkariya.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna tells Arjuna that fortunate are the warriors who meet, unsought, a righteous war like this one, which comes as an open door to heaven. To turn away from it would be to lose both duty and honour.
— Bhagavad Gītā 2.32–33 [3]
The battle has arrived by itself (yadṛcchayā) as a rare chance for the kshatriya, and to hesitate is to fail.
A rare, unsought opportunity that must be taken at once matches the Kural’s eydharkariyadhu iyaindhakkaal.
Christianity
“…behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation.”
— 2 Corinthians 6:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul quotes Isaiah to insist that the moment of grace is now and should not be put off.
‘Now is the accepted time’ carries the urgency of the Kural’s annilaiye, ‘at that very moment’.
Islam
Ibn ‘Umar, recalling the Prophet’s counsel to live in the world like a stranger, advised: if you reach the evening, do not wait for the morning, and if you reach the morning, do not wait for the evening. Take from your health for your sickness, and from your life for your death.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6416 [5]
Present opportunities are fleeting and should be used now, before they are gone.
Using the chance while it lasts is the Kural’s annilaiye, ‘then and there’.
Judaism
“Say not: ‘when I shall have leisure I shall study;’ perhaps you will not have leisure.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:4 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Hillel warns against putting off what matters until a better time that may never arrive.
The chance you have is the one to use, as the Kural says of the rare opportunity.
Buddhism
“Just as a border city is closely guarded both within and without, even so, guard yourself. Do not let slip this opportunity (for spiritual growth). For those who let slip this opportunity grieve indeed when consigned to hell.”
— Dhammapada 315 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Pali khaṇo vo mā upaccagā, ‘let not the moment pass you by’, treats human birth as a rare opportunity that must not be wasted.
This is the Kural’s rare moment, eydharkariyadhu, that must be seized annilaiye, then and there.
Jainism
“A rare chance, in the long course of time, is human birth for a living being; hard are the consequences of actions; Gautama, be careful all the while!”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.4 (refrain from 10.1) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira tells Gautama that human birth is a rare opportunity across vast ages and must be used without a moment's carelessness.
The kural urges seizing a rare opportunity at once to do what would otherwise be impossible; Jainism makes the same demand about the rarest opportunity of all, a human life in which liberation can be pursued.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, that which you have to do tomorrow - do it today instead; and that which you have to do now - do it immediately! Later on, you will not be able to do anything, when death hangs over your head.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 138) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir urges acting at once while the chance remains, because the moment, once lost, does not return.
The Kural says to seize a rare opportunity the moment it comes and do the hard deed; Kabir insists on acting now, before the opening closes.
Greek Mythology
Kairos, the god of the opportune moment, was portrayed in Lysippus’ famous bronze. A dialogue epigram attributed to Posidippus describes it. The god stands on tiptoe on a razor’s edge with wings on his feet and a lock of hair over his forehead, while the back of his head is bald. Whoever meets him must grab him from the front, because once he has passed no one can catch him from behind.
— Posidippus, epigram on Lysippus’ Kairos (Greek Anthology 16.275) [10]
Kairos personifies the fleeting right moment that must be seized at once or lost forever.
The Kural’s ‘when the rare thing comes, act then’ is the exact moral of the Kairos statue.
Philosophy
In Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar, Brutus argues for marching at once to Philippi. He says there is a tide in human affairs which, if taken at its flood, leads to fortune, and if missed leaves life stranded in shallows and misery.
— William Shakespeare, Julius Caesar, Act 4, Scene 3 [11]
Brutus insists that a favourable moment, once missed, does not come back.
The Kural’s rare opportunity is Shakespeare’s flood tide, and both must be taken the moment it comes.

Real Life Example

American envoys accept Napoleon's offer of all Louisiana
April 1803 · Paris, France
President Thomas Jefferson's envoys, Robert Livingston and James Monroe, were authorised to pay up to 10 million dollars for New Orleans and West Florida. On 11 April 1803, the French unexpectedly offered the whole Louisiana Territory, and although this went beyond their instructions, the envoys agreed without waiting the months needed to consult Washington. The treaty, dated 30 April 1803, transferred about 827,000 square miles for 15 million dollars, roughly doubling the size of the United States.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A chance that arose from Napoleon's sudden change of plans was seized at once and transformed the future of the United States.
The kural says that when a rare opportunity comes, one must at once do the deed that would otherwise be impossible, and the envoys closed the deal before the opportunity could vanish.
