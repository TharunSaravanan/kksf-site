Kural 242

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

நல்லாற்றாள் நாடி அருளாள்க பல்லாற்றால் தேரினும் அஃதே துணை.
Nallaatraal naadi arulaalga pallaatraal, Therinum aqdhe thunai.

Meaning

Think things through the right way and make kindness your rule for living. However many ways you test it, kindness is the one thing that truly helps you.

Elementary

Imagine trying every road on a map to find the safest way home. Valluvar says that whichever road you test, the one that always gets you there safely is being kind. Kindness is the friend that never lets you down.

University

The couplet is epistemological as well as ethical: naadi (having searched) and pallaatraal therinum (even if you examine by many methods) describe a process of inquiry whose result is always the same — arul is the only thunai, the companion or aid, that holds. Parimelazhagar reads pallaatraal as the conflicting doctrines of the various schools (samayams), so the kural makes a strikingly ecumenical claim: whatever creed one tests, compassion is the point on which all converge. This anticipates later comparative ethics, which often finds compassion to be the common denominator of otherwise divergent religious systems. It also sets up the chapter’s practical thesis — that arul is not one virtue among many but the reliable guide when theories disagree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, Prajapati answers gods, humans and asuras with the single syllable ‘Da’, which they hear as damyata (control yourselves), datta (give) and dayadhvam (be compassionate); the thunder repeats ‘Da Da Da’ as a teaching for all.
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.2.1-3 [3]
One divine utterance yields three disciplines, and compassion (dayā) is the one required of the asuras, the beings most prone to cruelty.
Like the Kural, the Upanishad treats compassion as a teaching that remains valid however the instruction is heard or interpreted.
Christianity
“Which now of these three, thinkest thou, was neighbour unto him that fell among the thieves? And he said, He that shewed mercy on him. Then said Jesus unto him, Go, and do thou likewise.”
— Luke 10:36-37 (KJV) [4]
Asked how to inherit eternal life, Jesus lets the lawyer test three candidates (a priest, a Levite and a Samaritan); the answer that survives is the one who showed mercy.
A near-dramatisation of ‘test it by many paths and mercy alone is the guide’: religious status fails the test and compassion passes.
Islam
“and—above all—to be one of those who have faith and urge each other to perseverance and urge each other to compassion.”
— Quran 90:17 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
Surah al-Balad describes the ‘challenging path’ (freeing slaves, feeding orphans and the destitute) and crowns it with a community that urges compassion (marḥama) on one another.
Both texts present compassion as the path that holds when the harder, steeper ways of virtue are weighed.
Judaism
“what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?”
— Micah 6:8 (KJV); cf. Talmud Sukkah 49b [4]
Micah reduces the many demands of religion to three, and the Talmud (Sukkah 49b) reads ‘to love mercy’ as gemilut chasadim, acts of lovingkindness.
Both texts sift many religious options and arrive at mercy or kindness as the essential requirement.
Buddhism
The Kalamas, confused by rival teachers, are told not to rely on tradition, scripture or logic alone but to see for themselves which states lead to harm; the Buddha then describes the disciple who pervades the whole world with a mind imbued with good will and compassion.
— Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 [6]
After a method of personal testing, the conclusion reached is the cultivation of boundless good will and compassion.
Exactly the Kural’s structure: examine by many paths, and compassion is what stands.
Jainism
“First the persuasion of every one should be ascertained, and then we will ask them severally: Ye professors! is pain pleasant to you, or unpleasant? If they give the right answer, reply: For all sorts of living beings pain is unpleasant, disagreeable. and greatly feared.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.4.2.3-6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [7]
The Acaranga answers rival teachers who claim to have examined the matter in every way and found killing harmless: tested properly, every inquiry leads back to the fact that all beings fear pain, so none should be harmed.
Valluvar says that however many ways one examines the paths, compassion alone proves the true support; the Acaranga likewise says that honest inquiry from every side always arrives at non-harm.
Sikhism
“Be kind to all beings-this is more meritorious than bathing at the sixty-eight sacred shrines of pilgrimage and the giving of charity.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 136, Raag Maajh, Baarah Maah, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Baarah Maah of Raag Maajh Guru Arjan weighs kindness to living beings against the most valued religious acts, pilgrimage and charity, and finds kindness greater.
The Kural says that however many paths one examines, compassion proves the only true support; Guru Arjan likewise sets compassion above all the other recognised forms of merit.
Greek Mythology
“an altar to Eleos (Mercy), of all divinities the most useful in the life of mortals and in the vicissitudes of fortune”
— Pausanias, Description of Greece 1.17.1 (trans. Jones) [9]
Pausanias describes the Athenian Altar of Pity, where suppliants such as the children of Heracles and Adrastus sought refuge.
Among all the gods, the one judged most useful through life’s changes is Mercy — close to the Kural’s claim that compassion is the one reliable aid.
Philosophy
“Boundless compassion for all living beings is the surest and most certain guarantee of pure moral conduct, and needs no casuistry.”
— Arthur Schopenhauer, On the Basis of Morality, Part III, ch. 8 (trans. A. B. Bullock) [10]
Schopenhauer argues that compassion, not rational rule-following, is the real foundation of morality.
‘Needs no casuistry’ is the Kural’s ‘however many ways you examine it’: when theories multiply, compassion is still the sure guide.

Real Life Example

Akbar's debates in the Ibadat Khana
1575-1579 · Fatehpur Sikri, Mughal India
In 1575 the Mughal emperor Akbar began building the Ibadat Khana, a hall of worship at Fatehpur Sikri, where weekly debates were held. From 1578 he opened the discussions to Hindu pandits, Jain teachers, Zoroastrian priests and Jesuit missionaries as well as Muslim scholars. Britannica records that this comparative study convinced him that there was truth in all religions but that none possessed the whole truth, and his court developed the policy of sulh-i kul, universal peace and tolerance among creeds.
— Sources: [11] [12]
An emperor deliberately examined many rival doctrines side by side and arrived at a policy of tolerance and protection for all his subjects.
The kural says that whatever paths one examines, compassion alone proves the true support, and Akbar's testing of many creeds led him to a policy of goodwill toward all.
