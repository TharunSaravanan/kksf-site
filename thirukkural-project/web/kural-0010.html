Kural 10

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

பிறவிப் பெருங்கடல் நீந்துவர் நீந்தார் இறைவன் அடிசேரா தார்.
PiRavi-p perungadal neendhuvar neendhaar, iRaivan adi seraadhaar.

Meaning

Only those who reach the feet of God can swim across the great ocean of births (being born again and again). No one else can.

Elementary

Life — with all its ups, downs, and being born again and again — is like a giant ocean. This last kural in the chapter says the only way to swim all the way across it is by holding onto God.

University

This closing verse restates and intensifies kural 8’s ocean imagery, now naming the object to be crossed explicitly as “piravi” — birth/rebirth itself — invoking the doctrine of samsara common to the Indian religious milieu. The repetition is a structural device: the chapter opens by establishing God as “first” of all things and closes by establishing God as the only viable “crossing” out of the cycle those origins set in motion.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“This divine energy of Mine... is difficult to overcome. But those who have surrendered unto Me can easily cross beyond it.”
— Bhagavad Gita 7.14 [3]
Krishna admits his own divine power is genuinely hard to overcome by yourself — but total surrender to him makes crossing it easy.
Same claim as the Kural: this particular ocean isn't crossed by self-effort alone, only by surrender to the divine.
Christianity
“For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works.”
— Ephesians 2:8-9 (KJV) [4]
Paul is emphatic that being saved is a gift, not an achievement — nothing you did earns it.
Backs the Kural’s exclusivity claim: crossing over isn't something you accomplish yourself, it's given.
Islam
“There is none of you except he will come to it [Hell]... Then We will save those who feared Allah and leave the wrongdoers within it.”
— Qur'an 19:71-72, Maryam [5]
Every person, the verse says, passes over Hell — but God then rescues those who were mindful of Him and leaves the wrongdoers there.
A vivid version of the Kural’s 'crossing' image — everyone faces the crossing, but only some make it fully across.
Judaism
In later Jewish mysticism, souls that haven't reached spiritual completion are said to be sent back into new bodies (gilgul, 'the wheel') again and again until they are pure enough to return to their heavenly source.
— Gilgul neshamot, the Zohar and later Kabbalah [6]
This is Judaism's own, much later version of the idea that a soul cycles through lives until it becomes fit to finally return to God.
A genuine, if late and minority, Jewish parallel to the Kural's 'ocean of rebirth' — crossed only by finally becoming fit to return to the divine source.
Buddhism
“Few among men are those who cross to the farther shore... those who act according to the perfectly taught Dhamma will cross the realm of Death, so difficult to cross.”
— Dhammapada, verses 85–86 [7]
The Buddha observes that almost everyone just paces along the near bank of existence — only a few actually cross to the far shore, and only through genuine practice.
Same 'few actually cross' structure as the Kural, and the same insistence that only real practice, not mere proximity, gets you there.
Jainism
“You have crossed the great ocean; why do you halt so near the shore? make haste to get on the other side”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 10.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
Mahavira urges his chief disciple Gautama, who has come almost all the way to liberation, not to stall at the brink of the ocean of samsara but to finish the crossing.
It shares the Kural's picture of rebirth as a vast sea that must be swum across to the far shore, reached by following the Jina's path to its end.
Sikhism
“There are no oars, nor any boatmen, to cross over the terrifying world-ocean... The True Name of the True Lord is there; it carries them across the terrifying world-ocean.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1009, Raag Maru, Ashtpadi, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says human means cannot cross the world-ocean; only the True Name carries a person over. In the same hymn he says the Gurmukhs reach the far shore and their comings and goings in rebirth come to an end.
Same picture as the Kural: only through God can one cross the great ocean of repeated births.
Greek Mythology
Empedocles and the Orphic tradition taught that souls are bound to a repeating 'Wheel' of births across many forms of life, escapable only through purification and right knowledge.
— Empedocles / Orphic tradition (the gold tablets) [10]
The soul is stuck cycling through different bodies and lives until it purifies itself enough to finally break free.
The closest Greek parallel to the Kural's literal 'ocean of rebirth' — a real doctrine of being trapped in repeated birth, escaped only by the right kind of transformation.
Philosophy
In the myth, most souls choose their next life poorly, out of habit or ignorance, and are swept back into the cycle — only the soul that has genuinely pursued wisdom chooses well enough to eventually break free.
— Plato, Republic, Book X (the Myth of Er) [11]
Choosing your way out of the cycle of rebirth takes real, hard-won wisdom, not luck or a passing wish.
Matches the Kural's claim precisely: escaping the cycle of rebirth takes something more than luck — a specific, higher orientation.

Real Life Example

Ibn Battuta sets out from Tangier on pilgrimage to Mecca
1325 · Tangier, Morocco, to Mecca, Arabia
In 1325, at the age of 21, Ibn Battuta left Tangier intending to perform the hajj, the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca, and to study Islamic law along the way. He reached Mecca and completed the pilgrimage, then kept travelling across the Islamic world and beyond for nearly three decades. His experiences were later recorded in the Rihla.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A young man's journey toward the holiest place of his faith became one of the great journeys of the medieval world.
The kural speaks of reaching God's feet as the only way across the great ocean of births, and Ibn Battuta's pilgrimage shows a life set in motion by the wish to reach the sacred centre of his faith.
