Kural 52

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

மனைமாட்சி இல்லாள்கண் இல்லாயின் வாழ்க்கை எனைமாட்சித் தாயினும் இல்.
Manaimaatchi illaaLkaN illaayin vaazhkkai, enaimaatchith thaayinum il.

Meaning

If a wife does not have the good qualities a home needs, then however grand a man’s life may be, it is worth nothing.

Elementary

A house can have the shiniest roof, the biggest garden and the nicest furniture — but if the roof leaks, none of the rest keeps you dry. This kural says the wife's goodness is the roof: if it's missing, everything else stops counting.

University

The rhetorical engine here is the word il, which Valluvar uses twice in different senses — il as 'home/household' and il as 'is not, amounts to nothing' — so the couplet ends on the flat negation that domestic splendour without domestic virtue simply is not. Parimelazhagar notes precisely this, glossing the final il as meaning 'useless', not 'absent'. The Tamil didactic tradition amplified the point: the Naaladiyaar declares that a mansion with jewelled lamps but no worthy mistress is 'a cruel forest', an image the anthologist of this chapter explicitly quotes. Comparative scholars often pair this couplet with the near-universal wisdom-literature topos that a household's moral weather is set by the spouse rather than by its wealth — a topos with strikingly parallel formulations in Hebrew Proverbs, Hesiod, and the Pali canon. It should be noted that Valluvar states the principle asymmetrically here; the Kural does address husbandly failure elsewhere (notably Chapter 91, Penvazhich cheral), but this chapter's frame is the wife's contribution.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Shakuntala reproves King Dushyanta by declaring that the wife is a man's own half and his first friend, the root of dharma, artha and kama and even of liberation, and that the house not occupied by a wife is as desolate as a wilderness however grand it may be.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Sambhava Parva §74 (Shakuntala to Dushyanta; trans. K. M. Ganguli, 1883–96) [4]
Shakuntala's speech makes the wife the constitutive element of the household, without which the other goods of life have no ground.
Both texts say the household's splendour collapses into nothing once wifely worth is subtracted.
Christianity
“It is better to dwell in a corner of the housetop, than with a brawling woman in a wide house.”
— Proverbs 21:9 (King James Version) [5]
Proverbs makes the same trade Valluvar makes: a spacious, splendid house is worth less than a bare corner if the marriage inside it is bad.
Both couplets weigh domestic virtue against domestic magnificence and find the magnificence worthless.
Islam
“The whole world is a provision, and the best object of benefit of the world is the pious woman.”
— Sahih Muslim 1467, Book of Suckling, Chapter: Advice with regard to women (narrated by 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr) [6]
The Prophet ranks a righteous wife above every other worldly good a person could possess.
Where the Kural says other splendours are worth nothing without her, the hadith says she is worth more than all of them.
Judaism
“Rabbi Tanḥum said that Rabbi Ḥanilai said: Any man who does not have a wife is left without joy, without blessing, without goodness.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 62b (Koren Noé / William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Gemara derives from three separate scriptural verses that joy, blessing and goodness all enter a man's life through his wife.
Both texts make the wife the channel through which a household's goods become real goods at all.
Buddhism
“When the husband is ethical, bountiful, rid of stinginess, but the wife is unethical, miserly and abusive, she's a zombie living with a god for a husband.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 4.53, Paṭhamasaṁvāsa Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato, SuttaCentral) [8]
The Buddha's four types of marriage make the point that one partner's virtue cannot carry a household whose other partner lacks it.
Even a wholly virtuous husband's life 'amounts to nothing' as a household when the wife's character is absent.
Jainism
“This Revai, the wife of that Mahasayaga, possessed ancestral property consisting of eight kror measures of gold and eight herds, each herd containing ten thousand head of cattle.” Yet in the canon's story Revati, ruled by greed and craving, does away with her co-wives, takes to meat and drink, and tries to pull her husband, a lay disciple of Mahavira, out of his vowed retreat, while he silently 'continued in the meditation of the Law'.”
— Uvāsagadasāo, Lecture 8 (Mahāsayaga), §§ 233–257, esp. § 234 and § 247 (tr. A. F. Rudolf Hoernle, Bibliotheca Indica, vol. 2, Calcutta, 1888; scan at archive.org) [9]
The seventh Anga sets a household of enormous wealth and a devout husband beside a wife without virtue. Revati's own riches cannot save her: she dies within seven days and, the text says, is reborn in hell.
Valluvar says that if a wife lacks the excellence of domestic virtue, whatever greatness a household has is nothing, and Mahasataka's house, though one of the richest in Rajagriha, is undone by Revati's conduct.
Sikhism
“O bride, decorate yourself, after you surrender and accept your Husband Lord. Otherwise, your Husband Lord will not come to your bed, and your ornaments will be useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 788, Raag Soohee, Soohee ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Using the image of the bride-soul, Guru Amar Das teaches that outward finery counts for nothing unless it comes with inner surrender and love; only then are the ornaments 'acceptable and approved'.
Like the Kural's claim that a household's splendour is worthless without the wife's excellence, the verse says that outer adornment is useless without the inner quality that gives it meaning.
Greek Mythology
“For a man wins nothing better than a good wife, and, again, nothing worse than a bad one, a greedy soul who roasts her man without fire, strong though he may be, and brings him to a raw old age.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 702–705 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White, Loeb Classical Library, 1914) [11]
Hesiod's farming poem states the good-wife/bad-wife polarity as a maxim of practical life, not as myth.
Almost the identical proposition: no external strength or wealth survives a bad marriage.
Philosophy
“Between husband and wife friendship seems to exist by nature, for man is naturally disposed to pairing.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.12, 1162a16–17 [12]
Aristotle grounds the household not in property or utility alone but in a natural philia between spouses, from which the rest of the household's goods follow.
If the marital bond itself fails, then on Aristotle's account the household has lost the very thing that made it a household.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria withdraws from public life after Prince Albert's death
1861 onwards · Windsor, Balmoral and Osborne, United Kingdom
Prince Albert died in December 1861 at the age of 42. Queen Victoria, who had relied on him as her husband and principal trusted adviser in affairs of state, wore black for the rest of her reign and for years rarely appeared in public, spending long periods at Balmoral and Osborne. The public, at first sympathetic, grew increasingly impatient with its absent sovereign, and only gradually, from the late 1860s and 1870s, did she resume her public duties.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Victoria's long seclusion shows how the loss of a life-partner could empty even the grandest position of its meaning for the one left behind.
The kural says that without a worthy life-partner a household's splendour amounts to nothing, and Victoria, still ruler of a vast empire, found the throne's splendour hollow once the partner who shared her life was gone.
