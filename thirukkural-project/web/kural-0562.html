Kural 562

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

கடிதோச்சி மெல்ல எறிக நெடிதாக்கம் நீங்காமை வேண்டு பவர்.
Kadidhoachchi mella eriga nedidhaakkam Neengaamai vaendu bavar.

Meaning

Rulers who want to stay successful for a long time should raise the stick sternly but bring it down gently. They should warn firmly but punish mildly.

Elementary

Think of a parent who says in a serious voice, ‘If you do that again, there will be no TV for a month!’, but when it happens, takes away TV for just one evening. The warning is strong, the punishment is gentle. The kural says leaders who do this keep their people’s trust for a long time.

University

The image is of a raised stick: கடிது ஓச்சி (lift it high, severely) but மெல்ல எறிக (bring it down softly). The classical commentary, followed by Mu. Varadarasanar, reads this as appearing to proceed with great severity while in fact not exceeding due measure, while Kalaignar’s modern gloss separates the stages as stern reproof but mild punishment. The reward named is நெடிது ஆக்கம் நீங்காமை, prosperity that does not depart for a long time, so the couplet argues from prudence as much as from mercy. Pope’s ‘raise the rod with brow severe, but let it gently fall’ has become the standard English rendering. thirukkural-thamizh.org prints the sandhi form ‘மெல்ல வெறிக’, which is the same words as ‘மெல்ல எறிக’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“No,” says Kauṭilya; for whoever imposes severe punishment becomes repulsive to the people; while he who awards mild punishment becomes contemptible. But whoever imposes punishment as deserved becomes respectable.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.4 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya rejects his teacher’s advice that a king should always hold the sceptre raised (udyatadaṇḍa), arguing for punishment that is deserved, neither harsh nor lax.
The raised-but-restrained sceptre in the Arthashastra is the same picture as the Kural’s rod raised high but lowered gently.
Christianity
“For he shall have judgment without mercy, that hath shewed no mercy; and mercy rejoiceth against judgment.”
— James 2:13 (KJV) [4]
James teaches that judgment stays real but mercy has the final word over it.
The rod is raised (judgment) but comes down gently (mercy), which is the Kural’s balance.
Islam
“Avert the legal penalties from the Muslims as much as possible, if he has a way out then leave him to his way, for if the Imam makes a mistake in forgiving it would be better than making mistake in punishment.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1424 [5]
This narration keeps the fixed penalties on the books but urges the ruler to avoid applying them wherever a way out exists.
Severe law, lenient application: the rod is raised high but falls softly.
Judaism
The Mishnah records that a Sanhedrin which executes someone once in seven years is called destructive; Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya says once in seventy years, and Rabbis Tarfon and Akiva say that had they sat on the Sanhedrin no one would ever have been executed.
— Mishnah Makkot 1:10 (Babylonian Talmud Makkot 7a) [6]
The rabbis kept capital law in the Torah but surrounded it with procedures so demanding that it was almost never carried out.
Stern law held high, applied with extreme restraint, is the Jewish legal form of the Kural’s gentle stroke.
Buddhism
“They are told that despite his remorse Beloved-of-the-Gods has the power to punish them if necessary, so that they should be ashamed of their wrong and not be killed.”
— Ashoka, Rock Edict XIII (trans. S. Dhammika) [7]
After Kalinga, Ashoka reminded restless forest peoples that he still held the power to punish, precisely so they would reform and he would not have to use it.
Ashoka raises the rod as a warning and hopes never to strike, which is the Kural’s strategy.
Jainism
“He should not, in every case, wait for the express command (of the teacher) like an unbroken horse for the whip (of the rider), but like a broken horse which sees the whip (of the rider) he should commit no evil act.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The first lecture on monastic discipline holds up the well-trained horse that needs only to see the whip, not feel it, as the model for a pupil.
Valluvar's ruler raises the rod sternly but lets it fall gently; the Jain verse likewise says that the visible rod, not the blow, should be what keeps a well-disciplined person from wrongdoing.
Sikhism
“Your child makes mistakes again and again, but still, You are pleased with him, O Father of the Universe.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1321, Raag Kalyaan, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das pictures God as a father who goes on loving his child in spite of repeated faults.
The Kural advises raising the rod sternly but letting it fall gently; Guru Ram Das gives the ideal of a guardian whose correction is always tempered by tender regard.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Eumenides, Athena founds the Areopagus court and tells the Athenians to honour neither anarchy nor despotism, yet not to banish fear entirely from the city; the terrifying Furies are then given an honoured, gentler role as the Kindly Ones.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides 696–699 and finale [10]
The myth keeps a measure of awe in the city’s justice but transforms raw vengeance into a restrained, protective institution.
Keep the dread in reserve, soften its actual use: this is the Kural’s raised rod and gentle stroke.
Philosophy
Machiavelli advises that a prince should make himself feared in such a way that, even if he does not win love, he avoids hatred, chiefly by keeping his hands off his subjects’ property and not being needlessly cruel.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 17 [11]
Even the most hard-headed theorist of power holds that the ruler’s severity must be displayed but kept within bounds, or it will destroy him.
Machiavelli and Valluvar reach the same practical conclusion by different moral routes: show sternness, avoid actual cruelty, and your rule will last.

Real Life Example

The Meiji government pardons the rebel admiral Enomoto Takeaki
1869-1872 · Hakodate and Tokyo, Japan
Enomoto Takeaki, who had led the last forces loyal to the former shogunate, surrendered to the new Meiji government at the fortress of Goryokaku in Hakodate in June 1869. He was sent to Tokyo and held in confinement for about three years, after which he was pardoned. He went on to serve the government as envoy to Russia and held several ministerial posts.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The new government crushed the armed rebellion completely, but the penalty that followed was limited, and the former rebel became one of its useful servants.
The kural advises raising the rod sternly but letting it fall gently, and Enomoto's defeat, brief confinement and pardon show severity in the contest and mildness in the punishment.
