Kural 740

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

ஆங்கமை வெய்தியக் கண்ணும் பயமின்றே வேந்தமை வில்லாத நாடு.
Aangamai veydhiyak kannum payamindre vendhamai villaadha naadu.

Meaning

Even if a land has all these good things, it is of no use if it does not have a good king who gets along with his people.

Elementary

You can have the best football, the greenest field and the fastest players, but without a good captain who works well with the team, you will not win. A country with every treasure still needs a good leader.

University

The chapter ends by subordinating everything it has praised to kingship. Aangu amaivu eydhiya kannum means ‘even when it has attained all the excellences set out there’ (in the previous nine kurals), and payam indre means ‘it is without fruit’. The key compound vendhamaivu is read by Parimelazhagar as ‘harmony, or fitness, between king and subjects’, which Pope follows with ‘not with its king at peace’; Mu. Varadarasanar, Kalaignar and Sivayogi Sivakumar read it more simply as ‘a good king or good government’, and Solomon Pappaiah as a government with love for its citizens. Either way, the verse links this chapter back to the Kural’s section on kingship (Arasiyal) and warns that natural wealth without right rule is wasted. The sandhi text agrees with ytamizh word for word.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The world depends upon agriculture and trade and is protected by the Vedas. All these again are duly protected by the king exercising his principal duty.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section 68 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Brihaspati tells King Vasumanas that without royal protection, farming and trade would collapse, the strong would rob the weak and famine would ravage the kingdom.
Brihaspati makes the Kural’s point: all a land’s wealth depends on a king who fulfils his duty.
Christianity
“The king by judgment establisheth the land: but he that receiveth gifts overthroweth it.”
— Proverbs 29:4 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says a just king makes a country stable, while a corrupt one brings it down.
Like the Kural, the proverb makes the fate of the land depend on the quality of its ruler.
Islam
“Surely! Everyone of you is a guardian and is responsible for his charges: The Imam (ruler) of the people is a guardian and is responsible for his subjects.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7138 [5]
The Prophet teaches that every person, and above all the ruler, is a guardian answerable for those in his care.
The ruler as guardian of his people is the bond (vendhamaivu) without which, the Kural says, a rich land is useless.
Judaism
“Rabbi Hanina, the vice-high priest said: pray for the welfare of the government, for were it not for the fear it inspires, every man would swallow his neighbor alive.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:2 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah teaches that even an imperfect government is a blessing, because without it society would fall into violence.
Rabbi Hanina, like Valluvar, holds that a land without working rule gains nothing from its other goods.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that when kings are unrighteous, their officials and then the whole people become unrighteous; the sun and moon go astray, the rains fail to come in season, the crops ripen badly, and people become weak and short-lived. When kings are righteous, the reverse happens.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.70, Adhammika Sutta [7]
In this teaching, the moral quality of the ruler flows down through the people into nature itself.
The Buddha’s point is the Kural’s: a land’s fertility and well-being are worthless, even lost, without a righteous ruler.
Jainism
“During his reign he taught, for the benefit of the people, the seventy-two sciences, of which writing is the first, arithmetic the most important, and the knowledge of omens the last, the sixty-four accomplishments of women, the hundred arts, and the three occupations of men.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Rishabha §§210-211 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Rishabha, called 'First King' among his five names, is remembered as the ruler who gave his people the arts, sciences and occupations; Jacobi's note adds that the Kulakaras before him were the first kings and founders of families.
The Kural says even a land with every excellence is fruitless without a fitting ruler; the Jain account of Rishabha likewise makes a wise king the one who turns a land's potential into a working society.
Sikhism
“The kings had lost their higher consciousness, reveling in pleasure and sensuality. Since Baabar’s rule has been proclaimed, even the princes have no food to eat.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa (Ashtapadi, Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Babarvani hymns, Guru Nanak laments the sack of the Punjab by Babur's armies and lays part of the blame on the rulers, who had squandered their wealth in pleasure and lost their awareness.
The Kural warns that even a land with every excellence is fruitless without a fitting ruler; Guru Nanak shows a rich realm brought to hunger because its rulers were unfit.
Greek Mythology
“You are like some blameless king, who upholds righteousness, as the monarch over a great and valiant nation: the earth yields its wheat and barley, the trees are loaded with fruit, the ewes bring forth lambs, and the sea abounds with fish by reason of his virtues.”
— Homer, Odyssey 19 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus, in disguise, praises Penelope by comparing her to a just king whose goodness makes the whole land fruitful.
Homer, like Valluvar, makes the land’s abundance depend on the quality of its king.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates says that cities will never have rest from their evils until philosophers become kings, or kings and rulers become true philosophers, so that political power and wisdom meet in one person.
— Plato, Republic V, 473c-d [11]
For Plato, the right ruler is the one condition without which no city can be good.
Plato and Valluvar both end their picture of the good city by insisting that everything depends on the right kind of ruler.

Real Life Example

The reign of Charles II, last Habsburg king of Spain
1665 to 1700 · Madrid, Spain
Charles II inherited the vast Spanish empire at the age of four in 1665. Chronically ill and widely regarded as unable to govern effectively, he was dominated by court factions while Spain suffered economic troubles and military setbacks against Louis XIV. He fathered no children, and the dispute over his succession after his death in 1700 led to the War of the Spanish Succession and the break-up of Spain's European possessions.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Spain still ruled one of the largest empires in the world, but without a capable ruler its great resources could not prevent decline.
The kural says that even a land with every good quality bears no fruit without a fitting ruler, and Spain under Charles II is a well-known case of vast wealth undone by a king unable to rule.
