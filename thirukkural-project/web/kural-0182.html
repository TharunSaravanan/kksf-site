Kural 182

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

அறனழீஇ அல்லவை செய்தலின் தீதே புறனழீஇப் பொய்த்து நகை.
Aranazheei allavai seydhalin theedhe, Puranazheeip poiththu nagai.

Meaning

Saying bad things about someone behind their back and then giving them a fake smile to their face is worse than turning away from goodness and doing wrong.

Elementary

Suppose a classmate says mean things about you when you’re gone, then comes up and grins at you like your best friend. That’s worse than someone who is just openly naughty — because the fake smile is a trick. This kural says the two-faced person is worse than the openly bad one.

University

The couplet is built on a pointed parallelism: aṟaṉ-aḻīi (“destroying virtue”) against puṟaṉ-aḻīi (“destroying [someone] at the back”), with the second judged worse (tītē). What tips the scale is the final phrase poyttu nakai, “smiling falsely” — the backbiter compounds slander with a feigned intimacy in the victim’s presence. Valluvar is thus condemning not only malicious speech but hypocrisy as a structure of relationship, a split between the face and the back that corrupts the trust on which the householder’s social world rests. The claim that this is worse than open wickedness anticipates a widely shared intuition across ethical traditions: the open enemy can be guarded against, but the smiling detractor disarms his victim first. The verse also sharpens 181: there, abstaining from slander redeemed a sinner; here, slander joined to false friendliness sinks a man below the sinner.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Parth, the qualities of those who possess a demoniac nature are hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness, and ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.4 (Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna names dambha — hypocrisy, showing a good face while hiding a bad heart — as the very first mark of a demonic nature.
Both texts rank the false face above open vice; the smiling backbiter of the Kural is a textbook case of dambha.
Christianity
“The words of his mouth were smoother than butter, but war was in his heart: his words were softer than oil, yet were they drawn swords.”
— Psalm 55:21 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist laments a companion who spoke sweetly to his face while plotting against him.
This is the Kural’s smiling backbiter exactly: soft speech in front, a drawn sword behind.
Islam
“The worst people in the Sight of Allah on the Day of Resurrection will be the double faced people who appear to some people with one face and to other people with another face.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6058 [5]
The Prophet calls the two-faced person (dhu’l-wajhayn) the worst of people before God.
Like Valluvar, the hadith ranks the double-faced person at the very bottom — below other kinds of sinners.
Judaism
“Their tongue is a sharpened arrow; it speaks deceit. One speaks peaceably to his neighbor with his mouth, but in his heart he lies in wait for him”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 15b, citing Jeremiah 9:7 (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
The Talmud’s discussion of malicious speech quotes Jeremiah’s picture of the peaceful-mouthed ambusher, and in the same passage the school of Rabbi Yishmael says lashon hara outweighs idolatry, sexual sin, and bloodshed combined.
Both the ranking (worse than the gravest sins) and the peaceful face over a hidden attack match the Kural’s verdict.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who flatters be understood as a foe in the guise of a friend: (i) he approves of his friend's evil deeds, (ii) he disapproves his friend's good deeds, (iii) he praises him in his presence, (iv) he speaks ill of him in his absence.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha teaches the layman Sigala to recognise the false friend: praise to your face, slander behind your back.
The Buddha’s ‘foe in the guise of a friend’ is the Kural’s backbiter who smiles falsely — almost word for word.
Jainism
“पिट्ठिमंसं न खाएज्जा” (piṭṭhimaṃsaṃ na khāejjā) — “One should not talk ill of others in their absence.”
— Daśavaikālika Sūtra 8.47, Prakrit line with English rendering as given on tattvagyan.com ('Do not talk ill behind someone's back') [8]
The Daśavaikālika forbids a monk to 'eat the flesh of the back', its idiom for running people down when they are not there, and the tradition treats such talk as cowardly.
Valluvar says tearing a man down behind his back and smiling at his face is worse than open wrongdoing, and the Jain sūtra uses the same 'behind the back' image to ban that kind of talk outright.
Sikhism
“Those who have one thing in their heart, and something else in their mouth, are judged to be false.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid says that those whose words do not match their hearts are counted false.
The Kural judges the man who tears others down behind their backs and then smiles falsely to their faces as worse than an open evildoer; Farid names that same split between heart and mouth as falseness.
Greek Mythology
“For hateful in my eyes, even as the gates of Hades, is that man that hideth one thing in his mind and sayeth another.”
— Homer, Iliad 9.312–313 (A. T. Murray) [10]
Achilles rebuffs Agamemnon’s envoys, declaring that he loathes a man who says one thing and hides another in his heart.
Achilles and Valluvar share a special loathing for the split between the face and the heart.
Philosophy
“But of all forms of injustice, none is more flagrant than that of the hypocrite who, at the very moment when he is most false, makes it his business to appear virtuous.”
— Cicero, De Officiis (On Duties), Book I, §41 (tr. Walter Miller (Loeb, 1913)) [11]
Having said that wrong is done either by force or by fraud and that fraud is the more contemptible, Cicero ranks the hypocrite who wears a mask of goodness while deceiving as the worst wrongdoer of all.
Like Valluvar, Cicero says the false friendly face is worse than open wrongdoing.

Real Life Example

Procopius writes the Secret History
c. 550 CE · Constantinople, Byzantine Empire (now Istanbul, Turkey)
Procopius of Caesarea served as secretary to the general Belisarius and became the official historian of Emperor Justinian's reign, writing the Wars and a work called Buildings that praised Justinian's building projects. At the same time he secretly composed the Anecdota, or Secret History, a fierce attack on Justinian, Empress Theodora, Belisarius and his wife Antonina, saying it contained what he could not write openly for fear of the rulers. It was not published until after his death.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The same author praised his rulers in public and savaged them in private, and the Secret History is now a famous example of hidden disparagement behind a flattering front.
The kural condemns tearing a person down behind his back while showing him a false smile to his face, and Procopius wrote praise for the court in public and invective against it in secret.
