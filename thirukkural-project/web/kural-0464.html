Kural 464

Acting after Due Consideration · Therindhu Seyalvakai · தெரிந்து செயல்வகை

தெளிவி லதனைத் தொடங்கார் இளிவென்னும் ஏதப்பாடு அஞ்சு பவர்.
Thelivi ladhanaith thotangaar ilivennum, Edhappaatu anju bavar.

Meaning

People who are afraid of being shamed by failure will not start anything whose plan and outcome are not clear to them.

Elementary

People who care about their good name don’t start things they don’t fully understand, because failing halfway would make them look foolish.

University

தெளிவு (clarity, certainty) is the key concept: an undertaking is ‘தெளிவு இலது’ when its means, outcome, or one’s own ability to finish it are uncertain. The motive for restraint is இளிவு (humiliation, contempt), described as an ஏதப்பாடு (a harm or calamity). Valluvar treats reputation as a real asset of the ruler, and public failure as damage to it, not just wounded pride. Commentators differ slightly. Parimelazhagar stresses that the doubtful venture ends in ridicule, while modern commentators such as Kalaignar read it more broadly as avoiding dishonourable acts. Both readings rest on the same logic: foreseeable shame is itself a cost to include in deliberation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna warns Arjuna that people will speak of his dishonour forever, and that for one who has been honoured, dishonour is worse than death.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 [4]
Krishna reminds Arjuna that a respected person has a reputation to protect, and losing it is a terrible thing.
Shares the Kural’s view of disgrace as a grave harm that must shape one’s choices.
Christianity
Lest haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all that behold it begin to mock him, Saying, This man began to build, and was not able to finish.
— Luke 14:29–30 (KJV) [5]
Jesus says the builder who starts without checking his resources will be laughed at when the half-built tower stands unfinished.
The very same motive the Kural gives: fear of public ridicule for an unfinished undertaking.
Islam
The Qur’an commands: do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge; hearing, sight and heart will all be questioned.
— Qur’an 17:36 (Sahih International) [6]
Don’t go after something you don’t really understand, because you will be held responsible for how you used your senses and mind.
Forbids acting without clear knowledge, as the Kural does, though the motive is accountability to God rather than fear of shame.
Judaism
Go not forth hastily to strive, lest thou know not what to do in the end thereof, when thy neighbour hath put thee to shame.
— Proverbs 25:8 (Hebrew Bible; KJV rendering) [5]
Don’t rush into a dispute you aren’t sure how to finish, or you’ll end up embarrassed in front of others.
Same logic as the Kural: an unclear beginning leads to shame at the end.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says a deed is not well done if one regrets it afterwards and reaps its fruit weeping; it is well done if one does not regret it and reaps its fruit with joy.
— Dhammapada 67–68 [7]
A good action is one you won’t be sorry for later.
Thinking ahead to avoid later regret and grief is close to the Kural’s concern with avoiding later disgrace.
Jainism
“Dirgha-darsi : Far-sighted.” … “Salajja : Actuated by a sense of shame.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of the thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, 'Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code' (jainworld.com) [8]
Hemacandra's list of the qualities of an ideal Jain layman includes being far-sighted and being moved by a sense of shame, alongside not engaging in a reprehensible occupation.
The Kural's prudent man avoids unclear ventures because he fears disgrace; Hemacandra's householder combines the same foresight with a lively sense of shame.
Sikhism
“Fareed, those deeds which do not bring merit - forget about those deeds. Otherwise, you shall be put to shame, in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 59) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid tells the seeker to drop any undertaking that brings no merit, lest it end in shame.
The Kural says those who fear disgrace will not begin unclear undertakings; Farid likewise steers clear of deeds that lead only to shame.
Greek Mythology
Bellerophon, after his triumphs, tried to ride Pegasus up to Olympus; Pegasus threw him, and he ended his life wandering alone and scorned.
— Pindar, Isthmian 7.44–48; cf. Iliad 6.200–202 [10]
A hero tried something far beyond his reach and ended up disgraced and alone.
An undertaking begun without regard to its real prospects ends in humiliation, as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises: in every affair consider what comes before and what follows, then undertake it; otherwise you will begin eagerly, and when difficulties appear you will give up shamefully.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 29 [11]
Think a project through first, or you’ll start with excitement and quit in embarrassment.
Matches the Kural’s link between lack of clarity at the start and disgrace at the end.

Real Life Example

The warship Vasa sinks on her maiden voyage
10 August 1628 · Stockholm, Sweden
A few weeks before the Swedish royal warship Vasa sailed, Admiral Klas Fleming had thirty men run back and forth across her deck to test her stability, and onlookers could see that she failed the test. The ship sailed anyway, and within minutes of setting out on her first voyage a gust of wind heeled her over, water poured in through the open lower gunports, and she sank in Stockholm harbour in front of a large crowd. Around 30 of those on board died, and the wreck was raised in 1961 and is now displayed in the Vasa Museum.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The most powerful warship in the Baltic became a public humiliation for the Swedish crown because doubts about whether she could sail safely were never resolved before she set out.
The kural warns that those who fear disgrace do not begin a venture whose outcome is unclear, and the Vasa sailed with exactly such an unresolved doubt and ended in public disgrace.
