Kural 94

Charming Utterance · Iniyavai Kooral · இனியவை கூறல்

துன்புறூஉம் துவ்வாமை இல்லாகும் யார்மாட்டும் இன்புறூஉம் இன்சொ லவர்க்கு.
Thunburooum thuvvaamai illaagum yaarmaattum, Inburooum inso lavarkku.

Meaning

People who speak words that make everyone happy will never suffer the pain of poverty.

Elementary

Think of someone at school everybody likes because they're kind to everyone — not just their own friends. When that person needs help, ten people offer. Being warm to everybody builds up a kind of invisible savings account. This kural says people like that don't end up with nothing.

University

The key word is யார்மாட்டும் (yaarmaattum, 'toward anyone at all'), which universalises the obligation and rules out selective charm directed at the useful. துவ்வாமை (thuvvaamai) is a striking choice: it derives from துவ்வு (to eat/enjoy) and so denotes destitution specifically as the inability to consume or enjoy — want at the level of the body. Valluvar claims that the person who gives everyone the enjoyment (இன்புறூஉம், inburooum) of a kind word will not suffer the want of enjoyment. Commentators from Parimelazhagar onward have read this both prudentially (goodwill converts into material support) and karmically (the merit of universal kindness bears fruit). The verse also uses the elongated alapedai forms துன்புறூஉம் / இன்புறூஉம் to set the two states of misery and delight in exact metrical opposition.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A widely transmitted Sanskrit maxim states that all creatures are gratified by the giving of pleasing words, and therefore one should speak only in that way — asking, pointedly, 'where is the poverty in words?'
— Subhāṣita, attributed to Chāṇakya Nīti 16.13 (also collected in the Subhāṣita Ratna Bhāṇḍāgāra) [4]
The verse notes that kind speech is the one currency that never runs out, so there can be no economic excuse for withholding it.
It joins kind speech directly to the vocabulary of poverty (daridratā), exactly as the Kural joins insol to thuvvaamai.
Christianity
“He that loveth pureness of heart, for the grace of his lips the king shall be his friend.”
— Proverbs 22:11 (King James Version) [5]
Proverbs links a pure heart and gracious speech to concrete advancement — securing the favour of the most powerful patron available.
It makes the Kural's prudential claim that gracious speech protects a person from destitution.
Islam
The Prophet taught that every good word is itself an act of charity (sadaqah), placing speech within the same economy of giving and reward as almsgiving.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2989 (Book of Jihad, chapter on charity) [6]
The hadith reclassifies speech as a transferable good, so the person with nothing to give materially is never actually empty-handed.
It supports the Kural's link between kind speech and freedom from want by making the kind word a form of wealth.
Judaism
“Sweet language will multiply friends: and a fairspeaking tongue will increase kind greetings.”
— Ben Sira / Ecclesiasticus 6:5 (King James Version, Apocrypha) [7]
Ben Sira presents pleasant speech as an investment that compounds: it generates a widening circle of allies and reciprocal goodwill.
This is the practical mechanism behind the Kural's promise that the kind speaker will not fall into destitution.
Buddhism
The Maṅgala Sutta, the Buddha's catalogue of the highest blessings (maṅgala) available in a human life, lists well-spoken speech alongside vast learning, mastered discipline and skilled craft as among the things that make a life auspicious and secure.
— Maṅgala Sutta, Sutta Nipāta 2.4 [8]
Pleasant, well-formed speech is counted as an actual component of worldly good fortune, not merely of moral character.
It matches the Kural's claim that kind speech produces the good fortune that keeps misery-bringing want at bay.
Jainism
“I forgive all living beings and may all living beings forgive me; I cherish feelings of friendship towards all and I harbour enmity towards none.”
— Saman Suttam v. 86 (English tr. as published on jainworld.com) [9]
This well-known Jain formula of mutual forgiveness and friendship toward all beings is recited at the close of Paryuṣaṇa. It extends goodwill to every creature without exception.
Valluvar's speaker gladdens everyone he meets, whoever they are, and the Jain vow of friendship toward all beings expresses the same universal goodwill.
Sikhism
“A mild spoken, humble and giver through his hands moves in equipoise and remains happy.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 12, Pauri 16 (English translation as given on BaniDB / searchgurbani.com) [10]
Bhai Gurdas holds that the person who speaks gently, walks humbly and gives freely lives in calm contentment.
Like the Kural's promise that those who speak gladdening words to all will escape misery, it ties mild speech to a life free of distress.
Greek Mythology
“...they pour sweet dew upon his tongue, and from his lips flow gracious words.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 81–84 (trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod describes the Muses' gift to a king they favour at birth: his gracious speech settles disputes, and in consequence the whole people look to him and he is conspicuous in the assembly.
It ties honeyed speech directly to standing and security, the same causal link the Kural draws.
Philosophy
“Acquire the Reputation of Courtesy; for it is enough to make you liked. Politeness is the main ingredient of culture,—a kind of witchery that wins the regard of all as surely as discourtesy gains their disfavour and opposition...”
— Baltasar Gracián, The Art of Worldly Wisdom, §118 (trans. Joseph Jacobs, 1892) [12]
Gracián, writing a manual of worldly survival for the seventeenth-century court, treats courtesy as the cheapest and most reliable instrument of advantage available to anyone.
It is the Kural's prudential argument stated frankly: kind speech is what keeps a person from being left with nothing.

Real Life Example

Fred Rogers testifies to the US Senate for public television
1 May 1969 · Washington, D.C., United States
When President Nixon proposed cutting the planned 20 million dollar budget of the new Corporation for Public Broadcasting by half, the children's television host Fred Rogers testified before the Senate Communications Subcommittee. In a calm, gentle statement he described how his programme dealt with the inner drama of childhood and recited the words of one of his songs. The subcommittee chairman, Senator John Pastore, said the testimony had given him goosebumps and that Rogers appeared to have earned the 20 million dollars.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Rogers won over a sceptical senator through gentle, sincere speech rather than argument or pressure, helping to protect the funding his work depended on.
The kural promises that those who speak words that gladden everyone will not be overtaken by want, and Rogers's kindly words helped secure the support public broadcasting needed.
