Kural 673

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

ஒல்லும்வா யெல்லாம் வினைநன்றே ஒல்லாக்கால் செல்லும்வாய் நோக்கிச் செயல்.
Ollumvaai ellaam vinai nandre ollaakkaal Sellumvaai nokkich cheyal.

Meaning

Whenever you can act directly, that is best. When you cannot, find another way that will work and do it that way.

Elementary

If the front door is open, you walk straight in. If it is locked, you don't give up; you go round to the back door or ask someone for the key. Pick the way that actually works.

University

Ollum vaai means 'where it is possible (feasible)', and sellum vaai 'the way that will go through, the effective route'. Parimelazhagar reads vinai here in its political sense of open action against an enemy, that is, danda (force), the last of the four classical expedients (sama, dana, bheda, danda); where force can succeed it is best, and where it cannot one must choose among the remaining expedients. Solomon Pappaiah follows this reading explicitly, while Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read the couplet more generally as 'do the work directly where you can, otherwise find a workable way'. The older sandhi text reads வினைநன்றே யொல்லாக்காற் with no difference of meaning. The teaching is pragmatic flexibility of means within a fixed end, a theme shared with the Arthashastra and later Machiavelli.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu teaches that among the four expedients, conciliation, gifts, division and force, the wise commend conciliation and force for the prosperity of a kingdom, choosing the means that suit the situation.
— Manusmriti 7.109 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
The law code gives the king a menu of methods and expects him to pick whichever will actually work.
Parimelazhagar reads this Kural within the same fourfold scheme of upayas: use force where it can succeed, otherwise another means.
Christianity
“To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.”
— 1 Corinthians 9:22 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes changing his approach for each audience so that his mission succeeds by whatever route is open.
Both texts hold the goal fixed and treat the method as flexible, choosing whatever way will get through.
Islam
“So, if I forbid you to do something, then keep away from it. And if I order you to do something, then do of it as much as you can.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7288 [5]
The Prophet ties duty to capacity: do what is possible, to the extent it is possible.
The hadith shares the Kural's ollum vaai logic: act fully wherever you are able.
Judaism
Before meeting his estranged brother Esau, Jacob prepares on three fronts at once: a gift to appease him, prayer to God, and a plan for battle, dividing his camp so that one part could escape.
— Genesis 32:8-9 with Rashi's commentary [6]
Rashi draws from the verses that Jacob readied himself for gifts, prayer and war, so that if one means failed another would be at hand.
Jacob embodies the Kural's teaching: prepare the direct way, and have other workable routes ready when it will not serve.
Buddhism
In the parable of the burning house, a father cannot get his playing children out by simply calling them, so he promises them wonderful carts outside, and they run out to safety.
— Lotus Sutra, chapter 3 (parable of the burning house) [7]
The Lotus Sutra uses this story to explain upaya, skilful means: when the direct way fails, a teacher finds another route that works.
The father does exactly what the Kural says: when the direct approach is not possible, look for the way that gets through.
Jainism
In the meeting of Kesi and Gautama, the followers of Parshvanatha (four vows) and of Mahavira (five vows) ask why two Jinas pursuing the same goal prescribed different forms of the Law. Gautama explains that the form was adapted to the people of each age, the earliest of whom could only with difficulty understand the precepts and the latest only with difficulty observe them.
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 23 (Kesi and Gautama), esp. 23.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jain tradition holds that the same end may be reached by different prescribed means, chosen according to what will actually work for those concerned.
The kural's advice to act directly where possible and otherwise to find a route that will work has a loose echo in this Jain acceptance of adapting means to circumstances.
Sikhism
In the Zafarnama, the Persian letter Guru Gobind Singh sent to the emperor Aurangzeb in 1705, verse 22 states the principle that when every other means of redressing a wrong has failed, it is lawful and just to take up the sword.
— Guru Gobind Singh, Zafarnama, verse 22 (Dasam Granth), as summarised in Wikipedia, Zafarnama (letter) [9]
The verse, borrowed almost word for word from Saadi, became a classic summary of the Sikh idea of righteous struggle (dharam yudh): force is legitimate only after other means have been tried and have failed.
The Kural teaches choosing the means that will actually work, direct action where possible and another route where not; the Zafarnama also grades the means, but in the reverse order, putting force last.
Greek Mythology
After ten years of failed assaults on Troy, the Greeks, on Odysseus' plan, build the wooden horse, and the city falls by stratagem where force had not succeeded.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.492-520 [10]
The bard Demodocus sings of the horse at the Phaeacian court, celebrating Odysseus' cunning.
When the direct way (siege) proved impossible, the Greeks looked for the route that would get through, just as the Kural advises.
Philosophy
Machiavelli says a prince must know how to fight both by law and by force, and must imitate both the lion and the fox, since the lion cannot avoid traps and the fox cannot drive off wolves.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 18 [11]
He argues that a ruler needs more than one way of acting and must switch according to what the situation allows.
The Kural likewise holds that where one means (force) will not work, the wise agent turns to another.

Real Life Example

The Berlin Airlift supplies a blockaded city by air
June 1948 - September 1949 · Berlin, Germany
Beginning on 24 June 1948, Soviet forces blocked rail, road and water access to the western sectors of Berlin, home to about two million people. Lacking the ground forces to break through, the United States and Britain flew in food, fuel and other supplies from airbases in western Germany, starting on 26 June 1948 and eventually landing aircraft every minute or so at the peak. The Soviets lifted the blockade in May 1949, and the airlift, which delivered about 2.3 million tons of cargo, continued until 30 September 1949.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Instead of forcing the land routes, which risked war, the Western allies found another route that worked, and the city was kept alive without a shot being fired.
The kural says direct action is best where it is possible, and where it is not, one should find a route that will work, and the Berlin Airlift replaced a blocked road with a highway in the sky.
