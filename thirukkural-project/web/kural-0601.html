Kural 601

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

குடியென்னும் குன்றா விளக்கம் மடியென்னும் மாசூர மாய்ந்து கெடும்.
Kudiyennum kundraa vilakkam madiyennum Maasoora maaindhu kedum.

Meaning

A family is like a lamp that should never go out. But when the dirt of laziness builds up on it, its light fades and dies.

Elementary

Think of a lamp that has shone in your home for years. If nobody cleans the soot off the glass, the light gets dimmer and dimmer until the room is dark. Laziness is that soot: a family stays bright only if its people keep working and caring.

University

The opening couplet sets up the chapter’s governing image: குடி (family, lineage) is a குன்றா விளக்கம், a lamp or radiance that of itself would never diminish, and மடி (sloth) is the மாசு, the grime or darkness that ‘spreads over’ (ஊர) it until it dims (மாய்ந்து) and perishes (கெடும்). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read மாசு as a film of dirt that accumulates by degrees, whereas Solomon Pappaiah and the Rev. Drew/Pope tradition render it as darkness; either way the point is that decline is gradual and self-inflicted rather than sudden and external. The chapter heading மடியின்மை (‘absence of sloth’) follows ஊக்கமுடைமை (ch. 60, energy) and precedes ஆள்வினையுடைமை (ch. 62, manly effort), so Valluvar moves from the positive virtue to the vice that undoes it before turning to sustained effort. The text agrees across ytamizh.com and thirukkural-thamizh.org; the latter prints the older sandhi-joined form (விளக்க மடியென்னு / மாசூர) with no change of wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Arjun, tamo guṇa, which is born of ignorance, is the cause of illusion for the embodied souls. It deludes all living beings through negligence, laziness, and sleep.”
— Bhagavad Gita 14.8 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna names tamas, literally ‘darkness’, as the quality that binds people through negligence (pramāda), laziness (ālasya) and sleep (nidrā).
Tamas as a darkness born of laziness is almost exactly the Kural’s image of sloth as the grime that dims the family lamp.
Christianity
“While the bridegroom tarried, they all slumbered and slept. … And the foolish said unto the wise, Give us of your oil; for our lamps are gone out.”
— Matthew 25:5, 8 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the ten virgins, the careless ones let their lamps go out and are shut out of the wedding feast.
A lamp that goes out through neglect is the same picture as the Kural’s family light dying under sloth.
Islam
“O Allah! I seek refuge with you (Allah) from (worries) care and grief, from incapacity and laziness, from miserliness and cowardice, from being heavily in debt and from being overpowered by other men.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6363 [5]
Anas reports that the Prophet prayed this often, listing laziness (kasal) among the things a believer should seek God’s protection from.
Treating laziness as a danger to be warded off matches the Kural’s view of sloth as the stain that ruins a household.
Judaism
“Through slothfulness the ceiling sags, / Through lazy hands the house caves in.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:18 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet notes that a house does not collapse all at once; it sags and leaks gradually when its owners are idle.
The slowly failing house is the Hebrew counterpart of the Kural’s slowly dimming family lamp.
Buddhism
In the Saṅgārava Sutta, the Buddha compares a mind overcome by sloth and torpor (thīna-middha) to a bowl of water covered with moss and water plants, in which a man cannot see the reflection of his own face.
— Saṃyutta Nikāya 46.55 (Saṅgārava Sutta) [7]
Sloth and torpor is one of the five hindrances; like a film over water, it hides what should be clearly seen, including one’s own good.
The Kural’s மாசு (grime) spreading over a lamp and the Buddha’s moss spreading over clear water are the same image of sloth as a covering film.
Jainism
“Wealth will not protect a careless man in this world and the next. Though he had seen the right way, he does not see it, even as one in the dark whose lamp has suddenly been put out.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira's sermon on carelessness (pramāda) says that a negligent person loses even the light he once had, like a man whose lamp suddenly goes out in the dark.
Valluvar's family-lamp that sloth dims and puts out has a close counterpart in the Jain image of the careless man whose lamp is put out.
Sikhism
“The self-willed manmukh is very lazy; he is trapped in the wilderness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1011, Raag Maaroo, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak names laziness as a mark of the manmukh, the self-willed person, and says it leaves him lost and trapped.
The Kural says the grime of laziness dims and puts out the lamp of a family; Guru Nanak likewise shows sloth leading to ruin, though of a person's spiritual life rather than a family's standing.
Greek Mythology
At the start of the Odyssey, the house of Odysseus is being eaten up by the suitors while young Telemachus sits passive, until Athena, disguised as Mentes, rouses him to act for his father’s house.
— Homer, Odyssey, Books 1-2 (trans. S. Butler) [10]
The great house is wasting away not because of an outside attack but because its heir has not yet stirred himself.
Like the Kural’s dimming lamp, a noble house fades while its heir stays inactive and revives only when he rouses himself.
Philosophy
In The Way to Wealth, Benjamin Franklin’s Poor Richard warns that sloth, like rust, eats away faster than work wears things out, while a key that is used stays bright.
— Benjamin Franklin, The Way to Wealth (1758) [11]
Franklin argues that idleness wears things away more than work does, and that constant use keeps things bright.
Franklin’s rust on an unused key and the Kural’s grime on a neglected lamp are the same image of sloth tarnishing what should shine.

Real Life Example

The Merovingian 'do-nothing kings' fade before the mayors of the palace
c. 639-751 · Frankish kingdom (now France, Belgium and western Germany)
After King Dagobert I died in 639, the later Merovingian kings of the Franks came to be called rois faineants, do-nothing kings, because real authority passed step by step to the mayors of the palace who governed in their name. By the 740s the mayors were placing Merovingians such as Childeric III on the throne only to keep up the appearance of royal rule. In 751 the mayor Pippin, with papal approval, had himself made king, and Childeric was deposed, tonsured and confined to the monastery of Sithiu near Saint-Omer.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The dynasty that had founded the Frankish kingdom did not fall in a single blow; its authority dimmed over about a century until it could be removed without a struggle.
The kural pictures a family as a lamp that grows dim as the grime of sloth settles on it, and the Merovingian line faded in exactly this gradual way until its light went out.
