Kural 1218

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

துஞ்சுங்கால் தோள்மேலர் ஆகி விழிக்குங்கால் நெஞ்சத்தர் ஆவர் விரைந்து.
Thunjungaal thoalmelar aaki vizhikkungaal nenjaththar aavar viraindhu.

Meaning

When I sleep, he rests on my shoulders. The moment I wake up, he quickly slips back into my heart.

Elementary

Think of a kitten that naps on your shoulder, and when you sit up it darts inside your jacket to stay warm. The husband in this poem is like that: in sleep he is right beside her, and when she wakes up he hurries to hide in her heart, so he is never really gone.

University

This is the chapter’s most celebrated couplet for its grace. Tuñcuṅkāl / viḻikkuṅkāl (‘when I sleep’ / ‘when I wake’) frame a perfect parallel, and tōḷmēlar (‘he who is on my shoulders’) answers neñcattar (‘he who is in my heart’), so that the beloved merely changes residence and is never absent. Viraintu, ‘swiftly’, gives the image a playful quickness, as if he darts inward at the first stir of waking. Pappaiah reads it as ‘my lord, who always lives in my heart, lies on my shoulder when I sleep’, while Kalaignar stresses that on waking ‘he went nowhere, but leapt into my heart and settled there’. The couplet softens the reproach of 1217 into consolation. Pope: ‘And when I sleep he holds my form embraced; / And when I wake to fill my heart makes haste!’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna sends word to the gopis of Vraja through Uddhava that he is never really separated from them, and that he stays far away so that their minds may dwell on him all the more constantly.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana teaches that the absent beloved is held all the closer within the heart of the one who longs.
The Kural’s husband who ‘hastens into the heart’ on waking is Krishna’s message in miniature: absence becomes indwelling.
Christianity
“His left hand is under my head, and his right hand doth embrace me.”
— Song of Solomon 2:6 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song of Solomon pictures her beloved holding her as she rests.
This is the first half of the Kural’s picture, the beloved resting close as she sleeps; the Kural adds that on waking he moves into her heart.
Islam
“And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein.”
— Qur’an 50:16 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that God, though unseen, is nearer to a person than his own life-vein.
The Kural’s beloved, unseen by day yet lodged within the heart, is near in this same intimate, invisible way.
Judaism
“Set me as a seal upon thy heart, As a seal upon thine arm; For love is strong as death.”
— Song of Songs 8:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Shulammite asks to be impressed on her beloved’s heart and arm, so that she is never separate from him.
Heart and arm in the Song match heart and shoulder in the Kural: the beloved is borne on the body and sealed within.
Buddhism
In the Dvedhavitakka discourse the Buddha teaches that whatever one frequently thinks and ponders becomes the inclination of one’s mind.
— Majjhima Nikaya 19, Dvedhavitakka Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Buddhism observes that sustained attention shapes what the mind turns to of its own accord.
The Kural’s beloved leaps into the heart the moment she wakes because, as the Buddha notes, the mind turns by habit to what it dwells on.
Jainism
When Neminatha abandons his wedding procession to renounce the world, his bride Rajimati, keeping him in her heart, refuses another match and herself takes vows, following him on the path.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22, Rathanemi (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain narrative tells of a bride whose devotion to an absent bridegroom becomes the guiding presence of her life.
Rajimati, like the Kural’s heroine, carries the departed beloved within; Jainism turns that inner presence toward renunciation.
Sikhism
“When I look within my being, I find that my Beloved is with me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1095, Raag Maaroo, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan's bride-soul discovers that the Beloved she longs for is not far off but present inside her.
The Kural's wife says her husband rests on her shoulders while she sleeps and slips into her heart the moment she wakes; Guru Arjan likewise finds the Beloved dwelling within, never truly absent.
Greek Mythology
In Plato’s myth of the winged soul, the lover parted from the beloved is in anguish, but the memory of the beloved’s beauty, held within, gives him joy, and he longs to return to his side.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-252a (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
The Phaedrus makes the beloved’s image, kept in the soul, the lover’s consolation in absence.
The Kural’s beloved who moves from shoulder to heart is the same inward image that sustains Plato’s lover between meetings.
Philosophy
Aristotle compares memory to the impression a signet-ring leaves in wax, a lasting imprint of what was once perceived.
— Aristotle, On Memory (De Memoria) 450a, Parva Naturalia (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, those we have known leave a stamp in the soul that remains after they are gone.
The beloved who ‘hastens into the heart’ the moment she wakes is such an imprint, always present to recall.

Real Life Example

Henri Rousseau paints The Dream for his long-lost Yadwigha
1910 · Paris, France
Henri Rousseau's last painting, The Dream, was shown at the Salon des Indépendants in Paris in 1910, a few months before his death. It shows a woman reclining on a red sofa in a dense jungle among animals and a flute-playing snake charmer. Rousseau explained that the sleeping woman dreams she has been carried into the forest, and he attached a poem addressed to Yadwigha, a Polish woman he had loved many years before; the painting is now in the Museum of Modern Art, New York.
— Sources: [12] [13]
At the end of his life Rousseau placed a love from his past inside a dream, where she still lived for him.
The kural says the beloved rests on the shoulders in sleep and slips into the heart on waking, never truly absent; Rousseau kept his Yadwigha present in a dream picture decades after she was gone from his life.
