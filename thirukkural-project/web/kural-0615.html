Kural 615

Manly Effort · Aalvinaiyudaimai · ஆள்வினையுடைமை

இன்பம் விழையான் வினைவிழைவான் தன்கேளிர் துன்பம் துடைத்தூன்றும் தூண்.
Inbam vizhaiyaan vinaivizhaivaan thankelir Thunbam thudaiththoondrum thoon.

Meaning

A person who wants to work more than they want fun becomes like a pillar for their family. They wipe away the family's troubles and hold them up.

Elementary

A house stands because of its pillars. They never get to go out and play; they just hold up the roof so everyone inside is safe. A person who chooses to work hard instead of just having fun becomes a pillar like that for their family.

University

The couplet contrasts two desires: இன்பம் விழையான் (one who does not crave his own pleasure) and வினை விழைவான் (one who craves the work). Such a man is a தூண் (pillar) that both துடைத்து (wipes away) and ஊன்றும் (props up) the sorrows of his கேளிர், his kin; Solomon Pappaiah widens கேளிர் to ‘relatives and friends’ and Kalaignar extends it to ‘the people of the country’, a modern civic reading. Parimelazhagar notes that the pillar image implies a household or lineage that would collapse without such a person. The couplet echoes the Arathuppal ideal of the householder (இல்வாழ்க்கை) as the support of all dependants, now reframed as a fruit of personal effort. Both editions agree on the wording, with sandhi-joined forms (வினைவிழைவான் றன்கேளிர்; துன்பந் துடைத்தூன்றுந் தூண்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna points to King Janaka and others who reached perfection through action, and urges Arjuna to work for lokasangraha, the holding-together and welfare of the world, rather than for himself.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.20 (Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Bhagavad Gita 3.20 presents selfless work as the means by which the wise sustain society.
Work done for others’ sake, not one’s own pleasure, is the Kural’s pillar that holds up the kin.
Christianity
“But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.”
— 1 Timothy 5:8 (KJV) [4]
The pastoral letter makes providing for one’s household a basic duty of faith.
Both texts make supporting one’s kin a central fruit of a working life.
Islam
“The upper hand is better than the lower hand (i.e. he who gives in charity is better than him who takes it). One should start giving first to his dependents.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1427 (Sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet ranks the giver above the receiver and says generosity begins with those one supports.
The Kural’s pillar is exactly this ‘upper hand’ that first sustains its own dependants.
Judaism
Joseph, having risen through years of labour in Egypt, tells his brothers he will sustain them in the famine, and then provides bread for his father, his brothers and all their households.
— Genesis 45:11 and 47:12 [6]
Genesis 45 and 47 show Joseph using his position to become the provider for his whole family.
Joseph is the Kural’s pillar who wipes away his kin’s distress and holds them up.
Buddhism
“But he who does not regard cold or heat any more than a blade of grass and who does his duties manfully, does not fall away from happiness.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha tells Sigala that the one who ignores comfort and discomfort alike and does his work well keeps his prosperity, and elsewhere lists supporting one’s parents as a son’s first duty.
Setting aside personal comfort to do one’s duties is the Kural’s man who desires work, not pleasure.
Jainism
“During his reign he taught, for the benefit of the people, the seventy-two sciences, of which writing is the first, arithmetic the most important, and the knowledge of omens the last, the sixty-four accomplishments of women, the hundred arts, and the three occupations of men.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Rishabha §211 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Jain tradition honours Rishabha, the first Tirthankara and first king, for teaching people the crafts and occupations that let them make a living, before he renounced the world.
The Kural's worker who wants work over pleasure and becomes a pillar holding up his kin is mirrored, on a civilisational scale, by Rishabha's labours that held up his people.
Sikhism
“One who performs selfless service, without thought of reward, shall attain his Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 286, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani Sahib), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan praises seva done for its own sake, with no thought of reward for oneself.
The Kural praises the one who craves the work rather than his own pleasure and so holds up his kin; Guru Arjan praises service done with no thought of reward for oneself.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads the young Heracles meets two women: Vice offers him the easy road of pleasure, and Virtue the hard road of toil that brings real honour. He chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus, The Choice of Heracles, in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 [10]
Prodicus’ allegory, retold by Xenophon, made Heracles the model of choosing labour over ease.
‘Not desiring pleasure but desiring work’ is exactly the choice of Heracles.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius tells himself that when he is reluctant to rise at dawn he should remember he is rising to do the work of a human being, and asks whether he was made to lie warm under the blankets for his own pleasure.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1 (trans. George Long) [11]
The emperor reminds himself that he exists for action and service, not for comfort.
Marcus chooses work over pleasure for the sake of others, as the Kural’s pillar does.

Real Life Example

Maria Sklodowska works as a governess to pay for her sister's medical studies
c. 1885-1891 · Poland (then under Russian rule) and Paris, France
As a teenager in Warsaw, Maria Sklodowska, later Marie Curie, made a pact with her elder sister Bronya: Maria would work to support Bronya through medical school in Paris, and Bronya would later help pay for Maria's studies. Maria worked for several years as a governess and tutor, sending money to her sister while studying on her own in her spare time. In 1891 she went to Paris, lived at first in Bronya's home and enrolled at the Sorbonne.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By putting aside her own ambitions for years and working for her sister, the future double Nobel laureate became the support on which her family's hopes rested.
The kural says one who prefers work to personal pleasure becomes a pillar who wipes away the troubles of kin and holds them up, and Maria's years as a governess held up her sister's future and then her own.
