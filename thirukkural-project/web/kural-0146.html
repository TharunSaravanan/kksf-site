Kural 146

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

பகைபாவம் அச்சம் பழியென நான்கும் இகவாவாம் இல்லிறப்பான் கண்.
Pagaipaavam achcham pazhiyena naangum, Igavaavaam illirappaan kan.

Meaning

Enemies, sin, fear and shame: these four never leave a man who wrongs another man’s home.

Elementary

Picture someone who takes a thing that belongs to another family. Now four shadows follow them everywhere: people are angry at them, they know they did wrong, they are always scared of being caught, and others think badly of them. This kural says those four shadows never go away.

University

This couplet is a compact catalogue: பகை (enmity from the wronged husband and his kin), பாவம் (sin, the moral or karmic stain), அச்சம் (constant fear of discovery and revenge), and பழி (public blame). The verb இகவா ('they do not depart') makes all four permanent companions. The list covers the social, spiritual, psychological, and reputational costs of the act, a strikingly complete analysis for two lines. Its closest parallel is Dhammapada 309, which also names exactly four misfortunes for the man who goes to another's wife, a resemblance that scholars have noted when discussing shared ethical currents in early South Asian literature.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to the hell of self-destruction for the soul—lust, anger, and greed. Therefore, one should abandon all three.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna names lust first among the three gateways that lead a person to self-destruction.
The Kural lists four evils that follow lust for another's wife; the Gita names lust itself as a gate to ruin.
Christianity
“That no man go beyond and defraud his brother in any matter: because that the Lord is the avenger of all such, as we also have forewarned you and testified.”
— 1 Thessalonians 4:6 (KJV) [4]
In a passage about sexual purity, Paul warns that anyone who wrongs his brother in this way will face God as avenger.
Both texts say the wrongdoer cannot escape consequences; Paul's 'avenger' corresponds to the Kural's enmity and fear.
Islam
“When an adulterer commits illegal sexual intercourse, then he is not a believer at the time, he is doing it”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2475 [5]
The Prophet taught that at the moment of committing adultery, the person's faith leaves him.
The Kural lists sin among the four things that cling to the adulterer; this hadith shows that sin as the loss of faith itself.
Judaism
“A wound and dishonour shall he get; and his reproach shall not be wiped away. For jealousy is the rage of a man: therefore he will not spare in the day of vengeance. He will not regard any ransom; neither will he rest content, though thou givest many gifts.”
— Proverbs 6:33-35 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs describes what follows adultery: injury, dishonour, lasting reproach, and a husband's jealous fury that no payment can calm.
Nearly the Kural's list: enmity (the husband's rage), fear (no ransom will help), and disgrace (dishonour and reproach).
Buddhism
“Four misfortunes befall the reckless man who consorts with another's wife: acquisition of demerit, disturbed sleep, ill-repute, and (rebirth in) states of woe.”
— Dhammapada 309 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha lists four misfortunes for the man who goes to another man's wife: bad karma, sleepless nights, a bad name, and a painful rebirth.
An almost exact match: four evils that cling to the man, including sin (demerit), fear (disturbed sleep), and disgrace (ill-repute).
Jainism
“Virtue, praise, friendship, greatness, all these four draw not anigh the men who covet their neighbour's wife. Hatred, disgrace and guilt, with fear, these four possessions abide with men who covet their neighbour's wife.”
— Nālaṭiyār 82 (ch. 9, 'The Not Desiring Other Men's Wives'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [7]
In its chapter on not desiring other men's wives, the Nālaṭiyār lists the four things the adulterer gains: hatred, disgrace, guilt and fear.
Valluvar names enmity, sin, fear and disgrace as the four things that never leave the man who wrongs another's home, and the Nālaṭiyār names the same four (பகை, பழி, பாவம், அச்சம்) in almost the same Tamil words.
Sikhism
“Caught in slander and attachment to the wealth and women of others, they eat poison and suffer in pain.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1255, Raag Malar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak describes those drawn to others' wealth, others' women and slander as eating poison and living in pain.
The Kural lists enmity, sin, fear and disgrace as companions that never leave the adulterer; Guru Nanak likewise says this desire brings lasting suffering.
Greek Mythology
“Alike with him who does wrong to a suppliant or a guest, or who goes up to his brother's bed and commits unnatural sin in lying with his wife, ... truly Zeus himself is angry, and at the last lays on him a heavy requittal for his evil doing.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days, ll. 327-334 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod warns that a man who lies with his brother's wife earns the anger of Zeus and a heavy punishment in the end.
Hesiod names the enmity of the gods and the sin and punishment that follow; the Kural adds fear and public disgrace to the list.
Philosophy
“It is just as if the wasps complained because no man cares for them, but all fly from them, and if a man can, he strikes them and knocks them down. You have such a sting that you throw into trouble and pain any man that you wound with it.”
— Epictetus, Discourses II.4 (trans. George Long) [10]
Epictetus tells the adulterer that he is like a wasp: everyone avoids him, and anyone who can will strike him down.
This captures the Kural's enmity, fear, and disgrace: the adulterer is shunned, hated, and always in danger.

Real Life Example

Alexander Hamilton publishes the Reynolds Pamphlet
1791-1797 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
In 1791, while serving as the first US Secretary of the Treasury, Alexander Hamilton began an affair with Maria Reynolds, a married woman. Her husband, James Reynolds, then demanded money to keep quiet, and Hamilton paid him, including $1,000 in two instalments by January 1792. When a journalist accused him in 1797 of illegal speculation with Reynolds, Hamilton published a pamphlet confessing the affair and the blackmail in order to clear himself of financial wrongdoing; the revelation humiliated his family and ended any hope he had of becoming president.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Hamilton cleared his name of corruption only by publicly confessing a private wrong, and the affair shadowed the rest of his career.
The kural names enmity, sin, fear and disgrace as the four companions of the man who transgresses against another's home; Hamilton met all four, from the husband's hostility and years of fear of exposure to lasting public disgrace.
