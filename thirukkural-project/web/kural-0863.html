Kural 863

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

அஞ்சும் அறியான் அமைவிலன் ஈகலான் தஞ்சம் எளியன் பகைக்கு.
Anjum ariyaan amaivilan eekalaan thanjam eliyan pakaikku.

Meaning

A person who is fearful, does not know what they should know, cannot get along with others, and will not give is very easy for enemies to beat.

Elementary

Picture a kid who is scared of everything, doesn’t learn the rules of the game, won’t cooperate with anyone, and never shares. When trouble comes, nobody stands with him and he doesn’t know what to do. Being brave, learning, getting along and sharing are what make you strong.

University

Four faults are stacked in quick succession: anjum (அஞ்சும், he who fears, which Pappaiah glosses as fearing what need not be feared), ariyaan (அறியான், one who does not know what should be known), amaivilan (அமைவிலன், lacking accord or adaptability with others) and eekalaan (ஈகலான், one who does not give). Thanjam (தஞ்சம்) is read by the commentators as an intensifier, ‘very easily’ (Varadarasanar: மிக எளியவன்). Parimelazhagar’s logic is cumulative: fear loses battles, ignorance loses policy, unsociability loses allies and stinginess loses the army, so the foe has little left to overcome. Kalaignar renders amaivilan as lacking the habit of living in harmony and eekalaan as lacking compassion. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the opening of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna rebukes the despondent Arjuna, urging him not to yield to unmanliness and to cast off his petty faint-heartedness and rise.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.2-3 [3]
Krishna treats Arjuna’s fear and confusion on the battlefield as a weakness that must be overcome before anything else.
Fear and not-knowing, the first two faults in the Kural, are exactly what Krishna addresses.
Christianity
Paul tells Timothy that God has not given a spirit of fear, but of power, love and a sound mind.
— 2 Timothy 1:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul sets fear against three positive qualities that a leader of the church should have.
The Kural’s fearful, unloving, ignorant man is the opposite of Paul’s ideal of power, love and a sound mind.
Islam
“O Allah! I seek refuge with You from miserliness; and seek refuge with You from cowardice; and seek refuge with You from being sent back to geriatric old age...”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6365 (Book of Invocations) [5]
The Prophet taught a supplication that names miserliness and cowardice side by side as evils to be protected from.
Two of the Kural’s four faults, fear and not giving, are paired in the same breath.
Judaism
Before battle, the officers of Israel are told to send home any man who is fearful and fainthearted, lest his brothers’ hearts melt like his.
— Deuteronomy 20:8 (Tanakh) [6]
Deuteronomy treats fear as contagious and dangerous to the whole army.
The Kural places fear first among the faults that make one easy prey.
Buddhism
The Buddha names four bases of social solidarity (generosity, kind speech, beneficial help and impartiality) and says they hold the world together like the linchpin of a moving chariot.
— Sangaha Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 4.32 [7]
Without these qualities, the Buddha says, people would not respect or support one another.
The Kural’s ungenerous, unaccommodating man lacks exactly these bonds, which is why he stands alone.
Jainism
“Weak men who are unable (to bear) these insults in villages or towns, become disheartened like cowards in the battle.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.3.1.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In its chapter on the hardships of monastic life, the Sūtrakṛtāṅga compares the faint-hearted novice, who breaks under insult and hardship, to a coward who loses heart once the battle begins.
The Kural calls the fearful man easy prey for his enemies, and the Jain verse likewise shows timidity defeating a man before any real foe has to strike.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else - says Nanak, listen, mind: call him spiritually wise.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 16), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur names fearlessness, joined to never making others afraid, as the mark of the spiritually wise.
The Kural says the man who is fearful and ignorant is easy prey for enemies; Guru Tegh Bahadur makes the opposite, fearlessness joined to wisdom, the ideal.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Paris shrinks back into the ranks in fear when Menelaus steps forward, and Hector rebukes him for his cowardice.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 3 [10]
Homer shows how a prince’s fear shames him before both armies.
Paris embodies the first fault of the Kural: the fearful man who cannot face his foe.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes courage as the mean between cowardice and rashness, and liberality as the mean between meanness and prodigality.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.6-9 and IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Cowardice and meanness are, for Aristotle, vices that undermine a person’s character and standing.
Two of Valluvar’s four faults appear in Aristotle’s list of vices.

Real Life Example

Ethelred the Unready flees to Normandy
1013 · England
Ethelred II, whose byname Unready meant ill-advised or lacking counsel, failed to keep the loyalty of many of his subjects and tried to protect England from Viking raids by paying the Danes large sums of tribute known as the Danegeld. The payments did not stop the raids, and by 1012 some 48,000 pounds of silver was being paid to Danes camped in London. In 1013 King Sweyn of Denmark invaded, was accepted as king, and Ethelred fled to Normandy.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A king remembered for poor counsel, weak military leadership and the loss of his subjects' allegiance was easily displaced by a determined foe.
The kural lists fear, lack of understanding, inability to keep others in accord and failure to give as the marks of one who is easy prey, and Ethelred's reign shows several of these at once.
