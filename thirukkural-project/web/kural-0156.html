Kural 156

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

ஒறுத்தார்க்கு ஒருநாளை இன்பம் பொறுத்தார்க்குப் பொன்றுந் துணையும் புகழ்.
Oruththaarkku orunaalai inbam poruththaarkkup, Pondrun thunaiyum pugazh.

Meaning

Getting even brings happiness for just one day. Staying patient brings praise that lasts as long as the world lasts.

Elementary

Getting back at someone can feel good, for about one day. Then the good feeling is gone. Being the person who stayed kind is different: people remember it and talk about it for a very, very long time.

University

The couplet sets two time-scales against each other: ஒருநாளை இன்பம் ('one day's pleasure') and பொன்றுந் துணையும் புகழ் ('fame until [the world] perishes'). Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah read பொன்றும் as the ending of the world, while Kalaignar reads it as 'throughout one's life.' Either way the basic contrast between passing gratification and lasting reputation holds. Valluvar is speaking the language of புகழ், the enduring renown he devotes chapter 24 to, where reputation is treated as the only thing that outlives the body. The psychological point is sharp: Aristotle also observes that revenge 'relieves' anger by 'producing ... pleasure instead of pain,' but Valluvar insists that this pleasure is the shortest-lived of goods.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man may attain renown in this world by doing two things, viz., by refraining from harsh speech, and by disregarding those that are wicked.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXIII, Vidura's counsel (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura names renown as the reward for not answering harshly and for disregarding the provocations of the wicked.
Like the kural, it makes lasting fame the fruit of forbearance.
Christianity
“Behold, we count them happy which endure. Ye have heard of the patience of Job, and have seen the end of the Lord; that the Lord is very pitiful, and of tender mercy.”
— James 5:11 (KJV) [5]
James points to Job, whose patience became proverbial and is still remembered long after his trials ended.
Job's lasting fame for endurance illustrates the kural's claim that praise for the patient outlives them.
Islam
“Indeed, the patient will be given their reward without account [i.e., limit].”
— Qur'an 39:10 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an promises the patient a reward so large it can't be measured.
The unlimited reward of the patient matches the kural's everlasting praise, set against revenge's one day.
Judaism
“whoever forgoes his reckonings with others for injustices done to him, the heavenly court in turn forgoes punishment for all his sins”
— Talmud Bavli, Rosh Hashanah 17a (William Davidson/Koren translation) [7]
Rava teaches that a person who lets injustices against him pass is himself treated with lasting divine mercy.
Forgoing retaliation brings a lasting reward, while insisting on your due brings nothing that endures.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada, verse 5 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha teaches that returning hatred never brings lasting peace, and that only non-hatred does so, as an eternal law.
Revenge brings only a short relief, while forbearance produces something lasting, which is the kural's contrast.
Jainism
“Leave off the causes of sin, acquire fame through patience! (A man who acts up to this) will rise to the upper regions after having left this body of clay.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 3.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
In the lecture on the four rare requisites of the spiritual life, Mahāvīra's disciples are told directly that patience is how lasting fame is won, and that it carries the soul upward after death.
Valluvar sets the one-day pleasure of revenge against the fame that lasts as long as the world, and the Uttarādhyayana likewise says that fame comes through patience and its reward outlasts this body.
Sikhism
Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, is remembered as Sikhism's first martyr: ordered by the emperor Jahangir to remove supposedly offensive passages from the Adi Granth, he refused and was tortured to death at Lahore in 1606.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Arjan' (Tamanna Nangia, fact-checked by Britannica Editors) [10]
Sikhs honour Guru Arjan's endurance of torture without yielding as one of the defining moments of their history.
The Kural says the pleasure of revenge lasts a day while the fame of the forbearing lasts as long as the world; the Guru's endured suffering has won him lasting honour among Sikhs.
Greek Mythology
“The fame, therefore, of her virtue shall never die, and the immortals shall compose a song that shall be welcome to all mankind in honour of the constancy of Penelope.”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book XXIV (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
In the Underworld, Agamemnon's ghost declares that Penelope's patient faithfulness through twenty years of pressure will be famous forever.
Patient endurance wins undying fame, just as the kural promises praise until the world ends.
Philosophy
“For remember that, if you adhere to the same point, those very persons who at first ridiculed will afterwards admire you.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 22 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [12]
Epictetus tells the new philosopher to expect mockery and to bear it without replying, because steady endurance eventually turns ridicule into admiration.
Enduring insult earns lasting esteem, while retaliating would forfeit it.

Real Life Example

Saladin's terms for the surrender of Jerusalem
1187 · Jerusalem
After defeating the crusader army at Hattin, Saladin besieged Jerusalem in 1187, and its defender, Balian of Ibelin, negotiated the city's surrender. Saladin allowed the Christian inhabitants to ransom themselves at fixed rates and leave, set free many elderly and poor people who could not pay, and allowed noblewomen to leave without ransom, although thousands who could not be redeemed were enslaved. His treatment of the city contrasted sharply with the massacre that had followed the crusaders' capture of Jerusalem in 1099.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Saladin's restraint, set against the bloodshed of 1099, inspired a lasting reputation for magnanimity that later writers and historians built upon.
The kural sets the one day's pleasure of revenge against fame that lasts as long as the world; Saladin passed over the chance to avenge 1099 and won a renown that has endured for more than eight centuries.
