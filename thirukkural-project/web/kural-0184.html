Kural 184

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

கண்ணின்று கண்ணறச் சொல்லினும் சொல்லற்க முன்னின்று பின்நோக்காச் சொல்.
Kannindru kannarach chollinum sollarka, Munnindru pinnokkaach chol.

Meaning

You may even say hard, unkind things to a person's face. But never say words behind their back without thinking about the harm those words will do.

Elementary

If your friend does something wrong, it’s okay to tell them straight to their face, even if it’s hard to hear. What’s not okay is saying it to others when your friend isn’t there. Say it to their face or don’t say it at all.

University

The couplet turns on a set of spatial and visual puns. Kaṇ niṉṟu (“standing before the eyes”) is paired with kaṇ aṟa (“with the eye cut off,” i.e., without kaṇṇōṭṭam, the courteous regard that Valluvar treats as a virtue in chapter 58), while muṉ niṉṟu (“having stood in front”) contrasts with piṉ nōkkā col, which carries two senses: words that do not look to consequences, and words aimed at someone’s back. Valluvar concedes a real cost — harsh, face-to-face speech is not ideal — but ranks it clearly above secret disparagement, because direct speech leaves the target free to answer, correct, or defend himself. This makes the verse a procedural ethic of speech: criticism is legitimate in principle, but its moral character depends on the presence of the person criticised. The same structure appears in legal ideals of hearing both parties and in religious norms of private, direct rebuke.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others…”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes the discipline of speech as saying what is true and beneficial without needlessly upsetting others.
The Gita’s standard of true and beneficial speech rules out consequence-blind talk behind someone’s back, which is exactly what the Kural forbids.
Christianity
“Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother.”
— Matthew 18:15 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his followers to take a grievance straight to the person, privately, before involving anyone else.
Jesus, like Valluvar, says to tell a person’s fault to his face rather than to others behind his back.
Islam
“Backbiting implies your talking about your brother in a manner which he does not like.” … “If (that failing) is actually found (in him) what you assert, you in fact backbited him, and if that is not in him it is a slander.”
— Sahih Muslim 2589 [5]
The Prophet teaches that talking about someone in a way he would dislike is ghībah even when it is true; if false, it is worse — slander.
Both texts agree that even true criticism becomes a wrong when it is spoken about the person rather than to him.
Judaism
“Any statement that is said in the presence of its master, i.e., if the subject of the statement was there, does not have any prohibition due to malicious speech.”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 15b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
Rabba rules that speech said in the subject’s presence is not lashon hara, citing Rabbi Yosei, who never said anything he would not say to the person himself; Abaye disagrees, calling it both impudence and lashon hara.
Rabba’s rule is the Kural’s rule: saying it to someone’s face is a different and lesser matter than saying it behind his back.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 58, Abhaya Sutta (Thanissaro) [7]
The Buddha explains that he will say unpleasant truths when they are beneficial, choosing the right time — as he did openly about Devadatta.
Both allow hard words spoken openly and for a purpose, while ruling out careless words that ignore consequences.
Jainism
“If our intention is to remove the fault of a man, then we should tell his faults in his presence only so that he can reflect on them and eliminate the same. To tell someone's mistake in his presence is an act of bravery; but we should take care to tell him or her in privacy.”
— Modern explanatory gloss on Daśavaikālika Sūtra 8.47, tattvagyan.com ('Do not talk ill behind someone's back') [8]
Explaining the Daśavaikālika's ban on talking ill of people in their absence, this Jain gloss says a fault should be named to the person's face, privately and kindly, and never behind their back.
Valluvar allows even harsh words spoken to a man's face but never thoughtless words behind his back, and the Jain gloss draws the same line between correcting someone to their face and talking about them when they are absent.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid forbids even one harsh word to anyone, since the Lord dwells in every person.
The Kural allows even harsh words spoken to a person's face, so long as one never speaks thoughtlessly behind his back; Farid's verse shares the concern for careful speech but goes further, forbidding harshness altogether.
Greek Mythology
“needs must I verily speak my word outright, even as I am minded, and as it shall be brought to pass”
— Homer, Iliad 9.309–310 (A. T. Murray) [10]
Achilles tells Odysseus he will speak bluntly to his face, and goes on to say he hates a man who hides one thing and says another.
Achilles chooses the harsh, open word over hidden speech — the very preference Valluvar states.
Philosophy
“Every such person is a skulker and a coward; he will not come into the open; he is an ambuscader shooting from a lurking-place”
— Lucian, Slander, a Warning §9 (Fowler) [11]
Lucian argues that the slanderer is a coward: an honest accuser makes his charge publicly and lets it be examined.
Lucian and Valluvar both say: if you have a charge, bring it into the open where the person can answer.

Real Life Example

Bishop Ambrose confronts Emperor Theodosius
390 CE · Milan, Western Roman Empire (now Italy)
In 390 CE, after Emperor Theodosius I ordered a reprisal at Thessalonica that led to a massacre of its citizens, Bishop Ambrose of Milan wrote to the emperor directly, telling him he could not excuse the act and that he would not celebrate the Eucharist in his presence until the emperor repented. Ambrose required Theodosius to do public penance. After some hesitation the emperor complied and was readmitted to communion at Christmas 390.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ambrose rebuked the most powerful man in the empire openly and in his own name, rather than undermining him in secret, and the emperor accepted the rebuke.
The kural says harsh words spoken to a person's face are better than thoughtless words behind his back, and Ambrose chose to confront the emperor directly.
