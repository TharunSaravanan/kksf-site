Kural 1188

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

பசந்தாள் இவள்என்பது அல்லால் இவளைத் துறந்தார் அவர்என்பார் இல்.
pasandhaal ival-enpadhu allaal ivalaith thurandhaar avar-enpaar il

Meaning

People only say, 'She has turned pale.' No one says, 'He has left her.'

Elementary

Imagine your friend broke your toy, and everyone said, ‘Why is that toy broken? You must be careless!’ but nobody said, ‘Your friend broke it.’ That feels unfair. The wife feels the same: everybody notices her pale face, but nobody says it’s because her husband went away.

University

A couplet about public opinion (alar, the village gossip that runs through the Kamattuppal): people say pasantāḷ ivaḷ (பசந்தாள் இவள், ‘she has grown pale’), but none says tuṟantār avar (துறந்தார் அவர், ‘he has abandoned her’). Tuṟa- is the same root as tuṟavu, ‘renunciation, forsaking’, giving the complaint a sharp edge. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read the first clause as blame (பழி) directed at the woman for showing her grief, while the man’s departure, the real cause, escapes comment. Pope: ‘On me, because I pine, they cast a slur; / But no one says, “He first deserted her.”’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Shakuntala arrives at Dushyanta’s court, the king, under Durvasas’s curse, does not remember her; it is she whose honesty and conduct are questioned before the assembly, while the husband who forgot her is not blamed.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 5 (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa stages a scene in which public suspicion falls on the wife rather than on the husband who failed her.
The Kural’s heroine complains of the same imbalance: people remark on her, not on the man who left.
Christianity
The scribes and Pharisees bring to Jesus a woman taken in adultery and set her in the midst for judgment; the man is not brought, and Jesus answers that whoever is without sin should cast the first stone.
— John 8:3-11 (KJV) (paraphrase) [4]
The episode exposes a public judgment that singles out the woman while the man escapes notice.
The Kural’s complaint has the same shape: the woman’s condition is talked about, the man’s part is ignored.
Islam
“Why, when you heard it, did not the believing men and believing women think good of themselves [i.e., one another] and say, ‘This is an obvious falsehood’?”
— Qur’an 24:12 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an rebukes the community for repeating talk that wrongly blamed a woman instead of assuming the best.
The Kural’s heroine also suffers from talk that blames her while ignoring the true cause.
Judaism
“Look not upon me, that I am swarthy, That the sun hath tanned me; My mother’s sons were incensed against me, They made me keeper of the vineyards; But mine own vineyard have I not kept.”
— Song of Songs 1:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The bride asks onlookers not to stare at her darkened skin, explaining that it was caused by others who sent her out under the sun.
Like the Kural’s heroine, she knows people will judge her changed colour without seeing who caused it.
Buddhism
“O Atula! Indeed, this is an ancient practice, not one only of today: they blame those who remain silent, they blame those who speak much, they blame those who speak in moderation. There is none in the world who is not blamed.”
— Dhammapada 227 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that public blame is universal and arbitrary.
The heroine discovers the same arbitrariness: the village blames her pallor, never his absence.
Jainism
Anjana Sundari, neglected for years by her husband Pavananjaya, is visited by him secretly before he leaves for war; when she is found to be with child, her mother-in-law accuses her of misconduct and drives her out, and even her own family refuses to take her in, while no one questions the absent husband.
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 7 (story of Anjana) (paraphrase) [8]
In this Jain narrative, the mother of Hanuman bears all the blame for a situation her absent husband created.
Her story is the Kural’s complaint writ large: everyone speaks of what has happened to her, and no one speaks of him.
Sikhism
“Dadda: Do not blame anyone else; blame instead your own actions.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 433, Raag Aasaa, Patti, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Patti (acrostic) in Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak teaches that one should not blame others for one's suffering but look at one's own deeds.
The Kural's wife complains that people blame her for turning pale while no one blames the husband who left; Gurbani turns this around, telling the sufferer never to blame the other but to look at her own actions.
Greek Mythology
Virgil’s Rumour (Fama) spreads through the cities of Libya the tale of Dido’s love for Aeneas and her neglect of her kingdom; when Aeneas sails away, it is Dido who is left disgraced and talked about.
— Virgil, Aeneid 4.173-197 (paraphrase) [10]
In the Aeneid public talk fastens on the abandoned woman while the departing hero sails on.
The Kural’s heroine complains of the same: the talk is all about her, never about the man who left.
Philosophy
Aristotle holds that praise and blame are properly given only for voluntary actions, while what is involuntary, done under compulsion or in ignorance, deserves pardon and sometimes pity.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 3.1 (1109b30-35) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, blame should follow responsibility.
By that standard the village has it backward: her pallor is involuntary, while his departure was his choice.

Real Life Example

Catherine of Aragon refuses to accept the annulment of her marriage
1533-1536 · England
Henry VIII sought to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon mainly because she had not given him a male heir. Archbishop Thomas Cranmer annulled the marriage on 23 May 1533, but Catherine refused to accept it and kept calling herself queen, until the 1534 Act of Succession required her to use the title Princess Dowager. She was separated from her daughter Mary, confined to residences including Kimbolton, and died there in January 1536.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The official reasons for ending the marriage centred on Catherine, while it was Henry who set her aside.
The kural complains that people say 'she has grown pale' but no one says 'he has left her', and Catherine bore the official blame for a marriage that her husband chose to end.
