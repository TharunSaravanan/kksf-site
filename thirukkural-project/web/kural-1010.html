Kural 1010

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

சீருடைச் செல்வர் சிறுதுனி மாரி வறங்கூர்ந் தனையது உடைத்து.
Seerutaich Chelvar Sirudhuni Maari Varangoorn Thanaiyadhu Utaiththu

Meaning

When good rich people who are known for giving become poor for a short time, it is like a rain cloud that is empty for a while after giving its rain.

Elementary

A rain cloud pours out all its water on the thirsty fields, and for a little while it looks thin and empty. But soon it fills up again. Valluvar says when kind, generous people run short for a while, it is just like that cloud: they gave a lot, and they will be full again.

University

After nine couplets on useless wealth, the chapter closes on its positive counterpart. Seerudaich chelvar (சீருடைச் செல்வர்) are 'rich men endowed with excellence', which Mu. Varadarasanar glosses 'famous' and Solomon Pappaiah 'famous for giving to others'. Their siRu thuni (சிறுதுனி) is 'a small distress', read by most commentators as a brief poverty; Pope renders 'brief poverty'. It is compared to maari vaRam koorndhanaiyadhu (மாரி வறங் கூர்ந்தனையது), 'like the rain cloud becoming impoverished', i.e. temporarily spent; Mu. Varadarasanar specifies 'the cloud that can protect the world'. The implication is that the generous man's want is temporary and itself a sign of his giving, as the cloud's emptiness follows its rain. Sivayogi Sivakumar takes a different line, reading the distress as the darkening that precedes rain and turns into good; Kalaignar reads the couplet as saying that even a shadow of poverty on such men is as grave as failed rain. The cloud image recalls Kural 211 on duty that expects no return, like the rain. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let the rich satisfy the poor implorer, and bend his eye upon a longer pathway. / Riches come now to one, now to another, and like the wheels of cars are ever rolling.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.5 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith, sacred-texts.com) [3]
The Vedic poet urges the rich to give, knowing that fortune turns like a chariot wheel.
Like the Kural, it treats a giver's changes of fortune as passing turns of the wheel rather than lasting loss.
Christianity
“If the clouds be full of rain, they empty themselves upon the earth: and if the tree fall toward the south, or toward the north, in the place where the tree falleth, there it shall be.”
— Ecclesiastes 11:3 (KJV, kingjamesbibleonline.org) [4]
In a passage urging generous giving (casting bread on the waters, giving a portion to seven and eight), the Preacher points to clouds that pour out what they hold.
The Kural uses the same image: the generous rich empty themselves like rain clouds.
Islam
The Qur'an assures believers that whatever they spend in charity, God will replace it, for He is the best of providers.
— Qur'an 34:39 (Saba') [5]
Spending for good does not finally impoverish the giver; it is replenished.
This is the Kural's point that the generous man's want is temporary, like a spent cloud that will fill again.
Judaism
“A person will never become impoverished from giving charity. No harm nor damage will ever be caused because of charity”
— Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:2 (tr. Eliyahu Touger, via Sefaria) [6]
Maimonides rules that charity never truly impoverishes the giver.
The Kural similarly treats the generous rich man's poverty as brief and not real ruin.
Buddhism
In a verse spoken to the Buddha, devas say that those who share even from the little they have, like fellow travellers on the road, do not die among the dead.
— Samyutta Nikaya 1.32, Macchari Sutta [7]
Generosity under scarcity is especially praised and never truly lost.
The Kural honours the generous rich even in their short-lived poverty.
Jainism
“Though they live near the mighty ocean whose waters overflow, men look on the spring of a small well whose water is almost dried up and live. The poverty of the great is better than the riches of those who know not of the next birth.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 28 ('Illiberality'), st. 5 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain poet says people still draw life from a nearly dry spring rather than the vast salt sea, and so the noble in poverty are worth more than the rich who give nothing.
Like the Kural's spent rain cloud, the noble man's depleted state is still a source of life for others.
Sikhism
“The Siblings of Destiny meet together, and eat and spend, but these resources do not diminish; they continue to increase.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 186, Raag Gauri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan speaks of a treasure that grows the more it is shared and spent together. In context the treasure is the Lord's Name inherited from the Guru.
The Kural says the brief poverty of generous men is like a cloud spent by its rain that soon fills again; Guru Arjan speaks of a store that never runs out however much is given away.
Greek Mythology
The poor old couple Baucis and Philemon generously share their meagre food with disguised gods whom richer households had turned away; their wine jug refills itself, and the gods reward them.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618–724 (Baucis and Philemon) [10]
Generosity from scant means is miraculously replenished.
Like the Kural's cloud that will fill again, the generous couple's want is made good.
Philosophy
“It is not easy for the liberal man to be rich, since he is not apt either at taking or at keeping, but at giving away, and does not value wealth for its own sake but as a means to giving. Hence comes the charge that is brought against fortune, that those who deserve riches most get it least.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross, MIT Internet Classics Archive) [11]
Aristotle observes that the generous often find themselves short of money precisely because they give.
This explains the Kural's 'brief poverty' of the generous rich: it is the natural cost of their giving.

Real Life Example

Mark Twain recovers from bankruptcy and repays his creditors
1894-1898 · United States and a world lecture tour
In 1894 Mark Twain's publishing firm, Charles L. Webster and Company, failed and, weakened further by heavy losses on the Paige typesetting machine, he was forced into personal bankruptcy. Though not legally required to repay his creditors in full, he resolved to do so, set out on a lecture tour around the world in 1895-96 and published an account of it, Following the Equator, in 1897. By 1898 the earnings from the tour and the book, with the help of his friend Henry H. Rogers's management of his money, had allowed him to pay his creditors in full.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Twain's reputation and talent remained intact through his financial collapse, and within a few years they had restored his fortune.
The kural says that the brief poverty of an honoured rich man is like a rain cloud that has emptied itself for a while and will soon be full again, and Twain's honoured name refilled his empty purse within four years.
