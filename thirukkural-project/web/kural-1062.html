Kural 1062

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

இரந்தும் உயிர்வாழ்தல் வேண்டின் பரந்து கெடுக உலகியற்றி யான்.
Irandhum uyirvaazhdhal vendin parandhu keduka ulagiyatri yaan.

Meaning

If the maker of the world meant some people to live by begging, then let that maker also wander about like a beggar and die.

Elementary

Imagine a game where the rules say some players must beg the others just to stay in. You would say the rules are unfair and the person who made them should try playing that way himself. That is how angry the Kural is that anyone should have to beg to live.

University

This is one of the boldest couplets in the Kural: Valluvar curses ulagiyatriyaan (உலகியற்றியான், 'he who fashioned the world') with the very fate of the beggar, parandhu keduga ('may he wander and perish'). Commentators in the tradition that follows Parimelazhagar read it as a conditional imprecation: if the creator had ordained begging as a means of life, he deserves to wander as beggars do; the force is ethical outrage that anyone should be reduced to irandhum ('even by begging') staying alive. Modern glosses differ in emphasis: Mu. Varadarasanar keeps the creator literal, while Kalaignar Karunanidhi's gloss pointedly says 'the one said to have created this world', distancing the verse from theism, and scholars have debated whether ulagiyatriyaan means a god, fate (ul), or the human order that permits destitution. Pope renders it 'If he that shaped the world desires that men should begging go, / Through life's long course, let him a wanderer be and perish so.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Beholding superior and well-behaved and modest persons persecuted, while the sinful are happy, I am sorely troubled. Beholding this thy distress and the prosperity of Suyodhana, I do not speak highly of the Great Ordainer who suffereth such inequality!”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section XXX (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
In exile, Draupadi openly reproaches the Ordainer for letting the virtuous suffer while the wicked prosper.
Like the Kural, Draupadi dares to blame the world's maker for an unjust distribution of fortune.
Christianity
“Men groan from out of the city, and the soul of the wounded crieth out: yet God layeth not folly to them.”
— Job 24:12 (KJV) [4]
Job complains that God seems to ignore the cries of the poor and oppressed.
Job, like the Kural, questions a creator who lets people be reduced to misery.
Islam
“And when it is said to them, ‘Spend from that which Allāh has provided for you,’ those who disbelieve say to those who believe, ‘Should we feed one whom, if Allāh had willed, He would have fed? You are not but in clear error.’”
— Qur'an 36:47 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an rebukes those who blame God's will for others' hunger as an excuse not to feed them.
Islam rejects blaming God for poverty, so this is a contrast: where the Kural curses the maker, the Qur'an places the duty on those who could feed the poor.
Judaism
“Far be it from You to do such a thing, to bring death upon the innocent as well as the guilty, so that innocent and guilty fare alike. Far be it from You! Shall not the Judge of all the earth deal justly?”
— Genesis 18:25 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Abraham challenges God directly, holding the Judge of all the earth to his own standard of justice.
Abraham, like Valluvar, holds the maker of the world to account for unjust suffering.
Buddhism
In the Bhuridatta Jataka, the Bodhisatta argues that if Brahma were truly lord and maker of the whole world, he would not have ordered it so full of misery and injustice, so the creator is either powerless or unjust.
— Bhuridatta Jataka (Jataka No. 543, tr. E. B. Cowell et al.) (paraphrase) [7]
The Jataka uses the suffering of beings as an argument against a just, all-powerful creator.
The Kural's 'if the maker willed begging' runs on the same logic: a world that forces people to beg indicts whoever made it.
Jainism
“Though Lakshmî withdraw from them and God be angry, the excellent will not stand with bended neck before the ignorant who bury their money in the earth.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 4 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil quatrains say that the noble will not bow to beg from hoarders even if fortune leaves them and heaven turns hostile.
Like the Kural, it defies whatever higher power might force a person to beg, though it defies by endurance rather than by cursing.
Sikhism
“Dadda: Do not blame anyone else; blame instead your own actions.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 433, Raag Asa, Patti, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his acrostic Patti, Guru Nanak teaches that suffering should not be blamed on anyone else, since it comes from one's own actions. This sits within the wider Sikh acceptance of hukam, the divine order.
The Kural boldly curses the world's maker if he meant some people to live by begging; Guru Nanak takes the opposite course and tells people not to blame others for their condition but to look at their own deeds.
Greek Mythology
“For the gods keep hidden from men the means of life. Else you would easily do work enough in a day to supply you for a full year even without working.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days ll. 42-44 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod says that Zeus in anger hid the easy means of life from mortals, so that people must struggle for their bread.
Hesiod, like the Kural, traces human want back to the will of the powers who ordered the world.
Philosophy
Lactantius reports the argument attributed to Epicurus: God either wishes to take away evils and cannot, or can and does not wish to, or neither wishes nor is able; if he both wishes and is able, where do evils come from and why does he not remove them?
— Lactantius, On the Anger of God 13 (argument attributed to Epicurus) (paraphrase) [11]
The classical problem of evil asks how a good and powerful maker could allow suffering.
The Kural puts the same dilemma as a curse: a maker who wills that people beg cannot be both just and worthy.

Real Life Example

New Zealand passes the Old-age Pensions Act
1 November 1898 · Wellington, New Zealand
Richard Seddon's Liberal government passed the Old-age Pensions Act in 1898, the first such scheme in the British Empire and paid for from general taxation. It gave a small, means-tested pension of up to 18 pounds a year to people aged 65 or over who had lived long in New Zealand and were judged to be of good character, though it shamefully excluded Chinese and other Asian residents.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The act treated support for the elderly poor as a public responsibility rather than leaving them to beg or to rely on charity, and it is regarded as the birth of social security in New Zealand.
The kural angrily rejects any order of the world in which people must beg to live, and New Zealand's pension law was an early attempt to change that order so that the old and poor need not beg.
