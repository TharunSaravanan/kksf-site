Kural 742

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

மணிநீரும் மண்ணும் மலையும் அணிநிழற் காடும் உடைய தரண்.
Maninheerum mannum malaiyum aninizhar kaadum udaiya tharan.

Meaning

A true fort has water as clear as a jewel, open land, hills, and a forest with thick shade.

Elementary

Imagine building a pillow fort. It is best to put it behind the sofa, beside a blanket ‘river’, with a big armchair ‘mountain’ next to it and lots of cushions like a forest. Anyone sneaking up has to cross all of that first. Valluvar says the best forts use nature in the same way: water, open ground, hills and forest.

University

Here Valluvar lists the natural defenses (iyarkai aran) that should surround a fort: mani neer (water clear as a gem, i.e. a moat or lake that never dries), mann (land, glossed by Varadarasanar and Pappaiah as open ground with no cover for an attacker), malai (hill) and ani nizhal kaadu (forest of beautiful or dense shade). Parimelazhagar and the later commentators read these as four types of fort: neer aran, nila aran, malai aran and kaattu aran. Pappaiah states this directly. The list closely parallels the Sanskrit arthashastra classification into water-fort (audaka), mountain-fort (parvata), desert or open-land fort (dhanvana) and forest-fort (vanadurga), which suggests a shared South Asian military science that the next couplet calls simply ‘the treatise’ (nool). Kalaignar reads mani neer as a deep, wide moat. The sandhi-joined text reads ‘மணிநீரு மண்ணு மலையு மணிநிழற் / காடு முடைய தரண்’, which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“a water-fortification (audaka) such as an island in the midst of a river, or a plain surrounded by low ground; a mountainous fortification (párvata) such as a rocky tract or a cave; a desert (dhánvana) such as a wild tract devoid of water and overgrown with thicket growing in barren soil; or a forest fortification (vanadurga) full of wagtail (khajana), water and thickets.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 2.3 ‘Construction of Forts’ (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya names four kinds of natural fortification to be built at the kingdom’s borders: water, mountain, desert and forest.
This is almost the same four-part list as the Kural’s water, land, hill and forest.
Christianity
“He shall dwell on high: his place of defence shall be the munitions of rocks: bread shall be given him; his waters shall be sure.”
— Isaiah 33:16 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah promises the righteous a high rocky stronghold with guaranteed bread and water.
Isaiah’s ideal refuge combines the height of hills with an assured water supply, two of the Kural’s natural defenses.
Islam
“And Allah has made for you, from that which He has created, shadows and has made for you from the mountains, shelters.”
— Qur’an 16:81 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an counts shade and mountain shelters among God’s gifts for human protection.
Shade and mountains are two of the natural protections the Kural wants around a fort.
Judaism
“A large force was assembled to stop up all the springs and the wadi that flowed through the land, for otherwise, they thought, the king of Assyria would come and find water in abundance.”
— 2 Chronicles 32:4 (JPS Tanakh 1985, via Sefaria) [6]
Before the Assyrian siege, Hezekiah’s men blocked the springs outside Jerusalem so that the besiegers would have no water.
It shows the Kural’s point that controlling water is itself a fortification.
Buddhism
“Furthermore, the royal frontier fortress has a moat, both deep & wide.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The second requisite of the Buddha’s model fortress is a deep, wide moat.
The moat is the Kural’s ‘gem-clear water’, the first natural defense on its list.
Jainism
“At each gate they made a tank with golden lotuses having four gates like the rampart of the samavasaraṇa.” … “In the midst of the samavasaraṇa, a caitya-tree made by the Vyantaras, rising for three kos, indicated the rise of the Three Jewels.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 1.3, part 10 ‘Description of a samavasaraṇa’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS, 1931; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Hemacandra's description of the samavasarana, the assembly hall the gods raise for a Tirthankara's preaching, sets pools of water at its gates and a great shade-giving sacred tree at its centre within its ramparts.
The Jain sacred enclosure combines clear water and a towering tree inside its walls, a distant echo of the Kural's natural defences of gem-clear water, open ground, hill and shady forest, though its purpose is sermon and refuge, not war.
Sikhism
“A fortress like that of Sri Lanka, with the ocean as a moat around it - there is no news about that house of Raavan.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 481, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir recalls Ravana's island citadel, defended by the sea itself, to show that even the strongest natural defences do not last.
The Kural lists clear water, open ground, hills and forest as a fortress's natural defences; Kabir names the greatest of water-moats, but only to say that it could not keep Ravana's house in being.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, the princess Nausicaa describes the city of the Phaeacians to Odysseus as surrounded by a high wall, with a fine harbour on either side and a narrow entrance, a site protected by the sea on both flanks.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.262-269 [10]
The ideal city of the Phaeacians is protected mainly by its natural setting of water and a narrow approach.
Like the Kural, Homer’s ideal city depends on natural surroundings as much as on its wall.
Philosophy
“There should be a natural abundance of springs and fountains in the town, or, if there is a deficiency of them, great reservoirs may be established for the collection of rainwater, such as will not fail when the inhabitants are cut off from the country …”
— Aristotle, Politics VII.11 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle requires the ideal city’s site to have a water supply that cannot fail even during a war.
Aristotle, like Valluvar, begins good fortification with the natural site, and above all with water.

Real Life Example

Rajput rulers build the hill forts of Rajasthan among hills, rivers, forest and desert
8th-18th centuries · Rajasthan, India
Between the 8th and 18th centuries the Rajput states of Rajasthan built a series of great hill forts, six of which, at Chittorgarh, Kumbhalgarh, Ranthambore, Gagron, Amber and Jaisalmer, now form a single UNESCO World Heritage property. UNESCO notes that the forts use the natural defences offered by the landscape, namely hills, deserts, rivers and dense forests, with Ranthambore set in forest, Gagron protected by a river and Jaisalmer standing in desert, and that they contain extensive water-harvesting structures. Gagron is surrounded on three sides by the waters of the Ahu and Kalisindh rivers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The forts show a long South Asian tradition of choosing sites where water, hills, forest and open ground did much of the work of defence, backed by secure water supplies.
Valluvar lists clear water, open ground, hills and dense forest as the marks of a true fortress, and the Rajput builders set their forts among exactly these natural defences.
