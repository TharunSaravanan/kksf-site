Kural 105

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

உதவி வரைத்தன்று உதவி உதவி செயப்பட்டார் சால்பின் வரைத்து.
Udhavi varaiththandru udhavi udhavi Seyappattaar saalbin varaiththu.

Meaning

The worth of help is not measured by the help itself. It is measured by the goodness of the person who receives it.

Elementary

If a friend gives you a small gift, how much you give back is not just about the size of their gift. It is about what kind of person you are. A really good-hearted person gives back even more than they got.

University

Built on a fourfold repetition of உதவி (help), the couplet admits two readings. Parimelazhagar, followed by Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah, takes the first உதவி as the return-favour: requital is not bounded by the size of the original help but by the சால்பு (nobility, fullness of character) of the one who was helped, so a noble recipient repays abundantly. Kalaignar reads it evaluatively: the value of a help is assessed according to the recipient's worth. Both readings converge on the claim that gratitude measures the recipient rather than balancing a ledger, linking this chapter to the later chapter on சான்றாண்மை (perfect character). Aristotle frames the same dispute, whether to measure a service by its utility to the receiver or by the benevolence of the giver (Nicomachean Ethics 8.13), and Cicero, citing Hesiod, urges us to imitate fertile fields that return more than they receive (De Officiis 1.48).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna's poor boyhood friend Sudama visits him bringing only a handful of flattened rice. Krishna eats it with delight, and Sudama returns home to find his hut transformed into a palace; the return is measured by Krishna's greatness, not by the rice.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.80-81 [3]
A poor friend gave Krishna a tiny gift, and Krishna repaid it with far more than it was 'worth' because of who Krishna is.
The repayment matched the giver-back's own greatness, not the size of the help, which is the Kural's point about சால்பு.
Christianity
“Give, and it shall be given unto you; good measure, pressed down, and shaken together, and running over, shall men give into your bosom. For with the same measure that ye mete withal it shall be measured to you again.”
— Luke 6:38 (KJV) [4]
Jesus promises that generosity returns in overflowing measure, pressed down and running over.
The return is not limited to the original amount but overflows, much as the Kural says a noble recipient's requital is not bounded by the original help.
Islam
“And when you are greeted with a greeting, greet [in return] with one better than it or [at least] return it [in a like manner]. Indeed, Allah is ever, over all things, an Accountant.”
— Qur'an 4:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an tells believers to answer a greeting with a better one, or at least an equal one.
A good person's response is set by their own goodness and aims higher than what they received, the Kural's standard for repaying help.
Judaism
“And he said unto his daughters, And where is he? why is it that ye have left the man? call him, that he may eat bread.”
— Exodus 2:20 (KJV) [4]
When the fugitive Moses drove off shepherds who were bullying Jethro's daughters at a well, Jethro scolded them for not bringing the stranger home; he took Moses in and later gave him his daughter in marriage.
One act of help at a well was repaid with a home and a family, a return set by Jethro's generosity rather than by the size of the favour.
Buddhism
“A person of integrity is grateful & thankful. This gratitude, this thankfulness, is advocated by civil people. It is entirely on the level of people of integrity.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.31 (Kataññu Sutta, Thanissaro Bhikkhu trans.) [6]
The Buddha teaches that gratitude is what marks a person of integrity, while ingratitude marks a person without it.
Like the Kural, it makes the way we respond to help a measure of our own character.
Jainism
“Krtajna : Grateful.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of 35 qualities of the ideal śrāvaka, no. 28, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, 'Prescription of Ethical Code' (jainworld.com) [7]
Hemacandra's list of thirty-five qualities of the ideal Jain householder includes kṛtajña, literally 'one who knows what has been done (for him)', alongside being ready to serve others and supporting dependents.
Jain householder ethics treats gratitude as part of a person's settled character, which matches Valluvar's claim that the worth of a requital depends on the nobility of the one who received the help.
Sikhism
Sikh tradition remembers that Amar Das, already an old man, became a disciple of Guru Angad and served him with extraordinary devotion for over a decade, fetching water before dawn and serving in the community kitchen; his humble service led Guru Angad to name him the third Guru in 1552.
— Wikipedia, 'Guru Amar Das' (paraphrase of traditional accounts) [8]
The episode shows a disciple whose response to the grace he received was measured not by any calculation but by the depth of his own devotion.
It illustrates the Kural's claim that the return for a benefit is bounded by the nobility of the one who received it rather than by the size of the benefit.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod advises taking fair measure from a neighbour and paying it back fairly with the same measure, or better if you are able, so that you may find him reliable when you are in need again.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 349-351 (H. G. Evelyn-White trans.) [9]
The old Greek poet tells farmers to repay what a neighbour lent them with at least as much, and more if they can.
Repaying 'better if you can' makes the requital depend on the character and ability of the recipient, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“But if, as Hesiod bids, one is to repay with interest, if possible, what one has borrowed in time of need, what, pray, ought we to do when challenged by an unsought kindness? Shall we not imitate the fruitful fields, which return more than they receive?”
— Cicero, De Officiis 1.48 (Walter Miller trans., Loeb 1913) [10]
Cicero says that if we repay loans with interest, we should repay an unasked-for kindness even more generously, like good soil that gives back more than was sown.
A good person's requital overflows the original favour, just as the Kural says it is measured by the recipient's nobility.

Real Life Example

Andrew Carnegie repays Colonel Anderson's library
1850s; monument unveiled 1904 · Allegheny (now part of Pittsburgh), Pennsylvania, United States
As a poor working boy in Allegheny, Andrew Carnegie borrowed books from Colonel James Anderson, a retired businessman who opened his personal library of about 400 volumes to working boys every Saturday. Carnegie later became one of the richest men in the world, and starting in 1881 he and later his foundation gave some $56 million to build 2,509 public libraries. In 1904 a monument by the sculptor Daniel Chester French honouring Anderson's generosity was placed near the Carnegie library in Allegheny.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A modest weekly loan of books was repaid on a vast scale by a recipient who never forgot it and who said he resolved to lend books to others if he ever had surplus wealth.
The kural says the worth of a favour is set by the character of the one who receives it; Anderson's small library became thousands of libraries because of the man who received the kindness.
