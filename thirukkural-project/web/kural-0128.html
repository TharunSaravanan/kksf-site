Kural 128

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

ஒன்றானுந் தீச்சொல் பொருட்பயன் உண்டாயின் நன்றாகா தாகி விடும்.
Ondraanum theechchol porutpayan undaayin Nandraakaa thaaki vidum.

Meaning

If even one word you speak hurts someone through its meaning, all the good you have done becomes useless.

Elementary

Imagine a big glass of milk with just one drop of poison in it. You can’t drink any of it now. In the same way, one really mean word can spoil lots of kind things you have said and done.

University

Parimelazhagar identifies ‘evil words’ (theechchol) as falsehood, slander (kuralai) and harsh speech, and reads the couplet to mean that if even one harm reaches another person through such words, the good earned by all one’s other virtues is turned to evil. Manakkudavar offers a subtler alternative: even a single word that sounds sweet but carries a harmful meaning (porutpayan) makes the speech bad. Karunanidhi’s image of a drop of poison in a pot of milk captures the moral asymmetry: harm is not simply offset by good but contaminates it. That puts the Kural at odds with purely additive ‘merit ledgers’ and aligns it with Ecclesiastes’ dead flies in the perfumer’s ointment.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Pride, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness and ignorance – these qualities belong to those of demoniac nature, O son of Pṛthā.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.4 [4]
The Gita lists harshness of speech (parushyam) among the marks of a demonic nature.
Both texts treat harsh speech as something that corrupts a person’s whole character, not a minor slip.
Christianity
“Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not so to be. Doth a fountain send forth at the same place sweet water and bitter?”
— James 3:10–11 (KJV) [5]
James says a mouth that both blesses and curses is as unnatural as a spring giving sweet and bitter water.
Both texts say that bad words poison the good that comes from the same mouth.
Islam
“A slave (of Allah) may utter a word which pleases Allah without giving it much importance, and because of that Allah will raise him to degrees (of reward): a slave (of Allah) may utter a word (carelessly) which displeases Allah without thinking of its gravity and because of that he will be thrown into the Hell-Fire.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6478 [6]
The Prophet warns that a single careless word can outweigh a person’s standing and cast him into the Fire.
Same asymmetry as the Kural: one evil word can overturn the good of a lifetime.
Judaism
“Dead flies make the ointment of the perfumer fetid and putrid; so doth a little folly outweigh wisdom and honour.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:1 (JPS 1917) [7]
Ecclesiastes teaches that a small amount of folly can spoil a great store of wisdom and honour, just as dead flies ruin fine perfume.
Almost the same image as Karunanidhi’s poison in milk: a small corruption spoils a large good.
Buddhism
“Surely, when a person is born, an axe is born in his mouth, with which he cuts himself— the fool saying a bad statement.”
— Kokalika Sutta, Sutta Nipata 3.10 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The monk Kokalika slanders the Buddha’s chief disciples Sariputta and Moggallana despite three warnings; though a monk, he falls into hell, and the Buddha speaks this verse.
Kokalika’s religious life counted for nothing once he spoke evil, which is exactly the Kural’s warning that one harmful word undoes all good.
Jainism
“One may have a tuft or matted hair on the head or a shaven head, remain naked or wear a rag. But if he tells a lie, all this is futile or fruitless.”
— Bhagavatī Ārādhanā 843 (Śivārya), English rendering as given on Jainworld, 'Jina Sutra – Quotations' [9]
The Digambara text Bhagavatī Ārādhanā teaches that a single untruth empties every outward austerity of its worth.
Valluvar says one harmful word turns all a person's good to no good; this verse makes the same asymmetric claim, that one false word cancels all accumulated religious effort.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are in the Guru Granth Sahib, forbids even one harsh word because the Lord lives in every person addressed.
The Kural says a single harmful word can turn all one's good to no good; Farid's 'not even a single harsh word' carries the same weight on one utterance.
Greek Mythology
Tantalus, a mortal so favoured that he dined at the table of the gods, is cast into eternal punishment. In Euripides’ Orestes, Electra says the cause was his ‘unbridled tongue,’ which she calls a most shameful disease.
— Euripides, Orestes 4–10 (paraphrase) [11]
In this telling, a man who had every divine honour lost all of it through unrestrained speech.
Tantalus shows the Kural’s asymmetry: a lifetime of divine favour undone by a failure of the tongue.
Philosophy
“out of words light as air, in very deed the greatest enmities and hatreds spring up.”
— Plato, Laws XI, 934e–935a (trans. Jowett) [12]
In the Laws, Plato forbids abusive speech because light words grow into the greatest enmities.
Plato and Valluvar both see that small, harmful words produce harm out of all proportion to their size.

Real Life Example

Gerald Ratner's speech destroys his company's reputation
April 1991 · Institute of Directors convention, London, England, United Kingdom
Gerald Ratner had built Ratners Group into Britain's biggest jewellery chain by selling inexpensive jewellery to a mass market. Speaking at the Institute of Directors' convention in London in April 1991, he joked that one of his firm's products was so cheap because it was rubbish, using a crude word, and that a pair of its earrings would probably not last as long as a prawn sandwich. The remarks were widely reported, the value of the group fell by around 500 million pounds, nearly causing its collapse, and Ratner was removed as chief executive the following year.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Years of commercial success were undone in a few seconds, and doing a Ratner became a British phrase for a self-inflicted business blunder.
The kural says that if even a single harmful word does its damage, all the good a person has done turns to no good; Ratner's few words wiped out the reputation he had spent years building.
