Kural 1111

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

நன்னீரை வாழி அனிச்சமே நின்னினும் மென்னீரள் யாம்வீழ் பவள்.
Nanneerai vaazhi anichchamae ninninum menneeral yaamveezh paval.

Meaning

Anicham flower (a flower so delicate it wilts if you just smell it), long may you live! You are truly soft, but the one I love is even softer than you.

Elementary

Imagine you hold the softest feather you have ever found and say, 'You are so soft!' Then you remember your baby sister's cheek and smile: 'But she is even softer.' The poet does the same with the softest flower he knows.

University

Chapter 112 belongs to the kalaviyal (the section on secret, premarital love) and follows ch. 111 (Punarchi Magizhthal, the joy of union): the hero now 'adorns in words' (punainthu uraiththal) the nalam (நலம், 'goodness, loveliness') of the heroine, a stock situation of Tamil akam poetry. The anicham (அனிச்சம்) is the proverbial most delicate flower in Tamil, said to wilt merely when smelled (cf. Kural 90, மோப்பக் குழையும் அனிச்சம்), and nanneerai (நன்னீரை, 'you are of fine nature') with the blessing vaazhi ('may you flourish') makes the flower a polite rival before it is surpassed by the beloved's men-neer (மென்னீர், 'softer nature'). In the line of Parimelazhagar the hero speaks this aside after union, praising what he has just experienced; Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss it as 'my beloved is softer than you'. Pope renders: 'O flower of the sensitive plant! than thee / More tender's the maiden beloved by me.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Your body, soft as siris-flowers, / Engages passion’s utmost powers; / How comes it that your heart is hard / As stalks that siris-blossoms guard?”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
King Dushyanta teases Shakuntala that her body is as soft as the shirisha blossom, the flower Sanskrit poets treat as the most delicate of all.
Like the Kural’s anicham, the siris flower is the poet’s yardstick for softness, and the beloved is measured against it.
Christianity
“As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters.”
— Song of Solomon 2:2 (KJV) [4]
The lover sets his beloved beside a lily and says she stands out among other women as a lily among thorns.
Both poems praise the beloved through a delicate flower and declare her finer than everything around her.
Islam
“I have never touched silk or Dibaj (i.e. thick silk) softer than the palm of the Prophet (ﷺ) nor have I smelt a perfume nicer than the sweat of the Prophet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3561 [5]
Anas, who served the Prophet for years, describes him by saying even the finest silk was not as soft as his hand.
The same rhetorical move as the Kural: take the softest thing known and declare the person softer still.
Judaism
“I am a rose of Sharon, A lily of the valleys.”
— Song of Songs 2:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The maiden of the Song of Songs describes herself in the language of wild flowers.
Jewish love poetry, like the Kural, reaches first for flowers when it speaks of the beloved’s loveliness.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada asks the reader to look closely at the body that is adorned and admired, calling it a painted image that is in truth frail and subject to decay.
— Dhammapada 147 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhism treats physical beauty as fragile and impermanent, and teaches that seeing this clearly loosens attachment.
The Kural delights in the beloved’s delicacy; Buddhism notes the same delicacy but reads it as a sign of how quickly beauty passes.
Jainism
“Do they look on a perishable body, i.e. the wise? and are they loud in praise of woman 's beauty?”
— Naladiyar 5.1 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poem Naladiyar opens its chapter on the impure body by asking whether the wise would ever loudly praise a woman’s beauty.
This is the exact act the Kural’s lover performs, and the Jain poet rejects it as a mistake born of attachment.
Sikhism
“You are the Emperor, and I call You a chief - how does this add to Your greatness?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 795, Raag Bilaval, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Bilaval, Guru Nanak says that any praise he can give the Lord falls short, like calling an emperor a mere chief.
The Kural praises the anicham flower as soft, then says the beloved is softer still; Guru Nanak likewise says every comparison falls short of the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
“I never yet saw any one so beautiful, neither man nor woman, and am lost in admiration as I behold you.”
— Homer, Odyssey 6 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Shipwrecked Odysseus, meeting the princess Nausicaa, addresses her with this superlative praise.
Like the Kural’s lover, Odysseus declares a young woman beyond comparison with anything he has known.
Philosophy
Edmund Burke argues that smoothness and softness are among the qualities that most constantly produce the feeling of beauty, in touch as well as in sight.
— Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757), Part III, sect. 14 'Smoothness' (paraphrase) [11]
For Burke, beauty is tied to qualities that please gently, such as smooth and soft surfaces, rather than to the rough or massive.
The Kural ranks the beloved’s beauty by its softness, exactly the quality Burke places near the heart of the beautiful.

Real Life Example

Robert Burns writes A Red, Red Rose
1794 · Scotland
In 1794 the Scottish poet Robert Burns produced the song now known as A Red, Red Rose. It compares the speaker's love to a red rose newly sprung in June and to a sweetly played melody, and goes on to promise that his love will last until the seas run dry. It became one of the best-known love lyrics in English and Scots.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The song shows how naturally poets in very different cultures reach for the freshest flower they know as the measure of the person they love.
The kural praises the delicate anicham flower and then says the beloved is more delicate still; Burns likewise reaches for the finest flower he knows, a rose just opened in June, as the measure of his love.
