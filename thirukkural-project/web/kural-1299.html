Kural 1299

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

துன்பத்திற்கு யாரே துணையாவார் தாமுடைய நெஞ்சந் துணையல் வழி.
Thunpaththirku yaarae thunaiyaavaar thaamudaiya nenjan thunaiyal vazhi.

Meaning

When you are in trouble, who will help you, if even your own heart does not?

Elementary

When you feel sad, the first one who can comfort you is you: you can tell yourself, ‘It will be okay.’ But if even your own feelings turn against you, who else can help? The wife asks this when her heart will not stand by her.

University

Tuṉpattiṟku yārē tuṇaiyāvār, ‘who indeed will be a companion in sorrow?’, with the emphatic -ē, and tām uṭaiya neñcam tuṇai al vaḻi, ‘when the heart that one owns is not a companion’. Here the wife generalises her complaint into a maxim (the plural tām gives it the force of ‘anyone’). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah stress tām urimaiyākap peṟṟa, ‘that one possesses by right’; Kalaignar reads tuṇai as ‘support to bear’ the sorrow. Pope renders ‘And who will aid me in my hour of grief, / If my own heart comes not to my relief?’ With 1300 this forms the chapter’s closing pair of general sayings. The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (thirukkural.net prints நெஞ்சந் with the same sandhi).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man must elevate himself by his own mind, not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna teaches that a person’s own mind is meant to be the helper that lifts him up.
The Kural assumes the same: one’s own heart should be the first companion in trouble.
Christianity
“The spirit of a man will sustain his infirmity; but a wounded spirit who can bear?”
— Proverbs 18:14 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says a person’s own spirit carries him through suffering, but nothing can help when that spirit itself is broken.
This is the Kural’s point almost exactly: if one’s own heart fails, who else can bear the sorrow?
Islam
“Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of Allah. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured.”
— Qur’an 13:28 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that a heart at peace is the source of steadiness.
The Kural shares the sense that the heart must be settled to sustain a person, though it sees no other source of help if the heart fails.
Judaism
“If I am not for myself, who is for me? But if I am for my own self [only], what am I? And if not now, when?”
— Pirkei Avot 1:14 (tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Hillel teaches that a person must first be his own support before he can look to others.
The Kural asks the same question: if one’s own heart will not stand by one, who will?
Buddhism
“One truly is the protector of oneself; who else could the protector be? With oneself fully controlled, one gains a mastery that is hard to gain.”
— Dhammapada 160 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that no one else can be one’s refuge if one is not one’s own.
The Kural uses the same ‘who else?’ question about one’s own heart as one’s companion.
Jainism
“Thus I became the protector of myself and of others besides, of all living beings, whether they move or not.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.35, with 20.23-30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Anathi tells how father, mother, brothers, sisters and loving wife all failed to relieve his pain, so that he learned to become his own protector.
As in the Kural, when trouble comes, no outside companion can take the place of one’s own self.
Sikhism
“He alone is called your companion, who will not be separated from you, here or hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 677, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan defines a true companion as the one who never leaves, which in Gurbani is God (and His Name).
The Kural asks who will stand by us in sorrow if our own heart does not; Gurbani answers that the only sure companion is the One who never departs.
Greek Mythology
Lying awake in his own palace among the suitors, Odysseus strikes his breast and speaks to his heart, telling it to endure, since it had endured worse on the day the Cyclops devoured his companions.
— Homer, Odyssey 20.17-21 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows a hero turning to his own heart as companion and support in a hard hour.
Odysseus’s heart stands by him as the Kural says one’s heart should; the wife’s, sadly, does not.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that the good person is of one mind with himself and is his own best friend, while the bad are at variance with themselves and cannot even be friends to themselves.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.4 and IX.8 (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, being a friend to oneself is the root of every other friendship.
The wife’s plight is the divided self Aristotle describes: her own heart is not her friend.

Real Life Example

Viktor Frankl holds on to the image of his wife in the camps
1944–1945 · Nazi concentration camps in Europe
The Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl was deported with his family in 1942 to Theresienstadt and in 1944 to Auschwitz; his wife later died at Bergen-Belsen. In his book Man’s Search for Meaning (1946) he described marching to a work site in the dark when a fellow prisoner whispered about their wives, and how his own mind then clung to his wife’s image with great vividness. He wrote that this inner image sustained him and taught him that love is the highest goal a person can aspire to.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Stripped of everything else, Frankl found that what he carried in his own heart could still keep him company in suffering.
The kural asks who will stand by one in sorrow if one’s own heart will not; Frankl’s experience shows the other side of the same truth, a heart that did stand by him when nothing else could.
