Kural 775

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

விழித்தகண் வேல்கொண டெறிய அழித்திமைப்பின் ஒட்டன்றோ வன்க ணவர்க்கு.
Vizhiththakan velkonda eriya azhiththimaippin Ottandro vankanavarkku.

Meaning

For truly brave soldiers, even blinking when the enemy’s spear comes at their angry eyes counts as running away.

Elementary

A goalkeeper facing a penalty kick tries hard not to flinch, because flinching means he can’t see the ball. The verse says a brave person keeps their eyes open and steady even when something scary is coming right at them.

University

Vizhiththa kan, the wide-open, glaring eye fixed on the foe, is set against azhiththu imaippin, “if it gives way and blinks,” when the enemy hurls (eriya) his spear (vel). For the vankanavar (the hard-eyed, stout-hearted), such a blink is ottu, which commentators gloss as tantamount to puRangodai, turning one’s back in flight; Kalaignar makes this equation explicit. The couplet sets an almost impossible standard to show that heroism is first an inner state: the smallest outward sign of fear already counts as defeat. The sandhi forms கொண டெறிய = kondu eriya and வன்க ணவர்க்கு = vankanavarkku; both base sources agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Pṛthā, do not yield to this degrading impotence. It does not become you. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise, O chastiser of the enemy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.3 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna rebukes Arjuna’s faltering at the start of battle as hrdaya-daurbalya, weakness of heart.
Arjuna’s hesitation is the inner “blink” the Kural says a true warrior must not allow.
Christianity
“Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God.”
— Hebrews 12:2 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews pictures steady, unwavering focus on Christ, who did not turn aside from suffering.
The fixed gaze that does not turn away echoes the Kural’s unblinking eye.
Islam
“O you who have believed, when you meet those who disbelieve advancing [for battle], do not turn to them your backs [in flight].”
— Qur’an 8:15 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an forbids fleeing when the enemy advances.
The Kural pushes the same prohibition inward: even a blink is a kind of turning back.
Judaism
“Is there anyone afraid and disheartened? Let him go back to his home, lest the courage of his comrades flag like his.”
— Deuteronomy 20:8 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Torah sends the fearful home before battle so that their fear does not spread through the ranks.
Both texts treat a soldier’s inner fear as already a breach in the line.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The wise remain unmoved by the forces that buffet others.
The Kural’s unblinking warrior and the Buddha’s unshaken rock share the ideal of steadiness, though the Buddha applies it to the mind, not the battlefield.
Jainism
“In a burial-place, or a deserted house, or below a tree he should sit down, alone, without moving, and he should not drive away any one. Sitting there he should brave all dangers; when seized with fear, he should not rise and go to some other place.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.20–21 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
A monk meditating alone must not move from his seat even when danger and fear come; leaving it would be a retreat.
The Kural says that for the truly brave even a blink before a flying spear counts as flight, and the Jain rule likewise treats the smallest yielding to fear as a failure of nerve.
Sikhism
Bhai Mati Das went with Guru Tegh Bahadur to Delhi in 1675 and was arrested with him. When he refused to give up his faith he was sawn in two from the head downwards; Sikh tradition adds that he went on reciting Japji to the end.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, 'Mati Das, Bhai (d. 1675)' (paraphrase); the Japji detail follows devotional tradition [9]
Bhai Mati Das is remembered daily in the Sikh Ardas among those who did not waver under the saw.
The Kural says that for the truly brave even a blink before the enemy's spear counts as retreat; Bhai Mati Das is honoured precisely because he showed no sign of flinching under torture.
Greek Mythology
Tyrtaeus urges the Spartan hoplite to stand firm with feet planted and lip bitten, eyes on the enemy, and never to turn away.
— Tyrtaeus, fragments 10-12 West (paraphrase) [10]
The Spartan war-poet makes holding one’s ground under the enemy’s charge the defining mark of the good man.
Tyrtaeus and the Kural both locate courage in not flinching at the decisive moment.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Laches, the general Laches first defines courage as a man’s willingness to remain at his post and face the enemy without running away.
— Plato, Laches 190e (paraphrase) [11]
Socrates goes on to show the definition is too narrow, but it captures the popular ideal of steadfastness.
The Kural’s unblinking eye is the most demanding form of “remaining at one’s post.”

Real Life Example

Theodore Roosevelt delivers his speech after being shot
14 October 1912 · Milwaukee, Wisconsin, United States
While campaigning for president as the Progressive Party candidate, Theodore Roosevelt was shot in the chest by a would-be assassin as he left his hotel in Milwaukee. The bullet passed through his folded speech manuscript and metal glasses case and lodged near his ribs. Refusing to go to hospital first, Roosevelt went on to the auditorium and spoke for about an hour, showing the audience the bullet-pierced manuscript, before he accepted medical treatment.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Roosevelt treated the attack as no reason to flinch from what he had come to do, and the bullet remained in his body for the rest of his life.
The kural says that for the truly brave even a blink in the face of the enemy's spear counts as retreat, and Roosevelt would not let a bullet make him turn from his audience.
