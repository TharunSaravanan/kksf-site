Kural 471

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

வினைவலியும் தன்வலியும் மாற்றான் வலியும் துணைவலியும் தூக்கிச் செயல்.
Vinaivaliyum thanvaliyum maatraan valiyum Thunaivaliyum thookkich cheyal.

Meaning

Before you act, compare four strengths: how much strength the job needs, how strong you are, how strong your opponent is, and how strong the helpers on each side are.

Elementary

Before you start something big, stop and measure four things: how hard the job is, how strong you are, how strong the other side is, and who will help each of you. Only after weighing all four should you decide to go ahead.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a four-part audit: vinai-vali (the strength the undertaking requires), tan-vali (one’s own strength), maatraan-vali (the adversary’s) and thunai-vali (the strength of allies, which Parimelazhagar reads as the allies of both parties). The verb thookki, ‘weighing as on a balance’, governs all four terms, so action (seyal) is justified only by comparison. Assessing yourself alone is not enough. This is the centre of the Kural’s political realism, and it closely parallels Kautilya’s Arthaśāstra, whose ninth book opens with a chapter on knowing power, place, time, strength and weakness before any campaign. Prudence here is not timidity. It is what makes effective action possible.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthaśāstra opens its book on the conqueror’s campaigns with a chapter on knowing power, place, time, strength and weakness. There the would-be invader compares his own powers with the enemy’s: the power of counsel, the power of treasury and army, and the power of energy and valour. He marches only when that comparison favours him.
— Kautilya, Arthaśāstra, Book IX, Chapter 1 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [4]
Kautilya makes assessment of relative strength a formal step that must come before any war. The king has to know both his own resources and the enemy’s, and has to account for place and season too.
This is almost exactly Valluvar’s fourfold weighing. Valluvar even adds allies (thunai-vali) as a separate item on the scale.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand? Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace.”
— Luke 14:31–32 (KJV) [5]
Jesus uses a king comparing troop numbers to illustrate counting the cost before committing. A king who finds himself outmatched negotiates instead of fighting.
It pairs one’s own strength against the opponent’s before acting, which is the core of the Kural’s weighing. The Kural adds the task’s strength and allies’ strength as further items.
Islam
“Now, Allah has lightened [the hardship] for you, and He knows that among you is weakness. So if there are from you one hundred [who are] steadfast, they will overcome two hundred. And if there are among you a thousand, they will overcome two thousand by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the steadfast.”
— Qur’an 8:66 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse sets a realistic ratio of strength for the believers’ military obligation. It takes their actual weakness into account and does not demand the impossible.
Like the Kural, it treats a sober calculation of one’s own force against the enemy’s as the basis for deciding whether to fight.
Judaism
“Every purpose is established by counsel: and with good advice make war.”
— Proverbs 20:18 (KJV) [5]
The proverb insists that war, of all undertakings, must be entered only after deliberation and sound advice.
It matches the Kural’s demand that action follow careful weighing, though the four strengths are not spelled out.
Buddhism
King Ajātasattu, planning to attack the Vajjians, sends his minister Vassakāra to ask the Buddha what will happen. The Buddha asks Ānanda whether the Vajjians still keep seven conditions of welfare, including meeting often, acting in concord and honouring their elders. He concludes that while they keep them, they can be expected to prosper and not decline.
— Dīgha Nikāya 16, Mahāparinibbāna Sutta 1.1–1.5 [7]
The episode is in effect an assessment of the opponent’s real strength. The minister leaves understanding that the Vajjians cannot be conquered by force while they remain united.
It dramatises maatraan-vali, knowing the enemy’s actual strength before attacking, which is one of the Kural’s four weights.
Jainism
“Balabalam janan : Aware of one's own strength and weakness.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, list of the thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, 'Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code' (jainworld.com) [8]
One of the thirty-five marks of the ideal Jain layman in Hemacandra's Yogashastra is knowing one's own strength and weakness. It sits next to far-sightedness and avoiding action unsuited to time and place.
The Kural asks a person to weigh their own strength against the demands of the task before acting, and Hemacandra names that self-assessment as a basic householder virtue.
Sikhism
“No power to speak, no power to keep silent. … He alone has the Power in His Hands. He watches over all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 7, Japji Sahib (pauri 33), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the thirty-third pauri of Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak denies that any human has power of their own, even to speak or keep silent; all power is in God's hand.
The Kural tells the actor to weigh the strengths of task, self, enemy and allies; Guru Nanak teaches that every such strength finally rests with God.
Greek Mythology
Before the Achaeans march on Troy, old Nestor advises Agamemnon to array the army by tribes and clans so that each group supports its own. That way he will learn which chiefs and men are brave and which are cowards, and whether any failure comes from heaven or from human weakness.
— Homer, Iliad 2.360–368 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Nestor proposes organising the army so that its real strength, including the reliability of allies and contingents, becomes visible before the decisive fight.
It reflects the Kural’s concern with knowing one’s own strength and one’s allies’ strength (thunai-vali) before acting.
Philosophy
“If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War III.18 (tr. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu holds that victory follows from accurate knowledge of both one’s own condition and the enemy’s. Ignorance of either brings defeat.
This is the closest single-sentence analogue to the Kural’s instruction to weigh one’s own and the opponent’s strength before acting.

Real Life Example

Japan secures the Anglo-Japanese Alliance before facing Russia
1902-1904 · London, United Kingdom, and Tokyo, Japan
In 1902 Japan concluded an alliance with Britain under which each partner would stay neutral if the other went to war with one power, but would come to its aid if a second power joined in against it. With this safeguard against a coalition, the Japanese government took a firmer line against Russian advances in Manchuria and Korea, and in 1904 it went to war with Russia.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Japan measured not only its own forces against Russia's but also the allies each side could call upon, and arranged matters so that it would face Russia alone.
The kural tells a ruler to weigh the task, his own strength, the enemy's strength and the strength of the allies on each side, and the alliance was Japan's way of fixing that last term in its favour before acting.
