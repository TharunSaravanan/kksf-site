Kural 1275

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

செறிதொடி செய்திறந்த கள்ளம் உறுதுயர் தீர்க்கும் மருந்தொன்று உடைத்து.
Seridhodi seydhirandha kallam urudhuyar theerkkum marundhondru udaiththu.

Meaning

As she left, my love with the tight bangles gave me a secret sign. That sign holds a medicine that cures my deep sorrow.

Elementary

Sometimes a friend gives you a small wink or a smile that only you understand, and it makes you feel better right away. The poet says the secret look his beloved gave him was like medicine for his sadness.

University

The heroine is named by her ceṟi toṭi (closely set bangles), and what she did and left behind (ceytu iṟanta) is a kaḷḷam, a stealthy or covert sign. That sign holds one maruntu (medicine) that cures uṟu tuyar, great sorrow. Varadarasanar and Kesikan read kaḷḷam as a covert sign; Solomon Pappaiah stresses that it was shown so that only he would understand it; Kalaignar makes it her mischievous glance. The traditional reading has the husband, anxious about her reaction to his journey, comforted by a hidden sign of her consent or reassurance. Both sites give the same text; ytamizh’s English explanation (‘The well-meant departure of her whose bangles are tight-fitting...’) is a loose rendering of ceytu iṟanta, while Pope’s couplet calls the sign ‘secret wiles’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the tenth canto, Krishna pleads with the offended Radha and begs her to place her tender foot on his head, calling that gesture the antidote that destroys the poison of love.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 10 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva makes a single gesture from the beloved the cure for the lover’s suffering.
The Kural, too, finds the cure for great sorrow in one small sign from the beloved.
Christianity
“Stay me with flagons, comfort me with apples: for I am sick of love.”
— Song of Solomon 2:5 (KJV) [4]
The bride calls herself lovesick and asks for remedies to restore her.
Both texts treat love’s sorrow as an illness needing a cure; in the Kural the cure is the beloved’s secret sign.
Islam
“Take this, my shirt, and cast it over the face of my father; he will become seeing.”
— Qur’an 12:93 (Saheeh International) [5]
Joseph sends his shirt as a token whose touch will heal his father’s grief-blinded eyes.
As in the Kural, a token from the one longed for becomes the medicine for long sorrow.
Judaism
“Hope deferred maketh the heart sick; but desire fulfilled is a tree of life.”
— Proverbs 13:12 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs says waiting sickens the heart, while a hope fulfilled restores life.
The beloved’s sign in the Kural works the same way, turning sickening worry into healing reassurance.
Buddhism
When Nanda pines for the beautiful Janapada Kalyani he left behind, the Buddha cures his longing by showing him the nymphs of the heavens, so that Nanda turns to the holy life.
— Udana 3.2, Nanda Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha treats lovesickness as a malady and applies a skilful remedy to cure it.
The Kural also speaks of a cure for love’s distress, but for Valluvar the cure is the beloved’s sign, while the Buddha’s cure leads away from her.
Jainism
“Afterwards he will feel remorse like one who has drunk milk mixed with poison; considering the consequences, a worthy monk should have no intercourse with women.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
For the Jain monk, what seems sweet in a woman’s company is a poison disguised as milk.
The Kural calls the beloved’s secret sign a medicine; the Jain text inverts the image, warning that such sweetness is poison for one seeking liberation.
Sikhism
“The Holy Saint has given me the Medicine of the Naam.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 101, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says the Saint gave him the Name of the Lord as a medicine that cures the soul's suffering.
The Kural says the secret sign the beloved gave as she left holds a medicine for deep sorrow; Guru Arjan likewise speaks of a gift from another that heals sorrow.
Greek Mythology
Helen puts into the wine a drug from Egypt that banishes grief and anger, so that whoever drinks it sheds no tear that day even for the dearest loss.
— Homer, Odyssey 4.219-226 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer imagines a medicine that dissolves sorrow.
The Kural’s secret sign does what Helen’s drug does: it cures sorrow at once.
Philosophy
Socrates describes the lover who finds in the beautiful beloved the only physician for his greatest pains.
— Plato, Phaedrus 252a (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
In the Phaedrus the beloved’s presence alone eases the anguish love produces.
The Kural makes the beloved’s sign the medicine for the lover’s sorrow, just as Plato makes the beloved the physician.

Real Life Example

American prisoners of war adopt the tap code
June-August 1965 · Hoa Lo Prison, Hanoi, North Vietnam
In June 1965 four American prisoners sharing a cell in Hoa Lo Prison, including Air Force Captain Carlyle Harris, who had learned the method from an instructor, began using a tap code in which letters were set in a five-by-five grid and sent as two groups of taps. When the guards separated them, they taught the code to others, and by August 1965 most of the prisoners knew it. The code spread through the camps and helped the prisoners keep up morale and their chain of command.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A secret sign that only the prisoners understood became their main source of comfort and companionship in isolation.
The kural's husband finds that the covert sign his beloved left him holds a medicine for his deep sorrow; for isolated prisoners, the secret taps on the wall were just such a medicine.
