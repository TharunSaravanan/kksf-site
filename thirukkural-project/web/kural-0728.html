Kural 728

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

பல்லவை கற்றும் பயமிலரே நல்லவையுள் நன்கு செலச்சொல்லா தார்.
Pallavai katrum payamilare nallavaiyul Nanku selachchollaa thaar.

Meaning

No matter how many subjects they have studied, people who cannot share good things clearly with a good group are of no use.

Elementary

A treasure chest that stays locked forever is no better than an empty box. If you learn lots of things but never share them clearly, nobody gets any good out of them.

University

The couplet plays on the syllable avai: pal-l-avai (“many things”) and nal-l-avai (“a good assembly”) echo each other. Payam ilar means “without benefit.” Here payam is the classical form of payaṉ (use, fruit), not the modern Tamil word for fear, a false friend worth pointing out in a chapter about fear. Nalla avai is the assembly of good or learned people, and naṉku cela-c-collutal again means speech that reaches and persuades (see 722). Solomon Pappaiah adds that such a person is useless to the world, extending the loss from the individual to society. The sandhi text reads “நல்லவையு / ணன்கு.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Taittiriya Upanishad pairs every duty with svādhyāya-pravacana, one’s own study together with teaching it to others. In the graduation address the teacher tells the departing student not to neglect study and teaching.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.9 and 1.11 (trans. F. Max Müller, Sacred Books of the East 15) [3]
In the Vedic ideal, learning is incomplete unless it is also passed on to others.
Learning that is never expounded falls short, which is why the Kural calls the silent scholar useless.
Christianity
“Neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel, but on a candlestick; and it giveth light unto all that are in the house.”
— Matthew 5:15 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says light is meant to be seen; hiding it defeats its purpose.
Hidden learning, like a covered lamp, benefits no one.
Islam
“He who is asked something he knows and conceals it will have a bridle of fire put on him on the Day of Resurrection.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 3658 (graded hasan sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet warns that withholding knowledge when asked is a serious fault.
Knowledge that is not conveyed fails its purpose, as the Kural says of the learned person who cannot speak.
Judaism
Moses tells God that he is slow of speech and slow of tongue. God answers by appointing Aaron as his spokesman, so that what Moses knows can reach the people.
— Exodus 4:10-16 [6]
Even Moses’ knowledge needed a voice before it could help anyone.
Learning has to be spoken well in order to be of use, which is the Kural’s point.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that a great deal of textual learning brings no benefit if it is never put to use.
Learning much but gaining nothing from it is the Kural’s verdict on the scholar who cannot speak.
Jainism
“Therefore, seeker after the highest truth, study the sacred lore, in order to cause yourself and others to attain perfection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The closing verse of 'The Very Learned' says the purpose of study is the good of others as well as oneself.
The Kural says many subjects studied are useless if one cannot put them well before a good assembly; the Jain verse similarly holds that learning is meant to benefit others, not only its owner.
Sikhism
“You may read and read loads of books; you may read and study vast multitudes of books. … O Nanak, only one thing is of any account: everything else is useless babbling and idle talk in ego.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 467, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa ki Vaar, Guru Nanak says cartloads of reading count for nothing without the one thing that matters, the Name.
The Kural says however many subjects one has studied, one is useless without the power to present good things to a good assembly; Guru Nanak too says vast reading alone is of no account.
Greek Mythology
“the gods do not grace all men alike in speech, person, and understanding. One man may be of weak presence, but heaven has adorned this with such a good conversation that he charms every one who sees him; his honeyed moderation carries his hearers with him so that he is leader in all assemblies of his fellows”
— Homer, Odyssey 8 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus tells Euryalus, who is handsome but tactless, that real standing in the assembly comes from skill in speech.
Only those who can speak well lead in the assembly, and other gifts count for little without that skill.
Philosophy
Isocrates, the most famous teacher of rhetoric in Athens, admits that he lacked a strong voice and the boldness needed to face crowds. So he never addressed the assembly himself and worked through written speeches and teaching instead.
— Isocrates, Panathenaicus 9-10 [11]
Here is a man of great learning who could not deliver it before the assembly in person.
This is a real case of the Kural’s learned man unable to speak in the assembly.

Real Life Example

Josiah Willard Gibbs's great work on thermodynamics goes long unappreciated
1876 to 1878 · New Haven, Connecticut, United States
Between 1876 and 1878 Josiah Willard Gibbs of Yale published On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances, a long paper that introduced the phase rule and is now seen as a landmark of physical chemistry. It appeared in the Transactions of the Connecticut Academy of Arts and Sciences in a dense, highly mathematical form that most contemporaries found very hard to follow. Its value was recognised only gradually, helped by James Clerk Maxwell and by later translations into German in 1892 and French in 1899.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gibbs's ideas were among the most important of his century, yet because they were not put in a form most scientists could grasp, years passed before they had their full effect.
The kural warns that however much a person has learned, it bears no fruit if it is not set persuasively before the assembly, and the slow reception of Gibbs's treatise shows that cost.
