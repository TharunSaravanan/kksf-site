Kural 1282

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

தினைத்துணையும் ஊடாமை வேண்டும் பனைத்துணையும் காமம் நிறைய வரின்.
Thinaiththunaiyum oodaamai vaendum panaiththunaiyum kaamam niraiya varin.

Meaning

When love comes in full, as tall as a palm tree, you should not sulk even as much as a tiny grain of millet.

Elementary

If you have a mountain of love for someone, it makes no sense to stay grumpy with them over a crumb-sized thing. The poet compares the love to a tall palm tree and the grumpiness to a tiny seed.

University

The couplet sets two proverbial Tamil measures against each other: tiṉai (Italian millet, the smallest grain) and paṉai (the palmyra palm, the tallest tree), each with tuṇai (‘measure, extent’). When kāmam comes niṟaiya (‘in fullness’) to the measure of a palmyra, one should not engage in ūṭal (lovers’ feigned sulking) even to the measure of a millet seed. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it as general advice, while Solomon Pappaiah applies it specifically to the wife and her husband; in the traditional reading the heroine says this when her companion suggests she show displeasure at her husband’s long absence. Ūṭal is otherwise a valued convention of akam poetry (the final chapters 130-133 are devoted to it), so the couplet’s point is that eagerness for reunion overrides it here. Pope’s translation (‘Distrust can find no place small as the millet grain’) renders ūṭal loosely as distrust. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna, coming to Radha after she has withdrawn in wounded pride, pleads with her to give up her anger, telling her that her displeasure torments him and asking her to let love win over pique.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 10 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s Radha nurses mana (proud sulking) against Krishna, and Krishna begs her to set it aside.
The Kural says the same: where love is great, even a little sulking has no place.
Christianity
“Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil;”
— 1 Corinthians 13:4-5 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as patient and slow to take offence.
The Kural’s advice not to sulk even a little when love is great is a lover’s version of love that ‘is not easily provoked’.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes marriage as a bond of tranquillity, affection and mercy.
The Kural’s ideal of spouses whose love leaves no room for petty sulking fits this picture of affection and mercy between mates.
Judaism
“Hatred stirreth up strifes; but love covereth all transgressions.”
— Proverbs 10:12 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs teaches that love passes over offences while hatred feeds quarrels.
As in the Kural, great love leaves no room for nursing a grievance.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada teaches that resentment is ended only by letting it go.
The Kural asks the lover to drop even the smallest resentment; the Buddhist verse gives the general principle, though in a far graver register.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra lists anger as the destroyer of love (priti), pride as the destroyer of humility, deceit as the destroyer of friendship, and greed as the destroyer of everything.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.38 (verse numbering varies by edition; paraphrase) [8]
Jain teaching names anger (krodha), one of the four passions (kashayas), as the specific enemy of affection.
The Kural warns against even a millet-grain of sulking because it cuts against great love; the Jain verse explains why: anger destroys love.
Sikhism
“Don't be angry - drink in the Ambrosial Nectar; you shall not remain in this world forever.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 931, Raag Raamkalee, Dakhni Oankar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak tells the listener to give up anger and drink in the nectar of the Name, because life is short.
The Kural says that when love is as tall as a palm tree one should not sulk even a millet-grain's worth; Guru Nanak likewise counsels dropping anger in favour of the sweetness of love while there is time.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, the poor old couple who welcomed the disguised gods, had lived together in perfect accord since youth, and asked only that neither should outlive the other.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s tale portrays a marriage so united that no discord ever came between the pair.
They are the picture of the Kural’s ideal: love so full that no sulking finds room.
Philosophy
Plutarch advises newly married couples to guard against small quarrels and petty grievances, since harmony (homonoia) between husband and wife is the foundation of their shared life.
— Plutarch, Conjugal Precepts (Moralia 138A-146A) (paraphrase) [11]
His Conjugal Precepts repeatedly counsel spouses to let minor offences go quickly.
The Kural’s advice to sulk not even a millet-grain’s worth is the same counsel, given in a lover’s voice.

Real Life Example

Young Gandhi’s jealous quarrels with Kasturba
1880s (early years of their marriage, which began in 1882) · Kathiawar, western India
Mohandas Gandhi and Kasturba were married in 1882, when both were about thirteen. In his autobiography, in the chapter Playing the Husband, Gandhi wrote that he soon became a jealous husband without any reason to suspect her, and insisted that she could not go anywhere without his permission. Kasturba did not accept these restrictions, and Gandhi recalled that the two child-spouses often ended up refusing to speak to each other; he later called her innocent in taking liberties with his rules and admitted his strictness had been misguided.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Gandhi himself presented these quarrels as the foolishness of a young husband whose petty suspicions were out of all proportion to the bond between them, a bond that lasted the rest of their lives.
The kural says that when love is as tall as a palmyra palm one should not sulk even as much as a millet seed, and Gandhi looked back on his small-minded jealous sulks as exactly that kind of mistake.
