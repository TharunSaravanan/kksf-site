Kural 739

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

நாடென்ப நாடா வளத்தன நாடல்ல நாட வளந்தரு நாடு.
Naadenpa naadaa valaththana naadalla naada valandharu naadu.

Meaning

Wise people say a true country is one whose riches come without having to search for them. A land that gives riches only after hard searching is not a true country.

Elementary

Some gardens grow fruit almost by themselves because the soil and rain are just right. Other gardens need you to dig, water and fight weeds every day for a tiny crop. Valluvar says the best country is like the first garden.

University

The kural is built on a pun between naadu (‘country’) and the verb naadu (‘to seek, to search for’): naadaa valaththana are lands ‘whose wealth is not sought’, and naada valandharu naadu is a land ‘that gives wealth only when sought’. Parimelazhagar explains that the best lands produce their riches by natural endowment, without great exertion by the inhabitants, while lands that yield only to heavy labour are not counted as countries in the full sense. The line is not a praise of idleness, since the Kural elsewhere (chapter 62, Aalvinaiyudaimai) insists on effort; it is a judgment on natural endowment. Kalaignar makes this explicit by contrasting lands naturally rich with lands that prosper only through constant struggle.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Mahabharata’s description of the Uttarakurus, the northern land of the blessed, tells of trees that bear fruit and flowers in every season and yield whatever the people need, so that they live in ease without labouring for their food.
— Mahabharata, Bhishma Parva, Jambu-khanda section (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Uttarakuru is the Indian image of a land that gives everything by nature.
Uttarakuru is the extreme case of the Kural’s ideal: a land whose wealth comes without being sought.
Christianity
“For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself; first the blade, then the ear, after that the full corn in the ear.”
— Mark 4:28 (KJV) [4]
In a parable of the kingdom of God, Jesus describes seed growing by itself while the farmer sleeps, without his knowing how.
‘Fruit of herself’ is close to the Kural’s naadaa valam, wealth that grows without being laboriously sought.
Islam
“And Allāh presents an example: a city [i.e., Makkah] which was safe and secure, its provision coming to it in abundance from every location, but it denied the favors of Allāh. So Allāh made it taste the envelopment of hunger and fear for what they had been doing.”
— Qur’an 16:112 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes a city whose provision came to it plentifully from everywhere, until its ingratitude brought hunger and fear.
Provision ‘coming to it’ from every side is the Kural’s land whose wealth arrives without being sought.
Judaism
“For the land, whither thou goest in to possess it, is not as the land of Egypt, from whence ye came out, where thou didst sow thy seed, and didst water it with thy foot, as a garden of herbs; but the land, whither ye go over to possess it, is a land of hills and valleys, and drinketh water as the rain of heaven cometh down.”
— Deuteronomy 11:10-11 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Moses contrasts Egypt, where fields must be watered by hard irrigation work, with the promised land, which is watered by rain from heaven.
This is the Kural’s very contrast: a land that yields only through toil versus a land that yields by its own nature.
Buddhism
In the Agganna Sutta the Buddha tells how, at the beginning of the world, rice grew by itself, clean and ready to eat, and whatever was gathered in the evening grew back by morning. Only when beings began to hoard it did the self-growing rice disappear, so that they had to plough, sow and divide the fields.
— Digha Nikaya 27, Agganna Sutta [7]
The sutta explains the origin of labour, property and kingship as a fall from a time of effortless plenty.
The self-growing rice is the Buddhist picture of wealth that comes without being sought, the Kural’s ideal.
Jainism
“The others are called the ‘regions of enjoyment’–bhogabhūmi as the objects of enjoyment are provided by the ten kinds of desire-fulfilling trees (kalpavṛkṣa).”
— Tattvartha Sutra 3.37, with Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Pujyapada contrasts the lands of labour (karmabhumi), where agriculture and the other occupations are needed, with the lands of enjoyment (bhogabhumi), where wish-fulfilling trees supply everything without effort.
The Kural ranks highest the lands whose wealth comes without being sought; Jain cosmology has exactly such lands, though it also teaches that liberation can be won only in the lands of labour.
Sikhism
“Why, O mind, do you plot and plan, when the Dear Lord Himself provides for your care? From rocks and stones He created living beings; He places their nourishment before them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 10, So Dar (Rehras), Raag Gujri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the So Dar portion of the evening prayer, Guru Arjan tells the anxious mind that God has already set sustenance before every creature, even those born among rocks and stones.
The Kural honours the land whose wealth comes without being laboriously sought; Guru Arjan teaches that provision is already laid out by the Creator before it is sought.
Greek Mythology
“For the fruitful earth unforced bare them fruit abundantly and without stint. They dwelt in ease and peace upon their lands with many good things.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 116-119 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod describes the golden race of men under Cronos, who lived without toil because the earth fed them of its own accord.
‘The earth unforced’ is almost a translation of the Kural’s naadaa valam, wealth that is not sought.
Philosophy
In the Statesman, Plato’s Stranger tells the myth of the age of Cronus, when people had fruit in plenty from trees and plants that the earth gave of its own accord, without farming, and needed no political constitutions.
— Plato, Statesman 271d-272b [11]
Plato uses the myth to contrast a divinely cared-for age of plenty with the present age, in which people must work and govern themselves.
Plato’s myth gives a philosophical frame to the Kural’s contrast between lands rich by nature and lands rich only through toil.

Real Life Example

Oil is struck at Burgan, Kuwait
February 1938 · Burgan field, Kuwait
Surface seepages of bitumen at Burgan had long hinted at oil, and in 1934 the ruler of Kuwait granted a concession to the Kuwait Oil Company, a joint venture of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company and Gulf Oil. Burgan No. 1, drilled at a seepage beginning in 1936, struck a high-pressure oil-bearing sandstone in February 1938. Exports began in 1946, and the Great Burgan field became one of the largest in the world, the main source of Kuwait's wealth.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A small country's prosperity came above all from the riches already lying beneath its soil.
The kural honours lands whose wealth comes from their natural endowment rather than from endless toil, and Kuwait's fortune from the Burgan field is a modern example of riches given by the land itself.
