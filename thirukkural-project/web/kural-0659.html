Kural 659

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

அழக்கொண்ட எல்லாம் அழப்போம் இழப்பினும் பிற்பயக்கும் நற்பா லவை.
Azhakkonda ellaam azhappoam izhappinum Pirpayakkum narpaa lavai.

Meaning

Everything gained by making others cry will be lost and leave you crying. Things gained in good ways may be lost for a while, but they will pay off later.

Elementary

If you take a toy by making another child cry, that toy will bring you trouble and tears in the end. But if you get something fairly and then lose it, good things will still come back to you later.

University

The first line hinges on the double use of aḻa, ‘so as to weep’: what was taken (koṇṭa) while making others weep will depart (pōm) making the taker weep. It is a compact statement of moral retribution. The second line balances it: naṟ-pālavai, ‘things of a good kind’, meaning wealth obtained by fair means, even if lost (iḻappiṉum), will give fruit afterwards (piṟ-payakkum). Parimelazhagar and Solomon Pappaiah read this ‘fruit’ as a return of benefit in this life or merit in the next, and Kalaignar says such wealth ‘comes back again’. The verse sharpens the chapter’s theme of means into a law of consequences. ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi form அழக்கொண்ட வெல்லா மழப்போ மிழப்பினும் / பிற்பயக்கு நற்பா லவை.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the rigged dice game the Kauravas win the Pandavas’ kingdom and drag Draupadi into the assembly in tears; her humiliation and curse are later repaid when the Kurukshetra war destroys the Kaurava line, and the Pandavas regain what they lost.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (the dice game) and its sequel [4]
Wealth and power taken through deceit and cruelty lead to the ruin of those who took them, while the wronged side is in the end restored.
The epic plays out both halves of the Kural: what is taken with tears goes with tears, and what is rightly held, though lost, returns.
Christianity
“Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth.”
— James 5:4 (KJV), with 5:1-3 [5]
James warns rich oppressors that the cries of the workers they cheated have reached God, and that their hoarded riches will rot.
The cries of the wronged, answered by the ruin of the wrongdoer, match the Kural’s ‘taken with tears, gone with tears’.
Islam
“Allah destroys interest and gives increase for charities. And Allah does not like every sinning disbeliever.”
— Quran 2:276 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran teaches that gain from exploitation (ribā) will be wiped out, while wealth given away in good faith grows.
Both texts set two opposite laws side by side: exploitative gain vanishes, and rightly used wealth brings later increase.
Judaism
Jeremiah compares the one who gets riches unjustly to a partridge that sits on eggs it did not lay: in the middle of his days the riches leave him, and at his end he is a fool.
— Jeremiah 17:11 (JPS 1917) [7]
The prophet’s image shows ill-gotten wealth deserting its holder before he can enjoy it.
This matches the first line of the Kural: what was wrongly taken will not stay.
Buddhism
“Ill done is that action of doing which one repents later, and the fruit of which one, weeping, reaps with tears.”
— Dhammapada 67 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada says a wrong action is known by the fruit it bears: the doer later reaps it in tears.
The Kural’s play on weeping (aḻa) has a close echo in this verse’s ‘weeping, reaps with tears’.
Jainism
“Like as when the glittering spear that he cast is caught by his enemy's hand, the theif's courage is destroyed, so since the gains of sin follow after and destroy the acquirer of these gains in two births, it is good to leave the ignorant altogether.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 13 'The fear of misconduct', stanza 9 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar teaches that sinful gains turn back on the one who acquired them, like a spear caught and turned by the enemy, and ruin him in this birth and the next.
The kural says what is taken by making others weep will leave the taker weeping; the Naladiyar's spear that comes back on the thrower shows the same return of wrongful gain on its owner.
Sikhism
“It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In his hymns on Babur's invasion Guru Nanak looks at the riches the defeated rulers lost and says that such wealth is gathered only through sin and departs from its owners in the end.
The Kural says whatever is taken by making others weep will leave its taker weeping; Guru Nanak watched wealth gained by oppression being stripped from its holders amid lament.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Hecuba, the Thracian king Polymestor murders the young Trojan prince Polydorus, entrusted to his care, to keep the boy’s gold; the grieving Hecuba lures him in, kills his sons and blinds him.
— Euripides, Hecuba [11]
Gold taken through a mother’s tears ends by bringing the same bereavement to its taker.
This is a vivid tragic version of the Kural’s first line: what is taken with tears goes with tears.
Philosophy
Lady Philosophy tells Boethius that the gifts of Fortune were never truly his, that she takes back what she gives, and that only goods of the mind cannot be taken away.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2 [12]
Boethius separates outside goods, which always slip away, from inner goods, which last.
The Kural’s ‘good things’ that return after loss are close to Boethius’ goods that Fortune cannot take away.

Real Life Example

Napoleon's looted art is returned from the Louvre
1815 · Paris, France
During his campaigns Napoleon's armies carried off great quantities of art from across Europe, often through treaties imposed on defeated states, to fill the Musee Napoleon at the Louvre, including the Horses of St Mark's from Venice and Raphael's Transfiguration from Rome. After his final defeat in 1815 the Allies, with the Duke of Wellington and the sculptor Antonio Canova as envoy for the Papal States playing leading roles, had much of this art removed from the Louvre and returned to its owners, while Parisians looked on in anger. Roughly half the seized works went back, the bronze horses reaching Venice in December 1815.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The collection had been gathered by force and to the grief of those who lost it, and it was stripped away again amid the grief of those who had come to see it as a symbol of national glory.
The kural says that what is taken by making others weep will go away and leave the taker weeping, and the dismantling of Napoleon's museum in 1815 is a vivid historical case.
