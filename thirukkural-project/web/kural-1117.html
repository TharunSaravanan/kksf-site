Kural 1117

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

அறுவாய் நிறைந்த அவிர்மதிக்குப் போல மறுவுண்டோ மாதர் முகத்து.
Aruvaai niraindha avirmadhikkup poala maruvundoa maadhar mukaththu.

Meaning

The bright moon grows full again after shrinking, yet it has a dark spot. Is there any such mark on this woman's face?

Elementary

The moon is lovely, but if you look closely you can see dark patches on it. The poet looks at his beloved’s face and says, 'The moon has spots, but her face has none at all.'

University

This kural answers the confusion of 1116: why should the stars be confused, when the face differs from the moon? Aruvaai niraindha (அறுவாய் நிறைந்த, 'whose cut-away parts have filled up') describes the moon that waxes after waning, and avir madhi (அவிர் மதி, 'shining moon') its full splendour; yet the moon has a maru (மறு, 'blemish, spot', the dark marks traditionally seen as a hare), which the woman’s face lacks. Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan make the link with 1116 explicit ('why should the stars be troubled?'); Sivayogi Sivakumar reads maru as 'change', an outlier gloss. The rhetorical question (வினா) carries the force of denial, as in Parimelazhagar’s construal. Pope: 'In moon, that waxing waning shines, as spots appear, / Are any spots discerned in face of maiden here?' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The moon in added splendour / Shines for its spot of dark; / Yet more the maiden slender / Charms in her dress of bark.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
Kalidasa’s king says that just as the moon’s dark spot only adds to its splendour, Shakuntala’s plain bark dress only adds to her charm.
Both poets set the beloved beside the moon’s spot: Kalidasa makes the flaw an ornament, the Kural says her face has no flaw at all.
Christianity
“Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee.”
— Song of Solomon 4:7 (KJV) [4]
The lover of the Song declares his beloved wholly beautiful and without blemish.
This is almost the Kural’s own claim: the beloved, unlike the moon, has no spot.
Islam
“And the moon - We have determined for it phases, until it returns [appearing] like the old date stalk.”
— Qur’an 36:39 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an points to the moon’s waxing and waning as a sign of God’s ordering of creation.
The Kural too notes the moon’s waning and filling out; Islam reads the phases as a sign, the Kural as a mark of the moon’s imperfection beside her face.
Judaism
“the maiden was of beautiful form and fair to look on”
— Esther 2:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Book of Esther introduces its heroine with a simple statement of her beauty.
Esther is presented as beautiful without qualification, as the Kural’s heroine is praised as having no blemish.
Buddhism
“He, who, like the moon, is spotless and pure, serene and clear, who has destroyed the delight in existence — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 413 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares the perfected person to a spotless, clear moon.
The Kural denies the moon is spotless in order to praise a face; the Dhammapada calls the moon spotless in order to praise a purified mind.
Jainism
“Even the hare in the wide, beauteous, and sublime heavens, since it is seen in the moon with refulgent beams, will be adored (by men).”
— Naladiyar 18.6 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar notes that the dark hare-mark in the moon is admired only because it sits in the bright moon, and applies this to people raised by good company.
The same hare-mark is the 'spot' the Kural says the beloved’s face is free of.
Sikhism
“He has no stain, so I have no pollution.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 391, Raag Asa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Asa, Guru Arjan says the Lord is untouched by any stain of Maya, and the devotee who belongs to Him becomes stainless too.
The Kural says the moon carries a spot but the beloved's face has none; Guru Arjan calls the Beloved Lord wholly without stain.
Greek Mythology
The Homeric Hymn to Selene praises the Moon goddess, whose radiance spreads over the earth when she bathes and puts on shining garments, and is brightest when her orb is full.
— Homeric Hymn 32, To Selene (paraphrase) [10]
Greek hymns treat the full moon as a figure of supreme radiance.
The Kural concedes the full moon’s brilliance before claiming that her face surpasses it by having no blemish.
Philosophy
Diotima describes beauty itself as everlasting, neither coming to be nor passing away, neither waxing nor waning, and not beautiful in one part and ugly in another.
— Plato, Symposium 211a (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato, true beauty is free of the changes and flaws found in beautiful things.
The Kural’s moon waxes, wanes and bears a spot; the beloved’s face, like Plato’s ideal, is praised as without blemish.

Real Life Example

Galileo shows that the Moon is not flawless
1609–1610 · Venice, Republic of Venice (now Italy)
From late November to mid-December 1609 Galileo Galilei observed and sketched the Moon through his telescope. In Sidereus Nuncius (The Starry Messenger), printed in Venice in March 1610, he reported that the Moon's surface was not smooth and perfect, as followers of Aristotle had taught, but rough and uneven, with dark markings, mountains and hollows.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Galileo's observation helped overturn the old belief that the heavens were perfect and unchanging.
The kural asks whether the beloved's face carries any blemish like the dark spots on the bright moon; Galileo's telescope showed that the moon really is marked and uneven, the very flaw the poet says her face does not share.
