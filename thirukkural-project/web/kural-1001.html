Kural 1001

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

வைத்தான்வாய் சான்ற பெரும்பொருள் அஃதுண்ணான் செத்தான் செயக்கிடந்தது இல்.
Vaiththaanvaai Saandra Perumporul Aqdhunnaan Seththaan Seyakkitandhadhu Il

Meaning

A person who fills their house with huge riches but never uses them is as good as dead, because those riches do nothing at all.

Elementary

Imagine a boy who fills his whole room with sweets but never eats one and never shares one. The sweets just sit there until they go stale. Valluvar says a person who piles up money like that and never uses it might as well not be there at all.

University

The chapter title நன்றியில் செல்வம் uses நன்றி in its older sense of 'benefit, good' rather than 'gratitude': this is wealth that does no good to anyone. In vaittaan-vaay (வைத்தான்வாய்) வாய் means 'place', so the wealth 'fills the place where he stored it'; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses இடமெல்லாம் நிறைந்த, and Puliyur Kesikan (thirukkural.net) adds that the man does not eat it 'through avarice' (உலோபத்தால்). The second line is elliptical: seththaan (செத்தான்) can be read either as 'he is (equivalent to) a dead man', as Solomon Pappaiah and Pope take it ('Is dead'), or as 'when he dies, there is nothing left done (by the wealth)', as Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar render it; both readings yield the same verdict that unused wealth achieves nothing. The chapter stands in the Kudiyiyal after Panbudaimai (ch. 100), turning from courtesy to the social uselessness of hoarded riches, and complements the earlier chapters on Ithal (ch. 23) and Oppuravu (ch. 22). The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Yajnavalkya prepares to divide his property and leave home, his wife Maitreyi asks whether the whole earth full of wealth would make her immortal; he answers that her life would merely be like that of the rich, for there is no hope of immortality through wealth.
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.1–2 (Yajnavalkya–Maitreyi dialogue) [3]
The Upanishad teaches that riches, however vast, cannot purchase what matters most and are left behind at death.
Like the Kural, it exposes wealth as powerless at the moment of death, so a life spent only storing it accomplishes nothing.
Christianity
Jesus tells of a rich man whose land yielded so much that he planned bigger barns to store it all and then take his ease; God calls him a fool, for that very night his soul is required of him, and the question is who will own what he has stored.
— Luke 12:16–21 (Parable of the Rich Fool) [4]
The parable condemns laying up treasure for oneself without being rich toward God or others.
Like the Kural's hoarder, the rich fool dies beside his full storehouses having done nothing with them.
Islam
The Qur'an urges believers to spend from what God has provided before death comes, when a person will plead to be granted a short delay so that he could give charity and be among the righteous.
— Qur'an 63:10 (Al-Munafiqun) [5]
Death ends the chance to make wealth do good, and the hoarder's regret comes too late.
It matches the Kural's picture of a man who dies with his wealth untouched and nothing accomplished.
Judaism
“Every person will have to justify himself for everything his eye saw and which he did not eat.”
— Jerusalem Talmud, Kiddushin 4:12 (tr. Heinrich W. Guggenheimer, via Sefaria) [6]
Rav teaches that refusing the lawful good things of life is itself something one must answer for; the Talmud adds that Rabbi Eleazar saved coins so he could taste every kind of food once a year.
The Kural likewise counts it a failure to own abundance and never enjoy it.
Buddhism
“Just now, lord, a money-lending householder died in Savatthi. I have come from conveying his heirless fortune to the royal palace: eight million in silver, to say nothing of the gold. But even though he was a money-lending householder, his enjoyment of food was like this: he ate broken rice & pickle brine.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 3.19, Aputtaka Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight) [7]
King Pasenadi reports a rich miser who lived like a pauper and died, his fortune passing to the crown; the Buddha says such wealth, not properly used, goes to waste.
This is the Kural's hoarder exactly: immense riches, none enjoyed, and nothing achieved by them at death.
Jainism
“He who employs not his time in enjoying his property, or gives not of it to ascetics, but lays it up (like a miser)--at him, the foolish one, about to perish, his hoarded wealth shall mock, and the favour of the world shall mock.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 28 ('Illiberality'), st. 3 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil quatrain pictures the hoarder's own treasure mocking him as death approaches.
It makes the same point as the Kural: wealth neither enjoyed nor given leaves its dying owner with nothing.
Sikhism
“Gathering in and hoarding what they can, the faithless cynics die, O Nanak, but the wealth of Maya does not go with them in the end.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 257, Raag Gauri, Bavan Akhri (salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Bavan Akhri, Guru Arjan describes those who spend their lives piling up wealth, die, and find that none of it goes with them.
The Kural's hoarder fills his house and dies with nothing done through his wealth; Guru Arjan pictures the same person dying in the middle of his gathering.
Greek Mythology
King Midas is granted his wish that whatever he touches turn to gold, and finds that his bread and wine also turn to gold, so that the richest man alive faces starvation amid his treasure until he begs the god to take the gift back.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85–145 (Midas) [10]
The myth shows that wealth which cannot be used for life's ordinary goods is worthless, even deadly.
Like the Kural's miser, Midas is surrounded by riches that do nothing for him and bring him close to death.
Philosophy
“Misers, he said, took care of property as if it belonged to them, but derived no more benefit from it than if it belonged to others.”
— Diogenes Laërtius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 4.50 (Bion), tr. R. D. Hicks (Wikisource) [11]
The Cynic Bion of Borysthenes mocks misers as mere caretakers of goods they never actually use.
The Kural likewise says the hoarder gets nothing from the wealth he guards, as if he were already dead to it.

Real Life Example

Hetty Green, America's richest woman, lives in cheap lodgings
1865-1916 · New York City and Hoboken, New Jersey, United States
Hetty Green inherited about 10 million dollars in 1865 and, through shrewd investment and lending on Wall Street, became reputedly the wealthiest woman in the United States, leaving an estate of more than 100 million dollars when she died in 1916. Despite her fortune she lived with her children in inexpensive rented lodgings, for years in a small apartment in Hoboken, avoiding any display of wealth and virtually all society. Newspapers circulated many stories of her frugality, some of them exaggerated, and she became known as the Witch of Wall Street.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Green's vast fortune grew year after year while she herself lived as though she had very little of it.
The kural says that a man who fills his house with vast wealth and never enjoys it is as good as dead, since his wealth does nothing through him, and Green amassed immense riches that she scarcely allowed herself to use.
