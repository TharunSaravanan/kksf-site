Kural 577

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

கண்ணோட்டம் இல்லவர் கண்ணிலர் கண்ணுடையார் கண்ணோட்டம் இன்மையும் இல்.
Kannottam illavar kannilar kannudaiyaar Kannottam inmaiyum il.

Meaning

People without kindness are really without eyes. And people who truly have eyes are never without kindness.

Elementary

Imagine your friend drops their lunch and everyone looks away, but one kid runs over to help. Valluvar would say only that kid truly ‘saw’. Having real eyes means noticing people and caring about them; if you never care, it is as if you have no eyes at all.

University

The couplet is a chiasmus built on the single word கண்: those without கண்ணோட்டம் are கண்ணிலர் (‘eyeless’), and those who are கண்ணுடையார் (‘possessed of eyes’) are never without கண்ணோட்டம். Parimelazhagar reads it as a definition by convertibility: in the moral sense, having eyes and having kannottam are one and the same thing, so that physical sight without kindness does not count as sight. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah render it almost identically, while Kalaignar reverses the emphasis, saying the eyes of the compassionate alone deserve the name. With 576 it completes a sequence (573-577) that systematically strips the unkind eye of every value. Both sources agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has sandhi forms கண்ணோட்ட மில்லவர் … மின்மையு மில்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The humble sages, by virtue of true knowledge, see with equal vision a learned and gentle brāhmaṇa, a cow, an elephant, a dog and a dog-eater [outcaste].”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.18 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
The Gita says the truly wise are sama-darśinaḥ, ‘those who see with equal vision’, regarding every being with the same respect.
For both texts, real seeing is a moral kind of sight; only the one who regards all beings rightly truly has eyes.
Christianity
“But when he saw the multitudes, he was moved with compassion on them, because they fainted, and were scattered abroad, as sheep having no shepherd.”
— Matthew 9:36 (KJV) [4]
In the Gospels, Jesus’ seeing the crowds leads directly into compassion.
This is the Kural’s claim in narrative form: one who truly sees cannot be without compassion.
Islam
“They have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. Those are like livestock; rather, they are more astray.”
— Quran 7:179 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran describes those who have working senses but do not use them rightly as worse than cattle.
Like the Kural, it says people with eyes that do not truly see are in effect eyeless.
Judaism
“A good eye, a humble spirit and a moderate appetite he is of the disciples of Abraham, our father. An evil eye, a haughty spirit and a limitless appetite he is of the disciples of Balaam, the wicked.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:19 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah makes the ‘good eye’, a generous and kindly way of seeing others, the first mark of a true disciple of Abraham.
As in the Kural, the eye that counts is the good, benevolent eye; the other kind belongs to the wicked.
Buddhism
The Buddha speaks of three eyes: the fleshly eye, the divine eye, and the eye of discernment, and teaches that the eye of discernment is the highest.
— Itivuttaka 61 (Cakkhu Sutta) [7]
Buddhist teaching separates physical sight from the higher ‘eyes’ that see truly.
Valluvar likewise separates the physical eye from the true eye, which in his case is the eye of kindness.
Jainism
“Without (right) faith there is no (right) knowledge, without (right) knowledge there is no virtuous conduct, without virtues there is no deliverance, and without deliverance there is no perfection.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 28.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's road to deliverance begins with right vision or faith (samyag-darshana), without which nothing else counts as true knowing or right action.
The Kural says that those without kindly regard are really eyeless; Jainism likewise separates physical sight from right 'seeing' (darshana), which alone counts as true vision.
Sikhism
“Do not call them blind, who have no eyes in their face. They alone are blind, O Nanak, who wander away from their Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 954, Raag Raamkalee (Raamkalee ki Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad redefines blindness: the truly blind are not those without physical sight but those who have strayed from God.
The Kural says those without kindly regard are really eyeless, and those who truly have eyes always have it; Guru Angad makes the same move of redefining sight and blindness in moral terms.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ Oedipus the King, the blind prophet Tiresias sees the truth, while Oedipus, with perfect eyesight, cannot see what he has done until he blinds himself.
— Sophocles, Oedipus the King [10]
The play turns on the irony that the sighted man is truly blind and the blind man truly sees.
The Kural’s paradox that the sighted can be ‘eyeless’ has its Greek counterpart in Oedipus and Tiresias.
Philosophy
“He is a runaway, who flies from social reason; he is blind, who shuts the eyes of the understanding; … he is a piece rent asunder from the state, who tears his own soul from that of reasonable animals, which is one.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book IV, §29 (tr. George Long) [11]
Marcus lists the ways a person fails his own nature: whoever cuts himself off from the shared reason and fellowship of rational beings is a fugitive, blind, and torn from the whole.
Like the Kural, Marcus calls the one who withdraws from fellow-feeling blind, though his blindness is of the understanding rather than a lack of kindly regard.

Real Life Example

Bartolome de las Casas gives up his encomienda in Cuba
1514 · Cuba
Bartolome de las Casas had come to the Caribbean as a settler, served as a chaplain in the Spanish conquest of Cuba, and was granted land and Indigenous labourers under the encomienda system. Influenced by Dominican friars who denounced the treatment of the Indigenous people, he renounced his encomienda and freed the people assigned to him around 1514, and began preaching that other colonists should do the same. When his preaching in Cuba had little effect he went to Spain to plead the case at court, and he spent the rest of his long life defending the Indigenous peoples of the Americas.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Las Casas had lived among the same suffering for years before he truly saw it; once he did, he could not look away for the rest of his life.
The kural says those without kindly regard are really eyeless and those who truly have eyes are never without it, and Las Casas's turning point was the moment his sight became real sight.
