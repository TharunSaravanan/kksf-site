Kural 1315

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

இம்மைப் பிறப்பில் பிரியலம் என்றேனாக் கண்நிறை நீர்கொண் டனள்.
Immaip pirappil piriyalam endraenaak kannirai neerkon danal

Meaning

When I said, "We will never be apart in this life," her eyes filled with tears, as if I meant we would part in the next life.

Elementary

Imagine telling your grandmother, ‘I will visit you every day this summer!’ and she asks, sadly, ‘Only this summer?’ The wife hears ‘in this life’ and worries it means ‘not in the next one’.

University

Immaip piṟappil means ‘in this present birth’ and piriyalam ‘we shall not part’; the wife takes the limiting phrase to imply that in a later birth (maṟumai) they will part, and her eyes fill (kaṇ niṟai nīr koṇṭaṉaḷ). The couplet assumes the belief in rebirth shared by the Tamil tradition and depends on the idea that true lovers should remain together across births. Puliyur Kesikan, Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah all read it this way, while Kalaignar has her ask, more sceptically, whether there is a next birth at all. Pope: ‘“While here I live, I leave you not,” I said to calm her fears. She cried, “There, then, I read your thought”; and straight dissolved in tears.’ The text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, when Yama comes to take the soul of Satyavan, his wife Savitri follows the god of death and, through her steadfast devotion and wisdom, wins her husband back from him.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Pativrata-mahatmya (Savitri story) (tr. K.M. Ganguli; paraphrase) [3]
Savitri refuses to let even death part her from her husband.
The wife in the Kural weeps because she wants exactly what Savitri won: a union that does not end with this life.
Christianity
“For in the resurrection they neither marry, nor are given in marriage, but are as the angels of God in heaven.”
— Matthew 22:30 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that marriage as we know it belongs to this age and does not continue in the same form after the resurrection.
This is the very thought that makes the Kural’s wife weep: that the bond of this life might not carry into the next.
Islam
“Gardens of perpetual residence; they will enter them with whoever were righteous among their fathers, their spouses and their descendants.”
— Qur’an 13:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an promises that righteous spouses will be reunited in Paradise.
It offers the hope the Kural’s wife longs for: that husband and wife will not be parted after this life.
Judaism
“Concerning anyone who divorces his first wife, even the altar sheds tears about him.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 22a (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Eliezer teaches that the parting of husband and wife is so grievous that even the altar weeps.
The wife’s tears at the mere thought of a future parting echo this rabbinic sense that separation of spouses calls for weeping.
Buddhism
“We want to see one another not only in the present life but also in the life to come.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Nakula’s parents tell the Buddha they wish to be together in future lives, and he explains that spouses matched in faith, virtue, generosity and discernment will meet again.
This is exactly the Kural wife’s wish: not only this life together, but the next one too.
Jainism
“Râgîmatî thought: ‘Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him! it is better I should turn nun.’”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28-29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
When Neminatha renounced the world on his wedding day, his bride Rajimati was overwhelmed with grief at being parted from him, and then followed him into the ascetic life.
The Kural’s wife weeps at the mere thought of a parting; Rajimati lived that parting and met it by following her beloved on the same path.
Sikhism
“This human body has been given to you. This is your chance to meet the Lord of the Universe.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan teaches that this present human life is the precious opportunity for union with God.
The Kural's husband promises they will not part in this birth and his wife weeps at the limit; Gurbani also treats this present life as the decisive time for union.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, the old couple Baucis and Philemon ask the gods that they may die at the same hour so neither must see the other’s grave, and they are turned together into an oak and a linden.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.703-720 (paraphrase) [10]
Their one wish is never to be parted, not even by death.
The Kural’s wife weeps because she wants the same thing: a love that no life or death divides.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes imagines Hephaestus offering two lovers to weld them into one, so that they live as one and, when they die, remain one even in Hades.
— Plato, Symposium 192d-e (paraphrase) [11]
Aristophanes’ myth says lovers long to be united not only in life but beyond it.
The wife’s tears express the same longing: she will not settle for a union that ends with this life.

Real Life Example

Etruscan artists make the Sarcophagus of the Spouses
c. 520-510 BCE · Cerveteri, Italy
In the Etruscan city of Cerveteri, artists modelled a nearly life-size terracotta sarcophagus showing a husband and wife reclining side by side on a banqueting couch, close and affectionate, as if sharing a meal. The work, now in the Louvre, has a nearly identical companion in the Villa Giulia museum in Rome, which was discovered near Cerveteri on 9 April 1881. Scholars note that the figures may once have held objects for the journey from this life to the next.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sarcophagus is one of the best-known ancient images of a married couple who expected to stay together beyond death.
The wife in this kural weeps because she hears in never part in this life a hint that they might part in the next, and the Etruscan couple show the opposite hope, of being together after this life too.
