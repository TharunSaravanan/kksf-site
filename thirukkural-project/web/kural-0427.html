Kural 427

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

அறிவுடையார் ஆவ தறிவார் அறிவிலார் அஃதறி கல்லா தவர்.
Arivudaiyaar aava tharivaar arivilaar Aqdhari kallaa thavar.

Meaning

Wise people know what is going to happen before it happens. Unwise people cannot know it.

Elementary

If you see dark clouds, you know rain is coming and take an umbrella. A wise person is good at spotting what is going to happen next, while a foolish person gets caught by surprise every time.

University

This couplet defines wisdom and its lack by one criterion: foresight. ஆவது அறிவார் means 'they know what will come to be'. The second line mirrors it: the unwise are precisely those who cannot know that (அஃது அறிகல்லாதவர்). Commentators take ஆவது to mean the outcome or consequence of present actions and circumstances, not supernatural prophecy. Arivu here is predictive practical judgment, the ability to read causes forward to their effects. The Talmudic definition 'who is wise? One who sees what will be born' is almost a translation. Aristotle's phronesis includes deliberating well about what conduces to the good in the long run, and Hesiod's Prometheus (forethought) and Epimetheus (afterthought) personify the Kural's opposition. The couplet also prepares for 429, where foresight becomes protection.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.37 [4]
Krishna teaches that sattvic understanding looks past immediate appearances to eventual outcomes, choosing what is bitter now but sweet in the end.
Both texts define higher understanding by the ability to see how things will turn out.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?”
— Luke 14:28-30 (KJV) [5]
Jesus points to the ordinary prudence of thinking ahead to where a project will end before starting it. He contrasts this with the builder who is mocked for being unable to finish.
Both texts define good sense as knowing in advance how things will turn out.
Islam
The Prophet said: the wise (al-kayyis) is one who holds himself to account and works for what comes after death. The incapable (al-'ajiz) is one who follows his desires and merely hopes in Allah.
— Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2459 [6]
The hadith defines intelligence by forward-looking action and folly by living only for the moment.
Both texts contrast the wise, who look ahead to what is coming, with the foolish, who cannot.
Judaism
The Talmud asks, 'Who is wise?' and answers, 'One who sees what will be born' (ha-ro'eh et ha-nolad), meaning one who foresees the outcome of things.
— Babylonian Talmud, Tamid 32a (cf. Pirkei Avot 2:9) [7]
In Alexander the Great's dialogue with the elders of the Negev, the rabbis define wisdom as foresight of consequences.
This is almost identical to the Kural: the wise know what will come to be.
Buddhism
In the Future Dangers suttas the Buddha urges monks to consider dangers not yet arrived (old age, illness, famine, strife, corruption of the teaching) and to strive now, while conditions are favourable.
— Anagata-bhaya Suttas, Anguttara Nikaya 5.77-78 [8]
The wise practitioner looks ahead to what is coming and acts before it arrives.
Both texts see knowing what is coming as the mark of wisdom.
Jainism
“'If he does not get (victory over his will) early, he will get it afterwards;' such reasoning presupposes the eternity of human life. But such a man despairs when his life draws to its close, and the dissolution of his body approaches.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
Mahavira's discourse on carelessness mocks the man who keeps putting off self-conquest as if life will last forever. Such a person fails to see what is coming and is left in despair when death arrives.
The Kural says the wise know beforehand what will come and the unwise cannot; the Jain verse gives the unwise person's typical mistake, reasoning as if the future will never arrive.
Sikhism
“If someone focuses on the Lord beforehand, then why should he be punished?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In one of the Babarvani hymns on Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak says that people who are mindful beforehand would not suffer such punishment; Sahib Singh reads it as keeping one's duty in mind in good time.
The Kural says the wise know beforehand what is coming; Guru Nanak says that being mindful in good time spares one the suffering that comes later.
Greek Mythology
Prometheus ('Forethought') warns his brother Epimetheus ('Afterthought') never to accept a gift from Zeus. Epimetheus accepts Pandora anyway and understands the harm only after it has come.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 83-89 [11]
Hesiod builds the two brothers' names and fates on the contrast between foresight and hindsight.
This personifies the Kural exactly: the wise know what will come; the unwise know only too late.
Philosophy
“It is thought to be the mark of a man of practical wisdom to be able to deliberate well about what is good and expedient for himself... about what sorts of thing conduce to the good life in general.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 6.5, 1140a (tr. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle defines phronesis as sound deliberation about what leads to good ends, which requires foreseeing consequences.
Both texts see practical wisdom as seeing where actions lead.

Real Life Example

Themistocles persuades Athens to build a fleet from its silver
483-480 BCE · Athens, Greece
When a rich new vein of silver was found at the state mines of Laurium in about 483 BCE, the Athenians would normally have shared out the income among the citizens. Themistocles persuaded them instead to spend it on a large fleet of warships, officially for the ongoing war with the island of Aegina. When the Persian king Xerxes invaded Greece in 480 BCE, Athens had about 200 triremes, and this fleet played the leading part in the Greek naval victory at Salamis.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Turning a windfall into a navy before the danger arrived gave Greece the means to defeat the largest invasion it had ever faced.
The kural says the wise know beforehand what is going to happen, and Themistocles had Athens build its fleet before the Persian invasion came.
