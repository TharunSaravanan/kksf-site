Kural 746

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

எல்லாப் பொருளும் உடைத்தாய் இடத்துதவும் நல்லாள் உடையது அரண்.
Ellaap porulum udaiththaai idaththuthavum nallaal udaiyathu aran.

Meaning

A true fort has everything that may be needed, and good, brave soldiers who will help when danger comes.

Elementary

A good school on a stormy day has torches, blankets and food in the cupboard. But it also needs calm grown-ups who know what to do when the lights go out. Supplies alone are not enough; you need good people too. Valluvar says the same about a fort.

University

The first line (ellaap porulum udaiththaai, ‘having all things’) sums up the material requirements of 745, and the second line brings in the human element that the chapter’s last couplet will make decisive: idaththu uthavum nallaal, ‘good men who help at the (critical) place or time’. Idam can mean both the spot where danger arises and the moment of need, and Varadarasanar reads it as the moment of crisis in battle. Nallaal (nal + aal, ‘good person’) is read by all the commentators as brave warriors: Pappaiah speaks of heroes who can defeat enemies trying to destroy the fort from outside, and Kalaignar of strong fighters who leap into battle. The pairing of stores and soldiers reflects the arthashastra and Dharmashastra habit of listing a fort’s supplies and its garrison together. The sandhi-joined text reads ‘எல்லாப் பொருளு முடைத்தா யிடத்துதவு / நல்லா ளுடைய தரண்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him build (there) a town, making for his safety a fortress, protected by a desert, or a fortress built of (stone and) earth, or one protected by water or trees, or one (formed by an encampment of armed) men or a hill-fort.”
— Manusmriti 7.70 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Among the six kinds of fort, Manu includes one made of nothing but armed men (the nri-durga).
Manu treats good fighting men as a fortress in their own right, which is the Kural’s point about ‘good men who help’.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand?”
— Luke 14:31 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says a wise king counts his soldiers before going to war.
Like the Kural, it treats having enough capable fighters as basic to military success.
Islam
“And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allah and your enemy.”
— Qur’an 8:60 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an tells the believers to prepare every kind of strength and cavalry they can to deter their enemies.
Being ready with ‘whatever you are able of power’ matches the Kural’s fort that has all things and able men.
Judaism
“From that day on, half my servants did work and half held lances and shields, bows and armor.”
— Nehemiah 4:10 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition 2023, via Sefaria; KJV 4:16) [6]
While Jerusalem’s wall was being rebuilt, Nehemiah kept half his men armed and on guard.
A wall needs armed men ready to defend it, which is the Kural’s ‘good men who help in need’.
Buddhism
“Furthermore, the royal frontier fortress has a large army stationed within — elephant soldiers, cavalry, charioteers, bowmen, standard-bearers, billeting officers, soldiers of the supply corps, noted princes, commando heroes, infantry, & slaves.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The fifth requisite of the Buddha’s fortress is a full garrison of every kind of soldier stationed inside.
This is the Kural’s ‘nallaal’, the fighters who make the stored fort useful.
Jainism
“On both sides of the east gate of the first rampart stood two gold-colored Vaimānika-gods as door-keepers.” … “At the four gates of the second wall, in the same order beginning with the east, stood the goddesses Jayā, Vijayā, Ajitā and Aparājitā, all having one hand in the position bestowing fearlessness …”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra 1.3, part 10 ‘Description of a samavasaraṇa’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS, 1931; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In Hemacandra's account of Rishabha's assembly hall, every gate of every rampart is held by divine guardians, and the goddesses at the second wall raise the hand that grants fearlessness to those within.
The Kural's fort is complete only when it has good and valiant defenders to help in danger, and the Jain samavasarana likewise stations guardians at each gate who give security to all inside.
Sikhism
At Anandpur, which Guru Gobind Singh had fortified, the Khalsa was given concrete form at the Baisakhi gathering of 1699: the Panj Piare, five Sikhs of different castes and regions, were initiated first, and the Khalsa were enjoined to succour the helpless and fight the oppressor, to have faith in one God and to consider all human beings equal.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entry Gobind Singh, Guru [9]
Guru Gobind Singh founded a disciplined, casteless fellowship of saint-soldiers whose duty was to stand by the weak in the hour of danger.
The Kural says a true fortress holds everything needed and good, brave men who help when danger comes; the Khalsa gave the fortress of Anandpur exactly such defenders.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Seven Against Thebes, as seven enemy champions prepare to storm the seven gates of Thebes, King Eteocles chooses a matching Theban champion to hold each gate, himself taking the seventh.
— Aeschylus, Seven Against Thebes [10]
The safety of Thebes depends on posting a fit defender at every gate.
The walls of Thebes are only as good as the men who help at each point of danger, which is the Kural’s ‘idaththu uthavum nallaal’.
Philosophy
“for when men are well prepared no enemy even thinks of attacking them.”
— Aristotle, Politics VII.11 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle ends his discussion of city defenses by saying that good preparation discourages attack in the first place.
A fort fully stocked and well manned, as the Kural requires, is one that enemies do not dare to attack.

Real Life Example

King George VI awards the George Cross to the island of Malta
15 April 1942 · Malta
From 1940 Malta was under almost constant air attack, and from January to July 1942 there was only one 24-hour period when no bombs fell; food, fuel and ammunition ran so short that the people came close to starvation and spent long periods in underground shelters. On 15 April 1942 King George VI awarded the George Cross to the island and its people in recognition of their courage under repeated attack. Later in 1942 the Operation Pedestal convoy fought its way through to Malta with vital supplies, despite the loss of many of its ships.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Malta's survival depended on both supplies brought in at great cost and the endurance of the people who held on under bombardment.
Valluvar says a true fortress holds every needed thing and has brave people who help when danger comes, and the island fortress of Malta survived only when both came together.
