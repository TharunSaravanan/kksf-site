Kural 476

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

நுனிக்கொம்பர் ஏறினார் அஃதிறந் தூக்கின் உயிர்க்கிறுதி ஆகி விடும்.
Nunikkombar erinaar ahdhirandhu ookkin Uyirkkirudhi aagi vidum.

Meaning

A person who has climbed to the very tip of a tree branch and tries to climb even higher will fall and lose their life.

Elementary

Imagine climbing a tree right out to the thinnest tip of a branch. If you try to go even further, the branch breaks and you fall. You need to know when you’ve gone as high as you safely can.

University

Nunikkombar, the thin end of a branch, marks the exact limit of what one’s strength, and the support one relies on, can bear. Commentators apply it to a ruler who has already stretched his power as far as it will go: any further push (ahdhu irandhu ookkin, ‘if he strives beyond it’) becomes fatal. The verse pairs with 475. There an external load exceeds capacity; here one’s own ambition exceeds the support beneath. The image is precise: the danger is greatest exactly when one is highest.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Triśaṅku wants to ascend to heaven in his own body. Refused by his guru Vasiṣṭha and cursed by Vasiṣṭha’s sons, he turns to Viśvāmitra, whose ascetic power lifts him up. The gods throw him back down, and he is left suspended head-down in a heaven of his own between earth and the gods’ realm.
— Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Bāla Kāṇḍa, sargas 57–60 [4]
Triśaṅku has already reached the height of a king’s station and tries to climb beyond the limit set for mortals. He ends suspended, neither risen nor safe.
It is a mythic version of trying to climb past the branch-tip.
Christianity
“Jesus said unto him, It is written again, Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God.”
— Matthew 4:5–7 (KJV) [5]
Set on the pinnacle of the Temple and urged to throw himself down trusting in rescue, Jesus refuses to go beyond the safe limit on the strength of presumption.
Standing at the highest point, he declines to overreach, which is the restraint the Kural urges on the climber at the branch-tip.
Islam
“And do not walk upon the earth exultantly. Indeed, you will never tear the earth [apart], and you will never reach the mountains in height.”
— Qur’an 17:37 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an reminds human beings of the fixed limits of their stature and strength against pride that imagines no ceiling.
Like the Kural, it names a height beyond which human striving cannot go.
Judaism
“Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.”
— Genesis 11:4 (KJV) [5]
The builders of Babel try to push human achievement past its proper limit. Their project is halted and they are scattered.
It is a biblical form of the Kural’s climbing past the tip: reaching for more than the limit allows ends in collapse.
Buddhism
Devadatta, already a respected monk with psychic powers and the patronage of Prince Ajātasattu, asks to take over leadership of the Saṅgha from the Buddha. When refused, he plots against the Buddha’s life and splits the order. The tradition records that he fell into ruin.
— Vinaya Piṭaka, Cullavagga VII (Saṅghabhedakkhandhaka) [7]
Having reached a high position, Devadatta reaches beyond it and destroys himself.
It illustrates the Kural’s warning that striving past the limit one has already reached is fatal.
Jainism
“The more you get, the more you want; your desires increase with your means. Though two māṣas would do to supply your want, still you would scarcely think ten millions sufficient.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 8.17, with Jacobi's note on the commentary's story of Kapila (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In the commentary's story, the student Kapila needs only two pieces of gold, but once a king offers him whatever he asks, he keeps raising the sum until he suddenly sees his folly and renounces.
Kapila's mounting wish is the Kural's climber who reaches the tip of the branch and still strives higher; in both, going past the proper limit is the danger.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, do not be so proud of your tall mansions. Today or tomorrow, you shall lie beneath the ground, and the grass shall grow above you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1366, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 38) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in the Guru Granth Sahib, Bhagat Kabir warns that whatever height one reaches is temporary and the fall to the ground is sure.
The Kural warns that climbing beyond the branch tip is fatal; Kabir warns that those proud of their height will soon lie low.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus makes wings of feathers and wax for himself and his son Icarus and warns him to fly a middle course, neither too low near the sea nor too high near the sun. Icarus, thrilled by flight, soars upward. The sun softens the wax, the feathers fall away, and he plunges into the sea.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.183–235 [10]
Icarus climbs past the height his means can bear and dies for it.
This is the closest mythic parallel to the Kural’s climber who goes beyond the branch-tip.
Philosophy
Artabanus warns Xerxes against invading Greece. He points out that the god strikes the tallest creatures, buildings and trees with his thunderbolt, and brings low whatever towers above the rest.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.10ε (tr. A. D. Godley) [11]
Herodotus’ wise adviser argues that greatness pushed further exposes one to catastrophe.
It matches the Kural’s insight that danger is greatest at the highest point and that pressing further brings destruction.

Real Life Example

The Ottoman siege of Vienna fails
July - 12 September 1683 · Vienna, Austria
The Ottoman grand vizier Kara Mustafa Pasha led a vast army to besiege Vienna in July 1683. On 12 September a relief army under King John III Sobieski of Poland attacked at the Kahlenberg and routed the besiegers. Kara Mustafa was put to death in Belgrade on the sultan's orders on 25 December 1683, and in the wars that followed the Ottomans lost large territories in central Europe.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The siege marked the furthest point of a long Ottoman advance, and the attempt to push beyond it began a long retreat.
The kural says that those who climb beyond the tip of the branch fall to their death, and Kara Mustafa's push to Vienna went past the limit his empire could support and cost him his life.
