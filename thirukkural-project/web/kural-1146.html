Kural 1146

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

கண்டது மன்னும் ஒருநாள் அலர்மன்னும் திங்களைப் பாம்புகொண் டற்று.
kaNdadhu mannum orunhaaL alarmannum thingaLaip paampukoN tatru

Meaning

I saw my love only once, yet the rumor has spread everywhere, like the news when the snake swallows the moon (an old way of describing an eclipse).

Elementary

When there is an eclipse and the moon goes dark, everybody in the whole town talks about it at once. The girl in this verse says she met her sweetheart just one single time, and now the whole town is talking about them, as if it were an eclipse!

University

Here the speaker changes. Parimelazhagar says the heroine, kept from the hero by obstacles and failed trysts, speaks to the tōḻi while the hero is within earshot (ciṟaippuṟam), announcing the rumour to press him to marry. Kaṇṭatu ‘what I saw’ took place ‘on one day’ (orunāḷ), but the alar is ‘like the serpent seizing the moon’ (tiṅkaḷaip pāmpu koṇṭaṟṟu), the eclipse caused by Rāhu that everyone sees and talks about; Parimelazhagar notes that the effect (the universal talk) is described by naming the cause, and that both maṉṉum and the um are expletives (acai nilai). His moral is that a love that draws rumour even without meetings cannot go on, and must end in marriage. Kalaignar glosses the image explicitly as the eclipse (kirakaṇam).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And from that time there is a long-standing quarrel between Rahu’s head and Surya and Soma. And to this day it swalloweth Surya and Soma (during solar and lunar eclipses).”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section 19 (Astika Parva) (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [4]
The Mahabharata tells how the demon Rahu was beheaded for tasting the nectar of immortality and ever since swallows the sun and moon at eclipses.
This is the myth behind the Kural’s image of the serpent seizing the moon, an event the whole world sees at once, just as the whole town hears of the lovers’ single meeting.
Christianity
After Jesus heals a leper and tells him to say nothing, the man goes out and begins to spread the story everywhere, so that Jesus can no longer openly enter a city.
— Mark 1:40-45 (KJV) (paraphrase) [5]
A single private event becomes, through one man’s telling, the talk of the whole region.
The Kural’s heroine experiences the same thing: one private meeting becomes the talk of the whole town.
Islam
“When you received it with your tongues and said with your mouths that of which you had no knowledge and thought it was insignificant while it was, in the sight of Allāh, tremendous.”
— Qur’an 24:15 (tr. Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an describes, in the setting of the slander against Aisha, how a rumour passes from tongue to tongue among people who know nothing of the truth.
The Kural’s heroine sees the same mechanism: a rumour about one meeting passes from mouth to mouth until it is everywhere.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that an eclipse of the moon is a bad omen, a sign seen and feared by all the world.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 29a (paraphrase) [7]
For the rabbis an eclipse is a public event that everybody notices and interprets.
The Kural also uses the eclipse as the image of something everyone sees and talks about at once.
Buddhism
“Now at that time the Moon God had been seized by Rāhu, lord of titans.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 2.9 (Candima Sutta) (tr. Bhikkhu Sujato) [8]
The Moon God, seized by Rahu, calls on the Buddha for refuge, and Rahu releases him.
This is the same eclipse image the Kural uses for a rumour that spreads everywhere at once.
Jainism
“When the royal Seer Nami retired from the world, at the occasion of his Pravragyâ there was an uproar in Mithilâ.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
Nami’s single act of renunciation sets the whole city of Mithila talking and crying out.
Like the Kural’s single meeting, one event in one life becomes, in a moment, the noise of an entire city.
Sikhism
“The news spread throughout the world, and the beings of the three worlds came.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1116, Raag Tukhaari, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Ram Das describes how word of the Guru's coming spread far and wide, drawing countless people.
The Kural's heroine saw her beloved only once, yet the news spread everywhere like word of an eclipse; Gurbani likewise pictures one event whose news travels through the whole world.
Greek Mythology
After Dido and Aeneas shelter together in a cave during a storm, Rumour (Fama), the swiftest of all evils, races through the great cities of Libya spreading the story of their love.
— Virgil, Aeneid 4.173-197 (paraphrase) [11]
Virgil personifies Rumour as a monster that grows as it travels, with as many tongues as feathers.
This is almost exactly the Kural: one meeting, and at once the rumour of it is everywhere.
Philosophy
Francis Bacon, in his unfinished essay ‘Of Fame’, takes up the poets’ picture of Fame as a monster with many feathers, eyes and tongues, and asks how rumours arise and spread.
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Fame’ (paraphrase) [12]
Bacon treats rumour as a force worth studying, with its own ways of growing and travelling.
The Kural’s heroine has seen how fast such a monster runs: one meeting, and the whole town knows.

Real Life Example

Princess Margaret's romance with Peter Townsend becomes known to all
1953-1955 · London, United Kingdom, and Brussels, Belgium
Princess Margaret wished to marry Group Captain Peter Townsend, a divorced royal equerry, but under the Royal Marriages Act she needed the queen's permission, and the Church of England did not accept remarriage after divorce. Townsend was posted overseas as an air attaché, and after the two met again in October 1955 she publicly announced that she would not marry him. Her decision was made known in a public statement setting out her reasons.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A private love quickly became a matter that everyone discussed and that touched Church and state.
In the kural the heroine says she saw her lover only once, yet the rumour has spread like news of an eclipse; Margaret's romance likewise became known far beyond the people involved.
