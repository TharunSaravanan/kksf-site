Kural 1081

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

அணங்குகொல் ஆய்மயில் கொல்லோ கனங்குழை மாதர்கொல் மாலும்என் நெஞ்சு.
Anangukol aaimayil kollo kananguzhai maatharkol maalumen nenju.

Meaning

Is she a goddess, a lovely peacock, or a human girl wearing heavy earrings? My heart is confused and cannot tell.

Elementary

Imagine seeing a sunset so beautiful that you stop and wonder whether it is real or a painting. The young man in this verse sees a girl for the first time and is so amazed that he cannot decide whether she is a goddess, a lovely peacock, or an ordinary girl.

University

This kural opens the Kamattuppal and its Kalaviyal (the section on premarital, 'secret' love), voicing the hero's (talaivan's) first sight of the heroine. The chapter title combines takai (தகை, 'beauty, grace'), anangu (அணங்கு, a goddess or spirit that afflicts those who approach it) and uruttal (உறுத்தல், 'causing'), so it means the distress her beauty inflicts. The three-way doubt, anangu-kol, aaimayil-kollo, maathar-kol (goddess? choice peahen? woman?), is a classical Tamil figure of bewilderment (aiyam), and maalum (மாலும், 'is confused, infatuated') names the state. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses kananguzhai as 'a woman wearing heavy ear-ornaments', the detail that hints she is human after all; Pope renders 'Goddess? or peafowl rare?... All wildered is my mind!' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And neither among celestials, nor among Yakshas, nor among men was anybody possessed of such beauty, seen or heard of before.”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section 53 (Nalopakhyana; tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The Nala episode of the Mahabharata describes Damayanti as a beauty that no god, spirit or human could match.
Like the Kural's hero, the narrator can place her beauty neither among gods nor among humans.
Christianity
“Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?”
— Song of Solomon 6:10 (KJV) [4]
In the Song of Songs onlookers ask who this woman can be, comparing her to dawn, moon and sun.
Both texts react to beauty with a startled question about who, or what, she really is.
Islam
“And when they saw him, they greatly admired him and cut their hands and said, ‘Perfect is Allāh! This is not a man; this is none but a noble angel.’”
— Qur'an 12:31 (Sahih International) [5]
When the women of Egypt first see Joseph, they are so struck that they cut their hands and declare he cannot be human.
As in the Kural, a first sight of beauty makes the viewers doubt whether the person is human or heavenly.
Judaism
The Talmud lists four women of the Bible who were of outstanding beauty: Sarah, Rahab, Abigail and Esther, and records rabbinic remarks on how powerfully their beauty affected those who saw or even named them.
— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 15a [6]
The rabbis treat extraordinary beauty as a real force that can unsettle the mind of an onlooker.
The Kural's bewildered hero is an example of exactly that unsettling force.
Buddhism
“What do you think, Nanda? Which is lovelier, better looking, more charming: the Sakyan girl, the envy of the countryside, or these 500 dove-footed nymphs?”
— Udana 3.2, Nanda Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
To cure Nanda's longing for a beautiful girl, the Buddha takes him to heaven and asks him to compare her with celestial nymphs.
The Kural's hero cannot tell a girl from a goddess; the Buddha deliberately sets a girl beside goddesses to loosen that spell.
Jainism
“Though those who possess the three Guptis, cannot be disturbed even by well-adorned goddesses, still it is recommended to monks to live by themselves, as this is wholesome in every way.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana says that a monk whose mind, speech and body are fully guarded (the three guptis) cannot be shaken even by adorned goddesses, but still advises seclusion.
The Kural shows the opposite case: an unguarded heart is thrown into confusion by a beauty that seems divine.
Sikhism
“Wonderful is the sound current of the Naad, wonderful is the knowledge of the Vedas.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 463, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak begins a long salok of vismad (wonder), listing one thing after another in creation that leaves him astonished.
The Kural's hero is so bewildered by beauty that he cannot tell whether he sees a goddess, a peacock or a girl; Guru Nanak's vismad is the Sikh name for being overwhelmed and wonder-struck before the beauty of creation.
Greek Mythology
“I implore your aid- but tell me, are you a goddess or are you a mortal woman?”
— Homer, Odyssey 6 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Shipwrecked Odysseus, meeting the princess Nausicaa on the shore, cannot tell whether she is the goddess Artemis or a human girl.
This is the Kural's exact question, goddess or human maiden, asked at a first meeting.
Philosophy
“...is amazed when he sees any one having a godlike face or form, which is the expression of divine beauty; and at first a shudder runs through him, and again the old awe steals over him.”
— Plato, Phaedrus 251a (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates describes the soul that sees earthly beauty and is reminded of divine beauty, so that it shudders and feels awe before the beloved as before a god.
Plato explains philosophically why the Kural's hero confuses a girl with a goddess.

Real Life Example

Dante first sees Beatrice Portinari
1274 · Florence, Italy
In 1274 the young Dante Alighieri, about nine years old, first saw Beatrice Portinari, a girl of about the same age, at her father's house in Florence. In his book La Vita Nuova he later described how she appeared dressed in modest red and how the sight overwhelmed him, so that from then on love ruled his soul. Beatrice married another man and died in 1290, and she became the guiding figure of his Divine Comedy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dante's account of this first sight became one of the most famous descriptions of love at first sight in world literature.
Like the hero of the kural who cannot tell whether he has seen a goddess or a human girl, Dante wrote of Beatrice as a heavenly presence from the moment he first saw her.
