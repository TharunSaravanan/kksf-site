Kural 973

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

மேலிருந்தும் மேலல்லார் மேலல்லர் கீழிருந்தும் கீழல்லார் கீழல் லவர்.
Melirundhum melallaar melallar keezhirundhum keezhallaar keezhal lavar.

Meaning

Low people are not great even when they sit in high places. Good people are not low even when they are in low places.

Elementary

Imagine a bully sitting on the tallest chair in class, and a kind child sitting on the floor. The tall chair does not make the bully great, and the floor does not make the kind child small. Who you are matters more than where you sit.

University

The verse is a chiasmus built on mel (மேல், 'above, superior') and keezh (கீழ், 'below, base'), each used both for physical or social position and for moral quality: melirundhum melallaar melallar, 'though they be above, those not superior are not superior'. Puliyur Kesikan makes the image literal, 'though they sit on high seats' (உயர்ந்த ஆசனங்களிலே), and ties superiority to having done 'deeds hard to do', linking back to Kural 971; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar render mel as high office or position. Pope's 'men of lofty line' reads it as birth, which carries the theme on from Kural 972. The repeated negatives (அல்லார், அல்லர், அல்லவர்) give the couplet its characteristic Valluvan density. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Yaksha's questioning of Yudhishthira, the Yaksha asks what makes a Brahmana, and Yudhishthira answers that it is neither birth nor study nor learning but conduct alone, which must always be guarded.
— Mahabharata, Vana (Aranyaka) Parva, Yaksha Prashna episode (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Yudhishthira insists that rank is earned by behaviour, not inherited by birth or bought by learning.
Like the Kural, it denies that a high station makes one high without the matching character.
Christianity
“He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree.”
— Luke 1:52 (KJV) [4]
Mary's song proclaims that God overturns the order of high and low.
Like the Kural, it says that sitting in a high seat does not secure true height.
Islam
“Verily Allah does not look to your faces and your wealth but He looks to your heart and to your deeds.”
— Sahih Muslim 2564c (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that God's measure of a person ignores appearance and riches and looks to heart and action.
The Kural's true height and lowness are measured the same way, by inner quality rather than outward place.
Judaism
“I saw an inverted world. Those above, i.e., those who are considered important in this world, were below, insignificant, while those below, i.e., those who are insignificant in this world, were above. He said to him: My son, you have seen a clear world.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 50a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
Rav Yosef, returning from near death, reports that the true world reverses this world's rankings, and his father calls that the clear view.
It dramatises the Kural's claim that worldly position and true standing do not match.
Buddhism
“A monk is not an elder because his head is gray. He is but ripe in age, and he is called one grown old in vain.”
— Dhammapada 260 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says the title of elder means nothing without the virtues that should go with it.
Like the Kural, it denies that outward rank alone makes one truly senior.
Jainism
“The value of penance has become visible, birth appears of no value! Look at the holy Harikêsa, the son of a Svapâka, whose power is so great.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Priests who mocked the low-born monk Harikesa are humbled, and the onlookers proclaim that his austerities, not his birth, decide his worth.
Harikesa is the Kural's person placed low by birth who is in no way low.
Sikhism
“Nanak seeks the company of the lowest of the low class, the very lowest of the low. ... In that place where the lowly are cared for-there, the Blessings of Your Glance of Grace rain down.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak deliberately takes his place among those society ranks lowest and says God's grace falls where the lowly are cared for. The verse overturns the idea that social position measures a person's worth.
The Kural says the base are not high even on a high seat and the noble are not low even when placed low, and Guru Nanak gives the same reversal, siding with the people the world calls low.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Odysseus returns home disguised as a ragged beggar and is mocked in his own hall by the suitors, who occupy the seats of honour while behaving shamefully, until he reveals himself as the true king.
— Homer, Odyssey 17-22 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
The epic contrasts the low appearance of a noble man with the lofty places held by base men.
The suitors are high without being high, and the beggar-king is low without being low.
Philosophy
“These reasonings are unconnected: ‘I am richer than you, therefore I am better’; ‘I am more eloquent than you, therefore I am better.’ … But you, after all, are neither property nor style.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 44 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus says that having more of something outward never makes a person better.
Like the Kural, it refuses to let external standing define who is truly superior.

Real Life Example

The former slave Epictetus becomes the most admired Stoic teacher of his age
c. 55 to c. 135 CE (school at Nicopolis from about 90 CE) · Rome, Italy, and Nicopolis, Epirus, Greece
Epictetus was born around 55 CE in Hierapolis in Phrygia and as a boy was brought to Rome as a slave of Epaphroditus, a rich freedman who had served the emperor Nero; while still enslaved he attended the lectures of the Stoic Musonius Rufus. After he was freed, he was expelled from Rome around 89-90 CE when the emperor Domitian banished the philosophers, and he opened a school at Nicopolis in Greece that attracted upper-class Roman students, including Arrian, who wrote down his teachings as the Discourses and the Handbook. Later writers report that he was more popular in his own day than Plato. The tutor of the future emperor Marcus Aurelius, Herodes Atticus, is reported to have called him the greatest of the Stoics.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Born into the lowest rank of Roman society and lame for much of his life, Epictetus became one of the most influential moral teachers of the ancient world, while the emperor who banished him is remembered as a tyrant.
The kural says that those who are not base are not low even when placed low, and Epictetus, a slave by station, stood morally far above many who sat on high seats.
