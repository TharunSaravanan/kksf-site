Kural 1045

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

நல்குரவு என்னும் இடும்பையுள் பல்குரைத் துன்பங்கள் சென்று படும்.
Nalkuravu ennum idumbaiyuL palkuraith thunbangaL sendru padum.

Meaning

Inside the one trouble called poverty, many kinds of sorrows gather and settle.

Elementary

Have you seen one leak in a roof let in rain that ruins the carpet, the books and the bed? Valluvar says poverty is like that one leak: many other troubles come pouring in through it.

University

Idumbai (இடும்பை, ‘affliction, distress’) names poverty as one trouble, and palkurai (பல்குரை, ‘of many kinds’) thunbangaL (‘sorrows’) are said to ‘go and settle’ (sendru padum) within it. Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah read this as all sorrows being contained in poverty, so that poverty is the sum of misfortunes; Mu. Varadarasanar says they ‘arise and grow’ inside it; Kalaignar reverses the image slightly, with many sorrows springing out of poverty. Either way poverty is the matrix of other ills, which prepares for the later couplets on lost respect (1046), estranged kin (1047) and sleepless dread (1048-1049). Pope’s ‘Attendant sorrows plenteous grow’ follows the ‘springing up’ reading. The text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The acts of a person who, possessed of little intelligence, suffers himself to be divested of wealth, are all dried up like shallow streams in the summer season.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna argues that once wealth is lost every undertaking withers, like streams in summer.
As in the Kural, the loss of means is not one trouble but the drying-up of everything else.
Christianity
“All the days of the afflicted are evil: but he that is of a merry heart hath a continual feast.”
— Proverbs 15:15 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs observes that for the afflicted every day brings trouble.
The afflicted person’s days filled with evils echo the Kural’s many sorrows gathered in poverty.
Islam
“And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient.”
— Qur’an 2:155 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an lists hunger and loss of wealth among a cluster of trials that come together.
Loss of wealth arrives alongside fear, hunger and other losses, as the Kural says many sorrows gather in poverty.
Judaism
“Poverty in a person’s household is more difficult than fifty plagues.”
— Talmud Bavli, Bava Batra 116a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz) [6]
Rabbi Pinehas ben Hama, reading the friends’ words to Job, concludes that poverty at home outweighs fifty plagues.
Fifty plagues gathered in one condition is the Kural’s ‘sorrows of every kind’ settling inside poverty.
Buddhism
“Poverty is called suffering in the world; so, too, is getting into debt. A poor person, in debt, partaking of sensuality, suffers hardship. Then they hound him and put him into bondage.”
— Ina Sutta, AN 6.45 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha traces how poverty leads to debt, interest, notices, harassment and finally bondage, one suffering after another.
The sutta lays out step by step how many sorrows flow into the one condition of poverty.
Jainism
“All men who are ignorant of the Truth are subject to pain; in the endless Samsâra they suffer in many ways.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira teaches that the root of manifold suffering is ignorance of the truth, which keeps beings bound in samsara.
Where the Kural finds many sorrows gathered in poverty, Jainism locates their common root in spiritual ignorance.
Sikhism
“So many endure distress, deprivation and constant abuse. Even these are Your Gifts, O Great Giver!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib (Pauri 25), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak acknowledges that countless people live with suffering, hunger and continual blows, yet asks that even this be accepted as falling within God's hukam (divine order).
The Kural says every kind of sorrow gathers and settles within the single affliction of poverty; Guru Nanak names distress, hunger and abuse together as the lot of many, though he teaches acceptance rather than lament.
Greek Mythology
Disguised as a beggar, Odysseus says that nothing is more shameless than the hungry belly, which forces a man to remember it and drives men to endure many evils for its sake.
— Homer, Odyssey 7.215-221 and 17.286-289 [10]
Homer’s hero, living as a poor wanderer, names hunger as the source of a whole train of hardships.
The beggar Odysseus shows the Kural’s point that want brings many sufferings with it.
Philosophy
“Of all the woes of luckless poverty none is harder to endure than this, that it exposes men to ridicule.”
— Juvenal, Satire 3 (tr. G.G. Ramsay, 1918) [11]
Juvenal’s Umbritius catalogues the many woes of the Roman poor: high rent, torn clothes, lost legacies, contempt and danger.
Juvenal’s ‘woes of luckless poverty’ is a satirist’s inventory of the Kural’s many sorrows gathered in want.

Real Life Example

Victor Hugo publishes Les Miserables
1862 · Brussels and Paris (written at Hauteville House, Guernsey)
Victor Hugo, living in exile at Hauteville House on Guernsey, published Les Miserables in 1862, and it was an immediate popular success that was quickly translated into other languages. In its short preface Hugo named the degradation of men through poverty, the ruin of women through hunger and the stunting of children as the problems of the age, and the novel follows the ex-convict Jean Valjean and the impoverished single mother Fantine through a chain of misfortunes that grow out of want.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The novel made the many-sided suffering of the poor the centre of a work read across the world, and it remains one of the best-known portraits of social injustice in nineteenth-century France.
The kural says that within the single affliction of poverty sorrows of every kind gather, and Hugo's preface and story trace exactly that: hunger, prison, outcasting, illness and the ruin of children all rooted in want.
