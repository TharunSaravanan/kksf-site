Kural 680

The Method of Execution · Vinai Seyalvakai · வினை செயல்வகை

உறைசிறியார் உள்நடுங்கல் அஞ்சிக் குறைபெறின் கொள்வர் பெரியார்ப் பணிந்து.
Uraisiriyaar ulnadungal anjik kuraiperin Kolvar periyaarp panindhu.

Meaning

Leaders of a small country, afraid that their own people are scared, will give in to a stronger power and accept peace if they are offered a chance.

Elementary

Imagine a small team facing a much bigger, stronger team and all your players are scared. A wise captain might agree to a fair deal instead of a hopeless match, to protect the team. Sometimes the brave choice is to make peace.

University

Uraisiriyaar, 'those whose dwelling (realm) is small', is read by Parimelazhagar as the ministers of weak states; ulnadungal anji means 'fearing the inner trembling' of their own subjects; kurai perin, 'if they obtain an opening' for reconciliation; periyaarp panindhu kolvar, 'they will submit to and embrace the great'. The Drew-Lazarus rendering on ytamizh follows this reading, while Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah read it more generally about the weak yielding to the strong for the sake of their people. The older sandhi text reads உண்ணடுங்க லஞ்சிக் குறைபெறிற். Placed at the end of the chapter, the kural completes the method of execution by admitting that sometimes the wise act is timely submission rather than conflict, a principle also central to the Arthashastra's teaching on the weak king and to Luke 14:31-32.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya teaches that a king who is weaker than his rival should make peace with him, and that a weak king attacked by a strong one should submit, bending like a reed, and wait for better times.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 7.1 and 12.1 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
The Arthashastra treats timely submission (samdhi) by the weaker side as sound policy, not cowardice.
This is the Kural's advice to the rulers of small realms: accept terms with the stronger party to protect one's people.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand? Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sendeth an ambassage, and desireth conditions of peace.”
— Luke 14:31-32 (KJV) [4]
Jesus describes a weaker king who, seeing he is outnumbered, sends envoys early to ask for terms.
This is almost exactly the Kural's scene: a smaller ruler seeking terms from a greater one.
Islam
At al-Hudaybiyyah the Prophet accepts a truce with Quraysh on terms many of his companions found humiliating, including turning back from Mecca that year; the Quran later calls the outcome a clear victory.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2731-2732; Quran 48:1 [5]
Accepting restrictive terms from a stronger party in the moment protected the community and opened the way to later success.
Like the Kural's rulers, the Prophet chose a negotiated settlement over a clash, for his people's sake.
Judaism
“Bring your necks under the yoke of the king of Babylon, and serve him and his people, and live.”
— Jeremiah 27:12 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah urges King Zedekiah to submit to Babylon's greater power rather than rebel and bring ruin on Jerusalem.
The prophet gives the Kural's counsel to a small kingdom: bow to the greater power for the survival of the people.
Buddhism
“Victory begets enmity; the defeated dwell in pain. Happily the peaceful live, discarding both victory and defeat.”
— Dhammapada 201 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
Traditionally linked to the war between King Pasenadi and King Ajatasattu, the verse says conflict harms winner and loser alike.
It gives an ethical reason to prefer peace to fighting on, which supports the Kural's settlement-seeking rulers.
Jainism
When Bharata, eldest son of Rishabha, set out to bring every realm under his rule as chakravartin, his ninety-eight younger brothers, each holding a smaller kingdom, were called on to submit. Jain tradition relates that rather than fight or bow, they became monks and gave up their kingdoms, leaving Bahubali alone to resist.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book I (life of Ṛṣabha and Bharata's conquest), as summarised in Wikipedia, 'Bharata chakravarti' [7]
In the Jain universal history the weaker rulers neither wage a hopeless war nor merely submit, but renounce kingship altogether.
The kural observes that small rulers, fearing for their people, will yield to a mightier power when an opening is offered; the Jain story shows lesser kings in the same position choosing renunciation instead.
Sikhism
“When something is placed on the balancing scale and weighed, the side which descends is heavier.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa ki Vaar Guru Nanak uses the scale to show that the one who bows is the one of real weight, so humility is strength, not loss.
The Kural says the rulers of a small realm, fearing for their people, will bow to a greater power when peace is offered; Guru Nanak's scale gives a moral reason why such bowing need not be shameful.
Greek Mythology
Old King Priam goes by night to the tent of Achilles, who has killed his son, kneels and kisses his hands, and wins back Hector's body so that Troy can mourn.
— Homer, Iliad 24.468-620 [9]
The weaker king humbles himself before the stronger warrior to obtain what his people need.
Priam, like the rulers in the Kural, bows to the greater power when an opening appears, for the sake of his people.
Philosophy
In the Melian Dialogue the Athenian envoys urge the small island of Melos to yield, arguing that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must; Melos refuses and is destroyed.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 5.84-116 (trans. Richard Crawley) [10]
Thucydides presents the negative case: a small state that refuses terms from a far stronger power is wiped out.
The fate of Melos shows, in reverse, the prudence the Kural credits to small states that bow when terms are offered.

Real Life Example

Siam accepts France's terms in the crisis of 1893
1893 · Bangkok, Siam (Thailand)
In 1893, after clashes with French forces in the Mekong valley, French gunboats forced their way up the Chao Phraya River and blockaded Bangkok, and France gave Siam an ultimatum. King Chulalongkorn's government accepted the French demands, agreeing to pay an indemnity and withdrawing from the east bank of the Mekong, which France took as part of its protectorate in Laos. The concession was part of a broader policy of yielding territory to Western powers in the hope that Siam could keep its overall independence, and Siam stayed independent while its neighbours fell under colonial rule.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Facing a far stronger power, Siam chose painful concessions over a war it could not win, and in doing so preserved the kingdom itself.
The kural says the rulers of a small realm, fearing for their own people, will bow to a mightier power and accept terms when an opening for peace is offered, which is what Chulalongkorn's government did under French guns in 1893.
