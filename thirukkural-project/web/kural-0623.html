Kural 623

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

இடும்பைக்கு இடும்பை படுப்பர் இடும்பைக்கு இடும்பை படாஅ தவர்.
Itumpaikku itumpai patuppar itumpaikku Itumpai pataaa thavar.

Meaning

People who do not let trouble make them sad turn things around. They end up making trouble itself suffer.

Elementary

A bully loves it when you get upset. If you stay calm and cheerful, the bully gets frustrated and gives up. Trouble is like that bully: if you refuse to be sad about it, the trouble is the one that ends up losing.

University

This couplet is a celebrated piece of Valluvar’s wordplay: iṭumpai (sorrow, affliction) occurs four times, arranged chiastically so that ‘those who do not suffer sorrow for sorrow’ are said to ‘cause sorrow to sorrow’. The form paṭā’a in the second line is an alapedai (metrical lengthening, அளபெடை), marked in thirukkural.net’s romanisation as paṭā'a and preserved in both sources consulted. The personification of trouble as an opponent who can itself be defeated recurs in 624 and 625, making the triad the rhetorical centre of the chapter; Parimelazhagar’s line of interpretation, followed by Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar, is that the undaunted person so frustrates misfortune that it withdraws defeated. Pope’s version, ‘From them griefs, put to grief, depart’, keeps the paronomasia.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O Arjun, noblest amongst men, that person who is not affected by happiness and distress, and remains steady in both, becomes eligible for liberation.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.15 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna teaches that the dhīra, the steady one whom pleasure and pain do not disturb (na vyathayanti), is fit for immortality.
The Kural’s person who is not pained by pain is the Gita’s dhīra, and in both texts that refusal to be disturbed is what defeats suffering.
Christianity
“O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?”
— 1 Corinthians 15:55 (KJV) [4]
Paul taunts death itself, the greatest affliction, declaring it has been robbed of its power.
Like the Kural, Paul turns the tables on the enemy, so that the affliction becomes the defeated party.
Islam
“So do not weaken and do not grieve, and you will be superior if you are [true] believers.”
— Qur’an 3:139 (Saheeh International) [5]
After the setback at Uhud, the Qur’an tells believers not to lose heart or sink into grief, promising that they will prevail.
The Kural and the Qur’an both link refusing to grieve with gaining the upper hand over misfortune.
Judaism
“the days wherein the Jews had rest from their enemies, and the month which was turned unto them from sorrow to gladness, and from mourning into a good day; that they should make them days of feasting and gladness, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor.”
— Esther 9:22 (JPS 1917) [6]
The Purim story ends with a total reversal: the decree meant to destroy the Jews recoils on its author, and their sorrow becomes celebration.
Esther dramatizes the Kural’s reversal, in which the trouble meant to crush people is itself overturned.
Buddhism
Throughout the Mara-samyutta, Mara the Evil One comes to the Buddha again and again in terrifying or tempting forms to shake him; each time the Buddha calmly recognizes him, and Mara, realizing he has been seen through, vanishes sad and disappointed.
— Samyutta Nikaya 4 (Mara-samyutta) [7]
The personified force of affliction and temptation fails against a mind that will not be disturbed, and it is Mara who leaves dejected.
This is the Kural’s paradox made into a story: the one who is not troubled by trouble leaves trouble itself defeated and grieving.
Jainism
“Suffering from insects a great sage remains undisturbed. As an elephant at the head of the battle kills the enemy, so does a hero (in self-control conquer the internal foe).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Enduring the trouble of biting insects, the sage is compared to a war elephant that turns on and destroys the enemy.
The Kural says that those who do not grieve at sorrow turn the tables and make sorrow itself suffer; the Jain verse likewise makes the one who endures the attacker who defeats the foe.
Sikhism
“That man, who in the midst of pain, does not feel pain, who is not affected by pleasure, affection or fear, and who looks alike upon gold and dust;”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 633, Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Raag Sorath, Guru Tegh Bahadur describes the person who is not shaken by pain even in its midst and who stays the same whatever comes.
The Kural says those who do not grieve at sorrow turn the tables on sorrow itself; Guru Tegh Bahadur praises the one who, in the midst of pain, does not feel pain.
Greek Mythology
Trapped in the Cyclops’ cave as Polyphemus devoured his men, Odysseus kept his head, called himself ‘Nobody’, blinded the giant and escaped under the rams, leaving Polyphemus roaring in pain that ‘Nobody’ was hurting him.
— Homer, Odyssey 9 [10]
The monster who meant to destroy Odysseus ends up the one in agony, outwitted by a man who did not panic.
Odysseus turns the Cyclops’ threat back on him, just as the Kural says the undaunted inflict trouble on trouble.
Philosophy
“Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus teaches that suffering comes from our judgments about events, so changing the judgment removes the disturbance.
The Kural’s person who is not pained by pain has done what Epictetus urges, taking away trouble’s power over the mind.

Real Life Example

Karoly Takacs wins Olympic gold shooting with his other hand
1948 and 1952 · London, United Kingdom, and Helsinki, Finland
In 1938 Hungarian army sergeant Karoly Takacs, a world-class pistol shooter, had his right shooting hand shattered when a faulty grenade exploded during army training. He taught himself to shoot with his left hand, won the Hungarian national pistol championship in 1939, and at the 1948 London Olympics won the rapid-fire pistol gold medal with a world record score. Four years later at Helsinki he won the event again, becoming its first repeat Olympic champion.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Takacs refused to let a career-ending injury end his career, and his comeback is still cited by the Olympic movement as one of its great stories of resilience.
The kural says those who do not let sorrow grieve them bring grief upon sorrow itself, and Takacs turned the accident that should have finished him into the beginning of two Olympic victories.
