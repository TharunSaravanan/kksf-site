Kural 760

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

ஒண்பொருள் காழ்ப்ப இயற்றியார்க்கு எண்பொருள் ஏனை இரண்டும் ஒருங்கு.
Onporul kaazhppa iyatriyaarkku enporul enai irandum orungu.

Meaning

For people who have earned plenty of money honestly, the other two goals of life, doing good and being happy, come easily.

Elementary

Think of a garden. Once the soil is rich and well watered, flowers and vegetables both grow easily. Valluvar says honest wealth is like that rich soil: once you have it, goodness and happiness grow easily too.

University

The closing verse ties the chapter to the three aims of life (muppaal: aram, porul, inbam) that give the Kural its three books. Onporul is ‘bright’ or excellent wealth, which the commentators take to mean wealth gained by good means, echoing 754; kaazhppa (from kaazh, hard core) means firmly and abundantly; enporul is ‘an easy thing’ (en, easy); and enai irandum orungu means ‘the other two, together’. Parimelazhagar explains that for such a person virtue and pleasure are both easily attained, and Kalaignar names the three purusharthas explicitly. The verse repeats the pairing in 754 and closes the chapter’s argument that honestly earned wealth underpins a complete life. The sandhi-joined text reads இயற்றியார்க் கெண்பொரு ளேனை யிரண்டு மொருங்கு, which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Kautilya holds that wealth and wealth alone is important, inasmuch as charity and desire depend upon wealth for their realisation.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book I, Chapter 7 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya argues that among the three aims of life, artha comes first, because dharma and kama both depend on it.
Kautilya and Valluvar make the same argument: once wealth is secured, the other two aims follow.
Christianity
“Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy; That they do good, that they be rich in good works, ready to distribute, willing to communicate.”
— 1 Timothy 6:17-18 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells Timothy to teach the rich to enjoy God’s gifts and use their wealth for good works.
Paul pairs enjoyment (‘all things to enjoy’) with good works, the same two ends the Kural says wealth makes easy.
Islam
“But seek, through that which Allah has given you, the home of the Hereafter; and [yet], do not forget your share of the world. And do good as Allah has done good to you.”
— Qur’an 28:77 (Sahih International) [5]
Qarun’s people advise him to use his wealth to seek the hereafter and do good, without neglecting his rightful share of worldly enjoyment.
The verse joins virtue and lawful enjoyment through wealth, which is the Kural’s ‘other two, together’.
Judaism
“Remember that it is the ETERNAL your God who gives you the power to get wealth, in fulfillment of the covenant made on oath with your fathers, as is still the case.”
— Deuteronomy 8:18 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Moses reminds Israel that the ability to prosper comes from God and serves the covenant.
Wealth is placed inside a larger moral purpose, as the Kural places porul in the service of aram and inbam.
Buddhism
“I’ve achieved the goal for which an astute layperson wishes to gain wealth. I don’t regret what I’ve done.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.61, Pattakamma Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The householder who has used legitimate wealth to support family, guard against loss, make offerings and give to the virtuous can look back without regret.
His fulfilled goal, joining enjoyment with merit, is the Kural’s aram and inbam gained through porul.
Jainism
“Anyonya-pratibandhena trivargam sadhayan : Fulfilling the three-fold aim of life - that is, dharma, artha and kama - without excluding any of its elements.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, no. 18, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, ‘Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code’ (jainworld.com) [8]
Hemacandra's ideal Jain householder pursues virtue, wealth and pleasure together in balance, none crowding out the others, alongside the first quality of honestly earned wealth.
The Kural closes by saying that those with abundant honest wealth easily attain the other two aims of virtue and happiness, and the Jain householder code likewise holds the three aims together as one balanced pursuit.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Saarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak sums up the Sikh householder ideal: earn honestly by one's own labour and share part of it with others.
The Kural says that for those who have gathered honest wealth, virtue and happiness follow easily; Guru Nanak says that the one who earns honestly and shares has found the true path of life.
Greek Mythology
King Midas, granted his wish that everything he touched would turn to gold, found that his food and drink also turned to gold, and he begged Dionysus to take the gift back.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book XI [10]
Midas learns that wealth pursued for its own sake destroys the very pleasures it was meant to buy.
Midas is the reverse of the Kural: wealth cut off from virtue gives neither happiness nor goodness.
Philosophy
In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle says that happiness also needs external goods, since it is impossible, or not easy, to do noble acts without the proper equipment, and many actions are performed through friends, riches and political power as instruments.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book I, Chapter 8 [11]
Aristotle holds that a complete happy life needs enough external resources to act on one’s virtue.
Aristotle’s riches as the instruments of noble action and happiness state the same idea as Valluvar’s wealth that makes virtue and pleasure easy.

Real Life Example

Banker Annamalai Chettiar founds Annamalai University
1 January 1929 · Chidambaram (Annamalainagar), Tamil Nadu, India
Annamalai Chettiar, from a Nattukottai Chettiar banking family whose business he expanded into South-East Asia, founded Sri Minakshi College and Tamil and Sanskrit colleges at Chidambaram in the early 1920s. In 1928 he agreed with the Madras government to hand these institutions over to form a university, and Annamalai University came into being on 1 January 1929, founded by a private philanthropist. He was knighted in 1923 and later received the hereditary title of Raja of Chettinad.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A fortune made in banking was turned into a university that promoted Tamil learning and served students across the region.
The kural says that for those who have built up abundant, honestly earned wealth, virtue and happiness come easily, and Annamalai Chettiar's banking wealth made a lasting good work almost a matter of course.
