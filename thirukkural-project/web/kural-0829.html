Kural 829

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

மிகச்செய்து தம்மெள்ளு வாரை நகச்செய்து நட்பினுள் சாப்புல்லற் பாற்று.
Migachcheythu thammellu vaarai nagachcheythu natpinul saappullar̲ paat̲ru.

Meaning

When people make a big show of friendship but secretly look down on you, smile back at them on the outside, but let that friendship die inside you.

Elementary

If a classmate is super-friendly to your face but laughs at you behind your back, you don’t have to start a fight. You can stay polite and friendly when you meet, but quietly stop trusting them and stop sharing your secrets.

University

Migachcheythu (மிகச்செய்து) is ‘making much of (friendship)’, and thammeḷḷuvaar (தம் எள்ளுவார்) are ‘those who scorn us’ within. The difficult phrase saappullaṟpaaṯru (சாப்புல்லற்பாற்று) is read by the commentators as ‘one should embrace (pullal) in such a way that it dies (saa)’: Varadarasanar says one should make them laugh in that friendship while acting so that the bond dies, and Pappaiah and Kalaignar read it as answering pretence with pretence. Pope’s explanation, in the tradition of Parimelazhagar’s royal-political reading, presents this as the duty of kings toward such foes. Whether read as defensive policy or as cynicism, the couplet assumes a political setting where open rupture is costly; ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net give identical text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If thou art angry, show thyself as if thou art not so, speaking even then with a smile on thy lips.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section 142, Kanika’s counsel (Ganguli translation) [3]
Kanika counsels Dhritarashtra to hide hostility behind a smiling manner in dealing with rivals.
This is the Kural’s strategy of keeping the false friend smiling while one’s own inner friendship has ended.
Christianity
“Behold, I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves: be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.”
— Matthew 10:16 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his disciples to combine shrewdness about hostile people with innocence of conduct.
Both counsel staying outwardly peaceable while inwardly alert to those who mean harm.
Islam
“Let not believers take disbelievers as allies rather than believers. And whoever [of you] does that has nothing with Allah, except when taking precaution against them in prudence.”
— Qur’an 3:28 (Saheeh International) [5]
The verse forbids real alliance with hostile parties, while permitting outward caution in prudence.
The Kural similarly permits outward civility toward the scornful while withholding real friendship.
Judaism
When Absalom rebels against David, David’s loyal friend Hushai goes to Absalom, greets him warmly and appears to join him, but inwardly remains David’s man and defeats the dangerous advice of Ahithophel.
— 2 Samuel 15:32-37; 16:15-19; 17:5-14 [6]
Outward friendship is offered to a hostile party while inner loyalty lies elsewhere.
Hushai keeps Absalom pleased on the surface while the ‘friendship’ is dead within, as the Kural advises.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that hostility is ended only by non-hostility.
A contrast: the Kural answers pretence with pretence, while the Dhammapada asks one to drop hostility altogether.
Jainism
“It is better to be without the love of those who are without sincerity. Death is preferable to an incurable disease. To kill him at once is more desirable than to vex a man so that he becomes sore at heart, and to abuse is better than to praise one for that which we do not possess.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 22 ('The choice of friends'), st. 9 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār says that affection from insincere people is worth less than having no affection at all, and that false praise is worse than open abuse.
Valluvar advises letting a pretender's friendship die inwardly while keeping him smiling; the Nālaṭiyār agrees that insincere love is better done without, though it does not recommend answering pretence with pretence.
Sikhism
“One who offers both respectful greetings and rude refusal to his master, has gone wrong from the very beginning. O Nanak! Both of his actions are false; he obtains no place in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok 2 before pauri 21), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa Ki Vaar Guru Angad condemns double conduct, outward respect together with inner refusal, as false from the start.
The Kural advises answering a scornful false friend with an outward smile while letting the friendship die inside; Guru Angad treats any such split between outward show and inner stance as falsehood.
Greek Mythology
Back in Ithaca disguised as a beggar, Odysseus endures the suitors’ mockery in his own house, keeping a calm face and even smiling grimly to himself, while he quietly prepares their destruction.
— Homer, Odyssey, Books 17-20 [10]
Odysseus answers scorn with outward composure while his inward attitude toward the suitors is settled.
Outward ease toward those who scorn you, inner withdrawal: the Kural’s strategy in epic form.
Philosophy
“The best composition and temperature, is to have openness in fame and opinion; secrecy in habit; dissimulation in seasonable use; and a power to feign, if there be no remedy.”
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Simulation and Dissimulation’ [11]
Bacon allows dissimulation and even feigning as a last resort, used only in season.
Like the Kural, Bacon treats measured counter-pretence as legitimate prudence against those who deceive.

Real Life Example

Cesare Borgia's reconciliation with his captains at Senigallia
October - 31 December 1502 · Senigallia, Marche, Italy
In October 1502 several of Cesare Borgia's own condottieri met at Magione and conspired against him, and over the following weeks they negotiated a reconciliation and returned to his service. At the end of December, Vitellozzo Vitelli, Oliverotto da Fermo and two members of the Orsini family met Borgia at Senigallia, which they had just taken in his name. Borgia separated them from their troops, arrested them, and had them put to death, an episode described by Machiavelli, who was then an envoy at Borgia's side.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Both sides made a show of renewed friendship while neither trusted the other, and the reconciliation ended in Borgia's sudden blow.
The kural describes those who make a great show of friendship while secretly despising you, and advises answering with outward cordiality while letting the bond die within, which is how Borgia treated the captains who had plotted against him.
