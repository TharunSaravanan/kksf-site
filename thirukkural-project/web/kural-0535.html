Kural 535

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

முன்னுறக் காவாது இழுக்கியான் தன்பிழை பின்னூறு இரங்கி விடும்.
Munnurak kaavaadhu izhukkiyaan thanpizhai Pinnooru irangi vitum.

Meaning

Someone who carelessly fails to guard against trouble before it comes will be sorry for their own mistake when the trouble hits.

Elementary

If you know a test is coming and you don't study, you'll feel terrible when you see questions you can't answer. Getting ready before trouble comes is much better than feeling sorry afterwards.

University

முன் உற (‘before it arrives’) and பின் ஊறு (‘when the blow has fallen’) frame the couplet in time, and இழுக்கியான் (‘one who slipped, was negligent’) is described by காவாது (‘without guarding’). Parimelazhagar says the troubles in view are those that can be guarded against, namely what enemies do when they spot a lapse. He treats ஊறு as standing for ஊற்றின்கண், with both the case-ending and the linking syllable dropped, and explains இரங்கிவிடும் as ‘will be left to grieve’, because once the trouble has come it can no longer be warded off. Pavanar widens the preventable troubles to include famine and flood, and takes விடும் in the sense of ‘perishing’. Manakkudavar's gloss is almost word for word the same as Parimelazhagar's. This couplet closes Parimelazhagar's group of three (533-535) on the harm that comes to the heedless.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Panchatantra three fish live in a pond: Anāgatavidhātā (‘Forethought’), Pratyutpannamati (‘Ready-wit’) and Yadbhaviṣya (‘Whatever-will-be’). When fishermen are overheard planning to net the pond, Forethought leaves at once and Ready-wit escapes by playing dead, but Whatever-will-be does nothing and is caught.
— Panchatantra, Book I (Mitrabheda), the fable of the three fish [4]
This nīti fable, told to princes, contrasts the one who guards in advance with the one who waits and is lost.
Yadbhaviṣya is exactly the Kural's heedless man, whose failure to act beforehand leaves no remedy afterwards.
Christianity
“And while they went to buy, the bridegroom came; and they that were ready went in with him to the marriage: and the door was shut. Afterward came also the other virgins, saying, Lord, Lord, open to us. But he answered and said, Verily I say unto you, I know you not. Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh.”
— Matthew 25:10-13 (KJV) [5]
The foolish virgins brought no spare oil. By the time they had gone to fetch it, the door was shut, and their pleading came too late.
This is the Kural's picture of someone who did not provide beforehand and can only lament afterwards.
Islam
“And spend [in the way of Allah] from what We have provided you before death approaches one of you and he says, "My Lord, if only You would delay me for a brief term so I would give charity and be among the righteous.”
— Qur'an 63:10 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur'an pictures the person who put off good deeds, begging too late for a little more time.
This is the Kural's pattern: neglect beforehand, then useless lament once the moment has passed.
Judaism
“one should repent today lest he die tomorrow; and by following this advice one will spend his entire life in a state of repentance.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 153a (William Davidson Edition) [7]
Rabbi Eliezer's advice is to ‘repent one day before your death’. Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai adds a parable of a king who invites his servants to a feast without fixing the hour: the wise dress and wait at the door, while the foolish go off to work and are caught unprepared when the call comes.
Like the Kural, the Talmud teaches that you should be ready before the day of reckoning, because afterwards there is only regret.
Buddhism
“Just as a border city is closely guarded both within and without, even so, guard yourself. Do not let slip this opportunity (for spiritual growth). For those who let slip this opportunity grieve indeed when consigned to hell.”
— Dhammapada 315, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [8]
The Buddha compares the self to a frontier town that has to be guarded before attack, and warns that those who let the moment slip will grieve afterwards.
It has the same structure as the Kural: guard in advance, or grieve when the harm arrives.
Jainism
“'If he does not get (victory over his will) early, he will get it afterwards;' such reasoning presupposes the eternity of human life. But such a man despairs when his life draws to its close, and the dissolution of his body approaches.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.9 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The monk who puts off the work of self-mastery, assuming there is time, ends in despair when the end arrives.
The Kural says the man who fails to guard against trouble in advance grieves over his mistake when it strikes; Mahavira describes the same belated despair of the procrastinator.
Sikhism
“If someone focuses on the Lord beforehand, then why should he be punished?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the Babarvani hymns on Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak says the suffering might have been avoided had people kept God in mind before the calamity struck.
The Kural says one who fails to guard against trouble before it comes is left grieving afterwards; Guru Nanak asks why anyone would suffer if he had paid heed beforehand.
Greek Mythology
Prometheus (‘Forethought’) warned his brother Epimetheus (‘Afterthought’) never to accept a gift from Zeus. Epimetheus forgot the warning and took Pandora, and he understood the evil only once it was already his.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 83-89 [11]
The two brothers' names are themselves a lesson: one sees ahead, the other only understands after the damage is done.
Epimetheus is the Kural's man who did not guard beforehand and could only realize his error afterwards.
Philosophy
Explaining why the Italian princes lost their states, Machiavelli says that in quiet times they never thought things could change, which he calls a common human fault of not reckoning on the storm during fair weather. When adversity came, they thought of flight instead of defence.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 24 (trans. W. K. Marriott) [12]
For Machiavelli, rulers fall less through bad luck than through failing to prepare while they still could.
This is the Kural's heedless ruler, who did not guard in advance and is left lamenting.

Real Life Example

The Vajont dam landslide
9 October 1963 · Vajont valley and Longarone, Italy
The Vajont dam was built in a gorge known to be geologically unstable, and several geologists and engineers warned of the risk of a single great landslide if the reservoir were filled. When filling began in 1960 the slope of Monte Toc started to move, and in November 1960 a slide of about 700,000 cubic metres fell into the lake, but the reservoir continued to be operated and raised again. On the night of 9 October 1963 a vast landslide fell into the reservoir, sending a wave over the dam that destroyed the town of Longarone below and killed about 2,000 people, although the dam itself survived; several people involved were later convicted in the courts.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The warning signs were seen and measured for three years, but the danger was not guarded against while there was still time, and once the mountain moved nothing could be done.
The kural says one who fails to guard against trouble before it comes will be left grieving over his own mistake once it strikes, and Vajont is remembered as a disaster foreseen but not prevented.
