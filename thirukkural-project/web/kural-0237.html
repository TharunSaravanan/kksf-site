Kural 237

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

புகழ்பட வாழாதார் தந்நோவார் தம்மை இகழ்வாரை நோவது எவன்.
Pukazhpada vaazhaadhaar thannovaar thammai Ikazhvaarai novadhu evan?

Meaning

People who do not live in a way that earns a good name should be upset with themselves. Why are they upset with the people who look down on them?

Elementary

If you didn’t study and got a bad grade, being angry at the teacher doesn’t help. It’s better to look at what you could do differently. The same goes for what people think of you: if they don’t think well of you, first check your own actions.

University

Valluvar turns from praising renown to diagnosing a common evasion: blaming one’s critics instead of oneself. The couplet sets தந்நோவார் (‘they do not grieve over themselves’) against இகழ்வாரை நோவது (‘resenting those who scorn them’) and ends in the rhetorical எவன், ‘why?’. The ethical claim is that reputation, though it lives in others’ mouths (Kural 232), is earned by one’s own conduct, so responsibility lies within. Commentators from Parimelazhagan to Karunanidhi read it as a call to self-correction rather than to resentment. The insight is widely shared: Jesus’ mote and beam, Lamentations’ question why a living man should complain of the punishment of his own sins, Zeus’s complaint in the Odyssey that mortals blame the gods for their own folly, and Epictetus’ portrait of the uninstructed person who lays the fault of his condition on others.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man must elevate himself by his own mind, not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna teaches that each person is responsible for lifting or lowering themselves; the self is its own friend or enemy.
Both locate the cause of one’s standing within oneself, not in others.
Christianity
“And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?”
— Matthew 7:3 (KJV) [4]
Jesus rebukes those who fixate on others’ small faults while ignoring their own large ones.
The Kural’s renownless person resents critics instead of examining himself: the same misplaced focus.
Islam
“And whatever strikes you of disaster - it is for what your hands have earned; but He pardons much.”
— Qur’an 42:30 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that misfortunes people suffer are largely the result of their own deeds.
The Kural traces a poor reputation to one’s own conduct; the Qur’an traces misfortune to one’s own hands.
Judaism
“Wherefore doth a living man complain, a man for the punishment of his sins?”
— Lamentations 3:39 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
The poet of Lamentations asks why anyone should complain about suffering that follows from their own wrongdoing, and urges self-examination instead.
Nearly the same rhetorical question as the Kural’s எவன் (‘why?’): why resent consequences you brought on yourself?
Buddhism
“Let none find fault with others; let none see the omissions and commissions of others. But let one see one’s own acts, done and undone.”
— Dhammapada 50 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha tells people to stop scrutinising others’ failures and to look at their own deeds, both done and left undone.
The Kural’s advice exactly: look at what you failed to do, not at those who criticise you.
Jainism
“By repenting of one's sins to oneself the soul obtains repentance, and becoming indifferent by repentance he prepares for himself an (ascending) scale of virtues, by which he destroys the Karman resulting from delusion.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.6 (cf. 29.7) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
In Jain practice, self-reproach for one's own faults (nindā) and confessing them before a teacher (garhā) are regular disciplines that purify the soul.
Valluvar tells those who lack renown to grieve over themselves instead of resenting their critics; Jainism likewise makes blaming oneself, not others, a path to moral growth.
Sikhism
“Slander me, slander me - go ahead, people, and slander me. Slander is pleasing to the Lord's humble servant.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 339, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir welcomes the criticism of others, saying that the devotee is glad to be slandered because it humbles and cleanses him.
The Kural asks why those who fail to earn renown resent their critics instead of grieving over themselves; Kabir models the opposite of resentment, accepting criticism without anger.
Greek Mythology
“See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly.”
— Homer, Odyssey 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Opening the Odyssey, Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for troubles they bring on themselves, citing Aegisthus.
The same diagnosis as the Kural: people blame outside forces for what their own conduct produced.
Philosophy
“An uninstructed person will lay the fault of his own bad condition upon others. Someone just starting instruction will lay the fault on himself.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus describes stages of moral progress: blaming others, then blaming oneself, and finally blaming no one.
Valluvar asks the renownless to take Epictetus’ second step: turn the blame inward rather than on critics.

Real Life Example

President Kennedy takes responsibility for the Bay of Pigs
21 April 1961 · Washington, D.C., United States
In April 1961 an invasion of Cuba by CIA-trained Cuban exiles at the Bay of Pigs failed within days. At his news conference on 21 April, President John F. Kennedy, pressed about the failure, noted the old saying that victory has a hundred fathers and defeat is an orphan, and stated that he was the responsible officer of the government. His public approval rating rose to 83 percent in the weeks that followed.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Instead of blaming advisers, agencies or critics, Kennedy publicly owned the failure, and the public respected him for it.
The kural asks why those who fall short resent their critics instead of grieving over themselves, and Kennedy answered a defeat by taking the blame on himself.
