Kural 266

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

தவஞ்செய்வார் தங்கருமஞ் செய்வார்மற் றல்லார் அவஞ்செய்வார் ஆசையுட் பட்டு.
Thavanjeyvaar thangarumanj seyvaar matru allaar Avanjeyvaar aasaiyut pattu.

Meaning

People who practice strict self-control are doing their own true work. Everyone else is caught in the trap of wanting things and only works toward their own harm.

Elementary

Think of a fly stuck in a spider’s web — the more it wriggles, the more stuck it gets. Valluvar says people who chase every want are like that fly, while people who practise self-control are doing the one job that really helps them.

University

The couplet rests on a sonic antithesis — தவம் (tavam, penance) against அவம் (avam, futility or self-harm) — so that tapas and its absence differ by a single consonant. தம் கருமம் (‘their own work’) implies that spiritual self-cultivation is a person’s genuine duty, while the ‘busy’ worldly life is in fact a failure to act for oneself. Parimelazhagar glosses அவம் as bringing harm upon oneself, since desire-driven action produces karmic bondage. The verse inverts ordinary valuations: the ascetic who seems idle is the only one truly working, and the striving householder, if driven by desire, is idle in the deepest sense.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material mode of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring sinful enemy of this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.37 [3]
Krishna names desire (kāma) as the root enemy that devours those it entangles.
Same diagnosis: desire (ஆசை) turns human effort into self-destruction.
Christianity
“For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul?”
— Matthew 16:26 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that worldly gain pursued at the soul’s expense is ultimate loss.
Mirrors தவம்/அவம்: desire-driven success is really self-ruin.
Islam
“But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge.”
— Qur’an 79:40-41 (Sahih International) [5]
Restraining the self from its desires (the basis of Sufi riyāḍa, self-discipline) is the path to salvation.
The self-restrained do their true work; the desire-led lose — the Kural’s dichotomy.
Judaism
“Rabbi Elazar Ha-kappar said: envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 (trans. J. Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah teaches that appetites and ambitions drive a person to ruin.
Same verdict as the Kural on those ‘caught in desire’.
Buddhism
“Those who are lust-infatuated fall back into the swirling current (of samsara) like a spider on its self-spun web. This, too, the wise cut off. Without any longing, they abandon all suffering and renounce the world.”
— Dhammapada 347 [7]
Desire is a self-spun trap; the wise cut it and renounce.
The same two paths: renunciation as true work, desire as self-made entanglement (ஆசையுட் பட்டு).
Jainism
“Pleasures are the thorn that rankles, pleasures are poison, pleasures are like a venomous snake; he who is desirous of pleasures will not get them, and will come to a bad end at last.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.53 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Nami, renouncing his throne, warns Indra that the pursuit of pleasure is self-destructive and ends in ruin.
Valluvar contrasts ascetics doing their true work with those who, caught in the net of desire, labour for their own ruin; Nami's verse gives the second half of that contrast in the strongest terms.
Sikhism
“This human body has been given to you. This is your chance to meet the Lord of the Universe. Nothing else will work.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 12, Raag Aasaa (So Purakh section; also at Ang 378), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan tells the listener that human birth is the one opportunity to meet God, and that every other pursuit is useless for that purpose.
The Kural says those who practise penance alone do their real work while everyone else labours for their own ruin; Guru Arjan likewise calls seeking God the one work that counts and the rest useless.
Greek Mythology
Erysichthon, cursed with insatiable hunger for cutting down Demeter’s sacred grove, devours all his wealth, sells his own daughter, and finally eats his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738-878 [10]
Ovid’s grim tale shows appetite consuming its owner.
A vivid image of desire-driven activity that is ultimately self-destruction (அவம்).
Philosophy
Socrates asks his fellow Athenians whether they are not ashamed to care so much about wealth, reputation and honour while giving no thought to wisdom, truth and the improvement of their souls.
— Plato, Apology 29d-e [11]
Socrates redefines a person’s real business as care of the soul.
Matches தம் கருமம் — the self-cultivator alone does his proper work; the acquisitive neglect it.

Real Life Example

Pope Celestine V resigns the papacy for the life of a hermit
13 December 1294 · Italy (Papal States and Kingdom of Naples)
Pietro da Morrone, an elderly hermit from the Abruzzi mountains, was elected pope in July 1294 and took the name Celestine V. After about five months he issued a decree that a pope could abdicate and then resigned, because he lacked administrative skill and believed the office endangered his soul. He hoped to return to his hermitage but was held under supervision by his successor, Boniface VIII, until his death.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Celestine was the first pope to resign of his own free will, and he chose the life of prayer over the highest office in the Western church.
The kural says those who practise penance are doing their own true work while others labour for their own ruin, and Celestine judged the busy papacy a danger and the hermit's life his real task.
