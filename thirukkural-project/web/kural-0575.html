Kural 575

Benignity · Kannottam · கண்ணோட்டம்

கண்ணிற்கு அணிகலம் கண்ணோட்டம் அஃதின்றேல் புண்ணென்று உணரப் படும்.
Kannirku anigalam kannottam aqdhindrel Punnendru unarap padum.

Meaning

Kindness is the jewel that makes the eye beautiful. Without it, the eyes will look like nothing more than two sores.

Elementary

People wear necklaces and bracelets to look nice. Valluvar says the nicest thing your eyes can ‘wear’ is kindness. If your eyes are always angry or cold, people will not see pretty eyes at all; they will see something that hurts, like a scrape.

University

அணிகலம் (‘ornament, jewel’) recalls the காரிகை (‘beauty’) of 571: kannottam is the eye’s jewellery, not merely its function. The shocking alternative, புண் (‘wound, sore’), plays on the near-rhyme கண்/புண், and the passive உணரப்படும் (‘will be perceived as’) implies the judgement of others; Solomon Pappaiah specifies ‘by the wise’ (பெரியோரால்), as does the Pope/Drew-style English gloss on ytamizh. Parimelazhagar’s point is that eyes are honoured only because they carry grace; without it they are merely openings in the flesh. This is the couplet the chapter is most remembered for, and its imagery of ornament versus wound is found in later Tamil ethical literature. The two sources agree; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi forms கண்ணிற் கணிகலங் … மஃதின்றேற் / புண்ணென் றுணரப்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Adi Shankara’s Kanakadhara Stotram, the poet praises the gracious sidelong glance (kaṭākṣa) of the goddess Lakshmi, whose kindly look alone is said to shower prosperity on a poor household.
— Adi Shankara, Kanakadhara Stotram (traditional attribution) [3]
In Hindu devotion the deity’s kindly glance is itself the gift and the adornment of divine beauty.
Like the Kural, the hymn treats the kindly look as the eye’s true jewel and source of blessing.
Christianity
“Then Jesus beholding him loved him, and said unto him, One thing thou lackest…”
— Mark 10:21 (KJV) [4]
Before challenging the rich young man, Jesus looks at him with love, so that even a hard word comes through a kindly gaze.
This is kannottam in action: the eye’s true adornment is the love it carries toward the person it looks at.
Islam
“Your smiling in the face of your brother is charity…”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1956 (graded hasan) [5]
The Prophet counts even a smile toward another as an act of charity (sadaqah).
A kindly face is itself a gift, just as the Kural calls kindly regard the eye’s jewel.
Judaism
“He that hath a bountiful eye shall be blessed; for he giveth of his bread to the poor.”
— Proverbs 22:9 (KJV) [4]
The Hebrew is tov ayin, literally ‘good of eye’, the biblical idiom for a generous, kindly disposition toward others.
The ‘good eye’ that is blessed is a close Hebrew counterpart to the kindly eye that the Kural calls adorned.
Buddhism
“One is dear to human beings, dear to non-human beings. The devas protect one. Neither fire, poison, nor weapons can touch one. One’s mind gains concentration quickly. One’s complexion is bright.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 11.16, Metta Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
Among the eleven benefits of cultivating goodwill (metta), the Buddha lists being dear to others and having a bright, clear complexion.
Goodwill brightens the face and makes one beloved, just as kannottam is the ornament of the eye.
Jainism
“As you look so happy in outward appearance, you must have peace of mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 18.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
A former Kshatriya who has turned monk reads Sanjaya's inner peace from the brightness of his face.
The Kural calls kindly regard the jewel of the eye; the Jain verse likewise assumes that a person's inner quality shows in, and adorns, the face.
Sikhism
“I am adorned with Truth, and I have applied the mascara of the Fear of God to my eyes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 737, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Speaking as the bride-soul, Guru Arjan says her true adornment is a virtue, and that what she puts on her eyes is reverence for God.
The Kural calls kindly regard the jewel that adorns the eye, without which eyes are mere sores; Guru Arjan likewise makes a virtue the real ornament of the eyes.
Greek Mythology
The Cyclops Polyphemus, with his single great eye, looks on Odysseus and his men not as guests but as food; Odysseus blinds that eye with a burning stake and escapes.
— Homer, Odyssey 9 [9]
Homer’s monster embodies the eye without regard for guests, and his punishment falls on the very eye that showed no kindness.
Polyphemus’s pitiless eye, turned into a wound, is a vivid picture of the Kural’s eye without kannottam becoming a sore.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes a nameless virtue of social life, a kind of friendliness, the mean between the obsequious flatterer and the quarrelsome, surly person who cares nothing for giving pain to others.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.6 [10]
For Aristotle the pleasant, considerate manner in dealings with others is a genuine virtue, and its absence makes one disagreeable.
Aristotle’s friendly mean is close to kannottam as the grace of social interaction, whose lack makes a person offensive to others.

Real Life Example

Audrey Hepburn's UNICEF mission to famine-struck Somalia
1992 · Baidoa and central Somalia
Audrey Hepburn, the film star, served as a UNICEF Goodwill Ambassador for a token salary of one dollar a year and had already visited Ethiopia, Sudan and Bangladesh for the agency. In 1992 she spent several days flying around central Somalia during the famine, visiting a feeding centre at Baidoa where starving children were being cared for. She was already seriously ill, underwent surgery in November 1992, and died in January 1993.
— Sources: [11] [12]
In her last working year, one of the world's most admired faces chose to be seen looking with compassion at suffering that much of the world had ignored.
The kural calls kindly regard the jewel of the eye, and Hepburn is remembered less for her famous looks than for the compassionate gaze she turned on Somalia's children.
