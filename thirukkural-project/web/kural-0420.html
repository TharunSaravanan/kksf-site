Kural 420

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

செவியிற் சுவையுணரா வாயுணர்வின் மாக்கள் அவியினும் வாழினும் என்.
Seviyir suvaiyunaraa vaayunarvin maakkal Aviyinum vaazhinum en.

Meaning

Some people know only the tastes of the mouth and not the tastes of the ear, like animals. What does it matter whether they live or die?

Elementary

Some people only care about yummy food and never enjoy listening to wise words. Valluvar says, sharply, that such people live like animals — whether they live or die makes little difference to the world.

University

The closing kural returns to the food metaphor of 412–413 with a stinging contrast between செவியிற் சுவை (the ear’s taste) and வாயுணர்வு (mouth-knowledge, i.e., the palate). மாக்கள் denotes people without discernment — often glossed as ‘beast-like men’. The rhetorical question அவியினும் வாழினும் என் (‘whether they perish or live, what of it?’) declares their existence inconsequential. Some commentators take வாயுணர்வு as knowing only what one’s own mouth says, i.e., talkers who never listen; either way the chapter closes by denying full humanity to those who never cultivate hearing.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Eating, sleeping, fear and mating are common to humans and animals; dharma alone is the distinguishing feature of humans, and without it they are equal to beasts.
— Hitopadesha, Prastāvikā (introductory verses) [4]
A famous verse in the introduction to the Hitopadesha defines human distinctiveness by something beyond the appetites.
Exactly the Kural’s judgment: those who know only the appetites are மாக்கள், beast-like.
Christianity
“Whose end is destruction, whose God is their belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who mind earthly things.”
— Philippians 3:19 (KJV) [5]
Paul condemns those governed by appetite and earthly concerns.
Those who know only the mouth’s taste — ‘whose God is their belly’ — are judged as worthless as the Kural’s மாக்கள்.
Islam
“…They have hearts with which they do not understand, they have eyes with which they do not see, and they have ears with which they do not hear. Those are like livestock; rather, they are more astray.”
— Qur’an 7:179 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an compares those with unused ears to cattle — even lower.
Almost exactly the Kural: those without the ‘taste of the ear’ are beast-like.
Judaism
“Man that is in honour, and understandeth not, is like the beasts that perish.”
— Psalm 49:20 (KJV) [5]
The Psalmist says a person without understanding, however prosperous, is no better than an animal.
Both texts equate a life without understanding with that of beasts.
Buddhism
“The man of little learning grows old like a bull. He grows only in bulk, but his wisdom does not grow.”
— Dhammapada 152 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Pali appassuta — ‘one who has heard little’ — is compared to an ox that merely grows fat.
The one who has not heard lives like a beast — precisely the Kural’s மாக்கள்.
Jainism
“Although they see by numerous examples the way by which their precious life may depart in the twinkling of an eye, yet they do not good even to the extent of a grain of millet. What does it matter whether such stupid, shameless (persons) live, or whether they die?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 33 'Defective knowledge', stanza 3 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar's chapter on defective knowledge dismisses people who learn nothing from what they see and do no good. It asks whether it matters at all if such people live or die.
Valluvar asks what it matters whether people who know only the tastes of the mouth, not of the ear, live or die. The Naladiyar asks the same question, almost word for word, about the stupid who take in nothing.
Sikhism
“O Baba, the pleasures of other foods are false. Eating them, the body is ruined, and wickedness and corruption enter into the mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 16, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Siree Raag Guru Nanak contrasts the true food of the Name with other foods, whose enjoyment harms the body and fills the mind with corruption.
The Kural scorns beast-like people who know only the tastes of the mouth and not those of the ear; Guru Nanak likewise rejects the palate's pleasures in favour of spiritual nourishment.
Greek Mythology
When King Midas judged Pan’s rustic piping superior to Apollo’s lyre, Apollo would not let such dull ears keep human shape and gave him the ears of an ass.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.146–193 (also 11.85–145) [10]
Ovid’s myth punishes an ear without taste by making it literally bestial; Midas had earlier shown the same folly in preferring gold, which turned even his food to metal.
Ears that do not ‘taste’ properly mark a person as beast-like — the Kural’s மாக்கள் image in myth.
Philosophy
Plato says those without wisdom and virtue, always occupied with feasting, look downward like cattle, bent over their tables, feeding and fattening and breeding.
— Plato, Republic IX, 586a–b [11]
The lover of bodily pleasure lives like grazing cattle, never tasting true pleasure.
A life devoted to the mouth rather than the mind is bestial — the Kural’s verdict.

Real Life Example

Crassus rejects advice before the Battle of Carrhae
53 BCE · Carrhae (near modern Harran, Turkey)
Marcus Licinius Crassus, one of the richest men in Rome, led a Roman army against the Parthian Empire in 53 BCE. According to Plutarch, he turned down the Armenian king Artavasdes' advice to advance through the hills of Armenia, where Parthian cavalry would be less effective, and instead followed an Arab chieftain, Ariamnes, onto the open plains of Mesopotamia. Near Carrhae the Parthian horse archers under Surena defeated his army, and Crassus was killed soon afterwards.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The campaign became a classic warning of how a powerful man who would not listen to counsel could bring ruin on himself and his army.
The kural dismisses those who know only the tastes of the mouth and never those of the ear, and Crassus, famous for his hunger for wealth, would not take in the advice that might have saved him.
