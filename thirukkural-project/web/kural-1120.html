Kural 1120

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

அனிச்சமும் அன்னத்தின் தூவியும் மாதர் அடிக்கு நெருஞ்சிப் பழம்.
Anichchamum annaththin thooviyum maadhar atikku nerunjip pazham.

Meaning

Even the anicham flower (a flower so soft it wilts at a touch) and a swan's soft feathers feel like prickly thorn burrs to this woman's feet.

Elementary

Imagine feet so tender that walking on a fluffy cotton ball feels like stepping on a prickly burr. That is how gently the poet imagines his beloved, as a playful, loving exaggeration.

University

The chapter ends as it began, with the anicham (1111), now joined by the annaththin thuvi (அன்னத்தின் தூவி, the swan’s down), the two classical standards of softness; both are said to be nerunjip pazham (நெருஞ்சிப் பழம், the spiny fruit of the caltrop, Tribulus terrestris) to her adi (அடி, feet). The figure is a hyperbole of delicacy in which the softest things become sharp; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses 'like nerunji thorns', Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan 'they cause pain as the nerunji fruit does'. Parimelazhagar’s tradition takes pazham here as the ripe, hardened fruit with its spines. Some commentators take maadhar as women in general, others (Pappaiah, Kalaignar) as the speaker’s own beloved; the context of the chapter supports the latter. Pope: 'The flower of the sensitive plant, and the down on the swan’s white breast, / As the thorn are harsh, by the delicate feet of this maiden pressed.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Anusuya, my foot is cut on a sharp blade of grass, and my dress is caught on an amaranth twig. Wait for me while I loosen it.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
As she leaves, Shakuntala claims a blade of grass has cut her foot, a pretext to linger and glance back at the king.
Both texts imagine the heroine’s feet hurt by the gentlest of things, though in Kalidasa it is a coy excuse.
Christianity
“How beautiful are thy feet with shoes, O prince's daughter!”
— Song of Solomon 7:1 (KJV) [4]
The Song begins its praise of the beloved with her feet.
Like the Kural, the Song lingers on the beauty of the beloved’s feet.
Islam
“As if they were [delicate] eggs, well-protected.”
— Qur’an 37:49 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an likens the companions of Paradise to delicate, carefully sheltered eggs.
Like the Kural, it describes a beauty so delicate it must be shielded from any roughness.
Judaism
“The tender and delicate woman among you, who would not adventure to set the sole of her foot upon the ground for delicateness and tenderness”
— Deuteronomy 28:56 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Deuteronomy describes a woman so pampered and delicate that she would not set her foot on the ground.
This is the Kural’s image almost exactly: feet too delicate to touch even the softest surface.
Buddhism
“As if they were stuffed with soft cotton, both my feet were once splendid. With age, they’re shriveled & cracked. The truth of the Truth-speaker’s words doesn’t change.”
— Therigatha 13.1, Ambapali (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Ambapali remembers her once soft and lovely feet and shows how age has changed them.
The Kural praises feet softer than swan’s down; Ambapali’s cotton-soft feet remind us that such softness does not last.
Jainism
“When the henna-dyed cotton was applied to the foot of my daughter of beauteous form, and whose mouth is perfumed like the red lotus, she would say, Gently, gently, and withdraw her foot lest it should be hurt by the cotton. How then will that foot be able to travel the gravelly paths of the forest?”
— Naladiyar 40.6 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
A mother in the Naladiyar remembers that her daughter’s feet were so tender she flinched even at soft cotton, and fears for her on the rough forest road.
This is the Kural’s own hyperbole of feet hurt by the softest things, in a Tamil Jain poem.
Sikhism
“Fareed, the path is muddy, and the house of my Beloved is so far away.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1379, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks, Sheikh Farid describes the lover hesitating at a muddy lane on the way to the Beloved's house; the following lines (paraphrased) weigh a soaked blanket against a broken heart, and he chooses to go.
The Kural pictures feet so delicate that even swan's down feels like thorns; Farid also dwells on the hardships of the lover's path, though for him love makes the rough road worth walking.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod tells that when Aphrodite first stepped ashore on Cyprus, grass grew up beneath her shapely feet.
— Hesiod, Theogony 194-195 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
The earth itself softens and blooms under the goddess of beauty’s tread.
The Kural imagines the softest things too rough for her feet; Hesiod imagines the ground turning green to receive them.
Philosophy
Agathon says Love is tender: as Homer says the goddess Ate has tender feet because she walks not on the ground but on the heads of men, so Love walks and dwells in the softest of all things, the hearts and souls of gods and men.
— Plato, Symposium 195d-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
In Plato’s dialogue, tenderness of foot becomes a sign of delicacy so great that only the softest things can bear it.
This is the Kural’s conceit in philosophical form: feet so tender that only the softest of things will do.

Real Life Example

Hans Christian Andersen publishes The Princess and the Pea
8 May 1835 · Denmark
On 8 May 1835 the Danish writer Hans Christian Andersen published The Princess and the Pea in his first collection of Fairy Tales Told for Children. In the tale an old queen tests a young woman who claims to be a real princess by placing a single pea under twenty mattresses and twenty eiderdown feather beds. In the morning the woman says she slept badly because something hard bruised her, and this extreme sensitivity proves she is a true princess.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The tale became one of the best-known fairy tales in the world and a byword for extraordinary delicacy.
The kural says that even the soft anicham flower and swan's down feel like thorny burrs to the beloved's tender feet, and Andersen's princess, bruised by a single pea through forty layers of bedding, is the most famous storybook version of the same playful exaggeration.
