Kural 908

Being Led by Women · Penvazhich Seral · பெண்வழிச் சேறல்

நட்டார் குறைமுடியார் நன்றாற்றார் நன்னுதலாள் பெட்டாங்கு ஒழுகு பவர்.
Nattaar kuraimutiyaar nandraatraar nannudhalaal pettaangu ozhuku pavar.

Meaning

Men who live only as their lovely wife wishes will not help their friends in need, and they will not do any good deeds.

Elementary

If you only ever do what one person wants, you might forget to help your other friends when they need you. Good friends keep room in their life for everyone who counts on them.

University

Nattaar (நட்டார்) are those who have formed friendship (natpu), and kurai mudiyaar (குறைமுடியார்) means they will not fulfil their friends' needs; nandru aatraar (நன்றாற்றார்) means they will not perform good deeds, which Parimelazhagar specifies as aram that aids the next world. Nan-nudhalaal (நன்னுதலாள், 'she of the fair forehead') is a conventional epithet he compares with 'bamboo-like shoulders' in 906, and pettaangu (பெட்டாங்கு) means 'as she desires'. His reasoning is that the wife cannot herself command and give wealth for these purposes, so both this-worldly and other-worldly duties lapse. Fitting the chapter's place in the Natpiyal (friendship) section, the verse highlights obligations to friends. The text is uniform across sources; Sivayogi Sivakumar's playful gloss reading pettaangu as 'like a pottu (forehead mark)' is a modern pun, not the classical sense. Modern readers may take the general point that any single relationship which crowds out duties to friends and community is unhealthy.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Sugriva, restored to his throne with Rama's help, forgets his promise to search for Sita while enjoying pleasures with Tara and Ruma, until Lakshmana confronts him in anger.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda [5]
A king indulging in the women's quarters neglects a debt to the friend who helped him.
This is a near-exact illustration of the Kural: absorbed in pleasure, a man fails to meet his friend's need.
Christianity
“And another said, I have married a wife, and therefore I cannot come.”
— Luke 14:20 (KJV) [6]
In Jesus' parable of the great supper, invited guests offer excuses, one citing his new marriage, and the host fills his table with the poor instead.
As in the Kural, attachment to a wife becomes an excuse to neglect a higher call and a host's invitation.
Islam
The Qur'an warns believers that if fathers, sons, brothers, spouses, relatives, wealth, trade and homes are dearer to them than God, His Messenger and striving in His cause, they should await God's decision.
— Qur'an 9:24 [7]
Family attachments, including to spouses, must not outrank one's higher obligations.
The Kural similarly warns that devotion to a wife must not displace duties to friends and to virtue.
Judaism
“Let thy house be wide open, and let the poor be members of thy household. Engage not in too much conversation with women. They said this with regard to one’s own wife, how much more [does the rule apply] with regard to another man’s wife.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:5 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [8]
Yose ben Yochanan pairs open-handed hospitality with a caution against excessive absorption in conversation with women, including one's wife.
Both texts link a man's generosity to others with not letting domestic intimacy consume his attention.
Buddhism
In his advice to the young householder Sigala, the Buddha lists reciprocal duties of husband and wife and also the duties one owes to friends and companions, such as generosity and help in need.
— Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31) [9]
The ideal householder balances obligations to spouse with obligations to friends and to religious teachers.
The Kural's man fails exactly this balance, letting one relationship eclipse friendship and good works.
Jainism
“Some people are (foolishly) attached to others, and are thereby deluded; the unrighteous make them adopt unrighteousness, and they exult in their wickedness.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.2.1.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [10]
The Sutrakritanga warns that foolish attachment to another person can draw a man away from right conduct altogether.
Kural 908 says men who live only as their wives wish neither help their friends nor do good deeds, and this verse gives the general Jain diagnosis behind that failure.
Sikhism
“In the midst of this world, do seva, and you shall be given a place of honor in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 26, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
Guru Nanak teaches that selfless service in ordinary life is what earns honour before God.
The Kural says a man who lives only to please his wife neither helps his friends nor does good deeds; Gurbani calls everyone to active service of others.
Greek Mythology
Mercury rebukes Aeneas for building Carthage for Dido while forgetting his destined kingdom and his people's future, and Aeneas departs.
— Vergil, Aeneid, Book 4 [12]
A leader absorbed in a queen's affairs neglects his obligations to his companions and his son.
As in the Kural, devotion to a beloved leads a man to fail those who depend on him.
Philosophy
Aristotle holds that friendship is among the greatest external goods and that the good person must act for his friends' sake, even at cost to himself.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Books VIII-IX [13]
For Aristotle, a flourishing life requires active care for friends.
The Kural's man who neglects his friends' needs forfeits this essential good.

Real Life Example

Edward IV's secret marriage to Elizabeth Woodville
1 May 1464 · England
While his chief supporter, Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, was negotiating a marriage for him with a French princess to secure an alliance with France, Edward IV secretly married Elizabeth Woodville, a young widow of no great rank, and disclosed the marriage later in 1464. The Woodville family then rose into positions of trust near the king, further estranging Warwick. The rift widened until Warwick turned against Edward and in 1470 drove him from the throne.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
The king's marriage cut across the plans and interests of the ally who had done most to put him on the throne, and the friendship never recovered.
The kural says those who live only as their wife wishes will not meet the needs of their friends, and Edward's marriage and favour to his wife's family cost him his greatest friend.
