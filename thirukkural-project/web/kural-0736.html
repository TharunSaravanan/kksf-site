Kural 736

The Land · Naadu · நாடு

கேடறியாக் கெட்ட இடத்தும் வளங்குன்றா நாடென்ப நாட்டின் தலை.
Kedariyaak ketta idaththum valangundraa naadenpa naattin thalai.

Meaning

Wise people say the best country is one that never meets ruin, and even when harm does come, does not lose its riches.

Elementary

A strong tree is rarely hurt by storms, and even when a branch breaks, it keeps giving fruit. The best country is like that tree: hardly ever harmed, and quick to stay fruitful even when it is.

University

The kural has two degrees of excellence: kedariyaa (‘not knowing ruin’) and, failing that, ketta idaththum valam kundraa (‘even when ruined, its richness does not decline’). Parimelazhagar takes the ruin to be damage from enemies, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah follow him, while Kalaignar reads it as any kind of harm that the land’s resources are great enough to repair. Enpa (‘they say’) marks the judgment as the view of the learned, and naattin thalai means ‘the head, or foremost, of lands’. The idea is resilience: the best land is judged not only by its good years but by how it comes through bad ones. The ytamizh text reads ‘நாட்டின் தலை’ and the sandhi text ‘நாட்டிற் தலை’, a difference of joining only.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Puranic story of King Prithu, the earth withholds her crops during a time of disorder and famine. Prithu pursues the earth, who has taken the form of a cow, and she agrees to yield again; he levels the land, and the earth is ‘milked’ so that grain and plenty return.
— Vishnu Purana 1.13 and Bhagavata Purana 4.18 (story of Prithu) [3]
The earth is named Prithvi after him, because he restored her fruitfulness after it had failed.
Prithu’s earth recovers its abundance after ruin, the second and harder test of the Kural’s best land.
Christianity
“And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock.”
— Matthew 7:24-25 (KJV) [4]
Jesus compares those who live by his words to a man who built his house on rock, which survived the storm.
The house that is struck but ‘fell not’ is a picture of the Kural’s land that stays whole even when ruin comes.
Islam
“Then will come after that seven difficult [years] which will consume what you saved for them, except a little from which you will store.”
— Qur’an 12:47-48 (Saheeh International) [5]
Joseph interprets the king’s dream and tells Egypt to store the surplus of seven good years, so that the land can survive seven years of famine.
Joseph’s Egypt is the Kural’s best land: when disaster strikes, its stores keep its richness from failing.
Judaism
Genesis tells that when famine struck the land, Isaac stayed in Gerar at God’s word, sowed there, and reaped a hundredfold in that same year, and grew very wealthy.
— Genesis 26:1, 12-13 [6]
Even in a year of famine, Isaac’s fields flourished.
Isaac’s hundredfold harvest in a famine year is an image of a land whose richness does not fail even in bad times.
Buddhism
“Truly, Ananda, as far as the Aryan race extends and trade routes spread, this will be the foremost city Pataliputta, a trade-center. But Pataliputta, Ananda, will be assailed by three perils — fire, water, and dissension.”
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahaparinibbana Sutta 1.28 (trans. Sister Vajira and Francis Story) [7]
Seeing the fort being built at Pataligama, the Buddha predicts it will become the greatest city of the region, but also names the dangers it will face.
The Buddha, like Valluvar, speaks of a ‘foremost’ land and knows that even the best land will meet danger.
Jainism
“The regions other than these are stable.”
— Tattvartha Sutra 3.28, with Pujyapada's Sarvarthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Jain cosmology teaches that only Bharata and Airavata pass through cycles of rise and decline, while the other regions (such as Videha) stay in a stable condition; Pujyapada's commentary adds that they have no ascending or descending half-cycles of time.
The Kural's best country is one that does not know ruin, or does not lose its richness even when harmed; Jain cosmology likewise imagines lands that never undergo decline.
Sikhism
The Harimandar at Amritsar was demolished by Ahmad Shah Durrani in 1762 and its sacred tank filled with debris; when the Sikhs sacked Sirhind in January 1764 they set aside several hundred thousand rupees for its restoration, the foundation of the new sanctum was laid on 19 April 1764, and the temple, bridge, gateway and cleared tank were completed by 1776.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University, Patiala), entry Des Raj, Bhai (by Sarmukh Singh Amole) [9]
Sikh history remembers the repeated destruction and rebuilding of its central shrine and city; within two years of ruin the community had funded and begun its restoration.
The Kural says the best land is one that does not lose its richness even when ruin comes; Amritsar, ruined in 1762 and rising again from 1764, is a Sikh example of that resilience.
Greek Mythology
In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, the grieving goddess makes the earth barren for a year, and famine threatens all mankind. When her daughter Persephone is returned, Demeter lets the fields grow again, and the whole earth is heavy with leaves and flowers.
— Homeric Hymn to Demeter (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
The myth explains how the land can be struck barren and then return to full growth.
Demeter’s earth, ruined and then made fruitful again, reflects the Kural’s land whose richness comes back even after harm.
Philosophy
Machiavelli argues that a prince whose city is well fortified, stocked with a year’s food, drink and fuel, and whose people are kept at work, cannot easily be taken, because an enemy cannot keep a siege going that long.
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, chapter 10 [11]
For Machiavelli, a city’s strength lies in its reserves as much as in its walls.
Machiavelli’s well-stocked city is the Kural’s best land: even when it is attacked, its wealth does not fail.

Real Life Example

Tokyo is rebuilt after the Great Kanto Earthquake
1923 to 1930 · Tokyo and Yokohama, Japan
On 1 September 1923 a magnitude 7.9 earthquake struck the Kanto region, and the fires that followed destroyed much of Tokyo. Within weeks the government set up the Imperial Capital Reconstruction Department under Home Minister Goto Shinpei to plan the city's recovery. Over the following years land readjustment and the rebuilding of streets, bridges, canals and parks were carried out across the burned districts, laying the foundations of modern Tokyo.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Even after one of the worst disasters of the century, Japan had the resources and organisation to rebuild its capital on a better plan.
The kural praises the land that, even when ruin comes, does not lose its richness, and the reconstruction of Tokyo after 1923 shows a country recovering its strength after catastrophe.
