Kural 117

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

கெடுவாக வையாது உலகம் நடுவாக நன்றிக்கண் தங்கியான் தாழ்வு.
Keduvaaga vaiyaadhu ulagam naduvaaga Nandrikkan thangiyaan thaazhvu.

Meaning

When a fair person who does what is right becomes poor, the world does not see it as a fall.

Elementary

If a shopkeeper loses money because she refused to cheat anyone, people don’t think of her as a failure. They respect her even more.

University

Kural 117 answers an objection that Kural 112 might invite: what if the just person does not prosper? Valluvar grants that the one who நடுவாக நன்றிக்கண் தங்கியான் (“abides in goodness, keeping to the middle”) may suffer தாழ்வு (“lowness, decline, poverty”), but insists the உலகம் will not regard it as கேடு (“ruin”). Here “the world” means the wise or discerning, as Pope (“wise man’s sight”) and Pappaiah (“the great”) make explicit. Social esteem is separated from material fortune: honour tracks justice, not wealth. The theme recurs across traditions: Proverbs’ “better is the poor that walketh in his uprightness,” the Beatitude for those persecuted for righteousness, Socrates’ poverty for the sake of his mission, and the Hindu legend of Harishchandra, who lost everything rather than break his word.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, to honour a promise made to the sage Vishvamitra, gives up his kingdom and wealth and is reduced to selling his wife and son and working at a cremation ground. He never abandons truth, and the gods finally restore and exalt him.
— Markandeya Purana, chapters 7–8 (Harishchandra legend) [4]
Harishchandra is the classic Indian example of a righteous man whose poverty is a mark of honour rather than disgrace.
The Kural’s point in story form: the poverty of one who stays righteous is not counted as ruin.
Christianity
“Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”
— Matthew 5:10 (KJV) [5]
Jesus declares that those who suffer because of their righteousness are blessed, not defeated.
Both reframe hardship caused by righteousness as honour rather than failure.
Islam
“Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you.”
— Qur’an 49:13 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an says true nobility is measured by righteousness, not by tribe or wealth.
Both put a person’s standing in their righteousness, not their material state.
Judaism
“Better is the poor that walketh in his uprightness, than he that is perverse in his ways, though he be rich.”
— Proverbs 28:6 (KJV) [5]
The proverb ranks an upright poor person above a crooked rich one.
Almost exactly the Kural: poverty with uprightness is not degradation.
Buddhism
“Not by matted hair, nor by lineage, nor by birth does one become a holy man. But he in whom truth and righteousness exist — he is pure, he is a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 393 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says true worth comes from truth and righteousness, not from outward status.
Both separate real standing from external circumstances.
Jainism
“The good, though they be poor and emaciated, will not guiltily ascend and rise over the bounds (of duty); binding their courage, as much as in them lies, with the cords of a mind free from anxiety, they will do the things that ought to be done.”
— Nālaḍiyār ch. 16 'The Good', st. 3 (= st. 153), English translation in The Indian Antiquary (wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaḍiyār describes the good as keeping within the bounds of right conduct even in poverty, unashamed and steady.
Valluvar says the world does not count the poverty of one who holds to fairness as a downfall, and the Nālaḍiyār shows such a person poor in means yet unbroken in duty.
Sikhism
“You are the wealth of the poor, the Guru of the guru-less, the honor of the dishonored.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 992, Raag Maaroo, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak praises God as the honour of those the world holds in no esteem.
The Kural says the world will not count the poverty of the upright as a fall; Gurbani goes further, saying the poor and dishonoured have God as their wealth and honour.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor old couple in Phrygia, are the only household to welcome the disguised gods Jupiter and Mercury. The gods spare them when the region is flooded, turn their hut into a temple, and grant their wish to serve it and to die together.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611–724 [10]
The myth honours a poor but righteous couple above their wealthier, inhospitable neighbours.
Their poverty is no mark of lowness in the gods’ eyes; their goodness is what counts.
Philosophy
“my occupation quite absorbs me, and I have no time to give either to any public matter of interest or to any concern of my own, but I am in utter poverty by reason of my devotion to the god.”
— Plato, Apology 23b–c (trans. Jowett) [11]
Socrates tells the jury that his poverty is the direct result of his commitment to his philosophical mission.
Socrates is a model of poverty that comes from integrity, and history has honoured rather than despised it.

Real Life Example

Sir Walter Scott chooses to repay his creditors
1826 - 1832 · Edinburgh and Abbotsford, Scotland, United Kingdom
In January 1826 the collapse of Scott's publisher, Archibald Constable and Co., brought down the Ballantyne printing firm in which Scott was a partner, leaving him liable for debts of about 121,000 pounds. Rather than take trade bankruptcy, which would have let him pay creditors only a fraction of what they were owed, he placed his entire literary income in a trust for his creditors, sold his Edinburgh house and wrote intensively for the rest of his life. The creditors accepted, partly from reluctance to ruin a man regarded as a national treasure whose misfortune had aroused great public sympathy, and the debt was finally cleared after his death through the sale of his copyrights.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Scott chose poverty and years of overwork over an easier legal escape that would have shortchanged the people he owed, and his public standing survived the financial collapse.
The kural says the world does not count it a downfall when one who holds to fairness becomes poor; Scott's ruin brought him sympathy rather than disgrace.
