Kural 326

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

கொல்லாமை மேற்கொண் டொழுகுவான் வாழ்நாள்மேல் செல்லாது உயிருண்ணுங் கூற்று.
Kollaamai merkon dozhuguvaan vaazhnaalmel Sellaadhu uyirunnum kootru.

Meaning

Death, which eats up all lives, will not come to cut short the days of someone who has made not killing the rule they live by.

Elementary

In old stories, Death is like a hunter who comes to take people away. Valluvar says that hunter holds back from someone who never hurts any creature — as if even Death respects a person who protects life.

University

Kūṟṟu, “the one who divides” (Yama, Death), is here personified as “the eater of lives” (uyiruṇṇum). Valluvar claims that it “will not go” (cellātu) against the life-span of one who lives by non-killing. Commentators differ: some read this literally as a promise of long life, others (like Kalaignar) as a poetic image of Death hesitating in awe, and still others spiritually, as freedom from the cycle of rebirth. The idea of karmic reciprocity — sparing life earns life — is explicit in Buddhist teaching on the karmic causes of long and short lives, and has echoes in the biblical promise of prolonged days for sparing the mother bird.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who does not seek to cause the sufferings of bonds and death to living creatures, (but) desires the good of all (beings), obtains endless bliss.”
— Manusmriti 5.46 (G. Bühler trans.) [4]
The Laws of Manu promise unending happiness to one who never inflicts captivity or death on living beings.
Like the Kural, it promises a reward beyond death’s reach to the one who refuses to kill.
Christianity
“What man is he that desireth life, and loveth many days, that he may see good? Keep thy tongue from evil, and thy lips from speaking guile. Depart from evil, and do good; seek peace, and pursue it.”
— Psalm 34:12–14 (KJV) [5]
The Psalmist teaches that long and good life belongs to those who turn from evil and pursue peace.
Shares the Kural’s link between a harmless, peaceful life and many days of life.
Islam
“The merciful are shown mercy by Ar-Rahman. Be merciful on the earth, and you will be shown mercy from Who is above the heavens.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1924 (graded Hasan) [6]
The Prophet teaches that mercy shown to those on earth is answered by divine mercy.
Reciprocity as in the Kural: one who spares and shows mercy to life is spared in turn.
Judaism
“But thou shalt in any wise let the dam go, and take the young to thee; that it may be well with thee, and that thou mayest prolong thy days.”
— Deuteronomy 22:7 (KJV) [5]
The Torah commands sparing the mother bird when taking from a nest, promising long life in return.
Very close to the Kural: sparing animal life is explicitly rewarded with prolonged days.
Buddhism
“…having abandoned the killing of living beings, abstains from killing living beings, lays aside the rod and lays aside the knife… If… he comes to the human state, he is long-lived wherever he is reborn.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 135, Cula-kammavibhanga Sutta (Ñanamoli Thera trans.) [7]
The Buddha explains to Subha that abstaining from killing is the karmic cause of long life.
Nearly identical to the Kural: non-killing keeps death at bay.
Jainism
“He should cease to injure living beings whether they move or not, on high, below, and on earth. For this has been called the Nirvâna, which consists in peace.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.11.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga identifies complete cessation of injury with nirvāṇa, the deathless peace beyond the round of births.
The Kural says death will not overtake the one who lives by non-killing; the Jain text gives a spiritual version, equating non-injury with the peace of liberation, beyond death's reach.
Sikhism
“The Messenger of Death does not approach those who are pleasing to the Lord God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 401, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Gurbani repeatedly promises that Death's messenger cannot come near those who live in God's favour; Sikh commentators usually read this as freedom from spiritual death and the fear of death.
The Kural says devouring Death will not advance on the days of one who keeps to non-killing; Guru Arjan makes a similar promise of protection from Death for the one pleasing to God.
Greek Mythology
The poor couple Baucis and Philemon try to kill their only goose to feed their disguised divine guests; the gods forbid its slaughter, then spare the couple from the flood and grant them to die together at the same hour.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.684–720 [10]
In Ovid’s tale the gods stop the killing and reward the couple with protection from death’s disaster.
An image of divine favour holding death back from the gentle and hospitable.
Philosophy
“no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death.”
— Plato, Apology 41d (Jowett trans.) [11]
At his trial, Socrates tells his judges that the gods do not neglect the good, so death holds no terror for them.
Like the Kural, it suggests death has no real power over the one who lives rightly.

Real Life Example

The Christmas Truce on the Western Front
24-25 December 1914 · Western Front, Belgium and northern France
On Christmas Eve 1914 British troops heard German soldiers singing carols and saw lights and small trees along the German trenches, and soldiers began calling greetings across the lines. On Christmas Day men from both sides met in no man's land, exchanged food, tobacco and souvenirs, buried their dead and in some places played football. The truce was unofficial and not observed everywhere, and commanders issued strict orders against repeating it.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
For a short time ordinary soldiers stopped killing one another, and the truce has become an enduring symbol of shared humanity in war.
The kural says Death will not advance on those who keep to non-killing, and while the men laid down their killing, the dying in those sectors paused too.
