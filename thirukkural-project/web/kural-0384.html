Kural 384

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

அறனிழுக்கா தல்லவை நீக்கி மறனிழுக்கா மானம் உடைய தரசு.
Aranizhukkaa thallavai neekki maranizhukkaa Maanam udaiya tharasu.

Meaning

A true king never turns away from what is right, removes what is wrong from the land, and keeps a brave sense of honor that never falls short.

Elementary

A good referee always follows the rules, sends off players who cheat, and is brave enough to make hard calls. This kural says a real ruler is like that: always fair, stops wrongdoing, and never loses courage or self-respect.

University

The couplet is built on a pair of parallel negatives: aran izhukkaa (not slipping from dharma) and maran izhukkaa (not slipping from martial valour), bound together by maanam — honour or self-respect. Between them sits the positive duty allavai neekki, ‘removing what is not right’, which commentators read both as the king avoiding vice himself and as his suppressing wrongdoing in the realm. The effect is to insist that courage and justice must travel together: valour without righteousness becomes tyranny, while righteousness without valour cannot protect anyone. This coupling of justice and courage has a close analogue in Cicero’s claim that greatness of spirit divorced from justice is a vice rather than a virtue.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If the king did not, without tiring, inflict punishment on those worthy to be punished, the stronger would roast the weaker, like fish on a spit;”
— Manusmriti 7.20 (tr. G. Bühler) [3]
The Laws of Manu say that if the king fails to punish wrongdoers, the strong will destroy the weak.
Supports the Kural’s ‘allavai neekki’ — the king’s duty to remove unrighteousness from the land.
Christianity
“Take away the wicked from before the king, and his throne shall be established in righteousness.”
— Proverbs 25:5 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says a king’s rule becomes secure when wicked people are removed from his presence.
Directly parallels ‘allavai neekki’ — removing the unrighteous so that rule rests on justice.
Islam
“O David, indeed We have made you a successor upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth and do not follow [your own] desire, as it will lead you astray from the way of Allah.”
— Qur’an 38:26 (Sahih International) [5]
God tells King David to rule with truth and not follow his own wishes.
Matches ‘aran izhukkaa’ — a ruler who never slips from righteousness.
Judaism
“Thus saith the LORD; Execute ye judgment and righteousness, and deliver the spoiled out of the hand of the oppressor: and do no wrong, do no violence to the stranger, the fatherless, nor the widow...”
— Jeremiah 22:3 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah tells the king of Judah to do justice, rescue the oppressed, and never do wrong himself.
Combines the Kural’s two duties: staying righteous oneself and actively removing oppression.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that when kings are unrighteous, their ministers, officials, and people become unrighteous too, and even nature falls out of order; when kings are righteous, righteousness spreads downward through the whole realm (AN 4.70).
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.70 (Adhammika Sutta) [6]
The Buddha says a king’s goodness or badness flows down to everyone in the kingdom.
Explains why the Kural requires the king to never slip from dharma — his conduct sets the moral tone of the land.
Jainism
“Indra: “Punishing thieves and robbers, cut-purses and burglars, you should establish public safety; thus you will be a Kshattriya.” Nami: “Men frequently apply punishment wrongly: the innocent are put in prison, and the perpetrator of the crime is set at liberty.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.28–30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Indra, disguised as a Brahmin, tests the renouncing king Nami by pressing royal duties on him, including rooting out wrongdoers. Nami answers that outward justice often misfires, and turns instead to conquering the passions within.
Valluvar praises the king who never slips from dharma and removes what is unrighteous from the land. The Jain dialogue accepts that this is the warrior's duty but questions how reliable punishment is, and puts the deeper removal of wrong inside the self.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Kabir, in a hymn included in the Guru Granth Sahib, says the true hero is one who fights for righteousness and does not abandon the field even when cut to pieces.
The Kural joins never swerving from righteousness with a valour and honour that never falls short; Kabir's hero is defined by exactly that union of dharma and unyielding courage.
Greek Mythology
On his way to Athens, the young Theseus deliberately takes the dangerous land route and kills a series of bandits (Sinis, Sciron, Procrustes and others) who preyed on travellers, before becoming king.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 8-11 [9]
Theseus bravely clears the road of robbers and murderers, making it safe for everyone, and later becomes a respected king.
Shows courage harnessed to justice — removing wrongdoers — which is the Kural’s joining of maram with aram.
Philosophy
Cicero argues that the loftiness of spirit shown in danger and toil, if it lacks justice and fights for selfish ends rather than the common good, is a vice rather than a virtue.
— Cicero, De Officiis 1.62 [10]
Cicero says bravery is only good when it serves justice; bravery for selfish ends is actually bad.
Mirrors the Kural’s insistence that valour (maram) and righteousness (aram) must both remain unbroken together.

Real Life Example

King Haakon VII refuses to appoint Quisling
10 April 1940 · Nybergsund, Norway
After Germany invaded Norway, the German envoy Curt Brauer met King Haakon VII on 10 April 1940 and demanded that he dismiss the lawful government and appoint a government headed by the collaborator Vidkun Quisling. At an extraordinary Council of State the king said he could not comply and would rather abdicate than appoint Quisling, while leaving the decision to his ministers, who supported him. German aircraft then bombed Nybergsund, and the king later continued the resistance from exile in Britain.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Haakon refused to lend royal authority to an illegitimate regime even under direct military threat. His refusal became a rallying point for Norwegian resistance throughout the occupation.
The kural describes a king who never swerves from righteousness, removes what is wrong and never loses courage or honour, and Haakon's refusal joined justice and courage at the cost of great personal danger.
