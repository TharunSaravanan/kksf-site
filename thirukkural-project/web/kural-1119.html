Kural 1119

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

மலரன்ன கண்ணாள் முகமொத்தி யாயின் பலர்காணத் தோன்றல் மதி.
Malaranna kannaal mukamoththi yaayin palarkaanath thoandral madhi.

Meaning

Moon, if you want to be like the face of the girl whose eyes are like flowers, do not show yourself where everyone can see you.

Elementary

Think of a special treasure you keep in a box just for yourself. The poet tells the moon: 'If you want to be as special as her face, don’t show yourself to everyone. Her face is like a treasure only I get to see.'

University

The moon sequence closes with a second condition: if the moon is to resemble (oththi, ஒத்தி, 'you resemble') the face of malaranna kannaal (மலரன்ன கண்ணாள், 'she whose eyes are like flowers'), it must not appear palar kaana (பலர் காண, 'for many to see'), since her face is seen by him alone. The idea picks up palar kaanum ('seen by many') from 1112, and Solomon Pappaiah draws the link: 'appear for me alone, not for all to see'. Kalaignar reads it as advice to the moon to stay hidden so as not to lose the comparison; Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan give the plain prohibition. The exclusivity reflects the kalavu (secret love) setting, in which the heroine is unseen by others. Pope: 'If as her face, whose eyes are flowers, thou wouldst have charms for me, / Shine for my eyes alone, O moon, shine not for all to see!' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“She seems a flower whose fragrance none has tasted, / A gem uncut by workman’s tool, / A branch no desecrating hands have wasted, / Fresh honey, beautifully cool.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act II (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
The king describes Shakuntala, raised in a forest hermitage, as a beauty no one else has yet known.
Both lovers treasure a beauty that is not on show to the world.
Christianity
“A garden inclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed.”
— Song of Solomon 4:12 (KJV) [4]
The lover pictures his beloved as an enclosed garden and a sealed fountain, kept for him alone.
This is the Kural’s wish for exclusivity: a beauty not open to every passer-by.
Islam
“Fair ones reserved in pavilions -”
— Qur’an 55:72 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the companions of Paradise as beauties kept secluded in pavilions.
Like the face the Kural would keep from 'many eyes', this beauty is reserved and hidden.
Judaism
“O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock, in the covert of the cliff, Let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; For sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.”
— Song of Songs 2:14 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover calls to the beloved hidden like a dove in the rocks, asking that her face be shown to him.
As in the Kural, the beloved’s face is hidden from the world and longed for by one lover.
Buddhism
The Buddha says he knows of no other single sight that so overpowers a man’s mind as the form of a woman.
— Anguttara Nikaya 1.1 (paraphrase) [7]
Early Buddhism recognises the overwhelming effect of visible beauty on the mind.
The Kural wants such a beauty kept from many eyes; Buddhism explains why it unsettles every beholder.
Jainism
“Though women be high in reputation and equal to the goddess Ayrani in conjugal fidelity, they must carefully avoid those who love them, and follow them in hopes of gratification, for such caution is the safeguard of the virtue of matrons with perfumed foreheads.”
— Naladiyar 39.1 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar counsels virtuous women to keep away from admirers who pursue them.
The Kural wants the beloved’s beauty kept from many eyes; the Jain poet makes that reserve a safeguard of virtue.
Sikhism
“The Gurmukh sees Him with intuitive ease.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 222, Raag Gauri, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gauri, Guru Nanak says the Lord, hidden from most people, is seen naturally by the Gurmukh, the one who follows the Guru.
The Kural asks the moon not to shine for all to see, since the beloved's face is seen by the lover alone; in Gurbani the Beloved also stays hidden from the many and is seen by the one who loves Him through the Guru.
Greek Mythology
King Candaules of Lydia, proud of his wife’s beauty, insists that his bodyguard Gyges secretly see her; the queen discovers it, and the episode costs Candaules his throne and his life.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.8-12 (paraphrase) [10]
Herodotus tells the story as a warning about a man who would not keep his wife’s beauty private.
The Kural’s lover wants the beloved’s face seen by no one else; Candaules’s ruin shows the opposite impulse punished.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that if a lover imagines the beloved joined to another as closely as to himself, he will feel hatred toward the beloved and envy toward the rival; this is jealousy.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Proposition 35 and Scholium (paraphrase) [11]
Spinoza analyses the wish to possess the beloved’s attention exclusively.
The Kural’s demand that the moon not appear for all to see is a gentle, poetic form of the possessive love Spinoza dissects.

Real Life Example

Elizabeth Barrett Browning's private Sonnets from the Portuguese
1845–1850 · England and Italy
During her courtship with the poet Robert Browning, around 1845 and 1846, Elizabeth Barrett wrote a sequence of 44 love sonnets that she had not intended to publish. Robert later persuaded her of their worth, and they appeared in her Poems of 1850. She called them Sonnets from the Portuguese, suggesting they were translations, to protect the privacy of such personal poems; the title probably came from Robert's pet name for her.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sequence became one of the best-loved collections of love poetry in English, yet it began as words meant for one reader only.
The kural tells the moon that to resemble the beloved's face it must not show itself for everyone to see, because her face is for one viewer alone; Barrett's sonnets were likewise written for one reader, and even when published they were disguised as translations.
