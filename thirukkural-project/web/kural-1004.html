Kural 1004

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

எச்சமென்று என்எண்ணுங் கொல்லோ ஒருவரால் நச்சப் படாஅ தவன்.
Echchamendru Enennung Kollo Oruvaraal Nachchap Pataaa Thavan

Meaning

What does a person whom no one loves think they will leave behind after they die?

Elementary

When a kind teacher retires, the children remember her stories and her hugs for years. But if someone never helped anyone, what will people remember about him when he is gone? Valluvar asks: what is left of a person nobody loved?

University

The key word is echcham (எச்சம், 'remainder, what is left over'), which in the Kural can mean posterity or a lasting name; Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss 'what remains after his death', Solomon Pappaiah 'what will stand to recall him after his time'. The man is described as oruvaraal nachchap padaaa-thavan, 'one not desired by even a single person'; nachchu (நச்சு, 'to desire, love') returns in Kural 1008, where the same unloved man's wealth is a poisonous tree, so the chapter plays on நச்சு 'love' and நச்சு 'poison'. Mu. Varadarasanar supplies the reason for his unpopularity, that he helped no one. Pope's explanation narrows echcham to what the miser can count as his own 'in the world to come', a reading that follows the classical tendency to interpret legacy in terms of merit for the next life. The rhetorical question (என் எண்ணும் கொல்லோ, 'what, pray, does he think?') turns the couplet into a mock of the miser's planning. The text is identical on both sources (ytamizh prints எண்ணுங் with the same ங்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu teaches that in the next world neither father nor mother, wife, sons nor kin remain as one's companions; leaving the body on the ground like a log, relatives turn away, and spiritual merit alone follows the dead.
— Manusmriti 4.239–242 (tr. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Only the good one has done survives a person; everything else is left behind.
The Kural's unloved hoarder has done no good, so nothing worthwhile remains of him.
Christianity
Jesus tells his hearers not to lay up treasures on earth, where moth and rust corrupt and thieves steal, but to lay up treasures in heaven, for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.
— Matthew 6:19–21 [4]
Earthly hoards vanish; only what is invested in goodness endures.
The Kural asks the same question of the miser: what lasting treasure has he laid up?
Islam
The Qur'an says that wealth and children are the adornment of worldly life, but the enduring good deeds are better with your Lord for reward and better for one's hope.
— Qur'an 18:46 (Al-Kahf) [5]
Possessions are passing ornaments; only lasting good deeds (al-baqiyat al-salihat, 'the things that remain') endure.
The Kural's miser, having done no good, has none of these 'remaining' deeds to leave behind.
Judaism
The Mishnah teaches that when a person departs, neither silver nor gold nor precious stones and pearls accompany him, but only Torah and good deeds.
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 6:9 [6]
Wealth stays behind at death; only learning and good deeds go with a person.
The Kural asks what the hoarder who has earned no one's love can possibly leave or take with him.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says the fool frets, thinking, I have sons, I have wealth, yet he is not even his own master, so how can sons or wealth be his?
— Dhammapada 62 [7]
Possessions and family give no lasting hold to someone who does not even possess himself.
The Kural's unloved miser likewise has nothing that will truly remain as his.
Jainism
“Whenever you die, O king, and leave all pleasant things behind, the Law alone, and nothing else in this world, will save you, O monarch.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The queen tells King Ishukâra that at death only dharma remains as a refuge, not property.
The Kural asks what the man who has won no one's love by giving can hope to have left; the Jain answer is only the Law he practised.
Sikhism
“The kings die, gathering and hoarding their money, burying great quantities of gold.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 654, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir, whose hymns are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, points to kings who spent their lives hoarding and burying gold, and died with it still in the ground.
The Kural asks what legacy a man whom no one loves can leave; Kabir's buried gold is the hoarder's only legacy, of use to no one.
Greek Mythology
Achilles, refusing Agamemnon's lavish gifts, says that cattle and treasure can be won by raiding but a man's life cannot be recovered, and that his mother foretold a choice between a long, obscure life and an early death with imperishable glory.
— Homer, Iliad 9.401–416 [10]
In heroic thought, what outlasts a person is kleos, the remembered name, not possessions.
The Kural's unloved miser has no such remembered name to leave behind.
Philosophy
The aged Cephalus tells Socrates that the greatest benefit of wealth for a decent man is that, as death approaches, he need not depart owing sacrifices to the gods or money to anyone, having cheated no one.
— Plato, Republic I, 330d–331b [11]
For Cephalus, wealth matters at the end only for the clean account it lets a man leave behind.
The Kural presses the same end-of-life question on the miser: what will he have left that counts?

Real Life Example

The miser John Camden Neild leaves his fortune to Queen Victoria
30 August 1852 · Chelsea, London, and North Marston, Buckinghamshire, United Kingdom
John Camden Neild inherited a fortune of about 250,000 pounds from his father in 1814 and lived as a recluse and miser in his house in Cheyne Walk, Chelsea. When he died in August 1852 his will left almost his entire estate, then worth about 500,000 pounds, to Queen Victoria, whom he had never met, while making no provision for his longtime servants. The Queen increased the small bequests to his executors, provided for his servants, and later restored the chancel of North Marston church, where he was buried, inserting a window in his memory.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Neild left behind no circle of family or friends, and the only lasting memorial to him was raised by a stranger who had received his money.
The kural asks what a man whom no one loves can expect to leave behind, and Neild's long, solitary hoarding left nothing of him but a bequest to someone he had never known.
