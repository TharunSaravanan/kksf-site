Kural 186

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

பிறன்பழி கூறுவான் தன்பழி யுள்ளும் திறன்தெரிந்து கூறப் படும்.
Piranpazhi kooruvaan thanpazhi yullum, Thirantherindhu koorap padum.

Meaning

A person who spreads other people's faults will have their own faults searched out, and the worst ones will be chosen and told about them.

Elementary

If you tell everyone about your friend’s mistakes, soon people start looking for your mistakes too — and they’ll pick the most embarrassing ones to share. Gossip is like a boomerang: it comes back to hit you.

University

The couplet states a law of reciprocity in the economy of reputation. Piṟaṉ paḻi kūṟuvāṉ (“one who speaks another’s blame”) will find that taṉ paḻi uḷḷum (“even among his own blameworthy deeds”) the ones chosen with discernment (tiṟaṉ terintu) will be spoken of. The phrase tiṟaṉ terintu is sharp: people will not merely repeat his faults but select the most damaging, just as the backbiter selected and polished the faults of others. Valluvar therefore presents slander as self-defeating — by making fault-finding normal, the slanderer licenses the same treatment of himself. The passive kūṟappaṭum (“will be spoken”) leaves the agent unspecified, allowing both a social reading (others will retaliate) and a moral-causal one akin to karma. The idea that judgment returns in the same measure is widely shared across ethical traditions.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As it does and acts, so it becomes; by doing good it becomes good, and by doing evil it becomes evil”
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4.5 (Swami Madhavananda) [3]
The Upanishad states the law of karma: a person becomes what he does.
The slanderer who deals in faults becomes a man of faults — and, as the Kural says, is spoken of for them.
Christianity
“For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.”
— Matthew 7:2 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that the standard you use to judge others will be used to judge you.
This is the Kural’s boomerang: pick at others’ faults and your own will be picked at the same way.
Islam
“For indeed whoever tries to expose his Muslims brother’s secrets, Allah exposes his secrets wide open, even if he were in the depth of his house.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2032 [5]
The Prophet warns that whoever hunts for and exposes others’ hidden faults will have his own hidden faults exposed by God.
Almost the Kural itself: the exposer of others’ faults will see his own faults exposed.
Judaism
“What is the meaning of that which is written: ‘This shall be the law of the leper [metzora]’ … This means that this shall be the law of a defamer [motzi shem ra].”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 15b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
Reish Lakish reads metzora (one afflicted with tzara’at) as motzi shem ra, a defamer: the one who hid behind secret speech is publicly marked for it.
In both, the slanderer’s own disgrace becomes public — his hidden fault is put on display.
Buddhism
“Whoever harasses an innocent man, a man pure, without blemish: The evil comes right back to the fool like fine dust thrown against the wind.”
— Sutta Nipata 3.10, Kokālika Sutta (Thanissaro) [7]
Said of the monk Kokālika, who slandered Sāriputta and Moggallāna and came to ruin.
Slander thrown at others blows back on the slanderer — the Kural’s point in a vivid image.
Jainism
“A man who insults another will long whirl in the Circle of Births; to blame others is not good. Considering this a sage is not conceited.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.2.2.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga warns that insulting and blaming others binds karma that keeps the insulter in the cycle of rebirth for a long time.
Valluvar says the man who spreads others' faults will have his own worst faults spread in turn, and the Jain text likewise says blaming others comes back on the one who blames, here as long bondage to rebirth.
Sikhism
“Fareed, if you have a keen understanding, then do not write black marks against anyone else. Look underneath your own collar instead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid, Salok 6 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid tells the discerning person to stop recording other people's faults and to look honestly at his own.
The Kural warns that one who spreads others' faults will have his own worst faults picked out and told; Farid turns the same scrutiny back on oneself before others do it.
Greek Mythology
“The best treasure a man can have is a sparing tongue, and the greatest pleasure, one that moves orderly; for if you speak evil, you yourself will soon be worse spoken of.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 719–721 (Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod advises restraint of speech because evil-speaking comes back as worse talk about oneself.
Nearly the Kural’s exact claim: speak ill of others and you will be spoken of worse.
Philosophy
“You look at the pimples of others when you yourselves are covered with a mass of sores”
— Seneca, On the Happy Life 27 (Basore) [11]
Seneca turns on the critics who mock philosophers’ faults, reminding them how much worse their own are.
The fault-finder invites a return inspection that shows his own, larger faults — as the Kural predicts.

Real Life Example

James Callender turns his pen on Thomas Jefferson
1802 · Richmond, Virginia, United States
The journalist James Thomson Callender had attacked John Adams and the Federalists in pamphlets such as The Prospect Before Us (1800), with financial help from Thomas Jefferson, and was jailed under the Sedition Act. After Jefferson became president, Callender expected a reward, including the post of postmaster of Richmond, and when it did not come he felt betrayed. In 1802, writing in the Richmond Recorder, he turned the same invective on Jefferson, publishing damaging allegations about Jefferson's private life and the letters showing Jefferson had given him money.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Jefferson had helped sponsor a writer whose trade was attacking others' reputations, and that writer ended by selecting and publishing the most damaging charges against Jefferson himself.
The kural warns that one who spreads others' faults will have his own worst faults hunted out and told, and Jefferson's support for Callender's attacks came back on him in exactly that way.
