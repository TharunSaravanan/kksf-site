Kural 828

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

தொழுதகை யுள்ளும் படையொடுங்கும் ஒன்னார் அழுதகண் ணீரும் அனைத்து.
Thozhuthagai yullum padaiyodungum onnaar azhuthagan neerum anaiththu.

Meaning

An enemy may hide a weapon in the hands they fold before you in respect. Their tears hide harm in the same way.

Elementary

Imagine someone bowing to you with hands pressed together, but hiding a water balloon behind them. Pretend sadness can be a trick too. If someone has been mean to you, a show of respect or tears doesn’t always mean they have changed.

University

Thozhuthagai (தொழுதகை) is the hand joined in worship or salutation, and paḍai oḍungum (படை ஒடுங்கும்) means ‘a weapon lies concealed’. The second line extends the image: onnaar azhutha kaṇṇeer (ஒன்னார் அழுத கண்ணீர், the tears foes have wept) is anaiththu (அனைத்து, of that same kind). Varadarasanar and Pappaiah read the couplet literally as a weapon concealed in the worshipping hand, and Kalaignar adds that even in weeping their hearts are full of plotting. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; thirukkural.net prints the words as தொழுதகை யுள்ளும் and கண் ணீரும் with the same metrical division.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And O Bharata, speak soft words before thou smitest and even while thou art smiting! After the smiting is over, pity the victim, and grieve for him, and even shed tears.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section 142, Kanika’s counsel (Ganguli translation) [3]
The minister Kanika gives Dhritarashtra a cynical lesson in statecraft: gentle words and tears can be used as cover for violence.
Kanika’s counsel is the exact practice the Kural warns about: a foe’s tears that are part of the weapon.
Christianity
“And he sent them to Bethlehem, and said, Go and search diligently for the young child; and when ye have found him, bring me word again, that I may come and worship him also.”
— Matthew 2:8 (KJV) [4]
Herod claims he wants to worship the child, while planning to kill him.
Herod’s ‘worship’ is the worshipping hand with a weapon hidden inside.
Islam
“Here you are loving them but they are not loving you, while you believe in the Scripture - all of it. And when they meet you, they say, "We believe." But when they are alone, they bite their fingertips at you in rage.”
— Qur’an 3:119 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes people who profess faith to the believers’ faces but rage against them in private.
Their profession of belief is the joined hand, and their private rage the hidden weapon.
Judaism
“Joab said to Amasa, “How are you, brother?” and with his right hand Joab took hold of Amasa’s beard as if to kiss him. Amasa was not on his guard against the sword in Joab’s [left] hand, and [Joab] drove it into his belly”
— 2 Samuel 20:9-10 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Joab greets his rival with a brotherly embrace while holding a sword in his other hand.
A hand of greeting with a weapon hidden in the other is the Kural’s image made into history.
Buddhism
In the Bilara Jataka, a jackal poses as a holy ascetic, standing on one leg and facing the sun in apparent worship. Rats come to pay him respect, and as they leave he seizes and eats the last of them each day, until the Bodhisatta, king of the rats, exposes him.
— Bilara Jataka (Jataka No. 128), trans. in E. B. Cowell (ed.), The Jataka [7]
A posture of worship is used as the cover for killing.
The jackal’s devotion is the worshipping hand that hides the weapon.
Jainism
“A little monkey breaking into a fruit with its finger, will strike and seize its own father, though coming to meet it. Lord of the hills! the friendship of those who are without unity (of mind) is not pleasant.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 24 ('Improper Friendship'), st. 7 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār uses the image of a young monkey that strikes even its own father as he comes toward it, to show that closeness without unity of mind is dangerous.
Valluvar says a weapon may hide in the very hands a foe joins in worship; the Nālaṭiyār's monkey that strikes the one approaching it likewise shows harm arriving through a gesture of closeness.
Sikhism
“The man-eaters say their prayers. Those who wield the knife wear the sacred thread around their necks. … in their hands they hold the knives - they are the butchers of the world!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 471, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok 2 before pauri 16), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa Ki Vaar Guru Nanak attacks oppressors of his day who perform prayers and wear religious marks while holding the knife against others.
The Kural says a weapon may lie hidden in hands joined in worship; Guru Nanak likewise shows the knife held by those who make a show of prayer.
Greek Mythology
“others were for letting it remain as an offering and propitiation for the gods. And this was how they settled it in the end, for the city was doomed when it took in that horse, within which were all the bravest of the Argives waiting to bring death and destruction on the Trojans.”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 8 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
The Greeks’ apparent religious offering hides an army inside it.
The wooden horse is a weapon hidden inside an act of worship, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“The great advantages of simulation and dissimulation are three. First, to lay asleep opposition, and to surprise.”
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Simulation and Dissimulation’ [11]
Bacon notes coolly that a show which hides one’s true intent serves to disarm others and take them by surprise.
This is why the Kural warns of foes’ worship and tears: such shows are designed to lull us before the blow.

Real Life Example

The Great Seal gift hides a Soviet listening device
1945-1960 · U.S. ambassador's residence, Moscow, Soviet Union
On 4 August 1945 a group of Soviet schoolchildren presented a hand-carved wooden replica of the Great Seal of the United States to the American ambassador, Averell Harriman, as a gesture of friendship between wartime allies. The carving hung in the ambassador's residence until 1952, when U.S. security officers discovered a hidden passive listening device, later called The Thing, inside it. In May 1960 Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge Jr. displayed the seal at the United Nations Security Council to show how it had been used for spying.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A gift offered in friendship, by children and in a time of alliance, turned out to conceal an instrument used against the recipient.
The kural says a weapon may lie hidden even in hands joined in worship, and the friendly gift of the Great Seal concealed a device for spying on the very people it honoured.
