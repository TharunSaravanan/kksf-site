Kural 952

Lineage · Kudimai · குடிமை

ஒழுக்கமும் வாய்மையும் நாணும்இம் மூன்றும் இழுக்கார் குடிப்பிறந் தார்.
Ozhukkamum vaaymaiyum naanum im moondrum izhukkaar kudippirandhaar.

Meaning

People born into a good family never slip from these three things: good behaviour, telling the truth, and a sense of shame about doing wrong.

Elementary

Think of a three-legged stool: good behaviour, telling the truth, and feeling bad about doing wrong. If one leg breaks, the stool falls. Valluvar says people from a good family keep all three legs strong.

University

The couplet names a triad: ozhukkam (ஒழுக்கம், right conduct, the subject of chapter 14), vaaymai (வாய்மை, truthfulness, chapter 30) and naan (நாண், shame, which will receive its own chapter, 102). The verb izhukkaar (இழுக்கார்) means 'they do not slip or lapse', so the claim is about steadiness over a lifetime, not occasional virtue. Mu. Varadarasanar adds that the well-born live in the right path 'by nature' (இயல்பாகவே), linking this couplet to the iyalpaaga of 951, while Kalaignar again turns it into a definition: only those who never lapse in these three are to be counted high-born, and he glosses naan as maanam (மானம், honour). The word division நாணும்இம் மூன்றும் (naanum im moondrum, 'shame, these three') appears in both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the close of Vedic study the teacher charges the departing student: speak the truth, practise dharma, do not neglect study, and do not swerve from truth or from right conduct.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11 [3]
The convocation address of the Taittiriya Upanishad joins truthful speech and righteous conduct as the lifelong duties of an educated householder.
Two of the Kural's three pillars, truth and conduct, are the heart of this charge.
Christianity
“Finally, brethren, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things.”
— Philippians 4:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Philippians to fix their minds on what is true, honest and pure.
Truth and honest conduct, two of the Kural's three, sit at the head of Paul's list.
Islam
“O you who have believed, fear Allah and be with those who are true.”
— Qur'an 9:119 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an joins God-consciousness with keeping company with the truthful.
It shares the Kural's insistence on truthfulness as a defining trait of the good.
Judaism
“Rabban Shimon ben Gamaliel used to say: on three things does the world stand: On justice, on truth and on peace.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:18 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah names three supports on which the whole world rests.
Like the Kural, it names a triad that must not be broken, with truth in the centre.
Buddhism
The Buddha says that one who has virtue and insight, who is principled, truthful and does his own duty is held dear by people.
— Dhammapada 217 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada joins moral conduct, truthfulness and diligence as the marks of a person others love.
Conduct and truth, two of the Kural's three, stand together here as well.
Jainism
“Doing no injury to living beings of the six orders, abstaining from lying and from taking what is not freely given, renouncing property, women, pride, and deceit, men should live under self-restraint.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.41 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Harikesa, born an outcast, tells proud Brahmins that true worth lies in non-injury, truthfulness and self-restraint.
Right conduct and abstaining from lies, two of the Kural's three marks, are what Harikesa sets above birth.
Sikhism
“Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 62, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a Sri Raag hymn, Guru Nanak puts truthful conduct above even truth as an idea: what counts is a life that does not depart from it.
The Kural says the well-born never slip from right conduct and truthfulness; Guru Nanak makes truthful living the highest of all.
Greek Mythology
In the embassy scene Achilles declares that he hates, as he hates the gates of Hades, the man who hides one thing in his heart and says another.
— Homer, Iliad 9 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
The noblest of the Greek heroes defines himself by a refusal to say what he does not mean.
Achilles shows the Kural's vaaymai, truthfulness, as part of a hero's nobility.
Philosophy
“For the man who loves truth, and is truthful where nothing is at stake, will still more be truthful where something is at stake; he will avoid falsehood as something base, seeing that he avoided it even for its own sake; and such a man is worthy of praise.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.7, 1127b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Describing the truthful man as one who is true 'both in life and in word', Aristotle says his honesty is so fixed in his character that he shuns falsehood as shameful even when nothing depends on it.
Like the Kural's well-born, who never slip from truth, Aristotle's truthful man is steady in truth because lying is base to him.

Real Life Example

John Quincy Adams returns to Congress after his presidency
1831-1848 · Washington, D.C., United States
John Quincy Adams, son of the second American president John Adams, was elected to the House of Representatives in 1830 after his own single term as president and served there until his death. For years he fought the House's gag rule, which tabled without debate all petitions against slavery, until it was repealed in 1844. On 21 February 1848 he collapsed at his desk in the House chamber and died in the Capitol two days later.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Adams accepted a lesser office and spent his last seventeen years steadily pressing an unpopular principle, showing a consistency of conduct that did not depend on rank.
The kural says those of good family never slip from right conduct, truthfulness and shame over a lifetime, and Adams kept to his course until the day he fell at his post.
