Kural 1139

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

அறிகிலார் எல்லாரும் என்றேஎன் காமம் மறுகின் மறுகும் மருண்டு.
Arikilaar ellaarum endrae-en kaamam marukin marukum marundu.

Meaning

As if it thinks not everyone knows about it yet, my love wanders confused through the streets.

Elementary

Imagine a puppy that has escaped from its yard and runs up and down the street, bumping into everyone. The speaker says her love is like that: it has got out and is wandering around town where everyone can see it.

University

Love itself is personified: thinking 'not everyone knows' (aṟikilār ellārum), it maṟukiṉ maṟukum (மறுகின் மறுகும்), 'roams about in the street', maruṇṭu (மருண்டு), 'in confusion, bewildered'. The pun on maṟuku (street) and maṟukum (wanders, whirls) is characteristic of Valluvar's sound-play. Commentators note the irony: because the speaker had kept quiet (Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar: 'because I endured in silence'), love itself seems to have decided to announce itself; Pappaiah says it goes about the streets as gossip (ampal and alar), anticipating chapter 115. The ytamizh.com text prints என்றேஎன் as one unit and thirukkural.net the same; Pope reads 'There's no one knows my heart, so says my love'.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Krishna vanishes from the circle dance, the gopis wander through the forest of Vrindavan as if mad, asking the trees, the creepers and the earth whether they have seen him.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.30 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana describes their bewildered search as a sign of love that has taken over their whole being.
Like the Kural's love that roams the streets bewildered, the gopis' love sends them wandering openly in search of the beloved.
Christianity
“I will rise now, and go about the city in the streets, and in the broad ways I will seek him whom my soul loveth: I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Solomon 3:2 (KJV) [4]
The beloved leaves her bed and searches the streets of the city for her lover.
The Kural's love wandering the streets in confusion is pictured here almost literally.
Islam
“Barira's husband was a slave called Mughith, as if I am seeing him now, going behind Barira and weeping with his tears flowing down his beard.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5283 [5]
Ibn 'Abbas recalls how Mughith followed Barira through Medina in tears after she left him, and the Prophet remarked on the strength of his love.
Mughith's love, like the Kural's, wandered openly through the streets for all to see.
Judaism
“I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Songs 5:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved goes out at night calling for her lover and cannot find him.
Her calling and searching through the city is the same restless, public wandering the Kural gives to love.
Buddhism
Kisa Gotami, grief-stricken at her child's death, carries him from house to house through the town asking for medicine, while people think she has lost her mind, until the Buddha gently leads her to understanding.
— Therigatha commentary, story of Kisa Gotami (paraphrase) [7]
The commentarial story shows how attachment can send a person wandering bewildered in public.
Like the Kural's love, her attachment drives her through the streets in confusion for all to see.
Jainism
The Sutrakritanga warns monks that one who gives way to infatuation with a woman loses the respect he had and ends up mocked and ordered about, his weakness known to all.
— Sutrakritanga 1.4 (paraphrase; tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The chapter on women in this early Jain text describes how desire, once yielded to, becomes a public humiliation.
The Kural describes love becoming public; the Jain text treats that exposure as a warning.
Sikhism
“Some call him a ghost; some say that he is a demon. ... Crazy Nanak has gone insane, after his Lord, the King.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 991, Raag Maaroo, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak reports what people say about him as he goes about absorbed in the Lord, and accepts the name of madman (baurana) for his love.
The Kural's love roams the streets bewildered, as if wanting everyone to know; Guru Nanak's love likewise makes him a public spectacle, talked about by all.
Greek Mythology
After losing Eurydice a second time, Orpheus wanders grieving through the wild lands of Thrace, refusing all other love and singing of his loss.
— Virgil, Georgics 4; Ovid, Metamorphoses 10-11 (paraphrase) [10]
Virgil and Ovid show love that has lost its object and roams openly in its sorrow.
Like the Kural's bewildered love wandering the streets, Orpheus' love wanders openly with nowhere to rest.
Philosophy
Spinoza remarks that people who are inflamed with love and dream night and day of nothing but their beloved are, like the greedy and the ambitious, in a kind of madness, though people do not usually call it so.
— Spinoza, Ethics IV, Prop. 44, Scholium (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, an emotion that fixes the mind on one object takes away its freedom.
The Kural's love, wandering bewildered through the streets, is that kind of mind-possessing passion made visible.

Real Life Example

Chagall paints himself floating to kiss Bella in Birthday
July 1915 · Vitebsk, Russian Empire (now Belarus)
In July 1915, on the occasion of Marc Chagall's birthday, Bella Rosenfeld came to his room in Vitebsk with food and flowers wrapped in embroidered shawls. Chagall painted the scene as Birthday, showing himself floating in the air to kiss her; the oil-on-cardboard painting is now in the Museum of Modern Art in New York. The two married on 25 July 1915, though her family still had doubts about the match.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Chagall turned his private joy into an image so open and exuberant that it has been shown to the world ever since.
The kural pictures love as if it were wandering bewildered through the streets so that everyone will know of it; Chagall's floating lovers show the same giddy love that cannot stay hidden.
