Kural 539

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

இகழ்ச்சியின் கெட்டாரை உள்ளுக தாந்தம் மகிழ்ச்சியின் மைந்துறும் போழ்து.
Ikazhchchiyin kettaarai ulluka thaandham Makizhchchiyin maindhurum pozhdhu.

Meaning

Whenever you feel carried away by your own happiness, remember the people who were ruined because they got careless.

Elementary

When you win a prize and feel like the best in the world, it's a good moment to remember the story of the hare who was so sure of winning that he took a nap and lost to the tortoise. Remembering other people's mistakes keeps you careful.

University

இகழ்ச்சி here means heedless disregard (it shares a root with இகழ்ந்தார் in 538), and மைந்துறும் போழ்து means ‘the moment one grows strong or elated’. Parimelazhagar makes kings the subject: when they grow confident in their own pleasures, they should remember those of old who perished through the lapse such pleasure breeds. He explains that the text says ‘their own delight’ because the king owns the means of pleasure, and that it ties elation to ruin because neglect and downfall arise together. The point, he says, is that one who recalls the ruined will think ‘I too shall perish that way’ and will not grow elated. He also records a variant reading, எண்ணுக for உள்ளுக, which Pavanar attributes to Manakkudavar. Manakkudavar gives the couplet a concrete application: kings should have their food, clothing and unguents examined, that is, guard against poisoning.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The mortal king Nahusha is raised to the throne of Indra while Indra is in hiding. Intoxicated by his new power, he forces the great sages to carry his palanquin and, in his impatience, kicks Agastya. He is cursed on the spot to fall from heaven as a serpent.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Sections 11-17 (the story of Nahusha), trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
The Mahabharata tells the story as a warning of how quickly exaltation turns into ruin when power makes one careless of what is due.
Nahusha is exactly the kind of figure the Kural tells rulers to remember when their own delight makes them feel invincible.
Christianity
“Now all these things happened unto them for ensamples: and they are written for our admonition, upon whom the ends of the world are come. Wherefore let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall.”
— 1 Corinthians 10:11-12 (KJV) [5]
Paul retells Israel's failures in the wilderness and says they were recorded precisely so that the confident would remember them and take heed.
This is the Kural's method: when you feel secure, remember those who fell.
Islam
“Do not exult. Indeed, Allah does not like the exultant.” … “And We caused the earth to swallow him and his home.”
— Qur'an 28:76 and 28:81 (Saheeh International) [6]
Qarun's people warn him against exulting in his vast treasures. He dismisses them, and the earth swallows him, so that those who had envied him the day before learn from his end.
Qarun is held up in the Qur'an as exactly the remembered ruin the Kural prescribes for moments of elation.
Judaism
“But when he was strong, his heart was lifted up so that he did corruptly, and he trespassed against the LORD his God; for he went into the temple of the LORD to burn incense upon the altar of incense.”
— II Chronicles 26:16 (JPS 1917) [7]
King Uzziah prospered greatly, but at the height of his strength he grew arrogant and overstepped, and he was struck with leprosy for the rest of his life.
The Chronicler's ‘when he was strong’ is the Kural's மைந்துறும் போழ்து, the very moment it warns about.
Buddhism
“Possessions, honor, and popularity came to Devadatta for his own ruin and downfall.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 17.35, trans. Bhikkhu Sujato [8]
The Buddha points to Devadatta as the cautionary example: success and fame swelled him and led to his downfall, like a banana plant killed by its own fruit.
Devadatta is the kind of ruined figure the Kural tells us to call to mind when success elates us.
Jainism
“And Brahmadatta, king of the Pañkâlas, did not act on the counsel of the saint; he enjoyed the highest pleasure, and (afterwards) sank into the deepest hell.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Jain canon holds up Brahmadatta, a universal monarch who stayed absorbed in pleasure and ignored all warnings, as an example of ruin through heedlessness.
The Kural tells a man swollen with delight to remember those who perished through heedlessness; Brahmadatta is just such a cautionary figure in Jain tradition.
Sikhism
“Many have proclaimed their sovereignty over millions of fortresses on the earth, but they have now departed.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 595, Raag Sorath, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak calls to mind the many rulers who beat the drums of their power and are now gone.
The Kural tells the one swelling with delight to remember those ruined by heedlessness; Guru Nanak recalls vanished rulers for the same sobering purpose.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus fits wings to his son Icarus and warns him to fly a middle course. But the boy delights in his daring flight, abandons his guide and soars higher, until the sun softens the wax and he falls into the sea.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.183-235 (trans. A. S. Kline) [11]
Icarus's fall became the classic Western example of elation making someone forget the limits he had been warned about.
He is the ruined figure the Kural bids us remember when delight makes us feel we can fly.
Philosophy
Lady Philosophy reminds the imprisoned Boethius that Fortune's nature is to turn her wheel. She recalls kings brought low, such as Croesus, who was placed on the pyre and saved only by a sudden rain, and tells him that no one should trust prosperity as if it were his own.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2 [12]
For Boethius, remembering the fallen is the cure for the illusion that good fortune is permanent.
This is the Kural's remedy of recalling the ruined when one is swollen with success.

Real Life Example

Scipio Aemilianus foresees Rome's own fall at Carthage
146 BCE · Carthage (near present-day Tunis, Tunisia)
In 146 BCE, at the end of the Third Punic War, the Roman commander Scipio Aemilianus captured and destroyed Carthage, Rome's great rival. The historian Polybius, who stood beside him, recorded that as Scipio watched the city's fall he took Polybius by the hand and said he felt a dread foreboding that the same fate might one day come to his own country. The later historian Appian wrote that Scipio wept as he looked on the city.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
At the very height of Roman triumph, the victorious general turned his thoughts to how great powers fall rather than to his own glory.
The kural tells those who feel strong and elated to remember others who were ruined, and Scipio did exactly that in the hour of his greatest victory.
