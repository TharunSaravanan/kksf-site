Kural 236

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

தோன்றின் புகழொடு தோன்றுக அஃதிலார் தோன்றலின் தோன்றாமை நன்று.
Thondrin pukazhodu thondruka aqdhilaar Thondralin thondraamai nandru.

Meaning

If you are born into the world, be born with the qualities that earn a good name. If you do not have them, it would be better not to be born.

Elementary

If you’re going to step onto the stage in the school play, practise hard and do your best. Showing up and doing nothing good can be worse than not showing up at all. Whatever you take part in, do it in a way people can be proud of.

University

The verb தோன்று (‘to appear, arise, be born’) occurs five times, giving the couplet an incantatory force. Two readings exist. Parimelazhagan and Pope take it as ‘being born’: if one is born, one should be born with qualities that bring renown, and for those without them it were better not to be born (Pope: ‘’twere better hide your face’). Mu. Varadarasan and Karunanidhi read it as ‘appearing in a field’: whatever role or arena one enters, one should enter it with distinction, or not at all. The first reading is existential, the second vocational; both insist that visibility without merit is a net negative. The thought has sharp parallels: Jesus’ ‘good for that man if he had not been born’, Qoheleth’s preference for an untimely birth over a life empty of good, and the Mahabharata heroine Vidula’s maxim that it is ‘better to blaze up for a moment than smoke for ever’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is better to blaze up for a moment than smoke for ever and ever.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 133 (trans. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Queen Vidula rebukes her son Sanjaya, who lies despondent after defeat, urging him to win distinction by his deeds rather than live on without honour.
Like the Kural, it prefers a brief appearance with glory to a long existence without it.
Christianity
“It had been good for that man if he had not been born.”
— Matthew 26:24 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says this of the one who would betray him, Judas.
The same judgement form as the Kural: some lives would have been better never begun.
Islam
“The disbeliever will say, “Oh, I wish that I were dust!”
— Qur’an 78:40 (Sahih International) [5]
On the Day of Judgment, seeing what his own hands have sent forth, the disbeliever wishes he had never existed as a person at all.
A life lived without worth leads to the wish for non-existence, echoing the Kural’s ‘better not to appear’.
Judaism
“If a man beget an hundred children, and live many years, so that the days of his years be many, and his soul be not filled with good, and also that he have no burial; I say, that an untimely birth is better than he.”
— Ecclesiastes 6:3 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
Qoheleth says that a long, prolific life that never attains good, and ends without honourable burial, is worse than never having been born.
Very close to the Kural: existence without the good (and without honour at the end) is worse than non-appearance.
Buddhism
“Ever grows the glory of him who is energetic, mindful and pure in conduct, discerning and self-controlled, righteous and heedful.”
— Dhammapada 24 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada lists the qualities by which a person’s glory steadily increases.
It names the qualities with which one should ‘appear’: those that naturally produce renown.
Jainism
“Death devours your days, using the sun from which they originate as the measure by which he metes them. Practise therefore virtue and be compassionate, for such as do not act thus, though they are born, must be esteemed as unborn.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 1 ('Unstable Wealth'), st. 7 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaṭiyār says that those who live without virtue and compassion may as well never have been born.
Valluvar says that if one is born, one should be born with the qualities that bring renown, and that otherwise it is better not to be born. The Nālaṭiyār passes the same verdict, that a birth without virtue counts as no birth.
Sikhism
“That family, whose son has no spiritual wisdom or contemplation - why didn't his mother just become a widow?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 328, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir asks why a mother should have borne a son who lives without spiritual wisdom, implying it would have been better had he never been born.
The Kural says that those without the qualities that bring renown would do better not to be born at all, and Kabir makes the same harsh judgement about a life empty of wisdom.
Greek Mythology
“It becomes us, therefore, to take our stand at the head of all the Lycians and bear the brunt of the fight.”
— Homer, Iliad 12 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Sarpedon tells Glaucus that because they enjoy honour and privilege as princes, they must justify it by fighting in the front ranks.
Those who appear in a prominent place must appear with deeds worthy of renown, or their position is empty.
Philosophy
“Honour is the prize of virtue, and it is to the good that it is rendered.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.3 (trans. W.D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle, discussing the great-souled person, says honour is properly given only to the genuinely good.
Renown should accompany real excellence; one who seeks the stage without virtue has no claim to it.

Real Life Example

Abebe Bikila wins the Olympic marathon barefoot
10 September 1960 · Rome, Italy
Abebe Bikila, a late addition to Ethiopia's team, ran the Rome Olympic marathon barefoot and won in a world-best time of 2 hours 15 minutes 16.2 seconds. He became the first Black African to win an Olympic gold medal, and in Tokyo in 1964 he became the first person to win two Olympic marathons.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Bikila's first appearance on the world stage was so distinguished that it changed how the world saw African athletes.
The kural says that whoever appears before the world should appear with qualities that bring renown, and Bikila's first Olympic appearance did exactly that.
