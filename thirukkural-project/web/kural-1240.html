Kural 1240

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

கண்ணின் பசப்போ பருவரல் எய்தின்றே ஒண்ணுதல் செய்தது கண்டு.
Kannin pasappoa paruvaral eydhindrae onnudhal seydhadhu kandu.

Meaning

Her bright forehead turned pale first. Did the paleness in her eyes see this and become even sadder?

Elementary

When one person in a family is sad, the others often feel sad too, just from seeing them. In this verse the husband imagines his wife’s face as a little family: when her forehead turned pale, her eyes saw it and became even sadder.

University

The closing couplet is a conceit (taṟkuṟippēṟṟa aṇi, poetic fancy) in which parts of the wife’s face react to one another. Kaṇṇiṉ pacappō paruvaral eytiṉṟē, ‘did the pallor of the eyes fall into distress?’, uses the particle ō for wondering doubt, and oṇ nutal ceytatu kaṇṭu, ‘having seen what the bright forehead did’, refers back to 1238, where the forehead paled first. Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar read: seeing the forehead turn sallow, the pallor of her eyes grew into greater suffering. Solomon Pappaiah offers a different reading, in which the eye, once ashamed before the forehead’s delicacy, now shares its distress. The chapter thus ends where it began, with the eyes (1231), now joined by the forehead, so that the ‘wasting of the limbs’ is complete from head to arm. Pope: ‘The dimness of her eye felt sorrow now, / Beholding what was done by that bright brow.’ Text identical on both sites; ytamizh’s transliteration renders the archaic eytiṉṟē as ‘eydhindrae’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Chandogya Upanishad, the senses and breath quarrel over which is greatest; each departs for a year and the body carries on, but when breath begins to leave, all the others are torn loose with it and beg it to stay.
— Chandogya Upanishad 5.1.6-12 (paraphrase) [3]
The Upanishad pictures the body’s faculties as a community that rises or falls together.
The Kural’s eye grieving at the forehead’s pallor is a small, tender version of the same solidarity of the body’s parts.
Christianity
“And whether one member suffer, all the members suffer with it; or one member be honoured, all the members rejoice with it.”
— 1 Corinthians 12:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes the community as a body whose parts feel each other’s pain.
The Kural pictures this literally: when the forehead suffers, the eyes suffer with it.
Islam
“You see the believers as regards their being merciful among themselves and showing love among themselves and being kind, resembling one body, so that, if any part of the body is not well then the whole body shares the sleeplessness (insomnia) and fever with it.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6011 [5]
The Prophet compares a loving community to a body whose parts share each other’s illness.
The Kural’s eyes share the forehead’s ‘illness’ of pallor in just this way.
Judaism
Isaiah pictures a people so afflicted that the whole head is sick and the whole heart faint, with no soundness from the sole of the foot to the head.
— Isaiah 1:5-6 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [6]
The prophet describes suffering that spreads through every part of the body.
The Kural’s wasting likewise spreads from part to part, until even the eyes grieve at the forehead’s pallor.
Buddhism
The Buddha summarises dependent arising: when this is, that is; from the arising of this comes the arising of that.
— Majjhima Nikaya 79, Culasakuludayi Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Buddhist teaching sees every state as conditioned by others, arising together.
The eyes’ grief arising from the forehead’s pallor is a poetic picture of one condition giving rise to another.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra states that the function of souls is to render service to one another (parasparopagraho jivanam).
— Tattvartha Sutra 5.21 (paraphrase) [8]
This aphorism, now the motto of the Jain community, teaches that living beings exist in mutual support.
The Kural’s eyes grieving with the forehead are a tender picture of mutual support between parts that share one life.
Sikhism
“I cannot survive without seeing my Beloved. My eyes are welling up with tears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 94, Raag Maajh, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says he cannot live without the sight of his Beloved, and his eyes fill with tears.
The Kural imagines the wife's eyes falling into distress on seeing what pallor has done to her forehead; in Guru Ram Das too it is the eyes that carry the grief of separation.
Greek Mythology
When Narcissus wastes away at the pool, Echo, though still hurt by his rejection, grieves to see him; each time he cries ‘alas’, she echoes ‘alas’, and when he says ‘farewell’ she says ‘farewell’ too.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.494-501 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Echo shares in the wasting of the one she loves, repeating his grief back to him.
The Kural’s eyes likewise mirror and deepen the grief they see in the forehead.
Philosophy
Socrates argues that the best-ordered city is most like a single person: when a finger is hurt, the whole community of body and soul feels it and suffers together with the injured part.
— Plato, Republic 462c-d (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato uses the body’s shared feeling of pain as the model for social unity.
The Kural pictures the same shared feeling within one face: the eyes suffer with the pale forehead.

Real Life Example

Rembrandt draws his bride Saskia three days after their betrothal
1633 · Dutch Republic (now the Netherlands)
In 1633 Rembrandt van Rijn made a silverpoint drawing on parchment of Saskia and wrote beneath it, in Dutch, that it was a likeness of his wife made when she was 21 years old, on the third day after their betrothal. The drawing is now in the Kupferstichkabinett of the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It is a rare record of an artist studying the face of the woman he loved at the very start of their life together.
The kural's husband remembers every small change in his wife's face, her brow and her eyes, and Rembrandt's drawing shows a lover attending closely to his bride's face.
