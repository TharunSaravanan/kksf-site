Kural 308

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

இணர்எரி தோய்வன்ன இன்னா செயினும் புணரின் வெகுளாமை நன்று.
Inar eri thoivanna innaa seyinum Punarin vekulaamai nandru.

Meaning

Even if someone hurts you as badly as throwing you into a blazing fire, it is good, if you possibly can, not to get angry with them.

Elementary

Sometimes people are so mean it feels like being burned. The kural says: even then, if you can, try not to become angry back. It is really hard, but it is the best and bravest thing to do.

University

This is the chapter's high-water mark of demand: inar eri, a fire of many clustered flames, is the image of the worst possible injury. The concessive punarin ('if it is possible', or in some readings 'if the offender seeks reconciliation') is realistic, acknowledging that non-anger in such extremity may exceed ordinary capacity, but it does not lower the ideal. Kalaignar's gloss takes punarin as the wrongdoer returning to seek friendship, making the couplet about accepting reconciliation without resentment. Either way, the teaching approaches the most radical ethics of non-retaliation found across traditions.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The boy Prahlada is subjected by his own father, Hiranyakashipu, to repeated attempts on his life, including poison, serpents, being thrown from heights, and fire, yet he keeps his devotion and shows no hatred toward his father.
— Bhagavata Purana, Canto 7, chapters 5-9 [3]
Prahlada becomes the Puranic model of a person who suffers extreme cruelty without answering in anger.
A living example of the kural's ideal: meeting fire-like torment without wrath.
Christianity
“Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do.”
— Luke 23:34 (KJV) [4]
On the cross, in extreme agony, Jesus prays for those who are crucifying him.
The fullest Christian expression of the kural's ideal: no anger even under torment 'like fire'.
Islam
“And not equal are the good deed and the bad. Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend.”
— Quran 41:34 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran instructs believers to answer harm with good, promising that this can turn an enemy into a close friend.
Matches both the non-anger ideal and Kalaignar's reading of punarin as reconciliation with the offender.
Judaism
“About those who are insulted and do not insult, who hear their shame and do not respond, who act out of love and are joyful in suffering, the verse says: ‘And they that love Him are as the sun going forth in its might’ (Judges 5:31).”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 88b (William Davidson/Koren-Steinsaltz trans., via Sefaria) [6]
The Sages praise those who absorb humiliation without striking back, comparing them to the rising sun in its strength.
Like the kural, the Talmud treats not answering injury with anger as a sign of greatness.
Buddhism
“Monks, even if bandits were to carve you up savagely, limb by limb, with a two-handled saw, he among you who let his heart get angered even at that would not be doing my bidding.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 21, Kakacupama Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Simile of the Saw, the Buddha sets the most extreme standard of non-anger imaginable.
Near one-to-one with the kural's 'even if they plunge you into fire', and even stricter, since it drops the 'if possible' qualification.
Jainism
“Angry at this thought, Somaśarman, malevolent, stood the neck of a water-jar, filled with blazing coals from a funeral-pyre, on his head. Though burned severely by it, absorbed in meditation, he endured it. The fuel of karma being consumed, omniscience having arisen, he went to emancipation.”
— Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book VIII (Nemināthacaritra), ch. 10, part 3, 'Gajasukumāla' (tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The newly ordained monk Gajasukumāla, Kṛṣṇa's younger brother, is standing in meditation in a cemetery when his father-in-law places live coals on his head. He bears it without breaking his meditation and attains liberation. Afterwards Neminātha tells the grieving Kṛṣṇa, 'Do not be angry at Somaśarman.'
Valluvar asks us to hold back anger even when the harm done to us is like being plunged into blazing fire. In Gajasukumāla's case the fire was literal, and he endured it without retaliating.
Sikhism
Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, was summoned by the Mughal emperor Jahangir and ordered to remove passages of the Adi Granth. He refused and was tortured to death at Lahore in 1606. Sikh tradition holds that he was made to sit on a red-hot iron plate while burning sand was poured over him. Through it he remained calm and without hatred, accepting God's will as sweet.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'How did Guru Arjan die?' (paraphrase); hot-plate details from Sikh tradition [9]
Guru Arjan is Sikhism's first martyr. His serenity under torture is remembered in Sikh tradition as the supreme example of bearing extreme injury without anger.
The Kural says that even if someone does you harm like plunging you into a blazing fire, it is good to refrain from anger. Guru Arjan's martyrdom is almost a literal enactment of that teaching.
Greek Mythology
King Priam goes alone to the tent of Achilles, who has killed his son Hector and defiled his body, and kisses the hands of his son's killer to beg for the corpse; Achilles, moved, puts aside his wrath and returns the body.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 24 [10]
The Iliad ends not with victory but with two enemies setting aside anger in the face of unbearable loss.
Priam's restraint before the man who burned his life to ashes reflects the kural's ideal of non-anger after the worst injury.
Philosophy
“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to anyone, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”
— Plato, Crito 49c (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
In prison awaiting execution, Socrates holds that retaliation is never just, no matter how much one has suffered.
Socrates' 'whatever evil we may have suffered' is the Greek counterpart of the kural's 'even if they harm you like fire'.

Real Life Example

Eva Mozes Kor forgives her Nazi persecutors at Auschwitz
January 1995 · Auschwitz-Birkenau, Poland
As a ten-year-old twin, Eva Mozes Kor had been subjected to Josef Mengele's experiments at Auschwitz, where her parents and two older sisters were killed. On the 50th anniversary of the camp's liberation, she returned to Auschwitz with Dr. Hans Munch, a former camp doctor who signed a document attesting to the operation of the gas chambers, and she read and signed her own declaration forgiving the Nazis. Her decision drew criticism from some fellow survivors, but she said it freed her from the pain she had carried for fifty years.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kor chose to give up resentment toward those who had inflicted the worst imaginable suffering on her family, and she described the act as her own liberation.
The kural says that even when someone does you harm as painful as being plunged into a blazing fire, it is good, if you can, to refrain from anger toward them, and Kor did so after enduring one of history's greatest injuries.
