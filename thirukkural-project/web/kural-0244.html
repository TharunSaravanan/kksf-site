Kural 244

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

மன்னுயிர் ஓம்பி அருளாள்வார்க்கு இல்லென்ப தன்னுயிர் அஞ்சும் வினை.
Mannuyir oambi arulaalvaarkku illenba, Thannuyir anjum vinai.

Meaning

Wise people say that those who protect all living things and live with kindness will never have to fear what their own actions bring back to them.

Elementary

If you spend your life looking after others — feeding a stray cat, helping a friend who fell, being gentle to little creatures — you will never have to lie awake worrying about the bad things you did. Protecting others makes your own heart safe.

University

Mannuyir means ‘the enduring lives’, the whole population of living beings that fills the world, and oambi (protecting, cherishing) describes an active guardianship, not mere harmlessness. The second line allows two readings. Parimelazhagar and Varadarasan take thannuyir anjum vinai as ‘evil deeds whose results one’s own soul would dread’, so the protector of life accumulates no karmic cause for fear. Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar take it more psychologically: such a person does not fear for their own life. Either way, the kural states a principle of moral reciprocity: fearlessness is earned by protecting others. The verb enba (‘they say’) presents this as received wisdom, and it has parallels in Jain and Buddhist teaching on abhaya-dāna, the ‘gift of fearlessness’ given to all beings.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Rantideva, after forty-eight days of fasting, gives away his food and water to a succession of hungry guests, including an outcaste with his dogs, and declares that he seeks neither mystic powers nor liberation, only to take on the sufferings of all beings so that they may be free of pain.
— Bhagavata Purana 9.21.2-18 [3]
Rantideva is the Bhagavata’s model of compassion that protects every living being, even at the cost of his own life.
A living picture of mannuyir oambi, cherishing all lives; the Bhagavata says the gods then reveal themselves to him, and he has nothing to fear.
Christianity
“Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.”
— Matthew 25:40 (KJV) [4]
In the Last Judgment, those who fed, clothed and visited the vulnerable are welcomed without fear.
Protecting the weakest leaves one with nothing to fear at the final reckoning, just as the kural promises.
Islam
“Allah thanked him for his (good) deed and forgave him.” The people asked, “Is there a reward for us in serving (the) animals?” He replied, “Yes, there is a reward for serving any animate.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2363 [5]
A thirsty traveller climbs back down a well and carries up water in his shoe for a dying dog, and is forgiven his sins for it.
Compassion toward any living thing removes the burden of past sins, which is the kural’s ‘no deed to fear’, and it extends explicitly beyond humans.
Judaism
Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi suffered for years after he dismissed a calf being led to slaughter with the words ‘Go, for this you were created’; his suffering ended when he stopped his maidservant from sweeping away young weasels, citing “The LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works.
— Talmud, Bava Metzia 85a; Psalm 145:9 (KJV) [6]
The Talmud presents the rabbi’s affliction and his release as mirrors of his lack of compassion and then his compassion for creatures.
Protecting even the smallest lives removes the suffering one’s soul dreads, exactly as the kural teaches.
Buddhism
“Even as a mother protects with her life / Her child, her only child, / So with a boundless heart / Should one cherish all living beings”
— Karaniya Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.8 (trans. Amaravati Sangha) [7]
The Metta Sutta asks for protective, maternal love toward every being, ‘whether they are weak or strong, omitting none’.
Oambi, protecting and cherishing, is exactly the mother’s guardianship the sutta describes, extended to all lives.
Jainism
“Be without fear, O king; but grant safety to others also; in this transient world of living beings, why are you addicted to cruelty?”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 18.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Sanjaya, out hunting, kills a frightened deer, then trembles before the meditating monk Gardabhali, who tells him he need not fear but must give the same safety to every creature; the king renounces his throne.
Valluvar says those who protect all living beings need never fear for themselves; Gardabhali links the king's own freedom from fear to his giving safety to all beings.
Sikhism
“They are kind to all beings; they have overpowered the five thieves.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1357, Salok Sahaskriti, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Salok Sahaskriti Guru Arjan describes the holy as those who are kind to every living being and have mastered the five passions.
The Kural praises those who cherish all living beings and so need not fear the consequences of their deeds; Guru Arjan joins universal kindness with victory over the inner thieves that produce wrongdoing.
Greek Mythology
Nausicaa tells her maids that strangers and beggars come under the protection of Zeus, so the shipwrecked Odysseus must be fed, washed and clothed.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.206-210 [10]
Protecting the helpless stranger was a religious duty under Zeus Xenios, and neglecting it risked divine anger.
Protecting the vulnerable keeps one right with the gods; the Greek scope is human strangers rather than all beings.
Philosophy
“A man is really ethical only when he obeys the constraint laid on him to help all life which he is able to succor, and when he goes out of his way to avoid injuring anything living.”
— Albert Schweitzer, Civilization and Ethics (The Philosophy of Civilization, vol. 2, trans. C. T. Campion) [11]
Schweitzer’s ‘reverence for life’ asks for active help as well as abstention from harm toward every living being.
This matches oambi exactly: an active, universal protection of life as the mark of the ethical person.

Real Life Example

The founding of the world's first animal protection society
16 June 1824 · London, England
In 1822 Parliament passed the Cruel Treatment of Cattle Act, promoted by the MP Richard Martin and often called Martin's Act, the first law protecting animals. On 16 June 1824 Martin, the Reverend Arthur Broome, William Wilberforce and others met at Old Slaughter's Coffee House in London and founded the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, the first national animal protection society in the world. In 1840 Queen Victoria allowed it to add Royal to its name.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Protection of living beings was extended beyond humans into law and organised public action for the first time.
The kural praises those who protect all living beings and live by compassion, and the founders of the society made the protection of animals a public cause.
