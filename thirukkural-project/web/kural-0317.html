Kural 317

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

எனைத்தானும் எஞ்ஞான்றும் யார்க்கும் மனத்தானாம் மாணாசெய் யாமை தலை.
Enaiththaanum ennaandrum yaarkkum manaththaanaam Maanaa seyyaamai thalai.

Meaning

The highest goodness is never to do anything low or hurtful to anyone, however small, at any time, not even in your thoughts.

Elementary

Some people only stop themselves from hitting. This kural asks for much more: don’t hurt anyone, not a little bit, not ever, and don’t even plan mean things in your head. That, it says, is the very best kind of goodness.

University

The couplet universalises the prohibition along every axis at once: degree (enaiththaanum, in any measure however small), time (ennaandrum, at any time), and person (yaarkkum, to anyone). The fourth axis is inward: manaththaan aam, ‘that which arises from the mind,’ which commentators read either as deliberate, knowing harm or as harm committed even in intention. Maanaa means base or ignoble acts. Calling this thalai (the head, the foremost virtue) places total non-harm at the top of the chapter’s ethical hierarchy. Similar demands to extend the prohibition into the inner life appear in Leviticus’ command not to hate one’s brother in the heart, in the First Letter of John, where hatred is equated with murder, and in the Metta Sutta’s wish that no one should even desire harm for another.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him not, even though in pain, (speak words) cutting (others) to the quick; let him not injure others in thought or deed; let him not utter speeches which make (others) afraid of him, since that will prevent him from gaining heaven.”
— Manusmriti 2.161 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu forbids injuring others by word, thought, or deed, even when one is suffering oneself.
Both extend the ban on harm to the mind itself: no injury in thought, word, or act.
Christianity
“Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer: and ye know that no murderer hath eternal life abiding in him.”
— 1 John 3:15 (KJV) [4]
The letter of John teaches that hatred in the heart is already murder in God’s eyes.
Both move the line of harm from the hand to the mind: hostile intent is itself a wrong.
Islam
“There should be neither harming (darar) nor reciprocating harm (dirar).”
— An-Nawawi’s Forty Hadith, no. 32 (Ibn Majah; sunnah.com) [5]
This hadith lays down a general rule of Islamic ethics and law: no one may harm another, and harm may not be answered with harm.
Like the Kural, it states a rule of non-harm without exceptions of person or occasion.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him.”
— Leviticus 19:17 (KJV) [4]
The Torah forbids hatred even when it stays hidden in the heart.
Both forbid harm at the level of the mind, not just outward acts.
Buddhism
“Let none deceive another, or despise any being in any state. Let none through anger or ill-will wish harm upon another.”
— Karaniya Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.8 (trans. Amaravati Sangha) [6]
The Metta Sutta asks that no one deceive, despise, or even wish harm on any being.
Both reach from deed into wish: harming anyone, even in the mind, is ruled out.
Jainism
“(E. g. big creatures) have one form of bodily existence and then another. But all are subject to pain; hence they should not be killed. This is the quintessence of wisdom: not to kill anything. Know this to be the legitimate conclusion from the principle of the reciprocity with regard to non-killing.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.1.4.9–10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga calls non-harm toward every being the very core of wisdom, a verse it repeats at I.11.10.
Valluvar calls not harming anyone, in any degree, at any time, even in thought, the highest virtue. The Jain verse likewise sets universal non-harm at the summit, as the 'quintessence' of wisdom.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all. Do not break anyone's heart; these are all priceless jewels.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid, Salok 129 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sheikh Farid forbids even the smallest hurt, a single harsh word, because God dwells in every person and every heart is precious.
The Kural calls it the highest virtue never to cause harm to anyone, however slight; Farid likewise forbids harm down to one unkind word, to anyone at all.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus tells of Glaucus of Sparta, famed for honesty, who was holding money in trust and asked the Delphic oracle whether he might keep it by swearing a false oath. The Pythia answered that tempting the god with the question was as bad as doing the deed. Though he returned the money, his whole family line was later wiped out.
— Herodotus, Histories 6.86 (the story of Glaucus) [9]
The legend teaches that merely intending a wrong is already a serious wrong in the eyes of the gods.
The Kural forbids harm ‘even in the mind’; the Glaucus story shows the Greek conviction that intending harm already carries guilt.
Philosophy
In Book I of the Republic, Socrates leads Polemarchus to abandon the traditional view that justice means helping friends and harming enemies. He argues that harming anyone makes them worse, and that it cannot be the work of the just man to harm anyone at all, friend or enemy.
— Plato, Republic I, 335b–e [10]
Socrates concludes that the just person harms no one, under any circumstances.
Both reject every exception to non-harm, including the traditional exception for enemies.

Real Life Example

The Nuremberg Code sets limits on human experimentation
19 August 1947 · Nuremberg, Germany
On 19 August 1947 the judges of the American military tribunal in the Doctors' Trial at Nuremberg, which tried German physicians for crimes committed in the concentration camps, delivered their verdict. In a section titled Permissible Medical Experiments they set out ten principles, later known as the Nuremberg Code. These require that the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential and that experiments be conducted so as to avoid all unnecessary physical and mental suffering and injury.
— Sources: [11] [12]
After revelations of terrible abuses, the Code became a landmark statement that medical research must not inflict needless suffering on any person.
The kural calls it the highest virtue never to do any harm, however small, to anyone at any time, and the Nuremberg Code wrote a version of that principle into the foundations of modern medical ethics.
