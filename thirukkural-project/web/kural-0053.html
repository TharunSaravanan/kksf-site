Kural 53

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

இல்லதென் இல்லவள் மாண்பானால் உள்ளதென் இல்லவள் மாணாக் கடை.
Illadhen illavaL maaNbaanaal uLLadhen, illavaL maaNaak kadai.

Meaning

If a man’s wife is truly good, what does he lack? If she is not, what does he really have?

Elementary

Think of a key. If you have the right key, every locked door in the house opens — so nothing is really shut to you. Lose the key and it doesn't matter how many rooms you own; you can't get into any of them. This kural says a good partner is that key.

University

The couplet is built as a matched pair of rhetorical questions — illadhu en? ('what is lacking?') against uLLadhu en? ('what is there?') — and the symmetry is the argument: presence and absence of wifely maaNbu are made to exhaust the possibilities, with nothing in between. This antithetical-parallelism structure is a signature of Near Eastern and Tamil wisdom poetry alike, and it produces here a deliberately absolute claim that later Tamil moralists softened or literalised in various directions; Avvaiyar's Moodhurai rewrites it as the image of a house turning into a sour thicket. Parimelazhagar's note that maaNbu, an abstract quality-noun, stands here for the person who has it is an important grammatical observation — Valluvar is not praising an attribute detachable from the woman but identifying her with it. Read together, Kurals 52 and 53 form a diptych: 52 denies that external goods can substitute for her, 53 denies that anything remains once she is subtracted. Comparative scholarship frequently notes that Proverbs 12:4 states the identical antithesis in a single verse, and the Tamil commentarial tradition itself has cited that biblical parallel since at least the nineteenth century.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Offspring, (the due performance of) religious rites, faithful service, highest conjugal happiness and heavenly bliss for the ancestors and oneself, depend on one's wife alone.”
— Manusmriti 9.28 (trans. G. Bühler, Sacred Books of the East XXV) [4]
Manu makes the wife the single point on which every major human good — progeny, ritual, pleasure, and even posthumous welfare — is said to rest.
Exactly the Kural's logic: with her excellence nothing is lacking, without it nothing stands.
Christianity
“A virtuous woman is a crown to her husband: but she that maketh ashamed is as rottenness in his bones.”
— Proverbs 12:4 (King James Version) [5]
Proverbs poses the same two-sided proposition in a single antithetical verse: the good wife adds everything, the bad one hollows out what is already there.
This is Kural 53's exact structure — the two halves of the couplet matched to the two halves of the verse.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquility in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy. Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought.”
— Qur'an 30:21 (Saheeh International translation) [6]
The Qur'an presents a good spouse as a sign of God — the source of sakina (tranquillity), mawadda (affection) and rahma (mercy) in a life.
If she supplies these, as the Kural says, there is nothing left for a person to lack.
Judaism
Reading the phrase 'a helper against him' (ezer kenegdo) in Genesis, the Talmud teaches that if a man is worthy his wife is his helper, and if he is unworthy she is against him — the same woman functioning as the whole of his fortune or the whole of his misfortune.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 63a (on Genesis 2:18) [7]
The rabbinic wordplay turns the ambiguous Hebrew preposition into a doctrine that the marriage is decisive either way.
Both texts make the marriage a binary on which a man's entire condition turns.
Buddhism
“Children are the site for human beings. A wife is the best comrade.”
— Saṁyutta Nikāya 1.54, Vatthu Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato, SuttaCentral) [8]
In this short exchange with a deity the Buddha names the wife the supreme companion (paramā sakhā) a person can have.
If the best of all comrades is secured, as the Kural asks, what is left to lack?
Jainism
“Though her house be open on the four quarters, though it be exceedingly small, and though the rain pour in on every side, a chaste and virtuous woman will be honoured in the place where she resides, and her habitation respected.”
— Nālaṭiyār ch. 39 'Chaste Women', st. 3 (= st. 383), English translation in The Indian Antiquary [9]
The Nālaṭiyār says a virtuous wife makes even a poor, leaking hut a place of honour.
This is the positive half of Valluvar's paired question: when the wife is excellent nothing is lacking, even in a house with nothing in it.
Sikhism
“From woman, man is born; within woman, man is conceived; to woman he is engaged and married. Woman becomes his friend; through woman, the future generations come... So, why call her bad? From her, kings are born.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak answers the low view of women common in his time by showing that every stage of a man's life, from birth to marriage to descendants, depends on woman.
Like Valluvar's paired questions (with her, what does a man lack; without her, what does he have?), the Guru shows that a man's whole life rests on woman.
Greek Mythology
“For nothing is greater or better than this, when man and wife dwell in a home in one accord, a great grief to their foes and a joy to their friends; but they know it best themselves.”
— Homer, Odyssey 6.182–185 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb Classical Library, 1919) [11]
Odysseus blesses Nausicaa with homophrosynē — like-mindedness in marriage — which he ranks above every other human good.
'Nothing greater or better' is Homer's way of saying what the Kural says: with this, nothing is lacking.
Philosophy
In the Consolation of Philosophy, Boethius — stripped of office, wealth, and freedom and awaiting execution — is reminded by Lady Philosophy that his wife still lives, surpassing all other women in purity of character, and that in her alone he retains a good that outweighs everything fortune has taken.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book II, prose 4 (c. 524 CE) [12]
Boethius's case is the kural's thought-experiment run in reverse: a man who has literally lost everything except a virtuous wife, and is told he is therefore not destitute.
'What does he lack?' — Philosophy's answer to Boethius is exactly Valluvar's.

Real Life Example

Empress Theodora counsels Justinian to stand firm in the Nika revolt
January 532 · Constantinople (now Istanbul, Turkey)
In January 532 the rival Blue and Green factions of Constantinople united in revolt against Emperor Justinian, burned much of the city centre and proclaimed a rival emperor. Justinian's advisers urged him to flee, but Empress Theodora, his most trusted adviser, counselled him to stay; according to the historian Procopius she told the council that she would rather die an empress than live as a fugitive. Justinian stayed, his generals put down the revolt, and he kept his throne for another thirty-three years.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Theodora's resolve at the decisive moment preserved Justinian's reign, and historians regard her as probably the most powerful woman in Byzantine history.
The kural asks what a man can lack if his wife is truly excellent, and Justinian, on the point of losing everything, kept his empire because of his wife's steadiness.
