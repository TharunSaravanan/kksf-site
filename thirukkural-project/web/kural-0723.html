Kural 723

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

பகையகத்துச் சாவார் எளியர் அரியர் அவையகத்து அஞ்சா தவர்.
Pakaiyakaththuch chaavaar eliyar ariyar Avaiyakaththu anjaa thavar.

Meaning

Many people are ready to die fighting among enemies. Few people can stand in front of a group and speak without fear.

Elementary

Lots of people are brave in a game or on the playground. Standing up in front of the whole school to say what you really think takes a different kind of courage, and fewer people have it.

University

The couplet is built as a sharp contrast: pakai-y-akattu (“inside the enemy ranks”) against avai-y-akattu (“inside the assembly”), and eḷiyar (“easy to find, many”) against ariyar (“rare”). Parimelazhagar takes the first group as warriors who throw themselves fearlessly among the enemy and die, and the second as those who are unafraid before the learned. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses the first as “many in the world.” The couplet ranks civil courage above martial courage, a bold claim in a culture whose puṟam poetry celebrated heroic death. The sandhi-joined text “பகையகத்துச் சாவா ரெளிய ரரிய / ரவையகத் தஞ்சா தவர்” divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Draupadi is dragged into the Kuru assembly hall after the dice game and asks whether she was lawfully staked. The great warriors and elders present, men who would later die bravely at Kurukshetra, sit silent. Only the young Vikarna, and Vidura, speak up for her.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Dyuta sub-parva), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
Heroes who feared nothing on the battlefield could not bring themselves to speak for justice in the assembly.
This is the Kural’s point in dramatic form: many will die in battle, few will speak without fear in council.
Christianity
“Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled; and they took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus.”
— Acts 4:13 (KJV) [4]
Brought before the high council, two fishermen speak so boldly that the learned rulers are astonished.
Fearless speech before a council is rare enough to make the members marvel, just as the Kural says such speakers are rare.
Islam
“The best fighting (jihad) in the path of Allah is (to speak) a word of justice to an oppressive ruler.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4344 (graded sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet ranks speaking a just word to a tyrant above other kinds of striving, including armed struggle.
Like the Kural, it places courageous speech before power above courage on the battlefield.
Judaism
“and so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law; and if I perish, I perish.”
— Esther 4:16 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Esther decides to enter the Persian king’s court unsummoned, which could mean death, in order to plead for her people.
Speaking before a throne asks more courage than the Kural’s easy battlefield death, and Esther finds it.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells Sariputta that there are eight kinds of assembly: nobles, brahmins, householders, ascetics, and four orders of gods. He recalls entering hundreds of assemblies of nobles, sitting and talking with them, and seeing no ground for fear or timidity to arise in him.
— Majjhima Nikaya 12, Mahāsīhanāda Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
Fearlessness in every kind of assembly (vesārajja, self-assurance) is listed among the Buddha’s marks of greatness.
Being fearless in the assembly is presented as rare and admirable, as the Kural says.
Jainism
“Monks who equal the ocean in depth, who are difficult to overcome, are frightened by nobody (or nothing), and are not easily assailed, who are full of extensive learning and take care of themselves, will go to the highest place, after their Karman has been annihilated.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture 'The Very Learned' compares the learned monk to heroes, kings and the ocean, and closes by naming fearlessness ('frightened by nobody') as a mark of deep learning.
The Kural says fearless speakers in an assembly are rarer than men willing to die among enemies; the Jain text also holds fearlessness joined to learning to be a rare and high attainment.
Sikhism
“Nanak speaks the Word of Truth; he proclaims the Truth at this, the right time.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 723, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
This closes the Tilang hymn in which Guru Nanak openly describes the slaughter of Babur's invasion; Sikh tradition remembers him speaking the truth while armies raged.
The Kural says many will die among enemies but few can stand fearless in an assembly; Guru Nanak's courage was to speak the truth openly in a time of terror.
Greek Mythology
“Son of Tydeus, in war your prowess is beyond question, and in council you excel all who are of your own years”
— Homer, Iliad 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Agamemnon proposes to give up and sail home, and everyone sits silent until the young warrior Diomedes rises and rebukes the king. Old Nestor then praises him for being brave both in war and in council.
In a room of silent chieftains, Diomedes shows the rarer courage of the council.
Philosophy
“forsook his post, cast away his arms, and ran away most disgracefully”
— Plutarch, Life of Demosthenes 20 (trans. Bernadotte Perrin) [11]
Plutarch says that Demosthenes, the boldest speaker in the Athenian assembly, fled from the battle of Chaeronea.
This is the mirror image of the Kural. Courage in council and courage in battle are separate virtues, and a person can have one without the other.

Real Life Example

Margaret Chase Smith delivers her Declaration of Conscience
1 June 1950 · United States Senate, Washington, D.C., United States
Senator Margaret Chase Smith of Maine, then the only woman in the Senate and a first-term member of Senator Joseph McCarthy's own party, rose in the Senate chamber to deliver a fifteen-minute speech criticising the use of the Senate for unproved charges and character attacks. McCarthy sat two rows behind her, and on her way to the chamber he had reminded her of his influence over convention votes. Six Republican colleagues joined her Declaration of Conscience, and McCarthy later had her removed from the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
At a time when many senators feared McCarthy, Smith chose to speak against him in front of the whole Senate and accepted the political cost.
The kural says that those willing to face death among enemies are many, but those who stand fearless in an assembly are rare, and Smith's speech is a celebrated example of that rarer courage.
