Kural 149

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

நலக்குரியார் யாரெனின் நாமநீர் வைப்பின் பிறர்க்குரியாள் தோள்தோயா தார்.
Nalakkuriyaar yaarenin naamaneer vaippin, Pirarkkuriyaal tholthoyaa thaar.

Meaning

In this world circled by the fearsome sea, who truly deserves every good thing? Those who do not touch a woman who belongs to another man.

Elementary

If you asked, 'Who deserves all the best things in the whole wide world?', this kural has an answer: the people who stay loyal and never go after someone else's husband or wife. Being faithful makes you worthy of good things.

University

The couplet is framed as a question and answer, a device Valluvar uses to give his verdict extra force. The phrase நாமநீர் வைப்பு ('the world set within fearsome waters') is a classical Tamil epithet for the earth encircled by the ocean, so the claim is universal: anywhere in the world. The one worthy of நலம் (goodness, blessing, well-being) is identified by a concrete image, தோள் தோயாதார் ('those whose shoulders do not touch'), an embrace withheld. After several couplets on the ruin that follows adultery, this one reverses the angle and promises reward, showing that the chapter aims to encourage as well as warn.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A widely quoted Sanskrit maxim, usually attributed to Chanakya's Niti, says: one who regards other men's wives as his mother, others' wealth as a lump of clay, and all beings as his own self, is truly wise (a pandita).
— Chanakya Niti (maxim 'matrvat paradareshu'; paraphrase; verse numbering and attribution vary between editions) [3]
This popular saying names respect for other men's wives as the first mark of a truly wise and worthy person.
Both texts answer the question 'who is truly worthy?' by pointing to the person who keeps away from another's wife.
Christianity
“Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God.”
— Matthew 5:8 (KJV) [4]
Jesus says the pure-hearted are blessed, and promises them the vision of God.
The Kural says the faithful deserve every good; the Beatitude promises the greatest good to the pure in heart.
Islam
“Whoever can guarantee (the chastity of) what is between his two jaw-bones and what is between his two legs (i.e. his tongue and his private parts), I guarantee Paradise for him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6474 [5]
The Prophet promised Paradise to anyone who guards his speech and his chastity.
The Kural says the chaste deserve all good in the world; the Prophet promises them the highest good of all.
Judaism
“Can a man take fire in his bosom, and his clothes not be burned? Can one go upon hot coals, and his feet not be burned? So he that goeth in to his neighbour's wife; whosoever toucheth her shall not be innocent.”
— Proverbs 6:27-29 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs compares touching a neighbour's wife to carrying fire against one's chest: no one can do it and stay unharmed or innocent.
The Kural praises those who do not 'touch the shoulder' of another's wife; Proverbs says whoever does touch her cannot remain innocent.
Buddhism
“To support mother and father, to cherish wife and children, and to be engaged in peaceful occupation — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Maha-mangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.4 (trans. Narada Thera) [6]
The Buddha lists caring for one's own spouse and family among the greatest blessings of life.
The Kural ties worthiness of good to faithfulness; the Buddha calls cherishing one's own wife a supreme blessing.
Jainism
“Virtue, praise, friendship, greatness, all these four draw not anigh the men who covet their neighbour's wife.”
— Nālaṭiyār 82, first half (ch. 9, 'The Not Desiring Other Men's Wives'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [7]
The first half of this Nālaṭiyār quatrain says that virtue, good name, friendship and greatness all stay away from the man who covets another's wife. Turned around, they belong to the man who does not.
Valluvar says that those who do not embrace another's wife deserve every good thing in the sea-girt world, and the Nālaṭiyār makes the same point in reverse: virtue, fame, friendship and greatness avoid the man who covets.
Sikhism
“whose eyes do not gaze upon the beauty of others' wives, … - O Nanak, among millions, there is scarcely one such 'touch-nothing Saint'.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 274, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 9, Pauri 1, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan closes his portrait of the truly pure person, whose eyes do not covet others' wives, by saying such a one is rare among millions.
The Kural asks who in the whole sea-girt world deserves every good and answers: those who do not embrace another's wife; Guru Arjan likewise singles out such a person as rare and honoured.
Greek Mythology
Pindar tells how the hero Peleus, while a guest of King Acastus, was pursued by the king's wife. He refused her out of reverence for Zeus, god of hospitality. After surviving her false accusations, Peleus was rewarded by the gods with marriage to the sea-goddess Thetis, a wedding the gods themselves attended.
— Pindar, Nemean Odes 4 and 5 (narrative summary) [9]
Peleus refused another man's wife and was rewarded with the greatest honour a mortal could receive.
This is the Kural's promise exactly: the one who does not embrace another's wife is the one who deserves every good thing.
Philosophy
“For the man who abstains from bodily pleasures and delights in this very fact is temperate, while the man who is annoyed at it is self-indulgent.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle says the truly temperate person is the one who takes joy in holding back from wrong pleasures.
The Kural promises good to those who refrain; Aristotle says the refraining itself becomes a source of joy for the virtuous.

Real Life Example

Charles and Emma Darwin's marriage at Down House
1839-1882 · Down House, Downe, Kent, England
Charles Darwin married his cousin Emma Wedgwood on 29 January 1839, and in 1842 they moved to Down House in Kent, where they raised their children and lived for the rest of his life. Emma supported his scientific work, handled correspondence for him, and nursed him through long periods of ill health. Their marriage lasted 43 years, until his death at Down House in 1882, and English Heritage describes it as very happy and loving.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Darwin's steady family life at Down gave him the security in which he carried out decades of research, and his marriage is remembered as a model of companionship.
The kural says that those who do not embrace another's wife are worthy of every good thing; Darwin's long, faithful marriage was accompanied by a full family life and a lifetime of honoured work.
