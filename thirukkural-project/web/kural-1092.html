Kural 1092

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

கண்களவு கொள்ளும் சிறுநோக்கம் காமத்தில் செம்பாகம் அன்று பெரிது.
Kankalavu kollum sirunokkam kaamaththil sempaakam andru peridhu.

Meaning

The quick look she steals at me when I am not looking is not just half of love's joy. It is more than half.

Elementary

Have you ever caught a friend secretly peeking at the drawing you were making, and then looking away? That tiny peek tells you they really like it, maybe even more than if they had said so out loud. A small secret look can say a lot.

University

Kan kalavu kollum (கண் களவு கொள்ளும்) is literally ‘the eye that steals’: a look taken by stealth (kalavu, the same word that names the whole kalavu, or secret-love, phase). Ciru nōkkam (சிறுநோக்கம்) is the brief, narrowed glance, which Puliyur Kesikan glosses as arukiya nōkkam, a sparing look cast without his knowledge. Sempākam (செம்பாகம்) means ‘exact half’; the commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar: nērpāti anru, atai vida periya pakuti) agree the stolen glance is greater than half of kāmam, the joy of union, because it reveals that his love is returned. Pope: ‘The furtive glance, that gleams one instant bright, / Is more than half of love’s supreme delight.’ No textual variants appear on the two source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“(She casts a lingering glance at the king, and goes out with her two friends.)”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I, stage direction (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
As Shakuntala leaves her first meeting with King Dushyanta, she makes an excuse to linger and looks back at him.
Her lingering backward look is exactly the kind of small, stolen sign the Kural says is worth more than half of love.
Christianity
“Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse; thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes.”
— Song of Solomon 4:9 (KJV) [4]
The lover says a single glance from the beloved has captured his heart.
One look accomplishes almost everything, which is the Kural’s claim about the brief stolen glance.
Islam
“Do not give a second look, Ali, (because) while you are not to blame for the first, you have no right to the second.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 2149 (graded hasan by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet tells Ali that an unplanned first glance carries no blame, but he must not follow it with a deliberate second look.
Islam, like the Kural, treats a single glance as weighty, but it counsels restraint where the Kural celebrates the glance between future spouses.
Judaism
“Young man, please lift up your eyes and see what you choose for yourself for a wife.”
— Mishnah Ta’anit 4:8 (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz, via Sefaria) [6]
On the joyous fifteenth of Av, the daughters of Jerusalem danced in the vineyards and invited young men to look and choose a bride.
The Mishnah gives the glance a real place at the start of courtship, as the Kural does.
Buddhism
“Lord, as I was leaving home, a Sakyan girl–the envy of the countryside–glanced up at me, with her hair half-combed, and said, ‘Hurry back, master.’”
— Udana 3.2, Nanda Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Nanda tells the Buddha he cannot keep up the monk’s life because he keeps remembering the glance of the girl he left behind.
One brief glance outweighed everything else for Nanda, just as the Kural says the small stolen look is more than half of love.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of ‘colours,’ will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira warns that being captured by what the eye sees leads to ruin, like a moth drawn into a flame.
Jainism agrees with the Kural that what the eye catches can seize the whole heart, but it sees this as danger rather than delight.
Sikhism
“He is so beautiful to my eyes; beholding Him, I have been bewitched.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 397, Raag Asa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Asa, Guru Arjan speaks as the bride-soul who has only to see her Lord to be completely captivated.
The Kural says even a stolen glance is more than half of love's joy; Guru Arjan likewise makes a single sight of the Beloved enough to captivate the soul.
Greek Mythology
“And the maiden looked at him with stealthy glance, holding her bright veil aside, her heart smouldering with pain.”
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.444-446 (tr. R. C. Seaton, 1912) [10]
Medea, struck by love for Jason, watches him leave her father’s hall with a secret look from behind her veil.
This is the Kural’s kan kalavu, the eye that steals a look, almost word for word.
Philosophy
Aristotle says that goodwill is the beginning of friendship in the way that the pleasure of the eye is the beginning of love, since no one falls in love without first being delighted by the beloved’s appearance.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.5, 1167a (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
For Aristotle, love begins with the pleasure of seeing.
The Kural goes further, saying that one glance is already more than half of love.

Real Life Example

Clara Wieck and Robert Schumann's secret engagement
1836–1840 · Leipzig, Germany
The pianist Clara Wieck and the composer Robert Schumann fell in love, but her father, Friedrich Wieck, forbade contact when she was sixteen and refused Robert's formal request to marry her. The couple kept their engagement secret and finally took the matter to court, which granted permission in 1840. They married on 12 September 1840.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Their love, carried on in secret against fierce opposition, became one of the best-known partnerships in the history of music.
The kural prizes the glance a lover steals when no one is looking; for years Clara and Robert's love could live only in such hidden moments and secret contact.
