Kural 564

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

இறைகடியன் என்றுரைக்கும் இன்னாச்சொல் வேந்தன் உறைகடுகி ஒல்லைக் கெடும்.
Iraikadiyan endruraikkum innaachchol vaendhan Uraikadugi ollaik kedum.

Meaning

A king whose people call him “our harsh ruler” will have his life cut short and will quickly be destroyed.

Elementary

If everyone at school whispers ‘the new class monitor is mean,’ that monitor won’t be monitor for long. The kural says when a whole country calls its king cruel, his time is almost up.

University

The couplet turns from the ruler’s acts to the people’s verdict: இறை கடியன் (‘the lord is harsh’) is the இன்னாச் சொல், the painful word, that the subjects speak. The ruler is thus defined by his reputation, and public opinion becomes a political force. உறை கடுகி is glossed by Mu. Varadarasanar as ‘his lifespan shrinking’ (ஆயுள் குறைந்து), while Pappaiah reads it as the term of his rule contracting; both readings fit, and Pope’s ‘his age shall dwindle’ follows the first. ஒல்லைக் கெடும் repeats the ‘swiftly perishes’ of 563, binding the two couplets together. The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That king who through folly rashly oppresses his kingdom, (will), together with his relatives, ere long be deprived of his life and of his kingdom.”
— Manusmriti 7.111 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu warns that an oppressive king loses both his life and his throne before long.
The same double loss (shortened life, quick ruin) that the Kural predicts for the king his people call cruel.
Christianity
“The kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them … But ye shall not be so: but he that is greatest among you, let him be as the younger; and he that is chief, as he that doth serve.”
— Luke 22:25–26 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts domineering rulers with the servant-leadership he asks of his disciples.
The Kural condemns the ruler remembered for harshness; Jesus redefines greatness as service.
Islam
“The best of your rulers are those whom you love and who love you … And the worst of your rulers are those whom you hate and who hate you and whom you curse and who curse you.”
— Sahih Muslim 1855a [5]
The Prophet measures rulers by the mutual regard between them and their people.
A ruler his people curse as cruel is ‘the worst of rulers’, the one the Kural says quickly perishes.
Judaism
“And the king answered the people roughly … saying, My father made your yoke heavy, and I will add to your yoke: my father also chastised you with whips, but I will chastise you with scorpions.”
— 1 Kings 12:13–14, 16 (KJV) [4]
Rehoboam rejects his elders’ counsel and threatens his people with harsher rule; ten tribes immediately break away.
Harsh words and a reputation for cruelty cost Rehoboam most of his kingdom at once, just as the Kural warns.
Buddhism
“Speak not harshly to anyone, for those thus spoken to might retort. Indeed, angry speech hurts, and retaliation may overtake you.”
— Dhammapada 133 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha warns that harsh speech rebounds on the speaker.
The king whose harshness becomes a byword finds it returning on him as the people’s bitter verdict.
Jainism
“As a bitch with sore ears is driven away everywhere, thus a bad, insubordinate, and talkative (pupil) is turned out.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Uttaradhyayana warns that a person whose conduct makes him disliked is rejected wherever he goes.
The Kural's king earns the people's bitter name 'cruel' and is cut off; in the Jain verse too, a reputation for bad conduct leads to rejection.
Sikhism
“Gathering the wealth of Maya, you earn an evil reputation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 42, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan warns the one who heaps up wealth by pressing on others that what he really earns is the ill name and ill wishes of the people.
The Kural says a king whom his subjects call ‘cruel’ will have his span cut short; Guru Arjan likewise notes that oppressive gain earns the people's bitter verdict.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ Antigone, Creon’s son Haemon warns him that the whole city is quietly mourning Antigone and condemning his harsh decree, but Creon refuses to listen and soon loses his son and wife.
— Sophocles, Antigone 683–723 [9]
The Theban legend shows a ruler deaf to his people’s murmurs about his cruelty, and destroyed as a result.
Haemon reports exactly the ‘இன்னாச் சொல்’ the Kural describes, and Creon’s ruin follows swiftly.
Philosophy
In Xenophon’s Hiero, the tyrant Hiero confesses that he knows he is hated, lives in constant fear, and cannot trust anyone; the poet Simonides advises him to win his citizens’ love by kindness instead.
— Xenophon, Hiero, chs. 2–7 and 11 [10]
Xenophon portrays the tyrant’s life as shortened and poisoned by the hatred he has earned.
The ruler known as cruel lives under the shadow the Kural describes; Xenophon offers the same cure the chapter implies.

Real Life Example

Nicolae Ceausescu is shouted down at his last rally
21-25 December 1989 · Bucharest and Targoviste, Romania
On 21 December 1989 Romania's ruler Nicolae Ceausescu addressed a large organised rally in Bucharest, broadcast live, to condemn the unrest in Timisoara. Partway through the speech people in the crowd began shouting against him, the broadcast was briefly cut, and he was unable to continue. The next day he and his wife fled the capital by helicopter, were taken into custody by the army, and on 25 December were tried by a special military tribunal and executed.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Within four days of the moment the crowd openly voiced its verdict on him, a ruler who had held power for nearly a quarter of a century had lost everything.
The kural says a king whom the people call cruel will have his span cut short and perish quickly, and the shouting at Ceausescu's rally was followed in days by the end of his rule and his life.
