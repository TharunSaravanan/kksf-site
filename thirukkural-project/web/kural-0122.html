Kural 122

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

காக்க பொருளா அடக்கத்தை ஆக்கம் அதனினூஉங் கில்லை உயிர்க்கு.
Kaakka porulaa adakkaththai aakkam Adhaninoovung killai uyirkku.

Meaning

Guard self-control like a treasure, because no living being has anything that does more good than this.

Elementary

People lock their money in a safe so nobody can take it. This kural says self-control is worth even more than money, so you should protect it just as carefully. You can lose toys or money, but if you hold on to your self-control, it keeps you good.

University

The key word is porulaa, ‘as wealth’ or ‘as a thing of real value’; Parimelazhagar glosses it as uruthip porul, an ultimate or assured good. The couplet turns ordinary economics upside down: restraint is not a sacrifice that costs us something but an asset (aakkam, ‘growth, prosperity’) that must be actively guarded (kaakka). That implies it can be lost or stolen, presumably by the passions. Parimelazhagar notes that uyir (‘living being’) here means human life in particular, since only humans can understand restraint and benefit from it. The idea that inner self-command is the truest possession has close relatives in the Stoic distinction between what is ‘up to us’ and what is not, and in the Mishnah’s redefinition of the mighty and the rich.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One must deliver himself with the help of his mind, and not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 [4]
Krishna teaches that a disciplined mind lifts a person up while an undisciplined one drags him down, so one’s own mind is either best friend or worst enemy.
Both texts treat mastery over oneself as the greatest resource a person has, something to be protected and used for one’s own uplift.
Christianity
“For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul?”
— Matthew 16:26 (KJV) [5]
Jesus sets the soul above the entire world’s wealth: nothing could be worth trading it for.
Like Valluvar, Jesus reorders what counts as ‘profit’: the inner life outweighs any outward gain.
Islam
“Wealth is not in having many possessions, but rather (true) wealth is feeling sufficiency in the soul.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6446 [6]
The Prophet defines real wealth as an inner state of contentment rather than an accumulation of things.
Both sayings move ‘wealth’ from outside to inside: a governed, satisfied soul is the true treasure.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Ben Zoma redefines might as self-conquest, and in the same teaching defines the rich man as ‘he who rejoices in his lot.’
Just as Valluvar calls restraint the highest wealth, the Mishnah calls self-mastery true strength and contentment true riches.
Buddhism
“Wonderful, indeed, it is to subdue the mind, so difficult to subdue, ever swift, and seizing whatever it desires. A tamed mind brings happiness.”
— Dhammapada 35 [8]
The Buddha praises the taming of the restless, grasping mind as the source of happiness.
Both texts name disciplining oneself as the single greatest good a person can obtain.
Jainism
“Subdue your Self, for the Self is difficult to subdue; if your Self is subdued, you will be happy in this world and in the next.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.15 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The first lecture of the Uttarādhyayana presents self-subjugation as the hardest task and the surest source of well-being in both lives.
Valluvar tells us to guard restraint as our treasure since no greater good exists for a living being; Mahāvīra's verse likewise makes the subdued self the source of happiness here and hereafter.
Sikhism
“Let self-control be the furnace, and patience the goldsmith.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 8, Japji Sahib, Pauri 38, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the closing pauri of Japji Guru Nanak lists self-control (jat) as the first part of the mint where the true coin of the Shabad is struck.
The Kural asks us to guard restraint like a treasure; Japji makes self-control the furnace in which life's real gold is made.
Greek Mythology
At a crossroads, the young Heracles meets two women, Vice and Virtue. Vice offers an easy life of pleasure; Virtue tells him the gods grant nothing truly good without toil and discipline. Heracles chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus’ ‘Choice of Heracles,’ in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21–34 (paraphrase) [11]
The fable presents disciplined virtue as the path to real and lasting goods, and easy pleasure as a false bargain.
Heracles treats disciplined virtue as the treasure worth choosing above all, just as the Kural urges.
Philosophy
“Temperance, I replied, is the ordering or controlling of certain pleasures and desires; this is curiously enough implied in the saying of ‘a man being his own master’”
— Plato, Republic IV, 430e (trans. Jowett) [12]
Plato defines temperance (sophrosyne), one of the four cardinal virtues, as the well-ordered rule of the self over its desires.
Plato ranks self-mastery among the soul’s most precious possessions, much as Valluvar ranks restraint above all wealth.

Real Life Example

Father Theobald Mathew's temperance pledge
10 April 1838 onwards · Cork and throughout Ireland
On 10 April 1838 the Capuchin priest Theobald Mathew presided at the first meeting of the Cork Total Abstinence Society in his own schoolhouse and took the pledge of total abstinence himself. Between 1838 and 1842 he travelled throughout Ireland, where whole crowds took the pledge, and by 1841 the number of abstainers in Ireland was estimated at 4,647,000. Britannica records that within three years consumption of spirits fell by about half, much of the decrease being attributed to his efforts.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Millions treated a vow of self-restraint as something worth guarding, and contemporaries credited the movement with improvements in public order, though historians note that its gains were weakened by the Great Famine.
The kural says to guard self-restraint like a treasure, since no living being has a greater good; the pledge movement asked ordinary people to do exactly that.
