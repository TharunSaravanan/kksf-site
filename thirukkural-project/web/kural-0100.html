Kural 100

Charming Utterance · Iniyavai Kooral · இனியவை கூறல்

இனிய உளவாக இன்னாத கூறல் கனிஇருப்பக் காய்கவர்ந் தற்று.
Iniya ulavaaga innaadha kooral, Kani iruppak kaaikavarn dhatru.

Meaning

Speaking hurtful words when kind words are ready to use is like picking unripe fruit when ripe fruit hangs right there.

Elementary

Picture a tree with sweet ripe mangoes hanging low and hard green ones next to them. Nobody would reach past the sweet ones to bite a sour green one. But that's exactly what people do when they say something harsh when a kind thing would have worked just as well.

University

The chapter closes on its most famous image, and the comparison is chosen with care: கனி (kani, ripe fruit) and காய் (kaai, unripe fruit) come from the same tree at the same moment, so the choice costs nothing and requires no extra effort — which is exactly the point. உளவாக (ulavaaga, 'there being available') carries the weight: the pleasant words are already present and to hand. The verse thus completes the chapter's argument by removing the last excuse, since harsh speech can no longer be defended as necessary, cheaper, or more efficient. The kani/kaai contrast belongs to a wider Tamil poetic register of fruit imagery, and the comparative particle அற்று (atru, 'is like that') is the classical Tamil marker of simile. Strikingly, three other traditions independently reach for fruit or tree imagery at exactly this point — James on the fig and the vine, the Qur'an on the good and evil tree, Proverbs on apples of gold — suggesting how naturally the ethics of speech attracts the metaphor of cultivation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him not, even though in pain, (speak words) cutting (others) to the quick; let him not injure others in thought or deed; let him not utter speeches which make (others) afraid of him, since that will prevent him from gaining heaven.”
— Manusmriti 2.161 (trans. Georg Bühler, Sacred Books of the East, vol. 25) [4]
Manu rules out cutting speech even under the one circumstance that might seem to excuse it — the speaker's own suffering — and attaches an eschatological penalty.
It removes the excuse for harsh words just as the Kural does, on the ground that nothing is gained by them.
Christianity
“Doth a fountain send forth at the same place sweet water and bitter? Can the fig tree, my brethren, bear olive berries? either a vine, figs? so can no fountain both yield salt water and fresh.”
— James 3:11–12 (King James Version) [5]
James's discourse on the tongue uses agricultural and hydrological images to argue that sweet and bitter speech cannot legitimately issue from the same source.
It reaches for exactly the Kural's register — fruit, sweetness, bitterness — to make the same point about the incoherence of choosing the bitter.
Islam
“Have you not considered how Allah presents an example, [making] a good word like a good tree, whose root is firmly fixed and its branches [high] in the sky? It produces its fruit all the time, by permission of its Lord... And the example of a bad word is like a bad tree, uprooted from the surface of the earth, not having any stability.”
— Qur'an 14:24–26 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an sets the good word and the bad word side by side as two trees, one endlessly fruitful and rooted, the other torn up and worthless.
It is the Kural's ripe-fruit / unripe-fruit choice extended into a full arboreal parable.
Judaism
“A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver.”
— Proverbs 25:11 (Hebrew Bible; King James Version rendering) [5]
Proverbs values the well-chosen word by comparing it to precious fruit set in a worked silver frame — something both beautiful and deliberately fashioned.
It shares the Kural's fruit-metaphor for the well-chosen word, treating the good option as the obviously superior one.
Buddhism
The Buddha sorts people into three kinds by their speech: those whose speech is like dung, those whose speech is like flowers, and those whose speech is like honey — the last being the person whose words are gentle, pleasing to the ear and spoken with affection.
— Gūthabhānī Sutta, Aṅguttara Nikāya 3.28 [7]
The image deliberately ranges from the repulsive to the delicious, making the choice between them look as obvious as a choice between foods.
It uses the same sensory-appetite logic as the Kural's ripe-versus-unripe fruit: nobody deliberately chooses the worse thing to consume.
Jainism
“What is not laudable? That which causes pain and suffering to the living beings is not commendable. It does not matter whether it refers to the actual fact or not.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.14 with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [8]
Pūjyapāda's commentary on the Tattvārtha Sūtra's definition of falsehood counts hurtful words as a breach of the vow of truth (satya) even when they are factually true, because the vows exist to guard non-violence.
Valluvar says choosing harsh words when kind ones are at hand is like picking unripe fruit beside ripe, and Jain ethics makes that choice a fault, since needlessly hurtful speech counts as falsehood.
Sikhism
“He thinks that the ripe coconut hangs on the barren simmal tree; what a stupid, idiotic fool he is!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 972, Raag Raamkalee, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a Raamkalee hymn Kabir mocks the deluded person who mistakes worthless fruit for good and turns from what would truly nourish him.
Like Valluvar's man who grabs unripe fruit while the ripe hangs beside it, Kabir's fool chooses the barren over the good through sheer perversity.
Greek Mythology
“The best treasure a man can have is a sparing tongue, and the greatest pleasure, one that moves orderly; for if you speak evil, you yourself will soon be worse spoken of.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 719–721 (trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod, in the practical wisdom that closes Works and Days, ranks a measured tongue as a man's chief asset and notes that abuse simply returns amplified.
It reaches the Kural's conclusion by the same practical route: the harsh option is plainly the worse buy.
Philosophy
“The man who is pleasant in the right way is friendly and the mean is friendliness, while the man who exceeds is an obsequious person if he has no end in view, a flatterer if he is aiming at his own advantage, and the man who falls short and is unpleasant in all circumstances is a quarrelsome and surly sort of person.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.6, 1126b (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle constructs a virtue of everyday social pleasantness, positioned between the flatterer who gives pleasure dishonestly and the churl who gives pain for no reason at all.
His 'quarrelsome and surly' person is precisely the Kural's fruit-picker who reaches past the ripe for the green.

Real Life Example

Bismarck edits the Ems Dispatch
13 July 1870 · Berlin, Prussia (now Germany)
On 13 July 1870 a telegram from Bad Ems reported to Otto von Bismarck a meeting between King William I of Prussia and the French ambassador, Vincent Benedetti, over the Spanish throne. Before releasing it to the press, Bismarck shortened it so that the king's courteous exchange read as a curt and final rebuff to France. The published version inflamed opinion in both countries, and France declared war on Prussia on 19 July 1870.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bismarck deliberately turned a measured, civil message into a wounding one, and the result was a war.
The kural likens harsh words chosen when pleasant ones are at hand to picking unripe fruit beside the ripe, and Bismarck had the courteous text in his hands and chose to publish the harsh one.
