Kural 839

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

பெரிதினிது பேதையார் கேண்மை பிரிவின்கண் பீழை தருவதொன் றில்.
Peridhinidhu pedhaiyaar kenmai pirivinkan peezhai tharuvadhon ril.

Meaning

Friendship with fools is very sweet, because when it ends, saying goodbye causes no pain at all.

Elementary

Some friends are like a balloon on a string: it is fun for a while, but when it floats away you don’t really miss it. The poet is joking that friends who are foolish are ‘lovely’, because losing them doesn’t hurt at all.

University

The couplet is ironic praise: peridhu inidhu (பெரிது இனிது, ‘exceedingly sweet’) is applied to pedhaiyaar kenmai (பேதையார் கேண்மை, friendship with fools), and the reason given is that at pirivu (பிரிவு, separation) there is no peezhai (பீழை, pain, affliction). Mu. Varadarasanar, Kalaignar and Puliyur Kesigan all read it as saying that a fool’s friendship is ‘sweet’ only in that losing it costs nothing, so its real worth is zero; Kesigan hedges with ‘in one way’. Solomon Pappaiah reads it slightly differently, as friendship among fools themselves, whose partings cause them no grief, which matches Drew’s ‘the friendship between fools’. The text is the same in ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari’s Nitishataka says one might pull a jewel from a crocodile’s jaws or wear a raging serpent like a flower, but one cannot win over a fool whose mind is set wrong.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka (verse beginning ‘prasahya manim uddharet’) [3]
The Sanskrit poet treats the fool as someone no real bond can be formed with.
Valluvar’s irony makes a similar point: friendship with a fool has so little in it that losing it costs nothing.
Christianity
“Be not deceived: evil communications corrupt good manners.”
— 1 Corinthians 15:33 (KJV) [4]
Paul, quoting a Greek proverb, warns that bad company damages character.
Both texts imply that friendship with fools is worth little and well lost.
Islam
“Close friends, that Day, will be enemies to each other, except for the righteous”
— Qur’an 43:67 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says friendships not grounded in righteousness will fall apart on the Day of Judgment.
Valluvar likewise implies that friendships without wisdom have nothing in them that lasts.
Judaism
“Go from the presence of a foolish man, when thou perceivest not in him the lips of knowledge.”
— Proverbs 14:7 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Proverbs advises simply leaving the company of a fool once you see he has no wisdom to offer.
The Kural’s irony points the same way: walking away from a fool costs nothing.
Buddhism
“Good is it to see the Noble Ones; to live with them is ever blissful. One will always be happy by not encountering fools.”
— Dhammapada 206 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada says plainly that life is happier without fools in it.
Valluvar’s joke rests on the same truth: separation from a fool brings no grief.
Jainism
“This is the road to it: to serve the Gurus and the old (teachers), to avoid throughout foolish people, to apply oneself earnestly to study, and to ponder zealously on the meaning of the Sûtras.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana counts keeping clear of foolish people among the steps on the road to final deliverance.
Valluvar jokes that friendship with fools is sweet because parting from them costs nothing; the Uttarādhyayana states the same lesson plainly, that fools are to be avoided altogether.
Sikhism
“Friendship with a fool never works out right. As he knows, he acts; behold, and see that it is so. … Friendship with a fool, and love with a pompous person, are like lines drawn in water, leaving no trace or mark.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (saloks 3 and 4 before pauri 22), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In two saloks of Asa Ki Vaar Guru Angad says friendship with a fool never works out, and that it is like a line drawn in water, gone without a trace.
The Kural's ironic praise is that friendship with fools is sweet because parting from it leaves no pain; Guru Angad's line drawn in water leaves the same lack of any mark.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the travellers and the bear, one companion scrambles up a tree and leaves his friend to face the bear; the bear seems to whisper to the man on the ground never to travel with a friend who deserts at the first sign of danger.
— Aesop, ‘The Travellers and the Bear’ (Townsend) [9]
The fable shows a friendship that proves worthless the moment it is tested.
Like Valluvar’s fool’s friendship, it is one whose loss is no loss.
Philosophy
Aristotle explains that friendships based on pleasure or usefulness are easily dissolved, since when the pleasure or use ends, the friendship ends too.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 [10]
For Aristotle, only friendship grounded in virtue endures; the rest come apart without much loss.
The Kural’s fool’s friendship is the shallow kind whose ending leaves no wound.

Real Life Example

General Charles Lee is suspended and then dismissed from the Continental Army
1778-1780 · Monmouth, New Jersey, and Philadelphia, United States
Major General Charles Lee, second in rank in the Continental Army, led the advance at the Battle of Monmouth in June 1778, but his attack faltered and his troops fell back in disorder until George Washington rallied them. At his own request Lee was court-martialed and was found guilty of disobeying orders, misbehaviour before the enemy and disrespect to Washington, and was suspended for a year. He continued to attack his accusers and Congress, was dismissed from the army in January 1780, and the army carried on under Washington without him.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Lee's departure removed a quarrelsome and unreliable senior officer, and it cost the American cause nothing.
The kural says ironically that friendship with fools is sweet because nothing is lost when it ends, and the army's parting with Lee caused no loss at all.
