Kural 320

Not Doing Evil · Innaa Seyyaamai · இன்னா செய்யாமை

நோயெல்லாம் நோய்செய்தார் மேலவாம் நோய்செய்யார் நோயின்மை வேண்டு பவர்.
Noyellaam noy seydhaar melavaam noy seyyaar Noyinmai vendu pavar.

Meaning

All suffering comes back to those who cause suffering. So people who want to be free of suffering do not cause it to others.

Elementary

If you plant thorns, you will get pricked walking through your own garden. If you never want to get pricked, don’t plant thorns. This kural says: if you want a life without pain, don’t give pain to anyone.

University

The chapter closes with a verbal tour de force: the word noy (pain, affliction, disease) appears four times in seven words, so that the sound of the couplet enacts its argument that pain circles back to its source. The first line states a law (all affliction settles on those who afflict), and the second draws the practical conclusion for anyone who wants noyinmai, freedom from suffering. The ethics of non-harm is here grounded partly in enlightened self-interest, rounding off a chapter that has moved from the stainless person’s principle (311–312), through empathy (315–318), to consequence (319–320). The idea is widely shared: Hesiod’s warning that the evil plotter harms himself most, the Qur’an’s statement that evil plotting backfires on the plotters, Marcus Aurelius’ remark that the wrongdoer wrongs himself, and the Dhammapada’s assurance that for one who does no evil there is no ill.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He by whom no one is put into difficulty and who is not disturbed by anyone, who is equipoised in happiness and distress, fear and anxiety, is very dear to Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.15 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes the ideal devotee as one who troubles no one and is troubled by no one.
The Gita pairs the two halves of the Kural: the one who causes no distress is also the one free of distress.
Christianity
“For with what judgment ye judge, ye shall be judged: and with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again.”
— Matthew 7:2 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that the standard we apply to others will be applied to us.
Both teach that what we deal out comes back to us, so the way to avoid harm is not to deal it.
Islam
“But evil plotting only backfires on those who plot.”
— Qur’an 35:43 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The Qur’an declares that evil schemes end up harming only the schemers.
The Kural’s first line in Qur’anic form: harm settles on those who cause it.
Judaism
“He made a pit, and digged it, and is fallen into the ditch which he made. His mischief shall return upon his own head, and his violent dealing shall come down upon his own pate.”
— Psalm 7:15–16 (KJV) [4]
The Psalmist says the evildoer falls into his own trap and his violence comes down on his own head.
Both declare that the pain one causes lands on oneself.
Buddhism
“If on the hand there is no wound, one may carry even poison in it. Poison does not affect one who is free from wounds. For him who does no evil, there is no ill.”
— Dhammapada 124 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha compares the one who does no evil to a hand without cuts: poison cannot harm it.
This matches the Kural’s second line: the person who does no harm stays free of harm.
Jainism
“Whatever cruelty he has done in a former birth, the same will be inflicted on him in the Circle of Births. Having been born in an extremely miserable state of life, the sufferer experiences infinite pain. A wise man hearing of these hells should not kill any living being in the whole world.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.5.2.23–24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
At the end of its description of the hells, the Sūtrakṛtāṅga states that the pain one inflicts is the pain one will suffer, and draws the practical conclusion of non-violence.
Valluvar says all suffering falls on those who cause it, so those who wish to be free of suffering do not cause it. The Jain verses state both halves, the law and the conclusion, in the same order.
Sikhism
“Do not harbor evil intentions against others in your mind, and you shall not be troubled, O Siblings of Destiny, O friends.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 386, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan ties freedom from one's own trouble directly to not wishing harm on others.
The Kural concludes that those who wish to be free of suffering do not cause it to others; Guru Arjan gives the same practical counsel: wish no one harm, and harm will not trouble you.
Greek Mythology
“He does mischief to himself who does mischief to another, and evil planned harms the plotter most.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 265–266 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod warns his brother Perses that anyone who harms another is really harming himself.
The Kural’s central claim in Greek form: pain caused to others falls on the one who causes it.
Philosophy
“Does any one do wrong? It is to himself that he does the wrong.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.26 (trans. George Long) [10]
Marcus Aurelius reminds himself that the wrongdoer is the real victim of his own wrong.
Both place the harm of wrongdoing on the one who commits it.

Real Life Example

Vidkun Quisling's collaboration ends in his trial and execution
1940-1945 · Oslo, Norway
During the German invasion of Norway in April 1940, Vidkun Quisling proclaimed himself head of a new government, and from 1942 he served as Minister President of a Nazi-controlled puppet government. He signed laws that enabled the arrest of Norway's Jewish men and the confiscation of Jewish property, and 772 Norwegian Jews were deported, most to their deaths at Auschwitz. After Norway's liberation in May 1945 he was arrested, convicted of treason and other crimes, and executed at Akershus Fortress in Oslo on 24 October 1945; his name became a synonym for traitor.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The man who helped bring great suffering on his own country ended his life condemned by it, his very name turned into a word of disgrace.
The kural says all suffering falls upon those who cause suffering, and Quisling's betrayal of his people returned on him as condemnation and a lasting byword for treachery.
