Kural 1083

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

பண்டறியேன் கூற்றென் பதனை இனியறிந்தேன் பெண்டகையால் பேரமர்க் கட்டு.
Pandariyen kootren pathanai iniyarinthen pendakaiyaal peramark kattu.

Meaning

I never knew before what people call Death. Now I know it: it has a lovely woman's form and big eyes that fight.

Elementary

People sometimes say a scary film 'nearly killed' them, even though they were safe. The young man jokes in the same way: he had only heard of Death in stories, but now he has met it, in the shape of a girl with big, powerful eyes.

University

Kootru (கூற்று) is Death personified, Yama, the 'one who divides' body from life. Pandariyen ('I did not know before') and iniyarinthen ('now I have learned') frame a mock discovery. The second line packs pen-takai (பெண்டகை, 'womanly grace') with per-amar-kan (பேரமர்க்கண், 'large eyes that do battle'); Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as 'it has a woman's nature and large eyes that make war', while Kalaignar speaks of eye-arrows. The commentarial tradition treats the hyperbole as the lover's helplessness, not a moral judgment on the woman. Pope: 'Death's form I formerly / Knew not; but now 'tis plain to me; / He comes in lovely maiden's guise.' Identical on both web sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is lust only, Arjuna, which is born of contact with the material modes of passion and later transformed into wrath, and which is the all-devouring, sinful enemy of this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.37 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, 1972) [3]
Krishna calls kama, desire, the all-devouring enemy of the world.
The Kural playfully treats the beloved's eyes as Death; the Gita seriously treats desire itself as a devouring enemy.
Christianity
“Lust not after her beauty in thine heart; neither let her take thee with her eyelids.”
— Proverbs 6:25 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that a woman's eyes can capture a man and lead him to ruin.
The Kural, too, makes the eyes the weapon that takes a man's life.
Islam
“He said, ‘My Lord, prison is more to my liking than that to which they invite me. And if You do not avert from me their plan, I might incline toward them and [thus] be of the ignorant.’”
— Qur'an 12:33 (Sahih International) [5]
Joseph asks God to protect him, knowing how strongly beauty and attraction can pull even a righteous man.
Both texts treat the pull of beauty as a danger strong enough to overcome a person.
Judaism
“For love is fierce as death, / Passion is mighty as Sheol; / Its darts are darts of fire, / A blazing flame.”
— Song of Songs 8:6 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs compares love to death and the grave and speaks of its fiery darts.
The Kural, too, puts love and Death side by side and gives love weapons.
Buddhism
“Realizing that this body is like froth, penetrating its mirage-like nature, and plucking out Mara's flower-tipped arrows of sensuality, go beyond sight of the King of Death!”
— Dhammapada 46 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada links Mara's flower-arrows of desire with the realm of the King of Death.
The Kural discovers that the eyes of desire are Death itself; the Dhammapada makes the same link and urges escape.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana compares pleasures to a lovely fruit that turns out to be deadly poison.
The Kural finds that something beautiful can be Death in disguise, which is the same idea.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, the world is afraid of death - that death fills my mind with bliss.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1365, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In another salok, Kabir says the dying that the world fears, which Sikh commentators read as dying to the ego, fills him with joy.
The Kural's lover says he has only now met Death, in the form of a woman's large eyes; Kabir also speaks of meeting a death that love makes welcome.
Greek Mythology
“Small wonder that Trojans and Achaeans should endure so much and so long, for the sake of a woman so marvellously and divinely lovely.”
— Homer, Iliad 3 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
The Trojan elders, watching Helen, admit that her beauty explains a war that has brought death to so many.
Like the Kural, the elders tie a woman's beauty directly to death and battle.
Philosophy
Plotinus says that the sight of true beauty brings wonder and a delicious trouble, longing and love, and a trembling that is all delight.
— Plotinus, Enneads I.6.4 (tr. Stephen MacKenna; paraphrase) [11]
For Plotinus, beauty strikes the soul with an overwhelming and pleasurable shock.
The Kural expresses the same overwhelming shock in the playful language of meeting Death.

Real Life Example

John Keats and Fanny Brawne
1818–1821 · Hampstead, London, England
The poet John Keats met Fanny Brawne in late 1818, and in April 1819 she moved into the other half of Wentworth Place in Hampstead, next door to him. They became secretly engaged, and he wrote her passionate letters. In February 1820 a lung haemorrhage convinced Keats he was dying of tuberculosis, and he died in Rome in February 1821, aged 25, without being able to marry her.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Their love letters are among the most famous in English and show love and mortal illness bound together.
The kural's lover says he has now learned what Death is, for it has a woman's grace; for Keats, love for Fanny and the approach of death arrived together.
