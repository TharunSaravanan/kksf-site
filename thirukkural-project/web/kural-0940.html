Kural 940

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

இழத்தொறூஉம் காதலிக்கும் சூதேபோல் துன்பம் உழத்தொறூஉம் காதற்று உயிர்.
Izhaththoruum kaadhalikkum soodhepol thunbam uzhaththoruum kaadhatru uyir.

Meaning

The more a gambler loses, the more they love gambling. In the same way, the more a soul suffers, the more it loves living in the body.

Elementary

Have you ever lost a game and immediately shouted, 'One more try!'? The more you lose, the more you want to play. Valluvar says life is like that too: even when it hurts, we hold on to it more tightly.

University

The closing couplet turns a psychological observation about gambling into a general statement about existence. Izhaththoruum (இழத்தொறூஉம்) and uzhaththoruum (உழத்தொறூஉம்), both with alapedai, mean 'every time one loses' and 'every time one suffers', placing loss and pain in parallel. Kaadhalikkum (காதலிக்கும், 'comes to love') and kaadhatru (காதற்று) carry the comparison. Most commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar, Kesikan, Pope) read kaadhatru as kaadhal + atru, 'has love, is fond': the soul (uyir) loves the body more as it suffers, just as the gambler loves gambling more as he loses. Sivayogi Sivakumar on ytamizh instead splits it as kaadhal + atru 'without love', giving the opposite sense that the soul does not love suffering, and adds that gambling can lead to despair. Pappaiah reverses which half is the comparison. The text is identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When I resolve to play with these no longer, my friends depart from me and leave me lonely. When the brown dice, thrown on the board, have rattled, like a fond girl I seek the place of meeting.”
— Rig Veda 10.34.5, trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith [3]
The Vedic gambler confesses that however much he resolves to stop, the rattle of dice pulls him back like a lover to a meeting.
This is the Kural's gambler, who loves the game more with every loss, described in his own words.
Christianity
“For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do.”
— Romans 7:19 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes being pulled again and again toward what he knows harms him.
The gambler who loses and keeps playing shows this same divided will.
Islam
“They ask you about wine and gambling. Say, ‘In them is great sin and [yet, some] benefit for people. But their sin is greater than their benefit.’”
— Qur'an 2:219 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an acknowledges the small benefit that makes gambling attractive while judging its harm greater.
That small benefit is the lure that keeps the Kural's gambler loving the game through every loss.
Judaism
“A lover of money is never satisfied with money, nor a lover of wealth, with income. That too is futile.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:9 (Hebrew numbering; JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, on Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet notes that desire for money only grows and is never satisfied.
Desire that feeds on itself is the Kural's gambler, whose love grows as he loses.
Buddhism
“...the noble truth of the origination of stress: the craving that makes for further becoming — accompanied by passion & delight, relishing now here & now there — i.e., craving for sensual pleasure, craving for becoming, craving for non-becoming.”
— Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
The Buddha's Second Noble Truth names craving for continued existence (bhava-tanha) as what binds beings to suffering.
The Kural's soul clinging ever harder to a suffering body is this craving for becoming.
Jainism
“Those will reap pains who, in thoughts, words, or acts, are attached to their body, to colours, and to forms.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the lecture on the false ascetic, Mahāvīra teaches that attachment to the body is itself a source of suffering, and in Jain thought this attachment keeps the soul (jīva) bound in saṃsāra.
Valluvar ends by saying the soul loves the body all the more the more it suffers, as the gambler loves the game the more he loses; the Jain verse reverses the direction, teaching that attachment to the body is what produces the pain.
Sikhism
“as the gambler’s addiction does not leave him, So is this mind of Nanak’s attached to the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 838, Raag Bilaaval, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a long list of passionate attachments (the moth to the flame, the elephant to its lust), Guru Arjan includes the gambler whose habit will not let him go. He then turns the image round: let the mind cling to the Lord just as tightly.
The Kural observes that gambling is loved all the more the more one loses, and Guru Arjan names that same unbreakable addiction, though he redirects its force toward God.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus reports a Lydian legend that during an eighteen-year famine the people kept up their dice and games, playing on alternate days so they could endure the hunger.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.94 (trans. George Rawlinson) [10]
A whole people held on to their games, and to life, through years of suffering.
Clinging to play and to life amid hardship echoes both halves of the Kural's comparison.
Philosophy
“Not that there is in fact any happiness in them, or that men imagine true bliss to consist in money won at play, or in the hare which they hunt; we would not take these as a gift. ... Reasons why we like the chase better than the quarry.”
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées §139 (trans. W. F. Trotter) [11]
Pascal observes that gamblers do not really want the winnings; they want the play itself, the chase rather than the prize.
That is why, as the Kural says, losing only deepens the gambler's love for the game.

Real Life Example

The Rigveda's Gambler's Lament is composed
Late second millennium BCE · North-western Indian subcontinent
Hymn 10.34 of the Rigveda, more than 3,000 years old, is spoken by a compulsive dice player who says that a losing throw has driven away his devoted wife, that her mother hates him and that his own family disowns him while he wanders in debt. He describes how, whenever he swears he will not play again, the sound of the dice falling sends him running back to the gaming place like a lover to a meeting. The hymn ends with the god Savitr's counsel to stop playing with dice and to till his field and value what he has.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The oldest known poem about gambling addiction already describes the pattern of ruin, resolve and relapse that later gamblers would recognise.
The kural says gambling is loved all the more the more one loses, and the Rigveda's gambler, having lost wife, family and wealth, still runs back to the dice whenever he hears them.
