Kural 724

Dread Avoidance · Avaiyanjaamai · அவையஞ்சாமை

கற்றார்முன் கற்ற செலச்சொல்லித் தாம்கற்ற மிக்காருள் மிக்க கொளல்.
Katraarmun katra selachchollith thaamkatra Mikkaarul mikka kolal.

Meaning

Share what you have learned with learned people so it reaches them. Then, from people who know even more, learn the best of what they know.

Elementary

Share what you know in class. Then listen to the teacher and the older kids who know even more. Speaking up and listening both help you grow.

University

The couplet repeats the formula kaṟṟār mun kaṟṟa cela-c-colli from 722 and adds a second duty: mikkāruḷ mikka koḷal, “take the surplus from those who surpass you.” Parimelazhagar reads mikkār as those superior in learning and mikka as whatever their learning holds beyond one’s own. The ytamizh commentators (Varadarasanar, Pappaiah, Kalaignar) agree. Speaking before the learned is therefore not a performance but one part of a cycle of learning: speak, be tested, learn more. The sandhi-joined text has “தாங்கற்ற” and “மிக்காருண்” for தாம்கற்ற and மிக்காருள்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized soul can impart knowledge unto you because he has seen the truth.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna to seek out those who know more and to learn from them through questions and service.
This echoes the Kural’s second half: take the greater knowledge from those who surpass you.
Christianity
“And he began to speak boldly in the synagogue: whom when Aquila and Priscilla had heard, they took him unto them, and expounded unto him the way of God more perfectly.”
— Acts 18:24-26 (KJV) [4]
Apollos, eloquent and mighty in the scriptures, preached boldly. Priscilla and Aquila, who understood more, then taught him further.
This is the Kural’s cycle almost step by step: speak boldly before the learned, then learn more from those who know more.
Islam
“Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you on [the condition] that you teach me from what you have been taught of sound judgement?’”
— Qur’an 18:66 (Saheeh International) [5]
Moses, a prophet already rich in knowledge, asks al-Khidr, who has knowledge Moses lacks, to teach him.
The learned take from someone greater the knowledge that goes beyond their own, which is exactly mikkāruḷ mikka koḷal.
Judaism
Ben Zoma asks, “Who is wise?” and answers that the wise person is one who learns from every person.
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 [6]
The Mishnah defines wisdom as an openness to keep learning from others, especially from those who know more.
Like the Kural, it treats continued learning from others as the sign of true learning.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure.”
— Dhammapada 76 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada urges us to seek out wiser people who will correct us, and to treat them as a treasure.
Taking correction and more knowledge from those who surpass us is the second half of the Kural.
Jainism
“At the right time he may put a question about living beings to a well-conducted (monk), who will explain the conduct of the virtuous; and what he hears he should follow and treasure up in his heart, thinking that it is the doctrine of the Kêvalins.”
— Sutrakritanga I.14.15; cf. I.14.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Nirgrantha lecture asks the monk both to teach others (I.14.5) and to go to better-informed monks with his questions, keeping what he learns from them.
The Kural's twofold duty, to present one's learning before the learned and then take from those who know more, matches the Jain monk who teaches yet keeps learning from his superiors.
Sikhism
“Let us form a partnership, and share our virtues; let us abandon our faults, and walk on the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 766, Raag Suhi, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak invites companions to pool their good qualities, each giving and receiving, and to leave their faults behind.
The Kural says to give what one has learned to the learned and take from those who know more; Guru Nanak's partnership of virtues is the same two-way exchange.
Greek Mythology
Telemachus tells Athena, who is disguised as Mentor, that he has no practice in careful speech and that a young man feels shy about questioning an elder. She answers that some things he will think of himself and a god will prompt the rest, and he goes to question old Nestor.
— Homer, Odyssey 3.14-28 [10]
A shy youth overcomes his fear in order to speak before, and learn from, a man far wiser than himself.
He speaks before the learned and draws out knowledge from the one who knows more, both halves of the Kural.
Philosophy
Told by the oracle that no one was wiser than he, Socrates went to politicians, poets and craftsmen who were reputed to be wise, and questioned them in order to test the claim and to learn from them.
— Plato, Apology 21b-22e [11]
Socrates’ method is to lay out questions before those with a reputation for learning in the hope of gaining from them.
He speaks before the learned and looks to learn more from them, even though he often found them lacking.

Real Life Example

Andrew Wiles announces, then repairs, his proof of Fermat's Last Theorem
23 June 1993 to 1994 · Isaac Newton Institute, Cambridge, England, and Princeton, United States
At the end of a series of three lectures at the Isaac Newton Institute in Cambridge on 23 June 1993, Andrew Wiles announced a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. Later in 1993 a referee checking the details found a serious gap. Wiles worked for about a year with his former student Richard Taylor to correct it, and the completed proof, with a companion paper by Taylor and Wiles, was published in the Annals of Mathematics in 1995.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Wiles put his work before the most expert audience available, accepted the flaw their scrutiny revealed, and drew on a colleague's help to complete it.
The kural asks the learned to set their knowledge before the learned and then take from others whatever lies beyond their own, which is the cycle Wiles went through between his 1993 lectures and the 1995 proof.
