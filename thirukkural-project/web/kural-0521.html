Kural 521

Cherishing One's Kindred · Suttrandhazhaal · சுற்றந்தழால்

பற்றற்ற கண்ணும் பழைமைபா ராட்டுதல் சுற்றத்தார் கண்ணே உள.
Patratra kannum pazhaimaipaa raattudhal Sutraththaar kanne ula.

Meaning

Even when someone has lost everything, it is only relatives who still honor the old bond they shared.

Elementary

When someone’s pockets are full, lots of people want to sit next to them at lunch. But if they lose everything, most of those people drift away. Your family is different: they still remember the good times and stay by your side. That is what makes family special.

University

The chapter title சுற்றம் தழால் means ‘embracing the circle of kin’; Parimelazhagar glosses it as the king holding his kinsmen close so that they never leave him, and he explains that it follows ‘Selection and Employment’ (Ch. 52) because, having spoken of those who do the king’s work, Valluvar now turns to the rest of his ‘surround’. In this opening couplet பற்று means wealth or support, so பற்றற்ற கண்ணும் is ‘even when all holdings are gone’; the concessive உம் implies that such a man is normally no longer honoured by anyone. Parimelazhagar reads பழைமை as the kindnesses the man once did for them in his days of plenty, and the emphatic ஏ in கண்ணே stresses that kin alone keep faith, since others cut ties the moment the wealth is gone. Varadarasan, Solomon Pappaiah and Karunanidhi all agree on the sense, and Karunanidhi turns it into a definition: those who show this loyalty are the true kin. Editions differ only in sandhi (உள / யுள) at the end of the second line.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Rama is stripped of his throne and sent into exile, his brother Bharata refuses the crown he has been handed, follows Rama into the forest to beg him to return, and then rules only as a regent, placing Rama’s sandals on the throne.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (Bharata’s visit to Chitrakuta and the sandals, sargas 100-115) [3]
Bharata stays loyal to Rama at the very moment when Rama has lost every worldly advantage, and he treats their old bond as more important than the kingdom.
This is the kind of kin the Kural describes, who honour the old tie even when the man has nothing left.
Christianity
“A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity.”
— Proverbs 17:17 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says that a brother shows his real worth when trouble comes.
Kin prove themselves in adversity, which is exactly the Kural’s point.
Islam
When the Quraysh boycotted the Prophet’s clan and pushed them into the valley of Abu Talib for about three years, his uncle Abu Talib and the Banu Hashim, most of whom did not share his faith, kept protecting him out of kinship and went hungry alongside him.
— Ibn Ishaq, Sirat Rasul Allah (trans. A. Guillaume, The Life of Muhammad), account of the boycott of Banu Hashim [5]
The clan stood by its kinsman when he had lost all outside support and was under economic siege.
This is kin keeping faith with one of their own when he has nothing, as in the Kural.
Judaism
“And Ruth said: ‘Entreat me not to leave thee, and to return from following after thee; for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge; thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God;”
— Ruth 1:16 (JPS 1917) [6]
Naomi has lost her husband, her sons and her livelihood, yet her daughter-in-law Ruth refuses to leave her.
Ruth keeps the family bond alive when Naomi is widowed and has nothing, just as the Kural expects of true kin.
Buddhism
“they do not forsake him in his troubles,”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha lists the ways friends and associates repay a clansman who has treated them well; one is that they stand by him in misfortune.
Those who have been cherished do not abandon the man when trouble comes, which matches the Kural’s kin.
Jainism
“We were brothers once, kind to each other, loving each other, wishing well to each other.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13.5 (see also 13.15) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The universal monarch Brahmadatta meets the monk Kitra, his brother in five earlier births, and honours the old bond even though Kitra now owns nothing; Kitra in turn, 'for old friendship's sake', loves the king and counsels him.
Like the kin in the Kural who keep the old bond with a man who has lost everything, the king does not let Kitra's poverty as a monk cancel their ancient tie.
Sikhism
“They alone are my friends, who travel along with me; and in that place, where the accounts are called for, they appear standing with me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a Soohee hymn Guru Nanak defines a true friend (sajan) as one who stays at your side to the very end, even at the final reckoning when everything else has fallen away.
The Kural says only kin keep faith with a man who has lost everything; Guru Nanak likewise measures a real bond by whether it still holds when nothing else remains.
Greek Mythology
After Polyneices dies attacking Thebes and is refused burial as a traitor, his sister Antigone buries him anyway and accepts death rather than abandon her disgraced brother.
— Sophocles, Antigone [10]
Antigone honours her kinship with a brother the whole city has rejected.
Only his kin stand by him once he has lost everything, as the Kural observes.
Philosophy
“And in poverty and in other misfortunes men think friends are the only refuge.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says that philia (the love of friends and family) matters most when fortune turns against us.
The Kural gives this refuge specifically to kin, whose attachment outlasts a man’s wealth.

Real Life Example

Napoleon's mother and sister join him in exile on Elba
1814 · Portoferraio, Elba, Italy
After his first abdication in 1814, Napoleon was sent to the small island of Elba and landed at its capital, Portoferraio, on 4 May 1814. His mother, Letizia Bonaparte, and his sister Pauline came to join him there, his mother living in a house in Portoferraio, while his wife Marie-Louise and their young son, for whom rooms had been prepared, never came. In 1818 his mother wrote to the Allied powers asking for his release from his final exile on Saint Helena, but her letter went unanswered.
— Sources: [12] [13]
When the emperor had lost his throne and the court that once surrounded him, it was his mother and sister who crossed the sea to share his exile, and his mother kept pleading for him long after his final defeat.
The kural says that even when a man has lost everything he owned, his kin alone keep honouring the old bond, and Letizia Bonaparte did exactly that for her fallen son.
