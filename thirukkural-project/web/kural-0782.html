Kural 782

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

நிறைநீர நீரவர் கேண்மை பிறைமதிப் பின்னீர பேதையார் நட்பு.
Niraineera neeravar kenmai piraimadhip pinneera pedhaiyaar natpu.

Meaning

Friendship with good people grows like the moon getting bigger each night. Friendship with fools shrinks like the moon after it is full.

Elementary

Think of the moon. Some nights it starts as a thin smile and gets bigger and brighter every night. Other times it starts big and round and shrinks a little every night. Good friends are like the growing moon: each week you know them, the friendship gets fuller. Silly friends are like the shrinking moon: exciting at first, then less and less.

University

The couplet is one of the Kural’s best-known lunar similes. Niraineera (நிறைநீர) means ‘of the nature of filling up’, that is, the waxing crescent (piRai, பிறை) that grows to fullness; piRaimadhip pinneera (பிறைமதிப் பின்னீர) means ‘of the nature of the moon after fullness’, the waning phase. Neeravar (நீரவர்) are ‘people of good nature’ (from நீர்மை, quality), and Parimelazhagar glosses them as the wise and good, set against pēdhaiyār (பேதையார், fools). Kēṇmai and natpu are near-synonyms for friendship, used for variety. Parimelazhagar notes that the first kind of friendship starts small and grows by the day, while the second starts at its fullest and then declines. The text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A widely quoted verse of Bhartrhari’s Nitishataka compares the friendship of the wicked to the morning shadow, long at first and shrinking as the day goes on, and the friendship of the good to the afternoon shadow, short at first and lengthening steadily.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka (verse on the two shadows; numbering varies by edition) [3]
Bhartrhari uses the changing length of a shadow to show that bad friendships start large and fade while good ones start small and grow.
This is the same image as the Kural’s, with the shadow in place of the moon.
Christianity
“A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity.”
— Proverbs 17:17 (KJV) [4]
The proverb defines a friend by constancy: love that does not rise and fall with circumstances.
The Kural’s waxing friendship of the worthy is the same constant, ever-growing love the proverb praises.
Islam
“Close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day, except the righteous.”
— Qur’an 43:67 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
On the Day of Judgement, friendships not rooted in righteousness turn into enmity, while those of the God-conscious endure.
Like the Kural’s waning moon, friendships built on folly decline into nothing, while those of the good last.
Judaism
“For there is a friend that is for a season; And that will not (?) continue in the day of affliction.”
— Ben Sira 6:8 (The Wisdom of Ben Sira, Cambridge, 1899) [6]
Ben Sira warns that some friendships last only for a season and then disappear when trouble comes.
The ‘friend for a season’ is the Kural’s waning-moon friendship of fools.
Buddhism
“Whoever through desire, hate or fear, Or ignorance should transgress the Dhamma, All his glory fades away Like the moon during the waning half. Whoever through desire, hate or fear, Or ignorance never transgresses the Dhamma, All his glory ever increases Like the moon during the waxing half.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha tells Sigala that the wrongdoer’s glory wanes like the moon while the good man’s grows like it.
The Buddha uses the same waxing and waning moon to separate the wise from the foolish, and the same sutta later uses it of those who keep low company.
Jainism
“Friendship with the wise, whose intelligence divines our thoughts, is like eating a sugar-cane from the top (as its sweetness increases more and more); connexion with persons without sweetness of disposition is like eating it from the opposite end (the flavour decreasing by degrees).”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 1 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil quatrain contrasts a friendship with the wise, which grows sweeter over time, with one with ill-natured people, which loses its sweetness bit by bit.
This is the same contrast the Kural draws between the waxing and waning moon: friendship with the good keeps growing, and friendship with fools keeps shrinking.
Sikhism
“Friendship with a fool, and love with a pompous person, are like lines drawn in water, leaving no trace or mark.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad's saloks in Asa di Vaar warn that friendship with a fool never works out right and leaves nothing lasting behind.
The Kural says friendship with fools wanes like the moon after the full; Guru Angad says it vanishes like a line drawn in water.
Greek Mythology
Pirithous raided Theseus’ cattle to test him; when the two met armed, each so admired the other that they swore friendship instead of fighting, and their bond grew into one of the most famous in myth.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 30 [10]
Their friendship began with almost nothing, a near-fight, and grew over a lifetime of shared adventures.
This is the crescent that becomes full: a small beginning that keeps growing.
Philosophy
“they cannot know each other till they have ‘eaten salt together’ … for a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says true friendship needs time and shared experience, unlike the quick wish to be friends.
Aristotle’s slow-growing friendship of the good is the Kural’s waxing crescent.

Real Life Example

Einstein and Michele Besso: from sounding board to collaborator
1905-1914 · Bern and Zurich, Switzerland
Albert Einstein's 1905 paper on special relativity famously acknowledged the help of his friend and colleague Michele Besso, who served as a sounding board for his ideas. In the following years the friendship grew into active collaboration: between June 1913 and early 1914 the two men worked together on a 54-page manuscript of calculations on the motion of Mercury's perihelion, written partly in Einstein's hand and partly in Besso's. In the same period Besso also acted as a go-between in Einstein's difficult family affairs.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Their relationship shows a friendship that kept deepening over the years, from conversation partner to working collaborator and family confidant.
The kural compares friendship with the worthy to the crescent moon that grows fuller night by night, and Besso's friendship with Einstein grew steadily in depth.
