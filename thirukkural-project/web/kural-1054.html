Kural 1054

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

இரத்தலும் ஈதலே போலும் கரத்தல் கனவிலும் தேற்றாதார் மாட்டு.
Iraththalum eedhale polum karaththal kanavilum thetraadhaar maattu.

Meaning

Asking people who would never hold back, not even in a dream, is as worthy of respect as giving.

Elementary

Some people love sharing so much that when you ask them, it's like you're doing them a favour. With friends like that, asking feels almost as good as giving.

University

The line iraththalum eedhale polum (இரத்தலும் ஈதலே போலும், 'begging too is just like giving') is one of the boldest claims in the Kural, and the emphatic ē in eedhale strengthens the equation. Its condition is that the giver karaththal kanavilum thetraadhaar (கரத்தல் கனவிலும் தேற்றாதார், 'does not know concealment even in a dream'). Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read it as conferring the same dignity (சிறப்பு, பெருமை) on such asking as on giving. The commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar explains the equation by the giver's joy: asking such a person gives him the chance to give. Pope: 'To beg of such ... is in fact the same as giving.' The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Vamana episode, Vishnu takes the form of a dwarf brahmin and asks King Bali for three paces of land. Bali gives without holding back, even after his preceptor warns him, and his giving earns him lasting glory and divine favour.
— Bhagavata Purana 8.18-22 (Vamana and Bali) (paraphrase) [3]
When God himself becomes the asker, the request turns into an honour and a blessing for the one who gives.
The story makes the Kural's paradox literal: asking a giver who never withholds is a gift to that giver.
Christianity
The prophet Elijah asks a starving widow of Zarephath for water and a little bread, made from her last flour and oil. She gives, and her barrel of meal and cruse of oil do not run out for the rest of the famine.
— 1 Kings 17:10-16 (KJV) (paraphrase) [4]
The prophet's request, which looked like a burden on the widow, turned out to be a blessing for her.
Asking a giver who does not hold back becomes, as the Kural says, like giving.
Islam
“[Saying], "We feed you only for the face [i.e., approval] of Allāh. We wish not from you reward or gratitude.”
— Qur'an 76:9 (Sahih International) [5]
The righteous feed the needy, the orphan and the captive, and tell them that they want no reward or even thanks.
When a giver wants nothing back, the receiver owes nothing and is not lowered, so asking such a person costs no honour.
Judaism
“More than what the homeowner does for the poor person, the poor person does for the homeowner”
— Vayikra Rabbah 34:8 (The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah, 2022, tr. Joshua Schreier) [6]
Rabbi Yehoshua reads Ruth's words about Boaz, 'for whom I acted today', as showing that by accepting his bread she did him a greater favour, giving him the merit of charity.
The midrash says almost exactly what the Kural says: the one who receives also gives.
Buddhism
“Monks, brahmans & householders are very helpful to you, as they provide you with the requisites of robes, alms food, lodgings, & medical requisites for the sick. And you, monks, are very helpful to brahmans & householders, as you teach them the Dhamma”
— Itivuttaka 107 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha describes a two-way exchange: lay people give material support, and the monks who receive it give the Dhamma in return.
The almsman who asks is also a giver, which is the Kural's equation of asking with giving.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra defines dana (giving) as parting with one's own things for anugraha, the benefit of both oneself and the other, and teaches that its fruit depends on the manner, the thing given, the giver and the recipient.
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.38-39 (Svetambara numbering; paraphrase) [8]
In Jain doctrine a gift benefits the giver as much as the receiver, through the merit and the loosening of attachment it brings.
If giving benefits both sides equally, then asking and giving share in the same good, which is the Kural's claim.
Sikhism
“The Great Giver does not hold back anything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib (Pauri 25), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak says God's gifts are beyond reckoning and the Giver keeps back not even a sesame seed's worth.
The Kural says asking is as honourable as giving when the giver would never think of withholding, even in a dream; Guru Nanak describes the Great Giver as exactly such a one, who holds nothing back.
Greek Mythology
“I too was a rich man once, and had a fine house of my own; in those days I gave to many a tramp such as I now am, no matter who he might be nor what he wanted.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
The disguised Odysseus tells Antinous that he was once a rich giver to every tramp, and now stands asking himself.
Asker and giver are the same man at different times, which hints at the Kural's equation of the two.
Philosophy
“Being asked why people give to beggars but not to philosophers, he said, ‘Because they think they may one day be lame or blind, but never expect that they will turn to philosophy.’”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.56 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
Diogenes says that people give to beggars because they can picture themselves in the beggar's place.
His answer touches the Kural's insight that giver and asker are not so far apart.

Real Life Example

Abbe Pierre's winter appeal sets off an uprising of kindness
1 February 1954 · Paris, France
During an exceptionally harsh winter, after learning that a woman evicted from her home had died of cold in a Paris street, the priest Abbe Pierre wrote an appeal for the homeless that was broadcast on Paris-Inter and Radio Luxembourg on 1 February 1954, asking for blankets, tents and stoves. The public response was so great that the press called it an uprising of kindness; within days the government released funds for emergency housing, and the appeal led to the rapid growth of the Emmaus movement.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single request on behalf of the destitute was answered so generously that the act of asking became the occasion for an entire nation's giving.
The kural says that asking of those who would never withhold, even in a dream, is as honourable as giving, and Abbe Pierre's appeal to a generous public was itself remembered as an act of great charity.
