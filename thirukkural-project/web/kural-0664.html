Kural 664

Power in Execution · Vinaithitpam · வினைத்திட்பம்

சொல்லுதல் யார்க்கும் எளிய அரியவாம் சொல்லிய வண்ணம் செயல்.
Solludhal yaarkkum eLiya ariyavaam Solliya vaNNam seyal.

Meaning

Anyone can talk about how to do something. The hard part is doing it exactly the way it was said.

Elementary

It is easy to say, “I will clean my whole room today!” It is much harder to actually pick up every sock and toy. Anyone can make a promise; the strong person is the one who does what they said.

University

The couplet balances eḷiya (easy) against ariya (hard, rare), and solludal (saying) against solliya vaṇṇam seyal (acting in the manner that was said). Parimelazhagar takes solludal as stating how a task ought to be carried out, the counsel a minister gives, so the couplet sets planning and advice against execution; Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan follow him, while Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar widen it to any promise or declaration. The plural eḷiya / ariya (literally ‘easy things’, ‘hard things’) is idiomatic and is normally rendered in the singular. In the chapter’s argument the couplet explains why firmness of mind is needed at all: plans are cheap, and only a firm mind turns word into deed. All three sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The mind is restless, turbulent, obstinate and very strong, O Kṛṣṇa, and to subdue it, I think, is more difficult than controlling the wind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.34 (Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [4]
After hearing Krishna’s teaching on meditation, Arjuna objects that it is easily described but extremely hard to put into practice.
Arjuna voices the Kural’s point: stating the method is easy, carrying it out is hard.
Christianity
“All therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works: for they say, and do not.”
— Matthew 23:3 (KJV) [5]
Jesus criticises teachers whose instructions are sound but who do not practise them themselves.
‘They say, and do not’ is the failing the Kural has in view.
Islam
“O you who have believed, why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do.”
— Quran 61:2-3 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran rebukes believers who announce intentions or commitments and then fail to act on them.
It condemns the very gap between saying and doing that the Kural calls the hard part.
Judaism
“Shammai used to say: make your [study of the] Torah a fixed practice; speak little, but do much...”
— Pirkei Avot 1:15 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [7]
Shammai tells students to promise little and accomplish much, putting deeds ahead of words.
A practical answer to the Kural’s observation that talk is easy and deeds are hard.
Buddhism
“Like a beautiful flower full of color but without fragrance, even so, fruitless are the fair words of one who does not practice them.”
— Dhammapada 51 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
Fine words not put into practice are compared to a flower that looks lovely but has no scent.
Both texts value the doing over the saying.
Jainism
“Acknowledging the truth about bondage and liberation, but talking only, not acting (in accordance with these tenets), they seek comfort for themselves in mighty words. Clever talking will not work salvation”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.9-10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira criticises those who can expound the doctrine of bondage and liberation but do not live by it, trusting in words instead of deeds.
The kural's point that saying how a thing should be done is easy while doing it as said is hard is exactly the gap this verse condemns.
Sikhism
“We are good at talking, but our actions are bad.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 85, Siree Raag (Siree Raag ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak admits the gap between fine words and actual conduct as a common human failing.
The Kural says anyone can say how a thing should be done, but doing it as said is hard; Guru Nanak names exactly that gap between speaking well and acting well.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the Boasting Traveler, a man brags of a giant leap he made at Rhodes and offers witnesses; a bystander tells him to suppose this is Rhodes and leap for them.
— Aesop’s Fables, The Boasting Traveler (trans. G. F. Townsend) [11]
The fable punctures boasting by demanding the deed itself, here and now.
The bystander’s challenge makes the Kural’s point: claiming is easy, doing is the test.
Philosophy
“Never call yourself a philosopher, nor talk a great deal among the unlearned about theorems, but act conformably to them.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 46 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [12]
Epictetus tells his students to show their philosophy by how they live, not by talking about it.
Like the Kural, he treats acting in line with one’s words as the real, and harder, achievement.

Real Life Example

Kennedy's Moon goal is carried out as stated
25 May 1961 - 20 July 1969 · Washington, D.C., and the Moon
On 25 May 1961 President John F. Kennedy asked a joint session of Congress to commit the United States to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the end of the decade. The goal required a huge national effort in funding, engineering and testing through the Mercury, Gemini and Apollo programmes. On 20 July 1969 Apollo 11 landed on the Moon, and its crew returned safely to Earth, within the deadline Kennedy had set.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Announcing the goal took one speech; meeting it exactly as stated took eight years of sustained work by hundreds of thousands of people.
The kural says anyone can say how a thing should be done but doing it exactly as it was said is hard, and the Moon landing is a rare case of a public declaration fulfilled to the letter.
