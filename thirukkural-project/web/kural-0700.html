Kural 700

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

பழையம் எனக்கருதிப் பண்பல்ல செய்யும் கெழுதகைமை கேடு தரும்.
Pazhaiyam enak karudhip panpalla seyyum Kezhudhakaimai kedu tharum.

Meaning

Being too relaxed because 'we are old friends of the king,' and then behaving badly, will lead a person to ruin.

Elementary

Suppose your best friend from kindergarten becomes the class president. If you start bossing them around or ignoring the rules because ‘we go way back’, the friendship and your place will both suffer. Old friendship is precious, but it is not permission to misbehave.

University

Pazhaiyam means ‘we are old [associates]’, and panpalla are ‘things that are not proper conduct’. The subject of the sentence is kezhudhakaimai, the intimate familiarity, or the privilege claimed through intimacy, that leads to such conduct, and it is this that tharum (brings) kedu (ruin). The word matters because chapter 81 (Pazhaimai, ‘Old Friendship’) uses kezhudhakaimai approvingly for the easy freedom old friends allow each other. Valluvar deliberately shows that the same freedom, turned toward a king, becomes fatal. The chapter thus ends where it began: the right distance from the fire is not closed by long acquaintance. The older text reads “பழைய மெனக்கருதிப் பண்பல்ல செய்யுங் / கெழுதகைமை கேடு தரும்.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Drona, now poor, goes to King Drupada and greets him as a boyhood friend, reminding him of the promises they made as students. Drupada scornfully replies that friendship between a king and a poor man cannot last, and the insulted Drona later takes revenge.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Sambhava sub-parva (Drona and Drupada), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
In the Adi Parva, Drona treats old intimacy as a claim on a reigning king, and the encounter ends in humiliation and lasting enmity.
This is the Kural’s pazhaiyam, ‘we are old friends’, presumed upon before a throne, with ruin following for both men.
Christianity
“A prophet is not without honour, but in his own country, and among his own kin, and in his own house.”
— Mark 6:4 (KJV) [4]
In his home town, people who have known Jesus since childhood dismiss him because of their familiarity with his family.
The same force the Kural warns about is at work: long familiarity erodes proper reverence.
Islam
“Do not make [your] calling of the Messenger among yourselves as the call of one of you to another.”
— Quran 24:63 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran tells the believers, many of them the Prophet’s long-time companions, not to address him as casually as they address one another.
Long companionship does not license casual treatment of the one in authority, which is the Kural’s warning against kezhudhakaimai.
Judaism
“Withdraw thy foot from thy neighbour’s house; lest he be weary of thee, and so hate thee.”
— Proverbs 25:17 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that visiting too often turns welcome into weariness and even hatred.
Both texts teach that intimacy pushed too far destroys the relationship it relies on.
Buddhism
Channa, the Buddha’s charioteer from his days as a prince, grows arrogant because of that old bond and treats other monks with contempt. Before his death the Buddha orders the ‘higher penalty’ (brahmadanda): the monks are simply not to speak to him. Channa is shaken into humility.
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahaparinibbana Sutta 6.4; background in Vinaya commentarial tradition [6]
Channa’s pride rests on his old closeness to the Buddha, and it earns him a formal penalty from the Buddha himself.
Channa is the Buddhist model of the Kural’s pazhaiyam enak karudhi: presuming on old intimacy, followed by consequences.
Jainism
Gosala travelled with Mahavira for six years as a self-declared pupil. He later broke away, proclaimed himself a Tirthankara, and picked a quarrel with his former teacher. He hurled his magic fire (tejolesya) at Mahavira, but it turned back on him, and he died after repenting.
— Bhagavati Sutra (Vyakhyaprajnapti), Shataka 15, as summarised by T.U. Mehta, The Path of Arhat, 'Association with Gosala' (jainworld.com) [7]
In Jain tradition Gosala's long familiarity with Mahavira did not breed reverence. It led to arrogance and open defiance, and that defiance brought about his own ruin.
The Kural warns that the intimacy of 'we are old friends of the king' leads people into improper conduct and destroys them; Gosala's story shows an old companion presuming on the past, with the same result.
Sikhism
According to Sikh tradition, Guru Angad's son Datu, resenting that Amar Das had been made Guru, came to Goindwal, taunted him that only yesterday he had been a water-carrier in their house, and kicked him from his seat; Amar Das humbly asked whether he had hurt his foot, withdrew for a time, and Datu, finding no following, went back to Khadur.
— Wikipedia, Guru Amar Das (citing Sikh tradition) [8]
Datu relied on his old family standing and familiarity with the new Guru to act with contempt, and lost all standing as a result.
The Kural warns that the familiarity which says 'we are old acquaintances' and then behaves improperly brings ruin; Datu's appeal to old household ties to justify his insult is exactly that, and it left him without followers.
Greek Mythology
Tantalus, a mortal favoured to dine at the gods’ own table, abuses that intimacy by stealing their nectar and ambrosia for his mortal friends. His punishment is eternal hunger and thirst, with food and water always just out of reach.
— Pindar, Olympian Ode 1.54-64 (Tantalus) [9]
Pindar says Tantalus could not digest his great good fortune and turned the gods’ closeness into offence.
Tantalus turned intimacy with the highest powers into improper liberties, which is exactly the kezhudhakaimai that brings ruin.
Philosophy
“… who with his own hand while at table stabbed Clitus, his dearest friend, who had been brought up with him, because he did not flatter him enough, and was too slow in transforming himself from a free man and a Macedonian into a Persian slave.”
— Seneca, De Ira (Of Anger), III.17 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [10]
Seneca recalls how Alexander killed Clitus, a friend he had grown up with, at a banquet for speaking too freely, and how he caged Lysimachus, another close friend, with a lion.
Clitus' fate shows the Kural's warning in action: an old intimacy with a king did not protect the friend who took liberties in his presence, and it led to his ruin.

Real Life Example

The fall of Ibrahim Pasha, Suleyman the Magnificent's boyhood companion
1536 · Constantinople (now Istanbul, Turkey)
Ibrahim, a slave of Christian birth from near Parga, became the inseparable companion of the future Sultan Suleyman before his accession, and in 1523 Suleyman raised him over more senior officials to be grand vizier, later marrying a sister to him. For thirteen years he ran the empire's affairs and led campaigns in Hungary and Persia, but he was unpopular with the Ottoman elite for his reputation for arrogance and was reported to have boasted publicly of his power over the sultan's decisions. Alarmed by his usurpation of the titles of sovereignty, Suleyman had him put to death in March 1536.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The closeness of a lifelong friendship gave Ibrahim power, but it also led him to behave as an equal of the sultan, and that familiarity ended in his ruin.
The kural warns that the familiarity which makes a man act improperly because he is an old friend of the king will bring him to ruin, and Ibrahim's story fits it closely.
