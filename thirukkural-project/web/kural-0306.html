Kural 306

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

சினமென்னும் சேர்ந்தாரைக் கொல்லி இனமென்னும் ஏமப் புணையைச் சுடும்.
Sinamennum serndhaaraik kolli inamennum Emap punaiyaich sudum.

Meaning

Anger is a fire that kills anyone who comes near it. It even burns up your family and friends, who are like a raft that carries you safely through life.

Elementary

Think of your family and friends as a raft that keeps you floating in a big river. Anger is like a fire on that raft. If you let it burn, it destroys the very thing that keeps you safe.

University

The couplet is built on a compound metaphor. Sinam is called serndhaaraik kolli, 'the killer of those who come close', a traditional kenning for fire, which destroys whatever touches it. Inam, one's kin and circle of friends, is called an emap punai, a 'safe raft' that carries one across the dangerous sea of life. Anger, being fire, burns the very raft that was meant to save its owner: it isolates the angry person by consuming the relationships that sustain them. Valluvar thus extends the self-harm argument of 305 to its social dimension.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After setting Lanka ablaze in fury, Hanuman is struck with horror that his fire may have burned Sita herself; he reproaches his own anger and praises those great souls who, by their wisdom, quench anger as it rises, as fire is put out with water.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, sarga 55 [3]
Hanuman's rage, turned into literal fire, threatens the very person he came to save.
Almost exactly the kural's image: anger is a fire that burns what we hold dear, the raft that was meant to save us.
Christianity
“But if ye bite and devour one another, take heed that ye be not consumed one of another.”
— Galatians 5:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns a quarrelling community that mutual hostility will consume them all.
Like the kural, anger among companions destroys the community that sustains each member.
Islam
“Anger comes from the devil, the devil was created of fire, and fire is extinguished only with water; so when one of you becomes angry, he should perform ablution.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4784 (graded da'if by al-Albani) [5]
This narration describes anger as a fire and prescribes water (ablution) to put it out.
Shares the kural's core metaphor of anger as a consuming fire.
Judaism
“Cursed be their anger, for it was fierce, And their wrath, for it was cruel; I will divide them in Jacob, And scatter them in Israel.”
— Genesis 49:7 (JPS 1917) [6]
On his deathbed Jacob condemns the violent anger of his sons Simeon and Levi and foretells that their tribes will be scattered.
The anger of Simeon and Levi ends up breaking apart their own tribes: the kural's burning of the 'raft of kin'.
Buddhism
“Now, when a person is angry — overcome with anger, oppressed with anger — his friends, companions, & relatives will avoid him from afar, all because he is overcome with anger.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.60, Kodhana Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Among the seven things an enemy wishes on an enemy is friendlessness, and anger brings it about.
Direct parallel: anger drives away the kin and friends who form one's safe raft.
Jainism
“Anger destroys love, pride destroys humility, deceit or cheating destroys friends; but greed destroys all.”
— Daśavaikālika Sūtra 8.38 (English rendering as given on tattvagyan.com, 'Greed destroys all') [8]
The Daśavaikālika lists what each passion ruins and gives anger as the destroyer of love (prīti) between people.
Valluvar's anger burns up 'the raft of kinsmen and friends'. The Jain verse names the same casualty: the bonds of affection that hold a person's circle together.
Sikhism
“The fire of anger is the outcaste who burns dead bodies at the crematorium.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Siree Raag Guru Nanak lists the inner outcastes that make a person impure. He names anger as a fire that burns as a cremation fire does.
The Kural calls anger a fire that kills whoever comes near and burns up the raft of kin and friends. Guru Nanak shares the image of anger as a consuming, death-dealing fire.
Greek Mythology
Consumed by rage at Jason's betrayal, Medea kills their own children to wound him, destroying her family and her own future.
— Euripides, Medea [10]
Euripides' Medea is the Greek archetype of anger turning against the angry person's own kin.
Medea's fury burns the very family that bound her life together, as the kural's fire burns the raft of kin.
Philosophy
“Such people are most troublesome to themselves and to their dearest friends.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle, describing the sulky temperament that nurses anger for a long time, notes that it torments both the person and those closest to him.
Like the kural, Aristotle sees lasting anger damaging one's closest relationships as well as oneself.

Real Life Example

William Bligh is cast adrift after the mutiny on the Bounty
28 April 1789 · Near Tofua, Tonga, South Pacific
Lieutenant William Bligh, commander of HMS Bounty, had become enraged at his crew's seamanship and had fallen out with his first mate and longtime friend Fletcher Christian. On 28 April 1789 Christian and his supporters seized the ship and set Bligh and 18 loyal men adrift in a small open launch. Bligh navigated about 3,600 miles to Timor, but historians note that while he used physical punishment less than other captains, he was verbally and personally abusive, and later he faced a court-martial (and acquittal) for abusive language.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bligh's skill and courage were never in doubt, but his abusive temper turned his own officers and crew, the people who should have carried him safely home, against him.
The kural says anger is a fire that burns even the raft of kinsmen and friends that would carry a person through life, and Bligh's anger literally cost him his ship and left him adrift in an open boat.
