Kural 229

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

இரத்தலின் இன்னாது மன்ற நிரப்பிய தாமே தமியர் உணல்.
Iraththalin innaadhu mandra nirappiya Thaame thamiyar unal.

Meaning

Eating alone, without sharing, just to keep your shelves full is surely worse than begging.

Elementary

Begging for food feels bad. But this kural says something feels even worse: sitting alone with a big pile of food and never sharing any of it. Being lonely and greedy is sadder than being poor.

University

The comparison is deliberately shocking. In Chapter 106 (இரவு) and elsewhere, begging is treated as the lowest and most painful condition, yet here Valluvar says solitary eating is more இன்னாது (painful, wretched) than begging, and seals it with மன்ற, ‘certainly’. நிரப்பிய is read by Parimelazhagar as ‘in order to fill up’ one’s wealth, so the target is not eating as such but the miser who refuses to share lest his stores shrink. தமியர் உணல் (eating alone) stands for a whole social failure; the Rig Veda makes the same point when it says the man who eats without a companion is ‘all guilt’, and Epicurus says a meal without a friend is the life of a lion or wolf.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The foolish man wins food with fruitless labour: that food -I speak the truth- shall be his ruin. He feeds no trusty friend, no man to love him. All guilt is he who eats with no partaker.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.6 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Rig Vedic hymn to Liberality condemns the man who eats alone without sharing, saying he is nothing but sin.
A near-exact parallel to the Kural’s condemnation of solitary eating (see also Gita 3.13: those who cook only for themselves ‘eat only sin’).
Christianity
“There was a certain rich man, which was clothed in purple and fine linen, and fared sumptuously every day: And there was a certain beggar named Lazarus, which was laid at his gate, full of sores, And desiring to be fed with the crumbs which fell from the rich man’s table.”
— Luke 16:19-21 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the Rich Man and Lazarus, the rich man feasts alone while a beggar starves at his gate; after death their fates are reversed.
Like the Kural, the parable judges the solitary feaster’s state to be worse than the beggar’s.
Islam
“Have you seen the one who denies the Recompense? For that is the one who drives away the orphan And does not encourage the feeding of the poor.”
— Qur'an 107:1-3 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an links refusing to feed the poor with denying the Day of Judgement.
Like the Kural, it treats refusal to share food as a serious moral failure.
Judaism
“There are four types of character in human beings: One that says: ‘mine is mine, and yours is yours’: this is a commonplace type; and some say this is a sodom-type of character.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 5:10 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
The Mishnah says the attitude ‘what’s mine is mine’ is, according to some, the character of Sodom, the city proverbial for refusing hospitality.
The solitary eater of the Kural is exactly this ‘mine is mine’ person, judged harshly.
Buddhism
“If beings knew, as I know, the results of giving & sharing, they would not eat without having given, nor would the stain of selfishness overcome their minds. Even if it were their last bite, their last mouthful, they would not eat without having shared, if there were someone to receive their gift.”
— Itivuttaka 26 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha says that anyone who understood the fruits of sharing would never eat without first giving.
Directly parallels the Kural’s condemnation of eating alone and unshared.
Jainism
“To give a part of their meal to the extent of their ability, both to the friendly and unfriendly, and after that to eat, is truly to eat a meal. To those who refuse their food to the needy, and so live and eat and pass on, the door of heaven will be closed.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 28 ('Illiberality'), st. 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār's chapter on illiberality says that only a meal shared first with others, whether friend or not, truly counts as eating. Those who eat without sharing have heaven closed to them.
Valluvar calls solitary eating, done to keep one's stores full, more wretched than begging; the Nālaṭiyār likewise denies that unshared eating is a real meal at all.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, those houses in which neither the Holy nor the Lord are served - those houses are like cremation grounds; demons dwell within them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1374, Salok Bhagat Kabir 192 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says that a household which serves neither holy people nor God is as desolate as a cremation ground.
The Kural calls eating alone to keep one's stores full more wretched than begging; Kabir likewise judges the closed, unsharing household as worse than poor, a place of death.
Greek Mythology
King Midas asks Dionysus that everything he touches become gold. His wish is granted, but his bread and wine turn to gold as he touches them, and he sits alone amid his treasure, starving, until he begs the god to take the gift back.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 [10]
Midas’ greed for gold leaves him hungry and isolated in the middle of great riches.
Illustrates the Kural’s point that hoarding for oneself can be more wretched than poverty.
Philosophy
“You must reflect carefully beforehand with whom you are to eat and drink, rather than what you are to eat and drink. For a dinner of meats without the company of a friend is like the life of a lion or a wolf.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 19.10, quoting Epicurus (tr. R. M. Gummere) [11]
Seneca quotes Epicurus, who held that eating without companions reduces a human being to the level of a solitary predator.
Like the Kural, it treats solitary eating as a degradation of human life.

Real Life Example

Spartan citizens share their meals in the common messes
Classical period, c. 6th-4th century BCE · Sparta, Greece
Adult male citizens of Sparta were required to belong to a common mess, the syssitia, of about fifteen men, where they ate together daily. Each member had to contribute a fixed amount of barley, cheese, figs, wine and other food, and a citizen who could not or would not make his contribution was expelled and lost his full civic rights.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Sparta made shared eating a condition of belonging to the community, treating a man outside the common table as no longer a full citizen.
The kural calls eating alone to keep one's stores full more wretched than begging, and Sparta built its whole citizen life around the shared table.
