Kural 1323

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

புலத்தலின் புத்தேள்நாடு உண்டோ நிலத்தொடு நீரியைந் தன்னார் அகத்து.
Pulaththalin puththelnaadu undoa nilaththodu Neeriyainh thannaar akaththu.

Meaning

Our hearts are mixed together like water and soil. With such a lover, is there any heaven as sweet as a little sulking?

Elementary

When rain soaks into soil, you cannot pull the water back out; the two become one. When two people are that close, even a pretend grumble between them feels happy, because nothing can really pull them apart.

University

Pulattal (‘sulking’, a synonym of ūṭal) is weighed against puttēḷ nāṭu (‘the land of the gods’, heaven), and the rhetorical uṇṭō (‘is there?’) denies that heaven could be sweeter. The simile nilattoṭu nīr iyaintu aṉṉār (‘those who are like water joined with earth’) recalls the famous Kuṟuntokai 40 image of hearts mingled like red earth and rain (cempulap peyalnīr), signalling an inseparable union in which a quarrel holds no danger. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan all read the line as ‘is the gods’ world sweeter than sulking with such a lover?’, while Kalaignar renders puttēḷ nāṭu more freely as ‘a new world’. The text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (ytamizh prints the sandhi form nīriyain tannār).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa opens the Raghuvamsha by saluting Parvati and Shiva, the parents of the world, as joined together like a word and its meaning.
— Kalidasa, Raghuvamsha 1.1 (paraphrase) [3]
The image describes a divine couple so united that one cannot exist apart from the other.
Kalidasa’s word-and-meaning union is the Sanskrit counterpart of the Kural’s earth-and-water couple.
Christianity
“Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.”
— Genesis 2:24 (KJV) [4]
Genesis, as Jesus repeats it, describes marriage as two becoming one.
The Kural’s couple, blended like earth and water, are ‘one flesh’ in just this sense, which is why their quarrel is safe.
Islam
“They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them.”
— Qur’an 2:187 (Saheeh International; excerpt) [5]
The Qur’an pictures husband and wife as garments to each other, giving closeness, comfort and covering.
Like the Kural’s water soaked into earth, the garment image describes spouses who are wrapped into one another.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that when a man and a woman are worthy, the Divine Presence dwells between them, but when they are not, fire consumes them.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 17a (paraphrase) [6]
A loving marriage is itself a dwelling place for God’s presence.
The Kural says no heaven surpasses a loving couple’s bond; the Talmud says such a bond brings heaven down between them.
Buddhism
When the Buddha’s half-brother Nanda pined for the beautiful Sakyan girl he had left behind, the Buddha took him to a heaven to see celestial nymphs, and Nanda admitted that beside them his earthly beloved looked like a burnt monkey.
— Udana 3.2 (Nanda Sutta; paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha uses heavenly beauty to loosen Nanda’s attachment, as a step toward letting go of desire altogether.
The Kural reverses this: for its lover, no heavenly world could outshine the joy of a quarrel with the one he loves.
Jainism
“After having enjoyed, in the company of the beautiful ladies of his seraglio, excellent pleasures which match those of the heavens, king Nami became enlightened and gave up his pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain text grants that household love can feel heavenly, then holds up giving it up as the higher path.
The Kural ranks a lover’s quarrel above heaven, while Jain scripture sets even heaven-like pleasures aside for liberation.
Sikhism
“I do not seek power, and I do not seek liberation. My mind is in love with Your Lotus Feet.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 534, Raag Dayv Gandhaaree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says he wants neither worldly power nor even liberation, only loving closeness to the Lord.
The Kural asks whether any heaven could match the sweetness of loving one whose heart is blended with one's own; Gurbani likewise prizes love for the Beloved above every heaven or reward.
Greek Mythology
“As the sight of land is welcome to men who are swimming towards the shore, when Neptune has wrecked their ship with the fury of his winds and waves- a few alone reach the land, and these, covered with brine, are thankful when they find themselves on firm ground and out of danger- even so was her husband welcome to her as she looked upon him, and she could not tear her two fair arms from about his neck.”
— Homer, Odyssey 23 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
When Penelope’s long testing of Odysseus ends, Homer compares their reunion to swimmers reaching land after shipwreck.
Homer’s sea reaching land, after a reunion delayed by the wife’s caution, echoes the Kural’s water joined with earth.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes says that human beings were once whole and were split in two, and that love is the desire and pursuit of that lost wholeness, so lovers long to be fused into one.
— Plato, Symposium 189c-193d, speech of Aristophanes (tr. Benjamin Jowett; paraphrase) [11]
The myth explains love as the longing of two halves to become one being again.
The Kural’s lovers, blended like earth and water, have found that wholeness, so even a quarrel between them is joy.

Real Life Example

A. K. Ramanujan publishes The Interior Landscape
1967 · United States
In 1967 the poet and scholar A. K. Ramanujan, then teaching at the University of Chicago, published The Interior Landscape: Love Poems from a Classical Tamil Anthology. The book brought classical Tamil love poetry to English readers in clear, modern translations. Ramanujan went on to translate many more classical Tamil poems and was honoured by the government of India with the Padma Shri in 1976.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Interior Landscape made the ancient Tamil poetry of love, with its famous images of lovers whose hearts mingle, known to readers far beyond India.
This kural compares lovers to water blended with earth, an image rooted in the classical Tamil love poetry that Ramanujan's book introduced to the wider world.
