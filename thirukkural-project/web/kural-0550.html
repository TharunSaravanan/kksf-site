Kural 550

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

கொலையிற் கொடியாரை வேந்தொறுத்தல் பைங்கூழ் களைகட் டதனொடு நேர்.
Kolaiyir Kotiyaarai Vendhoruththal Paingoozh Kalaikat Tadhanotu Ner.

Meaning

When a king punishes cruel killers with death, it is like a farmer pulling out weeds to protect the young crop.

Elementary

A gardener pulls out weeds not because she hates plants but because the weeds would choke the vegetables. The Kural says a ruler must stop people who seriously hurt others, so that everyone else can grow up safe. (Grown-ups still argue about how harsh such punishments should be.)

University

The final couplet puts the chapter’s argument about punishment in an agricultural image: பைங்கூழ் is the young green crop, and களை கட்டல் is weeding. The phrase கொலையிற் கொடியாரை is read in two ways. Varadarasan and Pappaiah take கொலையின் as instrumental, so the king punishes the cruel ‘by death’ (கொலைத்தண்டனையால்). Kalaignar takes it as the offence, so the cruel are ‘those who commit murder and similar cruelties’. Parimelazhagar’s commentary follows the first line of reading and presents capital punishment as the protection of the good, just as the weed is removed for the crop’s sake. Commentators note the tension with the Aram section’s உயிர் கொல்லாமை (not killing, Ch. 33). The usual resolution is that Aram states the individual’s ethic while Porul states the king’s duty. Modern readers such as Kalaignar keep the metaphor but stress that it applies to hardened offenders, not the ordinary wrongdoer. The next chapter, Tyranny, reverses the image. The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya calls the whole book of the Arthashastra devoted to criminal justice Kantakasodhana, ‘The Removal of Thorns’, treating the suppression of the wicked, the seizure of criminals and their trial as clearing thorns out of the realm.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book IV, ‘The Removal of Thorns’ (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Indian statecraft pictured criminals as thorns that a king must root out so that his people can move and work safely.
Thorns removed from the realm and weeds pulled from the crop are the same kind of image.
Christianity
“A wise king scattereth the wicked, and bringeth the wheel over them.”
— Proverbs 20:26 (KJV) [4]
The proverb uses the image of a threshing wheel: a wise king separates out the wicked as grain is separated from chaff.
A farming image for the king’s removal of evildoers, as in the Kural’s weeding.
Islam
“And there is for you in legal retribution [saving of] life, O you [people] of understanding, that you may become righteous.”
— Qur’an 2:179 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that lawful retribution for murder actually preserves life in the community.
Punishing killers to protect the living is the logic of weeding to save the crop.
Judaism
“Morning by morning will I destroy all the wicked of the land; to cut off all the workers of iniquity from the city of the LORD.”
— Psalm 101:8 (JPS 1917) [6]
In this royal psalm the king vows to purge the land of evildoers every day.
The king ‘cutting off’ the wicked to keep the city whole matches the Kural’s weeding.
Buddhism
In the Kutadanta Sutta, the Buddha tells of a king whose realm was plagued by bandits; his wise chaplain advises that execution, imprisonment and banishment will not end the plague, and that instead the king should give seed and fodder to farmers, capital to traders and proper wages to officials, after which the country became peaceful.
— Digha Nikaya 5, Kutadanta Sutta [7]
The Buddhist text answers crime by removing its economic causes rather than by killing offenders.
It is a deliberate counterpoint: where the Kural pulls the weed, the sutta improves the soil so that weeds do not grow.
Jainism
“Virodhi Himsa is the kind of injury which is necessarily committed in defense of person and property, against thieves, robbers, dacoits, assailants and enemies, in meeting their aggression, and in causing the least possible injury, necessary in the circumstances, in which one may find oneself.”
— ‘The Concept of Himsa’, jainworld.com (text from V.A. Sangave's Jaina Path of Ahimsa; page also credited to Acharya Mahapragya) [8]
Jain ethics allows a householder only defensive injury against aggressors, and only the least injury the situation requires. Monks must avoid even that.
The Kural approves of a king executing murderers the way a farmer pulls weeds. Jainism will accept force against violent offenders only as minimal, defensive injury and would not call execution a good.
Sikhism
“The slanderers are not allowed to stay; they are pulled out by their roots, like useless weeds.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 683, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says those who malign the Lord's servants cannot stand, but are uprooted like useless weeds.
The Kural likens punishing the cruel to a farmer pulling weeds to save the young crop; Guru Arjan uses the same weeding image for the removal of wrongdoers.
Greek Mythology
“And further, make a law by my order, that he who has no part in reverence and justice shall be put to death, for he is a plague of the state.”
— Plato, Protagoras 322d (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
In Protagoras’ myth, Zeus sends Hermes to give reverence and justice to all people, and orders that anyone who cannot share in them be put to death as a disease of the city.
Removing one who destroys the community to save the rest is the Kural’s weeding in divine terms.
Philosophy
“And the good physician and lawgiver of the State ought, like the wise bee-master, to keep them at a distance and prevent, if possible, their ever coming in; and if they have anyhow found a way in, then he should have them and their cells cut out as speedily as possible.”
— Plato, Republic VIII, 564b–c (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato likens the lawgiver who removes the city’s destructive ‘drones’ to a beekeeper cutting diseased cells out of the hive.
Cutting out what harms the hive is the same move as pulling weeds from the crop.

Real Life Example

The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg delivers its verdicts
1 October 1946 · Nuremberg, Germany
After the Second World War, the Allied powers set up the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg to try leading figures of Nazi Germany for crimes including war crimes and crimes against humanity. On 1 October 1946 the judges convicted 19 of the defendants and acquitted 3. Twelve were sentenced to death, three to life imprisonment and four to prison terms of ten to twenty years.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The tribunal held those responsible for mass killing to account through a court of law, with evidence and acquittals, rather than through vengeance.
The kural compares a king who punishes murderous criminals to a farmer pulling up weeds to save the green crop, and the Nuremberg tribunal removed the chief architects of mass murder so that a peaceful order could grow again.
