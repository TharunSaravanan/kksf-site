Kural 185

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

அறஞ்சொல்லும் நெஞ்சத்தான் அன்மை புறஞ்சொல்லும் புன்மையாற் காணப் படும்.
Aranjollum nenjaththaan anmai puranjollum, Punmaiyaal kaanap padum.

Meaning

When someone talks badly about others behind their backs, that small-minded habit shows that their heart is not truly good, no matter how much they talk about goodness.

Elementary

Someone might always say, “Be kind! Be fair!” But if you hear them gossiping meanly about people when those people leave, you know they don’t really mean what they say. Gossip is like a window that lets you see what’s really inside a person.

University

Here Valluvar turns backbiting into evidence. The phrase aṟañ-collum neñcattāṉ — one whose heart speaks of virtue, or, as many commentators read it, one who merely talks virtue — sets up a contrast between profession and inner reality, and puṟañ-collum puṉmai (“the meanness of speaking at the back”) becomes the diagnostic sign that exposes the gap. The verse therefore makes slander not only a wrong but a symptom: a person’s habitual talk about the absent is a more reliable index of character than his public moralising. The idea that inner character leaks out through small, unguarded habits is common to many ethical traditions, and it sharpens the chapter’s treatment of hypocrisy begun in 182. Valluvar implicitly invites the listener to read people by what they say about others when those others are not present.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who restrains the senses of action but whose mind dwells on sense objects certainly deludes himself and is called a pretender.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.6 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna calls a mithyācāra (pretender) anyone who shows outward restraint while his mind stays attached to what he claims to renounce.
Both texts expose the one whose outward religiosity hides an unchanged inner self; the Kural names backbiting as the giveaway.
Christianity
“If any man among you seem to be religious, and bridleth not his tongue, but deceiveth his own heart, this man’s religion is vain.”
— James 1:26 (KJV) [4]
James says an uncontrolled tongue shows that a person’s religion is empty, however devout he appears.
James and Valluvar agree: the tongue reveals whether the claimed virtue is real.
Islam
“O community of people, who believed by their tongue, and belief did not enter their hearts, do not back-bite Muslims, and do not search for their faults”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4880 [5]
The Prophet addresses backbiters as people whose faith is on their tongues but has not reached their hearts.
This is nearly the Kural’s own claim: backbiting shows that professed virtue has not entered the heart.
Judaism
“Anyone who speaks malicious speech is considered as though he denied the fundamental belief in God.”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 15b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
Rabbi Yohanan teaches that the one who speaks lashon hara, whatever he professes, is effectively a denier of God.
Both texts say slander cancels out religious profession and shows what the speaker really believes.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares someone who recites teachings without living them to a hired cowherd counting cattle that aren’t his.
Both expose the person who speaks virtue without owning it; the Kural finds the proof in backbiting.
Jainism
“Censuring others, praising oneself, concealing good qualities present in others and proclaiming noble qualities absent in oneself, cause the influx (āsrava) of karmas which lead to low-status (nīcagotra).”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.25 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [8]
Umāsvāti puts running others down and claiming virtues one does not have side by side as causes of karma that leads to rebirth in low status.
Valluvar says a man's backbiting shows that his talk of virtue is hollow, and the Tattvārtha Sūtra pairs that same censure of others with the false display of virtues one does not have.
Sikhism
“We are good at talking, but our actions are bad. Mentally, we are impure and black, but outwardly, we appear white.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 85, Sri Raag (Sri Raag ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Sri Raag ki Vaar, Guru Nanak confesses the human gap between fine words and bad conduct, and between a dark inner mind and a clean outward show.
The Kural says that however much a man talks of virtue, his backbiting shows his heart is not given to it; Guru Nanak exposes that same gap between virtuous talk and the inner heart.
Greek Mythology
Momus, the god of fault-finding, criticised the maker of man for not setting a door or window in the human breast, so that people’s real thoughts could be seen.
— Lucian, Hermotimus 20 (paraphrase of the Momus fable) [10]
In the tale Lucian retells, the carping god wanted people’s hidden intentions made visible.
Valluvar suggests there already is such a window: a person’s backbiting shows what his heart is really like.
Philosophy
“for the highest reach of injustice is, to be deemed just when you are not.”
— Plato, Republic 361a (Jowett) [11]
In Glaucon’s challenge in the Republic, the perfectly unjust man is the one who keeps a reputation for justice while being unjust.
Plato names the danger the Kural helps us detect: the person who seems virtuous but is not, whose backbiting gives him away.

Real Life Example

Richard Owen's anonymous review of the Origin of Species
April 1860 · London, England, United Kingdom
Richard Owen, Britain's most eminent anatomist and a colleague of Charles Darwin for twenty years, wrote a very long anonymous review attacking On the Origin of Species, published in the Edinburgh Review in 1860. Britannica notes that Owen misquoted passages, altering words inside quotation marks, and that he was moved by a sense that his own preeminence was about to be lost. Darwin privately called the review extremely malignant and clever, and wrote to Charles Lyell of how painful it was to be hated so intensely by Owen.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A great scientist who stood for impartial learning revealed, through a hidden attack on a colleague, a pettiness that damaged his own lasting reputation.
The kural says the meanness of backbiting shows that a man's heart is not truly given to virtue, however much he speaks of it, and Owen's anonymous attack exposed the gap between his public standing and his conduct.
