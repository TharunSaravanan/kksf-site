Kural 375

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

நல்லவை எல்லாஅந் தீயவாம் தீயவும் நல்லவாம் செல்வம் செயற்கு.
Nallavai ellaaan theeyavaam theeyavum Nallavaam selvam seyarku.

Meaning

When you are trying to get rich, fate can turn every good chance into a bad one, and every bad chance into a good one.

Elementary

Sometimes you plan a picnic on a sunny day and it rains anyway. Other times you get stuck indoors and end up finding something wonderful. This kural says that when it comes to making money, luck can flip good chances into bad ones and bad chances into good ones.

University

Commentators take நல்லவை and தீயவை here as circumstances favourable or unfavourable to earning, such as the right time, place, means and partners, rather than moral good and evil. Solomon Pappaiah glosses the kural this way: even with the right time, place and trade, an adverse fate brings loss, and even without them a favourable fate brings profit. The couplet warns against over-confidence in planning, since the same conditions can produce opposite results depending on forces beyond calculation. Across traditions this becomes the theme of reversal: the Qur'an’s ‘perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you’, Rabbi Akiva’s night journey, and Boethius’ turning wheel of Fortune.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That which in the beginning may be just like poison but at the end is just like nectar and which awakens one to self-realization is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.37 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita notes that what feels bitter at first can prove sweet in the end, and the reverse.
It shares the Kural’s theme of reversal: things that seem bad can turn out good.
Christianity
“And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose.”
— Romans 8:28 (KJV) [4]
Paul assures believers that even apparent misfortunes are turned to good by God’s purpose.
It matches the ‘bad becomes good’ half of the Kural under a higher governing power.
Islam
“But perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allāh knows, while you know not.”
— Qur'an 2:216 (Saheeh International) [5]
Human judgements of good and bad can be reversed by what God alone knows.
An almost exact two-way match: the favourable can prove harmful and the unfavourable beneficial.
Judaism
Rabbi Akiva, turned away from a town, sleeps in a field; wind blows out his lamp, a cat eats his rooster and a lion his donkey. That night an army captures the town, and his lack of light and noise saves him. He teaches that all the Merciful One does is for the good.
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 60b [6]
A string of misfortunes turns out to be what saved his life.
Circumstances that looked disastrous turn out favourable, which is exactly the Kural’s reversal.
Buddhism
“It may be well with the evil-doer as long as the evil ripens not. But when it does ripen, then the evil-doer sees (the painful results of) his evil deeds. It may be ill with the doer of good as long as the good ripens not. But when it does ripen, then the doer of good sees (the pleasant results of) his good deeds.”
— Dhammapada 119-120 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
Present circumstances can be misleading, because the results of past action ripen later and may reverse them.
Both texts describe good and bad conditions flipping because of a hidden force that ripens over time.
Jainism
“The Karman in question brings about an obstruction to the enjoyment, &c., though all other circumstances be favourable.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 33.15, Jacobi's footnote (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Explaining the obstructive karma, Jacobi, following the commentators, notes that it blocks enjoyment and gain even when every outward condition is right. Karma can override good circumstances.
Valluvar says that in getting wealth, fate can turn every favourable circumstance against a person. The Jain gloss makes the same point: obstructive karma defeats gain 'though all other circumstances be favourable'.
Sikhism
“Suffering is the medicine, and pleasure the disease, because where there is pleasure, there is no desire for God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak turns the usual valuation upside down: hardship heals by turning the mind to God, while comfort sickens by making one forget Him.
The Kural says fate can turn every favourable circumstance unfavourable and every unfavourable one favourable; Guru Nanak shows the same reversal, with good fortune becoming harmful and misfortune becoming a cure.
Greek Mythology
Croesus asks the Delphic oracle whether to attack Persia and is told that if he does he will destroy a great empire. Taking this as a promise, he attacks, and the empire he destroys is his own.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.53-1.91 [10]
What looked like the most favourable sign possible became the cause of his ruin.
Good omens and good conditions turn bad, the first half of the Kural in dramatic form.
Philosophy
Lady Philosophy tells Boethius that Fortune turns her wheel without pause, and that adverse fortune is often more useful than prosperous fortune, because it reveals true friends and false goods.
— Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy, Book II (Prose 1-2, 8) [11]
Boethius shows that good and bad fortune are always turning and can swap their value.
Like the Kural, it says that favourable and unfavourable circumstances readily change places.

Real Life Example

William Perkin's failed quinine experiment produces mauve
1856 · London, England, United Kingdom
In 1856 the eighteen-year-old chemistry student William Henry Perkin was trying, in a home laboratory in London, to make quinine, the costly anti-malaria drug, from coal-tar chemicals. The experiment failed, but it left a dark residue from which he extracted a brilliant purple substance that could dye silk. He patented it in August 1856 as mauve, the first synthetic dye, set up a factory with his father's help, and the success of his dyes made him rich enough to retire from business in his thirties.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Perkin's discovery turned coal tar, a waste product, into the raw material of a vast new chemical industry.
The kural says that in the pursuit of wealth fortune can turn unfavourable circumstances into favourable ones, and Perkin's failed attempt at a medicine became the source of his fortune and of the synthetic dye industry.
