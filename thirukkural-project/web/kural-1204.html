Kural 1204

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

யாமும் உளேங்கொல் அவர்நெஞ்சத்து எந்நெஞ்சத்து ஓஒ உளரே அவர்.
Yaamum ulaengol avarnenjaththu ennenjaththu oo-o ulare avar.

Meaning

Do I have a place in his heart too? Oh, he lives in my heart all the time.

Elementary

Imagine you think about your grandma every single day while she is far away. You might wonder, ‘Does Grandma think about me too?’ The lady in this poem asks the same: he is always in my heart, but am I in his?

University

The couplet sets two hearts side by side: avar neñcattu (‘in his heart’) and en neñcattu (‘in my heart’). The exclamation ōo (lengthened ō, ‘alas’ or ‘oh!’) marks her certainty that he is unceasingly present in her own heart (uḷarē avar), while the particle kol turns her own presence in his heart into an anxious question (yāmum uḷēṅ-kol). Mu. Varadarasanar glosses ‘do we too remain without leaving in his heart?’, and Pope renders: ‘Have I a place within his heart? / From mine, alas! he never doth depart.’ The royal plural yām is the heroine’s usual self-reference in the Kamattuppal. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s play, Shakuntala composes a love letter to King Dushyanta, saying that she does not know his heart, but that love torments her day and night because her heart is set on him.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 3 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [3]
Shakuntala is certain of her own love but uncertain of his, and writes to find out.
Her ‘I know not your heart, but mine is yours’ is the Kural’s question in dramatic form.
Christianity
“Even as it is meet for me to think this of you all, because I have you in my heart.”
— Philippians 1:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul, writing from prison, tells his distant friends that he holds them in his heart.
Paul states the half the Kural’s speaker is sure of, that the absent ones dwell in her heart, without her doubt about the other half.
Islam
Rumi teaches that no lover seeks union without the beloved also seeking him: when the lightning of love has struck this heart, know that there is love in that heart too.
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi, Book III (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.A. Nicholson) [5]
The Masnavi assures the lover that longing in one heart implies longing in the other.
Rumi answers the Kural’s anxious question with confidence: if he is in your heart, you are in his.
Judaism
“Set me as a seal upon thy heart, As a seal upon thine arm; For love is strong as death.”
— Song of Songs 8:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved asks to be set as a seal on her lover’s heart, permanently impressed there.
This is the wish behind the Kural’s question: to be held in the beloved’s heart as he is held in hers.
Buddhism
When the Buddha returns to Kapilavastu years after leaving home, Yasodhara does not go out to greet him but sends their son Rahula to ask for his inheritance, saying ‘That is your father.’
— Vinaya Pitaka, Mahavagga I.54 (paraphrase; cf. tr. T.W. Rhys Davids and H. Oldenberg, SBE 13) [7]
The wife left behind by the future Buddha waits to see whether he still holds a place for her and their son.
Yasodhara, who kept her husband in her heart through his long absence, embodies the Kural’s question whether she remains in his.
Jainism
The poet of the Kalyanamandira Stotra tells the Jina that when he dwells in a devotee’s heart, even tight bonds of karma loosen at once, as snakes coiled round a sandalwood tree slip away when a peacock arrives.
— Kalyanamandira Stotra, verse 8 (paraphrase) [8]
Jain devotional poetry speaks of the Jina abiding in the heart of the devotee and transforming it.
The heroine is sure her beloved dwells in her heart; the Jain devotee speaks of the Jina dwelling in his, though without any hope of a place in the Jina’s.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, my mind has become immaculate, like the waters of the Ganges. The Lord follows after me, calling, "Kabeer! Kabeer!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1367, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 55) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says that once his mind was made pure, the Lord Himself came after him, calling his name.
The Kural's wife knows her beloved lives always in her heart but anxiously asks whether she has a place in his; Kabir gives the answer of mutual love: the Beloved remembers the devoted one and calls after him.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s letter, Laodamia writes to her husband Protesilaus at Troy, telling him he is always before her mind, and anxiously wondering what he is doing and whether he remembers her among the fighting.
— Ovid, Heroides 13 (Laodamia to Protesilaus) (paraphrase) [10]
Laodamia’s letter is full of the asymmetry between her constant thought and her uncertainty about his.
Her question is the Kural’s: you never leave my mind, but do I stay in yours?
Philosophy
Montaigne, writing of his friend La Boétie, says their souls were so mingled and blended that the seam joining them could no longer be found, and that if pressed to say why he loved him, he could only answer: because it was he, because it was I.
— Michel de Montaigne, Essays 1.28, ‘Of Friendship’ (paraphrase; cf. tr. Charles Cotton) [11]
Montaigne describes a love so mutual that each soul dwells wholly in the other.
Montaigne’s fulfilled mutuality is what the Kural’s heroine hopes for when she asks whether she lives in his heart as he lives in hers.

Real Life Example

Napoleon's anxious love letters to Josephine from Italy
March–November 1796 · Nice, France, and northern Italy
Days after marrying Josephine in March 1796, Napoleon Bonaparte left to command the French Army of Italy. On 30 March he wrote from Nice that he had not spent a day without loving her and thought of her constantly, and he asked why she had addressed him with the formal vous. Through 1796 he begged her to join him and complained that she wrote too rarely; her letters to him have not survived.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters capture a lover sure of his own feelings and openly unsure whether the beloved feels the same.
The kural sets 'he is always in my heart' beside the anxious question 'do I have a place in his?', which is the exact tone of Napoleon's letters to Josephine.
