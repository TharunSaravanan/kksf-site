Kural 496

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

கடலோடா கால்வல் நெடுந்தேர் கடலோடும் நாவாயும் ஓடா நிலத்து.
Kadalodaa kaalval nedunther kadalodum Naavaayum odaa nilaththu.

Meaning

A tall chariot with strong wheels cannot run on the sea, and a ship that sails the sea cannot run on land.

Elementary

A bicycle is great on the road but useless in a swimming pool, and a boat is great in the pool but useless on the road. Every tool, and every person, has a place where it works best.

University

The kural uses a paired image: ‘kaal val nedunther’, the tall chariot with strong wheels (kaal, literally ‘leg’, here the wheel), and ‘naavaay’, the sea-going ship, a word related to Sanskrit nau and Latin navis, which shows early Tamil familiarity with maritime trade. The repeated verb ‘odaa’ (will not run) connects the two lines. Parimelazhagar reads the verse as an allegory for forces: land armies and naval forces each succeed only in their own element, so a king must match his forces to the terrain. Kalaignar makes it a general lesson that whoever chooses where to act must at least know that a chariot will not cross the sea. The thirukkural-thamizh.org gloss extends it to countries bordered by mountains, forests, sea or desert, each needing its own methods.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya describes different kinds of battlefield and matches each arm of the army to suitable ground: chariots need level, firm, open terrain, elephants can handle uneven and marshy ground, and infantry can fight almost anywhere.
— Kautilya, Arthaśāstra, Book X, Chapter 4 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Classical Indian military science treats choosing terrain for each arm as an exact skill.
The Kural’s chariot-and-ship image is the poetic form of this technical rule.
Christianity
“They ran the ship aground; and the forepart stuck fast, and remained unmoveable, but the hinder part was broken with the violence of the waves.”
— Acts 27:41 (KJV) [4]
In Paul’s shipwreck off Malta, the ship that had carried them across the sea was stuck and broken once it hit land.
This is the Kural’s ship that ‘does not run on land’, told as an actual event.
Islam
“And of His signs are the ships in the sea, like mountains. If He willed, He could still the wind, and they would remain motionless on its surface.”
— Quran 42:32–33 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran points to great ships as signs of God, whose power depends on the conditions of the sea and wind.
Like the Kural, it treats the ship’s strength as dependent on its proper element.
Judaism
“And took off their chariot wheels, that they drave them heavily: so that the Egyptians said, Let us flee from the face of Israel.”
— Exodus 14:25 (KJV) [4]
Pharaoh’s chariots, the best weapon of their time on dry land, got stuck in the seabed and lost their wheels.
The Kural’s chariot that ‘does not run on the sea’ appears literally in the Exodus story.
Buddhism
In the raft simile, the Buddha describes a man who builds a raft to cross a flood and then carries it on his head over dry land. He asks whether that is the right use of a raft, and the monks agree it is not.
— Majjhima Nikāya 22, Alagaddūpama Sutta [6]
A tool built for water becomes a burden on land; the Dhamma is to be used for crossing over, not clung to.
The Kural’s ship that cannot run on land makes the same point: something useful in one element is useless in another.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra teaches that a soul freed from all karma rises to the top of the inhabited universe (loka) and goes no further, because the medium of motion (dharmastikaya) does not exist beyond it. Even a perfected soul cannot move where its medium is absent.
— Tattvartha Sutra 10.8 of Umasvati (paraphrase) [7]
Jainism holds that even the highest being's movement is limited by the medium available to it.
The kural says the strong chariot cannot run on the sea nor the ship on land; Jain doctrine gives a cosmic version of the same rule, since no being, however perfect, can travel where its medium of motion is absent.
Sikhism
“If someone embarks on a boat of stone, he shall drown with its cargo.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 420, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak warns that a vessel unfit for the water sinks with everything aboard, an image for relying on the wrong support to cross the world-ocean.
The Kural says the chariot cannot run on the sea nor the ship on land; Guru Nanak likewise shows that a means unsuited to its medium brings only ruin.
Greek Mythology
At Pylos (425 BCE), Thucydides notes the strange reversal of the Athenians, a sea power, defending from land, while the Spartans, a land power, attacked from ships and tried in vain to force a landing on a rocky shore.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 4.11–14 (tr. Richard Crawley) [9]
Each side was fighting outside the element it was strong in, and the Spartan assault failed.
This is the chariot-at-sea and ship-on-land problem in real Greek history.
Philosophy
“Moor your craft higher up than the enemy, and facing the sun. Do not move up-stream to meet the enemy. So much for river warfare.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War IX.6 (tr. Lionel Giles) [10]
Sun Tzu gives separate rules for mountains, rivers, salt-marshes and flat country, because each kind of ground needs its own tactics.
Like the Kural, it holds that methods suited to one terrain fail in another.

Real Life Example

Napoleon's Army of England waits at Boulogne
1803-1805 · Boulogne-sur-Mer, France
After war with Britain resumed in 1803, Napoleon gathered more than 150,000 soldiers in camps along the Channel coast around Boulogne and had well over a thousand flat-bottomed boats and barges built to carry them to England. The crossing depended on gaining control of the Channel, but the Royal Navy kept the French fleets away and the invasion never took place. In late August 1805 Napoleon marched the army east against Austria, and on 21 October 1805 the French and Spanish fleet was defeated at Trafalgar.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The finest land army in Europe could not reach an island enemy that ruled the sea, and Napoleon had to seek his victories on the Continent instead.
The kural says a strong chariot cannot run on the sea and a ship cannot run on land, and Napoleon's Grande Armee, unbeatable on land, never crossed twenty miles of water.
