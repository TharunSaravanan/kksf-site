Kural 1189

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

பசக்கமன் பட்டாங்கென் மேனி நயப்பித்தார் நன்னிலையர் ஆவர் எனின்.
pasakkaman pattaangen maeni nayappiththaar nannilaiyar aavar enin

Meaning

If the man who sweetly talked me into letting him go is doing well, then let my body turn as pale as it wants.

Elementary

Imagine your big brother goes away to camp and you miss him so much you feel sad. But then you think, ‘As long as he’s having fun and is safe, I don’t mind being a bit sad.’ That is what the wife says: if her husband is doing well, she doesn’t mind the pale colour.

University

Here the heroine turns from complaint to self-sacrifice: pasakka maṉ (பசக்க மன், ‘let it grow pale indeed’), paṭṭāṅku (பட்டாங்கு, ‘as it actually is, to its full measure’) eṉ mēṉi, if nayappittār (நயப்பித்தார், ‘he who made me consent by sweet persuasion’, from nayam, pleasantness) is nannilaiyar (நன்னிலையர், ‘of good standing’). Commentators divide on nannilaiyar: Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah read it as ‘good, blameless’, so the verse is half ironic, while Kalaignar reads ‘is well, prospers’, making it a wish for his welfare; the Pope translation (‘in good estate remain’) supports the latter, and the alternative rendering on ytamizh, ‘if he is clear of guilt’, supports the former. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Narada Bhakti Sutra, taking the gopis of Vraja as its model, describes the highest love as finding one’s happiness in the beloved’s happiness, and says that a love lacking this is not true love.
— Narada Bhakti Sutra 21-24 (paraphrase) [3]
The bhakti tradition treats tat-sukha-sukhitvam, being happy in the beloved’s happiness, as the mark of pure love.
The Kural’s heroine shows exactly this: if he is well, her own suffering does not matter.
Christianity
“Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up …”
— 1 Corinthians 13:4 (KJV) [4]
Paul defines love as patient endurance that does not put itself first.
The heroine’s willingness to suffer the pallor so long as her husband prospers is love that ‘suffereth long’.
Islam
“… but give [them] preference over themselves, even though they are in privation. And whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul — it is those who will be the successful.”
— Qur’an 59:9 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who put others’ good before their own even when they themselves are in need.
The heroine likewise prefers her husband’s well-being to her own, accepting her pallor as the price.
Judaism
Jonathan, heir to Saul’s throne, loves David as his own soul; he strengthens David in hiding and tells him that David will be king over Israel and he himself will be second to him, gladly giving up his own claim for his friend’s good.
— 1 Samuel 18:1 and 23:16-17 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) (paraphrase) [6]
The Bible’s great story of devoted friendship shows a love that wishes the beloved well at one’s own cost.
The Kural’s heroine shows the same spirit: let her suffer, provided the one she loves fares well.
Buddhism
The Karaniya Metta Sutta teaches wishing that all beings be happy and at ease, and cherishing them with a boundless heart, as a mother would protect her only child with her own life.
— Karaniya Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.8 (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhist loving-kindness wishes others’ welfare without condition, even at the cost of oneself.
The heroine’s wish that her husband fare well, whatever it costs her own body, is a personal form of that goodwill.
Jainism
“Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him! it is better I should turn nun.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rather than resent Arishtanemi for leaving, Rajimati resolves to follow the same path of renunciation he has chosen, and later attains perfection.
Like the Kural’s heroine, she does not curse the one who left; she accepts her own loss and honours his choice.
Sikhism
“If You will bless me with happiness, then I will worship and adore You. Even in pain, I will meditate on You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 757, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says the devotee's love does not depend on comfort: in happiness and in pain alike the mind stays on the Lord.
The Kural's wife says her body may grow as pale as it likes so long as her husband is well; Gurbani likewise accepts pain without letting it lessen love for the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
When Admetus is fated to die unless someone takes his place, his wife Alcestis alone agrees to die in his stead so that he may live.
— Euripides, Alcestis (paraphrase) [10]
In Euripides’ play Alcestis is the model of a wife who accepts her own suffering for her husband’s welfare.
The Kural’s heroine shows the same spirit on a smaller scale: let her body suffer so long as he fares well.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that friendship consists more in loving than in being loved, and points to mothers who delight in loving their children and wish them well even when they receive no love in return.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.8 (1159a27-33) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, the deepest affection wishes the other’s good for its own sake, regardless of return.
The heroine’s wish that her husband prosper, whatever it costs her, is love of exactly this kind.

Real Life Example

George Washington writes to Martha on taking command of the army
18 June 1775 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
On 18 June 1775 George Washington wrote to his wife Martha from Philadelphia that Congress had placed the army under his command and that he must go to Boston. He told her he had tried to avoid the post, partly because he did not want to leave his family, that his chief unhappiness would be knowing she was uneasy at being left alone, and he asked her to summon her fortitude and spend the time as agreeably as she could. In the months that followed Martha remained at home amid fears for her safety, and later joined him at his winter camps.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter shows a husband persuading his wife to accept a separation for the sake of his duty and standing.
The kural's wife says that if the man who sweetly persuaded her to let him go is well and in good standing, her own pallor does not matter, and Washington's letter is exactly such a gentle persuasion to bear his absence for the sake of his public duty.
