Kural 582

Detectives · Otraadal · ஒற்றாடல்

எல்லார்க்கும் எல்லாம் நிகழ்பவை எஞ்ஞான்றும் வல்லறிதல் வேந்தன் தொழில்.
Ellaarkkum ellaam nigazhpavai enjnjaandrum Vallaridhal vaendhan thozhil.

Meaning

A king’s job is to find out quickly, at all times, everything that happens to everyone, whether they are enemies, friends, or neither.

Elementary

A good school principal wants to know right away if someone is being bullied, if a pipe is leaking, or if a new student feels lost, not a month later. This kural says a ruler’s real work is the same: always finding out quickly what is happening to everyone.

University

The couplet stacks three universals, எல்லார்க்கும் (to all persons), எல்லாம் (all things) and எஞ்ஞான்றும் (at all times), then adds வல் (‘swiftly’) to அறிதல் (knowing). Parimelazhagar spells out ‘all persons’ as the king’s enemies, friends and neutrals (பகைவர், நட்பினர், நொதுமலர்), and Pappaiah and Kalaignar follow this three-way division, which matches the Arthashastra’s mandala theory of foreign relations. Most commentators add ‘through spies’ in brackets because the chapter context requires it, even though the word ஒற்று is not in the line. The closing தொழில் (‘occupation, duty’) presents knowing as the king’s trade and not something he does now and then. The wording is the same on all three sources consulted; thirukkural-thamizh.org has the sandhi form எல்லார்க்கு மெல்லா.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As the Wind moves (everywhere), entering (in the shape of the vital air) all created beings, even so let him penetrate (everywhere) through his spies; that is the office in which he resembles the Wind.”
— Manusmriti 9.306 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
Manu compares the king to the wind god, whose ‘office’ is to reach every being through his spies.
Getting everywhere, into everyone’s affairs, is the Kural’s ‘all things of all people’; both texts call it the king’s office or duty.
Christianity
“Watch ye therefore: for ye know not when the master of the house cometh, at even, or at midnight, or at the cockcrowing, or in the morning … And what I say unto you I say unto all, Watch.”
— Mark 13:35-37 (KJV) [5]
Jesus demands watchfulness at every hour of the night, because what matters may arrive at any time.
Being alert at every hour echoes the Kural’s எஞ்ஞான்றும், ‘at all times’.
Islam
“And with Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them except Him. And He knows what is on the land and in the sea. Not a leaf falls but that He knows it.”
— Quran 6:59 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran describes God’s complete knowledge of everything that happens, down to a single falling leaf.
The Kural asks the human king to aim, as far as he can, at the same complete knowledge of events in his realm.
Judaism
“And see the land, what it is; and the people that dwelleth therein, whether they be strong or weak, few or many; And what the land is that they dwell in, whether it be good or bad; and what cities they be that they dwell in, whether in tents, or in strong holds.”
— Numbers 13:18-19 (KJV) [5]
Moses gives the twelve spies a full intelligence brief covering people, strength, numbers, land and fortifications.
Moses wants to know ‘everything about everyone’ in Canaan, which is the scope the Kural sets for a king.
Buddhism
In the Mahā-Ummagga Jātaka the Bodhisatta, as the sage Mahosadha, sends spies to every royal court, and they bring home reports that let him learn of plots against his king before they happen.
— Mahā-Ummagga Jātaka (Jātaka 546), as summarised in the Dictionary of Pali Proper Names (paraphrase) [7]
The future Buddha is shown as a wise minister who keeps his king informed by a wide network of agents.
Reports from every court, arriving continually, are the Kural’s ideal of knowing all things about all parties.
Jainism
“He was a Kevalin, omniscient and comprehending all objects, he knew all conditions of the world, of gods, men, and demons; whence they come, where they go … their food, drink, doings, desires, open and secret deeds, their conversation and gossip, and the thoughts of their minds; he saw and knew all conditions in the whole world of all living beings.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra 2.15.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; wisdomlib.org) [8]
On attaining kevala-jñāna Mahavira is described as knowing everything about every being, including their hidden deeds and thoughts, at all times.
The Kural asks the king to know everything that happens to everyone, at all times; Jainism places that complete, all-encompassing knowledge in the omniscient Jina rather than in any ruler or his spies.
Sikhism
“The recording scribes of the conscious and the unconscious, Chitr and Gupt, know all actions and karma.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 838, Raag Bilaaval, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan uses the inherited figure of Chitr and Gupt, the recorders who note every deed of every person, to say that nothing anyone does goes unknown.
The Kural makes it the king's trade to know at all times everything that happens to everyone; Gurbani gives such total and constant knowledge only to the divine order and its recorders.
Greek Mythology
Hera set Argus Panoptes, the ‘all-seeing’ giant with eyes all over his body, to watch without rest, because some of his eyes were always awake while others slept.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.1.3; Ovid, Metamorphoses 1 (paraphrase) [10]
Argus is Greek myth’s image of a watcher who never stops seeing.
His sleepless, many-eyed watch pictures the Kural’s demand for knowing everything at all times.
Philosophy
“Now this foreknowledge cannot be elicited from spirits; it cannot be obtained inductively from experience, nor by any deductive calculation. Knowledge of the enemy’s dispositions can only be obtained from other men.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 13.5-6 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu argues that the knowledge a sovereign needs most can come only from living informants.
Like Valluvar, Sun Tzu makes gathering human intelligence a core duty of the wise ruler.

Real Life Example

Britain's Room 40 intercepts the Zimmermann Telegram
January-February 1917 · London, United Kingdom
In January 1917 the German foreign secretary Arthur Zimmermann sent a coded telegram proposing that, if the United States entered the war, Mexico should ally with Germany. British naval codebreakers in Room 40 intercepted and decoded it as it passed over British-controlled cable routes. Britain passed the text to the American ambassador Walter Page on 22 February, its publication helped turn American opinion, and the United States declared war on Germany in April 1917.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Room 40 was reading not only enemy messages but the traffic that touched neutral countries, and so learned an enemy plan aimed at two neutrals before either of them knew of it.
The kural makes it a ruler's duty to learn quickly what is happening with enemies, friends and neutrals alike, and the Zimmermann intercept shows that wide watchfulness turning the course of a war.
