Kural 270

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

இலர்பல ராகிய காரணம் நோற்பார் சிலர்பலர் நோலா தவர்.
Ilar palar aagiya kaaranam norpaar Silar palar nolaadhavar.

Meaning

Many people in the world have nothing because only a few practice strict self-control, while most do not.

Elementary

In a class, only a few kids practise every day, and most don’t. That is why only a few become really good. Valluvar says the same about the whole world: most people are ‘poor’ inside because only a few do the hard work of tapas.

University

The closing couplet uses a neat chiastic arithmetic — இலர் பலர் (the have-nots are many) because நோற்பார் சிலர், பலர் நோலாதவர் (the practisers are few, the non-practisers many). இலர் is ambiguous: Solomon Pappaiah reads material poverty, Varadarasan and Kalaignar read lack of strength, and Puliyur Kesikan reads lack of true knowledge. The choice matters: a literal economic reading makes tapas a cause of prosperity, while the spiritual reading makes it the cause of inner wealth. Either way the verse ends the chapter with a sober sociology of virtue — excellence is rare because discipline is rare — and turns the whole chapter into an exhortation to join the few.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Out of many thousands among men, one may endeavor for perfection, and of those who have achieved perfection, hardly one knows Me in truth.”
— Bhagavad Gita 7.3 [3]
Krishna observes how rare the serious spiritual aspirant is.
The same ‘few strive, many don’t’ observation that grounds the Kural.
Christianity
“Enter ye in at the strait gate: for wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction, and many there be which go in thereat: Because strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life, and few there be that find it.”
— Matthew 7:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts the many on the easy road with the few on the hard road to life.
Same arithmetic of many and few, with the hard path as the source of true wealth.
Islam
“And the forerunners, the forerunners - … A [large] company of the former peoples And a few of the later peoples.”
— Qur’an 56:10, 13-14 (Sahih International) [5]
The foremost in devotion, brought nearest to God, are few among later generations.
The spiritually foremost are few, as the Kural’s practisers are few.
Judaism
Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai remarks that he has seen the ‘people of ascent’ — those of the highest spiritual rank — and they are few.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 45b [6]
The Talmud acknowledges that those who reach the highest spiritual level are a small minority.
Echoes the Kural’s ‘the practisers are few’.
Buddhism
“Few among men are those who cross to the farther shore. The rest, the bulk of men, only run up and down the hither bank.”
— Dhammapada 85 [7]
Only a few achieve liberation; the majority stay in the round of existence.
Near-identical picture of the few who strive and the many who do not.
Jainism
“And though they have heard the Law and believe in it, it is difficult for them to fulfill it strenuously; many who approve of the religion, do not adopt it.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 3.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The 'four requisites' lecture says that human birth, hearing the Law, faith and strenuous practice are each rare, so very few actually carry the discipline through.
Valluvar says the many are destitute because those who practise penance are few; the Jain text likewise laments that many approve the path but few take it up.
Sikhism
“Out of millions, hardly anyone is a servant of the Lord. All the others are mere traders.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 495, Raag Gujri, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Gujri Guru Arjan observes that among millions there is scarcely one true devotee, while the rest practise religion as a bargain for their own gain.
Valluvar explains the world's want by noting that those who practise are few and those who do not are many; Guru Arjan makes the same observation about true devotees and the many who only trade.
Greek Mythology
Sparta’s agoge subjected boys to hunger, cold, one cloak a year and harsh discipline; the few full citizens so trained dominated a far larger population of helots.
— Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 16-18 [10]
Rigorous austerity produced a small elite of exceptional capability.
Illustrates the Kural’s claim that the disciplined few hold what the undisciplined many lack; but the Spartan aim was military, not spiritual.
Philosophy
“For as they say in the mysteries, ‘the thyrsus-bearers are many, but the mystics few’; and these mystics are, I believe, those who have been true philosophers.”
— Plato, Phaedo 69c-d (trans. H. N. Fowler) [11]
Socrates distinguishes the many who carry the outward emblems from the few who are genuinely initiated through philosophical purification.
The many/few contrast between those who merely participate and those who truly undergo the discipline.

Real Life Example

Ninomiya Sontoku revives impoverished villages through diligence and thrift
early to mid-19th century (Sontoku lived 1787-1856) · Japan
Ninomiya Sontoku, a farmer who had rebuilt his own family's ruined farm, was engaged to restore struggling villages and estates. He taught sincerity, diligence, living within one's means and giving back, and he preferred loans repaid from the villagers' own harvests to outright grants. The districts he developed suffered very little in the great famine of 1836, and his teachings grew into the Hotoku movement.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Sontoku showed that the poverty of many villages could be reversed when people adopted steady self-discipline, and he became a national model of diligence.
The kural says so many are destitute because few practise disciplined effort, and Sontoku's work turned poor villages around by teaching exactly that discipline.
