Kural 813

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

உறுவது சீர்தூக்கும் நட்பும் பெறுவது கொள்வாரும் கள்வரும் நேர்.
Uruvadhu seerthookkum natpum peruvadhu kolvaarum kalvarum ner.

Meaning

A friend who weighs what they can get from you, a person who loves only for money, and a thief are all alike.

Elementary

If someone is your friend only after checking what they can get from you, they are a bit like a person who takes things without asking. A real friend doesn’t keep a price list.

University

Valluvar sets three figures in a row and declares them ner (நேர், ‘equal’): the friend who does seerthookkal (சீர்தூக்கல், ‘weighing on a balance’) of uruvadhu (உறுவது, ‘what will come to him’); those who take only peruvadhu (பெறுவது, ‘what they receive’); and kalvar (கள்வர், ‘thieves’). The middle phrase, peruvadhu kolvaar, is read by Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesigan and Kalaignar, following the traditional gloss, as women who sell their company for payment; Solomon Pappaiah renders it as sex workers. The point is not to single anyone out but to define calculating friendship as a transaction for gain and to class it with taking what is not freely given. All three web texts agree on the wording; the sandhi-joined edition writes ‘சீர்தூக்கு நட்பும்’ and ‘கள்வரு நேர்’, a matter of spelling convention.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The prostitute has to forsake a man who has no money, the subject a king that cannot defend him, the birds a tree that bears no fruit, and the guests a house after they have finished their meals.”
— Chanakya Niti 2.17 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya lists relationships that last only as long as there is something to take from them.
The list even begins with the same figure as the Kural, and makes the same point: such attachment is calculation, not friendship.
Christianity
“This he said, not that he cared for the poor; but because he was a thief, and had the bag, and bare what was put therein.”
— John 12:6 (KJV) [5]
John explains that Judas objected to an act of generosity not out of concern for the poor but because he kept the common purse and stole from it.
A disciple who measured everything by what he could take is the Kural’s gain-weighing friend, and John, like Valluvar, calls him a thief.
Islam
“And among them are some who criticize you concerning the [distribution of] charities. If they are given from them, they approve; but if they are not given from them, at once they become angry.”
— Qur’an 9:58 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse describes people whose approval of the Prophet rose and fell with what they personally received.
Their goodwill is the Kural’s seerthookkum natpu, friendship weighed against personal gain.
Judaism
“Many will intreat the favour of the prince: and every man is a friend to him that giveth gifts.”
— Proverbs 19:6 (Hebrew Bible, KJV rendering) [5]
This proverb in the Hebrew Bible observes that friendliness flows toward whoever has something to give.
Friendship that follows gifts is the calculated kind the Kural classes with taking for gain.
Buddhism
“(i) he appropriates his friend’s wealth, (ii) he gives little and asks much, (iii) he does his duty out of fear, (iv) he associates for his own advantage.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha describes the ‘appropriator’, a foe disguised as a friend whose companionship is a way of taking.
Valluvar’s gain-weighing friend and the Buddha’s appropriator are the same person, and both texts treat him as a taker rather than a friend.
Jainism
“If you impartially investigate the two things, it will be found that there is no difference between the shining light of a lamp and the love of courtesans. When the oil is exhausted, the light of the lamp vanishes, and when the money of their lovers is gone, their love also evaporates.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 38 'Courtesans', stanza 1 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet compares mercenary affection to a lamp that shines only while there is oil, so that the love ends as soon as the money runs out.
The Kural puts the calculating friend on a level with those who love only for what they receive, whom the commentators identify as courtesans, and the Naladiyar's lamp image shows exactly that love which lasts only as long as the payment.
Sikhism
“Farid, when there is greed, what love can there be? When there is greed, love is false. How long can one remain in a thatched hut which leaks when it rains?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid 18 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, says that love mixed with greed (lab) is false and gives no lasting shelter, like a leaking hut in the rain.
The Kural puts the friend who weighs what he stands to gain in the same class as the hired companion and the thief; Farid likewise says love that calculates its profit is not love at all.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Hecuba, King Priam sends his youngest son Polydorus, with a store of gold, to his guest-friend Polymestor of Thrace for safekeeping; when Troy falls, Polymestor murders the boy and keeps the gold.
— Euripides, Hecuba [10]
A sworn bond of guest-friendship is weighed against profit, and profit wins.
Polymestor is the Kural’s calculating friend who turns out to be no better than a thief.
Philosophy
“Now those who love each other for their utility do not love each other for themselves but in virtue of some good which they get from each other.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.3 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle defines friendship of utility as love directed not at the person but at the benefit.
This is the analytical version of the Kural’s friend who weighs what he will gain.

Real Life Example

Bessus seizes Darius III and claims the Persian throne
330 BCE · Northeastern Iran and Bactria (present-day Afghanistan)
After his defeat by Alexander the Great at Gaugamela, the Persian king Darius III retreated east, accompanied by Bessus, his relative and satrap of Bactria. In 330 BCE Bessus and other satraps arrested the king, and as Alexander's forces closed in, Darius was killed by his captors. Bessus then proclaimed himself king under the name Artaxerxes V, but within a year his own officers handed him over to Alexander, who had him executed.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A kinsman and trusted governor treated his fallen king as a prize to be used for his own advancement, and his gain lasted barely a year.
The kural sets the friend who weighs up what he stands to get beside the thief, and Bessus weighed his king's misfortune as a chance to seize the crown for himself.
