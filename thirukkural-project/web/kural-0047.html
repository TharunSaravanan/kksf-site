Kural 47

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

இயல்பினான் இல்வாழ்க்கை வாழ்பவன் என்பான் முயல்வாருள் எல்லாம் தலை.
Iyalpinaan ilvaazhkkai vaazhpavan enpaan, Muyalvaarul ellaam thalai.

Meaning

A person who lives family life the way it should be lived is the best of all who try to do good.

Elementary

In a race, lots of people train in fancy ways. But the one who simply runs correctly, step after step, finishes first. The person who does ordinary family life properly is that runner — ahead of everyone who is trying harder in more dramatic ways.

University

The decisive word is இயல்பினான் (iyalpinaan) — “by [its] nature,” the same root as இயல்புடைய in kural 41. Valluvar is not praising domestic life as such but domestic life lived *according to what it intrinsically is*, which chapters 41-46 have just specified: support of dependants, the fivefold obligation, blameless earning, shared food, love and virtue. Against that, முயல்வாருள் எல்லாம் தலை — “the head of all who exert themselves” — uses முயல் (muyal, to strive/labour), a word that in the religious register covers ascetic exertion and spiritual practice. The claim is competitive and startling for its time: the householder is not merely an acceptable alternative to the striving religious, he outranks them. Parimelazhagar's commentary reads this as Valluvar explicitly ranking இல்லறம் above துறவறம்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And in accordance with the precepts of the Veda and of the Smriti, the housekeeper is declared to be superior to all of them; for he supports the other three.”
— Manusmriti 6.89 (Bühler, Sacred Books of the East XXV) [4]
Having described the student, hermit and ascetic orders at length, Manu concludes by ranking the householder above all of them on the ground that he sustains them.
This is the Kural's exact claim — the householder is the head of all who strive — and with the same supporting reason.
Christianity
“One that ruleth well his own house, having his children in subjection with all gravity; (For if a man know not how to rule his own house, how shall he take care of the church of God?)”
— 1 Timothy 3:4-5 (KJV) [5]
The qualification for the highest office in the early church is not ascetic achievement but demonstrated competence as a householder.
Both texts treat well-run domestic life as the benchmark against which other forms of religious striving are measured.
Islam
“The best of you is the best to his wives, and I am the best of you to my wives, and when your companion dies, leave him alone.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 3895 (graded Sahih by Darussalam) [6]
The Prophet locates the measure of a person's excellence squarely in domestic conduct, and names himself as the example.
Like the Kural, it makes 'best of all' a ranking decided inside the home rather than by any extraordinary religious exertion.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that a man without a wife lives without joy, without blessing and without goodness, and in the surrounding discussion treats marriage and the household as the normal setting in which a Jew fulfils his obligations.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 62b [7]
Rabbinic culture made the married scholar, not the celibate ascetic, the ideal type; an unmarried man was considered incomplete for religious as well as social purposes.
Both traditions put the householder, not the solitary religious striver, at the top of the scale.
Buddhism
Instructing the layman Dighajanu, who says he cannot live as a renunciant, the Buddha lays out four conditions for welfare in this life — persistent effort, protecting what one earns, good friendship, and balanced livelihood — and four for the life to come, treating lay life as a complete and honourable path.
— Anguttara Nikaya 8.54, Dighajanu (Vyagghapajja) Sutta [8]
The Buddha gives householders a full curriculum rather than a consolation prize, though the monastic life remains the higher road in the Theravada scheme.
It affirms the Kural's respect for the striving householder without going as far as ranking him first.
Jainism
When Gautama, Mahāvīra's chief disciple, doubted the lay votary Ānanda's claim to have attained avadhi-jñāna (clairvoyant knowledge), Mahāvīra told him Ānanda was right and that Gautama himself should ask forgiveness, and Gautama went back to the householder and apologised.
— Uvāsagadasāo (Upāsakadaśāḥ) ch. 1, story of Ānanda (tr. A. F. R. Hoernlé, 1890); retold in Jain Story Series, 'Anand Shravak' [9]
In the Uvāsagadasāo a disciplined householder reaches a degree of spiritual knowledge that the foremost monk had wrongly doubted, and the Jina publicly confirms the layman's standing.
Valluvar places the true householder at the head of all who strive, and the story of Ānanda has Mahāvīra rank a householder's attainment above the senior monk's judgment.
Sikhism
“Yoga is not the patched coat, Yoga is not the walking stick. Yoga is not smearing the body with ashes... Remaining unblemished in the midst of the filth of the world - this is the way to attain Yoga.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 730, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Answering yogis, Guru Nanak lists the outward marks of the ascetic striver one by one and rejects them all, placing true discipline in staying pure while living in the world.
Like the Kural's householder who stands at the head of all who strive, the person who stays pure in worldly life is here given the very title the ascetics claimed.
Greek Mythology
“So Zeus the Father gave her an high honour instead of marriage, and she has her place in the midst of the house and has the richest portion. In all the temples of the gods she has a share of honour, and among all mortal men she is chief of the goddesses.”
— Homeric Hymn 5, To Aphrodite, ll. 21-32 (Evelyn-White) [11]
Hestia, goddess of the hearth at the centre of every Greek house, received the first portion of every sacrifice; the household fire was literally the first claim in Greek worship.
Greek cult makes the household hearth the foremost object of honour, as the Kural makes the householder foremost among all who strive.
Philosophy
“Remember that you are an actor in a drama, of such a kind as the author pleases to make it. If short, of a short one; if long, of a long one. … For this is your business, to act well the character assigned you; to choose it is another's.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 17 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [12]
Epictetus insists that excellence consists in performing well the role one actually has, not in acquiring a grander role.
The Kural's இயல்பினான் says the same: greatness comes from living domestic life according to its own proper nature, not from trading it for a loftier path.

Real Life Example

Guru Nanak settles as a farmer at Kartarpur
c. 1520-1539 · Kartarpur, Punjab (now in Pakistan)
After about twenty years of travel preaching his message, Guru Nanak settled again with his family in the Punjab, became a farmer and established the village of Kartarpur. Disciples gathered around him in a community of work and worship, and he devoted the last twenty years of his life to shaping the first Sikh community there. Before his death in 1539 he appointed a faithful follower, Guru Angad, as his successor.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The founder of Sikhism chose to lead his community as a working family man rather than as a wandering ascetic, setting a pattern for the faith that followed.
The kural says one who lives household life as it should be lived stands foremost among all who strive, and Guru Nanak made a farming family life the base from which he guided his followers.
