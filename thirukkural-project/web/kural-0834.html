Kural 834

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

ஓதி உணர்ந்தும் பிறர்க்குரைத்தும் தானடங்காப் பேதையின் பேதையார் இல்.
Odhi unarndhum pirarkkuraiththum thaanadangaap pedhaiyin pedhaiyaar il.

Meaning

No one is a bigger fool than someone who has studied, understood, and even taught others, yet does not live by what they learned.

Elementary

Imagine a swimming coach who teaches everyone how to stay safe in the pool but then jumps into the deep end without looking. Knowing the rules and telling others is not enough; the biggest silliness is not following them yourself.

University

The couplet climbs three steps, odhi (ஓதி, having recited or studied), unarndhum (உணர்ந்தும், having understood) and pirarkku uraiththum (பிறர்க்கு உரைத்தும், having explained to others), and then collapses on thaan adangaa (தான் அடங்கா, himself not restrained, not conforming). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar take adangudhal as living within the discipline the texts teach, and the superlative pedhaiyin pedhaiyaar il (‘there is no fool beyond this fool’) makes the learned hypocrite the extreme case of the chapter. Puliyur Kesigan glosses adangaa as lacking self-restraint (adakkam), linking the couplet to the chapter on Adakkamudaimai. All four commentaries and the two base texts agree on the reading.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A well-known verse of the Hitopadesha says that people remain fools even after studying the scriptures, and that the one who acts on them is the truly learned person.
— Hitopadesha, Mitralabha (verse on ‘kriyavan’) [3]
The Sanskrit fable collection holds that learning proves itself only in practice.
Valluvar makes the same point: knowing and teaching without living it is the deepest folly.
Christianity
“All therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works: for they say, and do not.”
— Matthew 23:3 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells the crowds to follow what the teachers of the law teach but not to copy their conduct, because they do not practise it.
‘They say, and do not’ is Valluvar’s learned fool in four words.
Islam
“Do you order righteousness of the people and forget yourselves while you recite the Scripture? Then will you not reason?”
— Qur’an 2:44 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an rebukes those who preach goodness and recite scripture but neglect it in their own lives, and calls this a failure of reason.
Both texts name reciting, preaching, and not practising as the height of unreason.
Judaism
“Study is not the most important thing, but actions.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:17 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Shimon son of Rabban Gamaliel teaches that learning finds its purpose in deeds.
Valluvar likewise judges study worthless, even foolish, without conduct to match.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares the learned non-practitioner to a hired herdsman counting cattle that are not his.
Both texts say that reciting without practising gains a person nothing.
Jainism
“Acknowledging the truth about bondage and liberation, but talking only, not acting (in accordance with these tenets), they seek comfort for themselves in mighty words. Clever talking will not work salvation; how should philosophical instruction do it? Fools, though sinking lower and lower through their sins, believe themselves to be wise men.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.9-10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In 'The False Ascetic' Mahāvīra rebukes those who know and speak the doctrine but do not live by it, calling them fools who think themselves wise.
Valluvar says no fool is greater than one who has studied, understood and taught, yet does not himself conform; the Uttarādhyayana likewise calls those who talk the truth without acting on it 'fools' who sink while believing themselves wise.
Sikhism
“He says one thing, and does something else. There is no love in his heart, and yet with his mouth he talks tall. … One who does not practice what he preaches to others, shall come and go in reincarnation, through birth and death.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 269, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib (ashtapadi 5, stanza 7), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sukhmani Sahib Guru Arjan condemns the person who teaches others but does not live by it, and says such a one stays bound to birth and death.
The Kural calls the person who studies, understands and teaches others but does not live by it the greatest fool; Guru Arjan names the same failure and its cost.
Greek Mythology
Plato recounts the story of Thales, who fell into a well while gazing at the stars, and a Thracian servant girl joked that he was eager to know the heavens but could not see what lay at his feet.
— Plato, Theaetetus 174a [10]
The anecdote mocks learning that never reaches one’s own steps.
Like Valluvar’s fool, the scholar knows much but fails in his own conduct.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises never to call yourself a philosopher or talk about principles among ordinary people, but to act on them, just as sheep show how much they have eaten not by bringing up grass but by producing wool and milk.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 46 [11]
For Epictetus, philosophy is proven by conduct, not by lecturing.
Valluvar equally scorns the one who teaches but does not live the teaching.

Real Life Example

Charles Van Doren admits the quiz show Twenty-One was rigged
2 November 1959 · Washington, D.C., United States
Charles Van Doren, an English instructor at Columbia University from a celebrated literary family, became a national celebrity by winning $129,000 on the NBC quiz show Twenty-One in 1956-57. After denying the rigging to the press, the public and a grand jury, he testified before a congressional committee in November 1959 that he had been given the answers in advance and coached on how to perform. Columbia accepted his resignation, NBC ended his contract, and he later pleaded guilty to perjury and received a suspended sentence.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A scholar who taught literature and was celebrated as a model of learning had taken part in a deception watched by millions.
The kural says no fool is greater than one who has studied, understood and taught others yet does not live by what he learned, and Van Doren, a university teacher, lived for years in a public lie.
