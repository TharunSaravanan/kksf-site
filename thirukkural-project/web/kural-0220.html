Kural 220

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

ஒப்புரவி னால்வரும் கேடெனின் அஃதொருவன் விற்றுக்கோள் தக்க துடைத்து.
Oppuravi naalvarum kedenin aqdhoruvan Vitrukkol thakka thudaiththu.

Meaning

If helping others leads to ruin, that ruin is worth having, even if you had to sell yourself to get it.

Elementary

Some people say, ‘If you keep giving, you will end up with nothing!’ This kural answers: even if that happens, it is worth it — being someone who helps is worth more than anything you could lose.

University

The closing couplet answers a prudential objection: oppuravu may lead to kedu, material loss. Valluvar's reply is deliberately extreme — such loss is worth acquiring by selling oneself — and Parimelazhagar notes the logic: nothing is normally bought by selling oneself, so this loss is valued above everything, because it yields lasting renown; hence ‘ruin through oppuravu is not ruin.’ Manakkudavar reframes it as a trade in which one thing is sold to buy a greater, so the apparent loss should be counted as gain. The hyperbole caps the chapter's argument: from rain (211) through wealth (212-217) and poverty (218-219) to self-sacrifice (220), oppuravu is presented as a value that no calculation of self-interest can outweigh.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Harishchandra, to honour his promise of a gift to the sage Vishvamitra, gives away his kingdom, sells his wife and son, and finally sells himself into servitude at a cremation ground.
— Markandeya Purana, Harishchandra legend (chs. 7–8), as summarised in secondary sources [4]
In the Markandeya Purana, Harishchandra literally sells himself rather than go back on his pledged gift, and is ultimately exalted by the gods.
A literal enactment of the Kural's ‘worth buying by selling oneself.’
Christianity
“Jesus said unto him, If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven: and come and follow me.”
— Matthew 19:21 (KJV) [5]
Jesus tells a rich young man to sell everything he owns and give to the poor.
Shares the Kural's verdict that material ruin for the sake of others is a worthwhile exchange.
Islam
“And of the people is he who sells himself, seeking means to the approval of Allah. And Allah is kind to [His] servants.”
— Qur'an 2:207 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an praises the one who ‘sells himself’ — gives up his very self — to seek God's pleasure.
Uses the same language of selling oneself as the Kural for a supremely worthwhile sacrifice.
Judaism
“In Usha the Sages instituted that one who dispenses his money to charity should not dispense more than one-fifth ... lest he render himself destitute and need the help of other people.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 50a (Sefaria, William Davidson ed.) [7]
The rabbis limited charitable giving to a fifth of one's assets so that the giver would not become dependent on others.
An honest contrast: Jewish law praises charity but deliberately guards against the self-ruin the Kural welcomes.
Buddhism
“If it so happens that, when a disciple of the noble ones obtains these five benefits from wealth, his wealth goes to depletion, the thought occurs to him, ‘Even though my wealth has gone to depletion, I have obtained the five benefits that can be obtained from wealth,’ and he feels no remorse.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.41, Adiya Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha says that if wealth runs out because it was used well — for family, friends, guests, and the virtuous — the wise feel no regret.
Like the Kural, it treats loss that comes from good use of wealth as no real loss.
Jainism
“The king said to him, “I will give you my own flesh, weighing it with the dove. Be satisfied. Do not die.” … When the king saw that the dove kept increasing in weight, he himself got on the scales with unequaled courage.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book V (Śāntināthacaritra), ch. IV, part 7 'Story of the dove and hawk' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [9]
King Megharatha, in a former birth of the sixteenth Tīrthaṅkara Śāntinātha, protects a dove from a hawk by offering his own flesh and finally his whole body, while his ministers protest at the loss.
Valluvar says that if helping others brings ruin, that ruin is worth buying even by selling oneself; Megharatha literally puts himself on the scales to save another being.
Sikhism
Guru Tegh Bahadur, the ninth Sikh Guru, was executed by the Mughal authorities in Delhi in 1675. He became the second Sikh martyr and is remembered particularly for his stand for religious freedom.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Tegh Bahadur (paraphrase) [10]
Sikhs honour Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom as giving his own life in defence of the freedom of conscience of others.
The Kural says that if helping others leads to ruin, that ruin is worth buying even by selling oneself; Guru Tegh Bahadur paid with his own life for others' freedom of faith.
Greek Mythology
Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humankind, and Zeus punishes him by chaining him to a rock where an eagle eats his liver each day.
— Hesiod, Theogony 507–616 [11]
Prometheus accepts terrible suffering as the price of benefiting humanity.
Embodies the Kural's idea that ruin incurred for others' good is worth bearing.
Philosophy
“I should not have neglected all my own concerns or patiently seen the neglect of them during all these years, and have been doing yours ... And I have a sufficient witness to the truth of what I say—my poverty.”
— Plato, Apology 31b–c (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Socrates tells the jury that he neglected his own affairs to serve Athens, and his poverty proves it.
Like the Kural's benefactor, Socrates accepts material ruin as the price of serving others.

Real Life Example

Oskar Schindler spends his fortune to protect his Jewish workers
October 1944 - May 1945 · Brunnlitz (Brnenec), Sudetenland (now Czech Republic)
Oskar Schindler, a German businessman and Nazi Party member who had grown rich in occupied Krakow, moved his armaments factory to Brunnlitz in 1944 and devoted himself until liberation in May 1945 to protecting its roughly 1,000 Jewish prisoners, paying for scarce food and medicine himself and deceiving the SS with false production figures. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum records that this required him to spend the fortune he had made in Krakow; he later claimed to have spent 2.64 million Reichsmarks. After the war he struggled financially and failed in business, and in 1993 Yad Vashem named him and his wife Emilie Righteous Among the Nations.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Schindler gave up a fortune and ended his life in financial hardship, but the more than 1,000 people he helped are why he is remembered.
The kural says that if helping others brings ruin, that ruin is worth buying even by selling oneself, and Schindler exchanged his wealth for the survival of the people in his care.
