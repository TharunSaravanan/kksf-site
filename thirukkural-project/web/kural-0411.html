Kural 411

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

செல்வத்துள் செல்வம் செவிச்செல்வம் அச்செல்வம் செல்வத்துள் எல்லாம் தலை.
Selvaththul selvam sevichchelvam achchelvam Selvaththul ellaam thalai.

Meaning

The riches you gain through your ears, by listening to good teaching, are the treasure of all treasures. Of all riches, they come first.

Elementary

People think of money, gold and houses as riches. Valluvar says the best treasure of all is what you gather by listening carefully to wise people. That kind of treasure goes into your mind, nobody can steal it, and it is worth more than any other.

University

The chapter opens with a deliberately repetitive line — செல்வம் occurs four times — which enacts its claim: among all the ‘wealths’, செவிச்செல்வம் (the wealth of the ear) is தலை, the head or chief. Placed in Porutpaal immediately after the chapters on learning (கல்வி) and its lack (கல்லாமை), this chapter supplements book-learning with கேள்வி, knowledge received orally from the learned — the dominant mode of transmitting knowledge in a largely oral culture. Parimelazhagar notes that hearing is especially valuable because it is available even to those who cannot study texts themselves, and because the ear receives the distilled result of another’s long learning. Calling it the ‘wealth’ of a treatise on polity is pointed: Valluvar ranks a cognitive possession above the material resources the rest of the book is about.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Vedas themselves are called śruti, “that which is heard”; and in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad Yajnavalkya tells Maitreyi that the Self is to be seen, to be heard (śrotavyaḥ), reflected upon and meditated upon — after she has refused his offer of worldly wealth, asking what use riches are that cannot make her immortal.
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.1–5 (also 4.5) [4]
Maitreyi turns down her husband’s property and asks instead to hear his teaching; Yajnavalkya then names hearing as the first step toward knowing the Self, and the whole Vedic canon is named after the act of hearing.
Maitreyi’s choice is the Kural’s ranking acted out: the wealth received through the ear is preferred over all material wealth.
Christianity
“But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part, which shall not be taken away from her.”
— Luke 10:39–42 (KJV) [5]
Martha is busy serving while her sister Mary sits at Jesus’ feet listening to his word; Jesus says Mary chose the better portion, one that cannot be taken away.
Mary’s ‘good part’ is precisely a treasure acquired by listening — like the Kural’s ear-wealth, it is the one possession that cannot be lost.
Islam
“…and say, ‘My Lord, increase me in knowledge.’”
— Qur’an 20:114 (Sahih International) [6]
The Prophet is instructed to ask God for an increase in knowledge — classical commentators note that knowledge is the only thing he is commanded to ask more of. Early Islamic knowledge was transmitted aurally (samāʿ, ‘hearing’ from a teacher).
Knowledge, not wealth, is the thing worth seeking more of — echoing the Kural’s ranking of ear-acquired knowledge above all riches.
Judaism
“Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:”
— Deuteronomy 6:4 (KJV) [5]
The Shema, recited twice daily, is the central declaration of Jewish faith, and it begins with the imperative ‘Hear’. Jewish tradition treats hearing (shemaʿ, also ‘obey/understand’) as the foundational religious act.
Both traditions place hearing at the head of all things — the Shema makes listening the gateway to faith; the Kural makes it the chief of all wealth.
Buddhism
The Buddha lists seven ‘treasures’ (dhana) of a noble disciple: faith, virtue, conscience, concern for wrongdoing, learning (suta-dhana — literally ‘the treasure of what has been heard’), generosity and wisdom — declaring these superior to any worldly treasure.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 7.5–7.6 (Dhana Sutta) [7]
Suta means ‘heard’; a learned person in early Buddhism is bahussuta, ‘one who has heard much’. The Buddha explicitly calls this a form of wealth that cannot be taken by kings, thieves, fire or flood.
An almost literal parallel to செவிச்செல்வம்: hearing counted as a treasure, and ranked above material wealth.
Jainism
“Four things of paramount value are difficult to obtain here by a living being: human birth, instruction in the Law, belief in it, and energy in self-control.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 3.1 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jainism lists hearing the Law (Prakrit sui, Sanskrit sruti, i.e. hearing) among the four rarest and most valuable things a soul can gain. Jacobi translates it as 'instruction in the Law'.
Valluvar calls the wealth of the ear foremost among all kinds of wealth. The Uttaradhyayana likewise ranks hearing the Law among the few gifts of 'paramount value'.
Sikhism
“Listening-truth, contentment and spiritual wisdom.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib (pauri 10), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the tenth pauri of Japji Sahib Guru Nanak lists what deep listening to the Name brings, starting with truth, contentment and spiritual wisdom.
The Kural calls the wealth gained through the ear the foremost of all wealth; Japji says the highest treasures, truth, contentment and wisdom, come through listening.
Greek Mythology
Agamemnon, after hearing Nestor’s counsel at Troy, wishes he had ten such counsellors among the Achaeans — then, he says, Priam’s city would soon fall.
— Homer, Iliad 2.370–374 [10]
The commander of the largest Greek host values one man’s spoken advice more than numbers of soldiers or treasure.
Nestor’s counsel is wealth received through the ear; the most powerful king in the story ranks it above his other resources.
Philosophy
Plutarch opens his essay On Listening by arguing that right listening is the beginning of right living, since the young receive virtue first through the ear, the one sense by which reason enters the soul.
— Plutarch, Moralia, De auditu (On Listening to Lectures) 1–2 [11]
Plutarch treats hearing as the principal channel of moral formation, superior to the other senses because speech (logos) arrives through it.
Like Valluvar, Plutarch places the ear first among the means of acquiring what is truly valuable.

Real Life Example

Charlemagne learns by listening at his court
c. 780-814 · Aachen, Frankish Empire (now Germany)
According to his courtier and biographer Einhard, Charlemagne had books read aloud to him at meals, especially histories and Augustine's City of God, and took lessons in rhetoric, logic and astronomy from the English scholar Alcuin of York, whom he invited around 781 to lead the circle of learning at his court. Einhard also records that the emperor tried to learn to write late in life, keeping writing tablets under his pillow, but with little success. Charlemagne's patronage of scholars helped launch the revival of learning known as the Carolingian Renaissance.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The ruler of the largest Western empire since Rome, who never mastered writing, acquired wide learning chiefly through his ears and made the pursuit of learning a priority of his reign.
The kural calls the wealth gained through the ear the foremost of all riches, and Charlemagne, master of vast lands and treasure, built much of his knowledge on what he heard read and taught.
