Kural 45

Domestic Life · Ilvaazhkkai · இல்வாழ்க்கை

அன்பும் அறனும் உடைத்தாயின் இல்வாழ்க்கை பண்பும் பயனும் அது.
Anpum aranum utaiththaayin ilvaazhkkai, Panpum payanum adhu.

Meaning

If family life has love and goodness, then that is both its true nature and its reward.

Elementary

Think of a fruit tree that is both beautiful and gives fruit. You don't grow it for some other prize somewhere else — the tree being what it is *is* the prize. A home with love and goodness in it is the same: it is not a step toward the good life, it already is the good life.

University

The verse turns on a deliberate identity statement: பண்பும் பயனும் அது — “its quality and its fruit are *that*.” Valluvar collapses the usual ethical distinction between a virtue and the reward the virtue earns. அன்பு (anpu, love) and அறன் (aran, virtue/dharma) are not instruments for obtaining household happiness; they *constitute* it. This is a genuinely Aristotelian move — the household, rightly constituted, is an end in itself, not a means — and it is what allows Valluvar in the following verses to argue that the householder need not look elsewhere (46) and outranks the ascetic (48). அன்பு gets a full chapter later (8, அன்புடைமை); here it is paired with அறன் so that affection alone is not enough, nor correctness alone.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“In that family, where the husband is pleased with his wife and the wife with her husband, happiness will assuredly be lasting.”
— Manusmriti 3.60 (Bühler, Sacred Books of the East XXV) [4]
Amid a chapter of ritual prescriptions, Manu states plainly that mutual delight between spouses is the condition of a household's durable well-being.
Both texts locate the household's happiness in the quality of affection inside it rather than in any external result.
Christianity
“And above all these things put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness.”
— Colossians 3:14 (KJV) [5]
Writing to a household-based congregation, Paul lists the virtues and then names love as the binding agent that holds them together and completes them.
The Kural makes the same claim structurally: love plus virtue is not a route to completeness but is the completeness itself.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy. Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought.”
— Qur'an 30:21, Ar-Rum (Sahih International) [6]
Marriage is presented as a sign of God, its purpose being sakīnah (tranquillity), with mawaddah (affection) and raḥmah (mercy) placed between the couple.
Affection and mercy are named as the constituents of married life exactly as அன்பு and அறன் are in the Kural, and the tranquillity is the state itself, not a later payoff.
Judaism
“Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith.”
— Proverbs 15:17 (KJV, Tanakh) [5]
Proverbs weighs a household's material abundance against its emotional quality and decides unambiguously for love.
Both couplets say that the household's worth lies in the love inside it, not in what the household yields.
Buddhism
“If both husband & wife want to see one another not only in the present life but also in the life to come, they should be in tune [with each other] in conviction, in tune in virtue, in tune in generosity, and in tune in discernment.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
Answering the devoted couple Nakulapita and Nakulamata, the Buddha says that a marriage endures on shared moral qualities, not on feeling alone.
Like the Kural, it insists on the pairing: affection must be matched by shared virtue for domestic life to be what it should be.
Jainism
In the Uvāsagadasāo the wealthy merchant Ānanda of Vāṇijyagrāma takes the twelve lay vows from Mahāvīra, and his wife Śivanandā goes to Mahāvīra and takes the same vows, so that the couple share a single discipline as householders.
— Uvāsagadasāo (Upāsakadaśāḥ) ch. 1, story of Ānanda (tr. A. F. R. Hoernlé, 1890); summary at jainworld.com [8]
The first and model story of the Jain lay canon shows a household whose love is joined to shared observance of the vows, with both husband and wife counted as members of the fourfold community.
Valluvar says love joined with virtue is both the nature and the fruit of household life, and Ānanda and Śivanandā are the Jain canon's model of that union of affection and dharma in one home.
Sikhism
“They are not said to be husband and wife, who merely sit together. They alone are called husband and wife, who have one light in two bodies.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 788, Raag Soohee, Soohee ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das defines marriage not by living under one roof but by an inner unity of love and spirit, a verse often read at Sikh weddings.
Like Valluvar, who says love and virtue are both the nature and the fruit of domestic life, the Guru places the whole meaning of the household in the shared inner life of the couple.
Greek Mythology
“May heaven grant you in all things your heart's desire — husband, house, and a happy, peaceful home; for there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house. It discomfits their enemies, makes the hearts of their friends glad, and they themselves know more about it than any one.”
— Homer, Odyssey VI.180-185 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus, shipwrecked and naked, blesses Nausicaa with the highest good he can name: homophrosynē, the like-mindedness of husband and wife.
This is the Kural's claim in Homeric form — that a household of shared mind and heart is simply the best thing there is, not a means to something better.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that the friendship between husband and wife exists by nature, combines the useful and the pleasant, and becomes best of all when the partners are good, so that each delights in the other's virtue; on that basis children too are a bond.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.12 [11]
For Aristotle the highest form of philia is between good people who love each other for their virtue, and he explicitly applies this to marriage.
This is the Kural's exact combination — love and virtue together — as what makes a household both what it ought to be and worth having.

Real Life Example

The von Trapp family leaves Nazi-annexed Austria
1938 · Salzburg, Austria, to Stowe, Vermont, United States
The von Trapps of Salzburg had turned their family love of singing into a professional choir after losing most of their wealth when their bank failed in the early 1930s. After Nazi Germany annexed Austria in 1938, Georg von Trapp refused to fly the Nazi flag on their house, declined a naval command and turned down a request for the family to sing at Hitler's birthday party. Unwilling to compromise their principles, the family left Austria by train for Italy and went on to America, where they toured as the Trapp Family Singers and settled on a farm in Stowe, Vermont.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The real von Trapps chose to give up home and possessions together rather than serve a regime they abhorred, and they rebuilt their life as a family.
The kural says that love and virtue are both the character and the reward of domestic life, and the von Trapps, bound by affection and holding to principle, found their wealth in each other rather than in what they left behind.
