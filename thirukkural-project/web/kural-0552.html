Kural 552

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

வேலொடு நின்றான் இடுவென் றதுபோலும் கோலொடு நின்றான் இரவு.
Velodu nindraan iduven rathupolum Kolodu nindraan iravu.

Meaning

When a king, who has the power to punish, asks his people for money, it is like a robber with a spear on a lonely road saying, “Hand it over.”

Elementary

If a big kid holding a stick says ‘Can I please have your lunch?’, it isn’t really a question, because you know what happens if you say no. Valluvar says that when a king with power over you ‘asks’ for extra money, it is the same as a robber demanding it.

University

The image is of the solitary highwayman (வேலொடு நின்றான்) and the king ‘standing with the rod’ (கோலொடு நின்றான்), the rod here meaning the power to punish (ஒறுத்தல் தொழில்). Parimelazhagar draws two inferences: ‘standing with a spear’ implies the robber is alone on a deserted road, and the word இரவு (begging, request) shows that what the king asks for is not the lawful revenue (இறைப்பொருள் அன்மை). Because the king’s request carries an unspoken threat to punish anyone who refuses, even ‘asking’ becomes tyranny; he adds that kurals 551-552 together state the fault of tyranny itself. Manakkudavar reads கோலொடு நிற்றல் as standing in the posture of one who administers justice, and adds that even a voluntary-seeming levy above the fixed dues is like robbery on the road. Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar take the couplet as a comment on extortionate taxation. The text is the same across the sources consulted.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu warns that a king who gives no protection yet takes his share of the produce, his taxes, tolls and fines will soon sink into hell, since he takes on the sins of the people he fails to protect.
— Manusmṛti 8.307-308 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
In the Dharmashastra, taxation is fair only as payment for protection; without it, the levy becomes a sin.
This is the Kural’s point: a levy without righteous rule is just robbery.
Christianity
“And he said unto them, Exact no more than that which is appointed you. … Do violence to no man, neither accuse any falsely; and be content with your wages.”
— Luke 3:13-14 (KJV) [5]
John the Baptist tells tax collectors and soldiers, the two faces of state power, not to extort beyond what is due.
Like Valluvar, John treats demands backed by force above the lawful due as extortion.
Islam
After the woman of Ghamid was stoned for adultery she had confessed, the Prophet said her repentance was so sincere that even a collector of unjust taxes (ṣāḥib maks) would be forgiven if he repented like that.
— Sahih Muslim 1695 [6]
The hadith treats the taker of unjust levies as the extreme case of a grave sinner.
It shares the Kural’s view that exacting money by the power of office is among the worst wrongs.
Judaism
When Israel asks for a king, Samuel warns that the king will take their sons, their daughters, their best fields and vineyards, and a tenth of their grain and flocks, and they will become his servants.
— 1 Samuel 8:11-17 (JPS 1917) [7]
The prophet describes kingship as, by nature, taking from the people.
Samuel’s ‘he will take’ corresponds to the king’s demand in the Kural, which his subjects cannot refuse.
Buddhism
A wise chaplain advises King Mahāvijita that a kingdom plagued by bandits cannot be cured by more levies and punishments; instead the king should give seed to farmers, capital to traders and wages to officials, and the country then becomes prosperous and safe.
— Dīgha Nikāya 5, Kūṭadanta Sutta [8]
The Kūṭadanta Sutta sets out a Buddhist economics of rule that relies on giving, not taking.
It is the positive counterpart to the Kural’s warning against a king who rules by demanding.
Jainism
“A man who returns, as it were, to the vomit, is not praised; but you want to confiscate the property left by the Brâhmana.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Queen Kamalāvatī shames King Ishukāra for trying to seize the property a Brahmin gave up when he renounced the world. Jacobi notes that confiscating heirless property was then a royal privilege.
The Kural likens a king who demands his subjects' wealth to a highwayman with a spear. The queen likewise condemns a ruler's grab for his subjects' goods as shameful.
Sikhism
“Bringing the marriage party of sin, Babar has invaded from Kaabul, demanding our land as his wedding gift, O Lalo.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 722, Raag Tilang (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Addressing his disciple Bhai Lalo, Guru Nanak describes Babar's invasion as a sinful wedding party that seizes by force what it pretends to receive as a gift.
The Kural likens a sceptred king's demand for money to a highwayman with a spear saying ‘Hand it over’; Guru Nanak likewise exposes armed power that takes wealth by force under a respectable name.
Greek Mythology
On his road to Athens the young Theseus killed a series of highway robbers, including Sciron, who made travellers wash his feet and then kicked them off the cliff, and Sinis the pine-bender. By clearing the road he showed what kind of king he would be.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 8-10 [11]
In Plutarch’s account, a true ruler is the one who ends robbery on the roads, not the one who practises it.
Valluvar’s image of the road robber stands for the king who has forgotten this duty.
Philosophy
“Justice being taken away, then, what are kingdoms but great robberies? For what are robberies themselves, but little kingdoms?”
— Augustine, City of God IV.4 (trans. Marcus Dods) [12]
Augustine argues that without justice, the state is simply a robber band on a larger scale.
This is the Kural’s simile turned into a general principle: a king without justice is just a robber.

Real Life Example

Charles I's forced loan and the Five Knights' Case
1626-1628 · England
Unable to get the taxes he wanted from Parliament, Charles I demanded a forced loan from his subjects in 1626-27 and had more than 70 gentlemen who refused to pay imprisoned. Five of them, led by Sir Thomas Darnel, sought release through the courts in the Five Knights' Case of 1627, but the judges refused bail. In 1628 Parliament answered with the Petition of Right, which declared that no one should be taxed without Parliament's consent or imprisoned without cause shown.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The king called his demand a loan, but because refusal meant prison it was really taking by force.
The kural says that when a ruler holding the sceptre asks his subjects for money it is like a robber with a spear saying 'hand it over', which is exactly how Charles I's forced loan worked.
