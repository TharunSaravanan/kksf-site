Kural 233

Renown · Pukazh · புகழ்

ஒன்றா உலகத்து உயர்ந்த புகழல்லால் பொன்றாது நிற்பதொன் றில்.
Ondraa ulagaththu uyarndha pukazhallaal Pondraadhu nirpadhu ondril.

Meaning

In this world, nothing lasts forever except a great good name, which has no equal.

Elementary

Toys break, houses get old, and even mountains slowly wear down. But a story about someone who was truly good can be told again and again for a thousand years. Good fame is the one thing that doesn’t wear out.

University

The syntax hinges on ஒன்றா, which commentators read two ways: as qualifying புகழ் (‘peerless, matchless renown’) or as describing உலகு (‘this world, where nothing stays constant’). Either way, the couplet sets renown against the Indian commonplace of impermanence (நிலையாமை), which Valluvar treats in its own chapter (34): bodies, riches and kingdoms are பொன்றும் (perishable), but உயர்ந்த புகழ், exalted renown, alone stands. This is Valluvar’s this-worldly answer to the question of immortality: not the soul’s survival, but the survival of a good name in human memory. The claim parallels the Greek heroic ideal of kleos aphthiton (‘imperishable fame’), though Valluvar grounds renown in giving rather than martial glory. Philosophers such as the Stoics would dispute it, counting reputation among things outside our control and therefore not a true good.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for one who has been honored, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna warns Arjuna that fleeing battle would earn him a disgrace that people would talk about forever (the Sanskrit calls the ill-fame avyayā, ‘imperishable’).
The Gita presupposes the Kural’s point from the negative side: reputation, good or bad, outlasts the person and is what endures.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt.”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts perishable earthly wealth with imperishable treasure stored with God.
Both texts sort things into the perishable and the imperishable, but Jesus locates the imperishable in heaven, Valluvar in renown on earth.
Islam
“And grant me a mention [i.e., reputation] of honor among later generations.”
— Qur’an 26:84 (Sahih International) [5]
Abraham prays that he will be remembered with honour by the generations who come after him.
A prophet explicitly asks for the very thing the Kural calls the one imperishable possession: an honoured name that outlives him.
Judaism
“The memory of the just is blessed: but the name of the wicked shall rot.”
— Proverbs 10:7 (Tanakh; KJV rendering) [4]
Proverbs says a righteous person’s memory remains a blessing, while the wicked person’s name decays.
Only the good name survives decay: the same contrast between lasting renown and everything that rots away.
Buddhism
“Even gorgeous royal chariots wear out, and indeed this body too wears out. But the Dhamma of the Good does not age; thus the Good make it known to the good.”
— Dhammapada 151 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha observes that splendid possessions and the body itself decay, but the teaching and way of the good do not grow old.
Both divide the world into what wears out and the one thing that does not; for Valluvar it is renown, for the Dhammapada the Dhamma of the good.
Jainism
“Whenever you die, O king, and leave all pleasant things behind, the Law alone, and nothing else in this world, will save you, O monarch.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Queen Kamalāvatī reminds King Iṣukāra that at death every possession is left behind, and only dharma goes with a person.
Valluvar says nothing in this world lasts except matchless renown. The Jain text also picks out one thing that survives impermanence, but that thing is dharma, not fame.
Sikhism
“Raam Chand passed away, as did Raawan, even though he had lots of relatives. Says Nanak, nothing lasts forever; the world is like a dream.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1429, Salok Mahala 9, Salok 50, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Tegh Bahadur reminds the reader that even the most celebrated figures of legend, hero and villain alike, passed away, and that nothing in the world is permanent.
The Kural says nothing in the world endures except peerless renown; Guru Tegh Bahadur agrees that everything perishes but does not exempt fame, pointing instead to God alone as lasting.
Greek Mythology
“Thus even in death your fame, Achilles, has not been lost, and your name lives evermore among all mankind.”
— Homer, Odyssey 24 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
In the underworld, Agamemnon’s shade tells Achilles that his fame survived his death and lives on among all people.
This is kleos aphthiton, imperishable fame, the Greek counterpart of Valluvar’s புகழ் that alone does not perish.
Philosophy
“Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus lists reputation among external things that are not truly ours and should not be treated as goods.
A deliberate counterpoint: where the Kural calls renown the one lasting possession, the Stoic calls it something never really possessed at all.

Real Life Example

James Prinsep deciphers the edicts of Ashoka
1837 · Calcutta (Kolkata), India
Before the 1830s the Mauryan emperor Ashoka was known almost only from Buddhist chronicles. In 1837 the British scholar James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script of inscriptions on pillars and rocks, revealing edicts issued by a king who called himself Beloved of the Gods, Piyadasi. These edicts were identified as Ashoka's, restoring a major ruler to history after some two thousand years.
— Sources: [11] [12]
An emperor whose empire, cities and dynasty had all vanished was brought back to the world's memory through the record of his moral teaching carved in stone.
The kural says nothing in this world stands except exalted renown, and Ashoka's palaces perished while the edicts recording his ideals survived to restore his name.
