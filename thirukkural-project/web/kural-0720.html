Kural 720

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

அங்கணத்துள் உக்க அமிழ்தற்றால் தங்கணத்தார் அல்லார்முன் கோட்டி கொளல்.
Anganaththul ukka amizhdhatraal thanganaththaar Allaarmun kotti kolal.

Meaning

Speaking to people who are not like-minded is like spilling amrita (the drink that makes gods live forever) into a dirty drain.

Elementary

Imagine pouring the most delicious juice in the world down the drain. What a waste! Explaining something wonderful to people who don’t care at all is like that. The juice was good; it just went to the wrong place.

University

The chapter closes with its most memorable image. Amizhdhu (amrita, the nectar of immortality) is ukka (spilled) into an anganam, the drain of a house’s open courtyard: Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss it as an unclean courtyard, Solomon Pappaiah as a sewer. Thanganaththaar allaar are those who are not of one’s own gana (group, circle), meaning not one’s equals in learning and outlook. Kotti kolal (to hold a discourse) uses kotti, cognate with Sanskrit goshthi (assembly, learned conversation). The simile, like 718’s, is exact. Nectar is not spoiled by being spilled, but it can never be recovered or drunk, so it is wasted. The sources differ slightly on one form: ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net read தங்கணத்தார், while the sandhi-joined text reads “தங்கணத்த ரல்லார்முற்” (தங்கணத்தர்). The meaning is unaffected.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Even in times of dire distress a teacher of the Veda should rather die with his knowledge than sow it in barren soil.”
— Manusmriti 2.113 (with 2.112), trans. G. Bühler [4]
Manu tells the teacher that it is better to take his learning to the grave than to waste it on unfit recipients. The verse before it compares such teaching to good seed thrown on barren land.
Seed in barren soil and nectar in the gutter are the same waste: precious knowledge poured where it cannot be received.
Christianity
“Give not that which is holy unto the dogs, neither cast ye your pearls before swine, lest they trample them under their feet, and turn again and rend you.”
— Matthew 7:6 (KJV) [5]
Jesus warns against offering sacred things to those who will only trample them, and may turn on the giver.
Pearls before swine and nectar in the gutter are twin images of treasure wasted on the unworthy.
Islam
“The example of those who disbelieve is like that of one who shouts at what hears nothing but calls and cries … deaf, dumb and blind, so they do not understand.”
— Qur’an 2:171 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an likens preaching to those who will not understand to a herdsman shouting at animals that hear only noise.
Words wasted on hearers who cannot take them in are the Kural’s spilled nectar.
Judaism
“Speak not in the ears of a fool: for he will despise the wisdom of thy words.”
— Proverbs 23:9 (KJV) [5]
The proverb forbids speaking wisdom to a fool, who will only scorn it.
This is the Kural’s conclusion in plain words: wisdom poured before the wrong hearers is wasted.
Buddhism
Just after his awakening, the Buddha at first hesitates to teach. He reflects that the Dhamma is deep and subtle, and that a generation delighting in attachment would not understand it, which would only weary him. He agrees to teach only when he sees that some beings have little dust in their eyes.
— Ayacana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 6.1 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha weighs whether his teaching would simply be wasted on listeners unable to receive it.
The Buddha’s hesitation is the Kural’s worry about pouring nectar where it will be lost.
Jainism
“As a pig leaves a trough filled with grain to feed on faeces, so a brute (of a man) turns away from virtue, and takes to evil ways.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana uses this coarse image for the unfit pupil who turns from the good food of instruction to filth, and in the next verses says such a pupil is driven away.
The Kural's nectar spilled into a gutter and the Jain pig leaving grain for filth both picture something precious wasted on those unfit to receive it.
Sikhism
“If you take Ambrosial Nectar and irrigate the neem tree with it, still, says Kabeer, its natural qualities are not changed.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 481, Raag Aasaa, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir pictures nectar poured on the bitter neem: the nectar is wasted and the neem stays bitter. Commentators such as Sahib Singh read the hymn as warning against pressing spiritual teaching on the unreceptive.
The Kural likens speaking before those not of one's kind to nectar spilled into a gutter; Kabir likewise shows nectar wasted on something that cannot receive it.
Greek Mythology
Cassandra, given true prophecy by Apollo but cursed never to be believed, foretells the murder of Agamemnon and her own death. The elders of Argos listen, but they cannot grasp or accept her words.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1072-1330 (trans. H. W. Smyth) [10]
Her true and precious speech falls on hearers who cannot receive it, and it is completely lost.
Cassandra’s wasted prophecy is nectar spilled into the gutter, the Kural’s picture of words lost on the wrong audience.
Philosophy
“As Heraclitus says ‘asses would prefer sweepings to gold’; for food is pleasanter than gold to asses.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 10.5 (trans. W. D. Ross), quoting Heraclitus (DK B9) [11]
Aristotle quotes Heraclitus to show that different kinds of creatures take pleasure in different things. The unfit cannot value what is precious.
Gold before asses is the Kural’s nectar before those not of one’s kind: worth that the audience cannot recognise.

Real Life Example

Mendel's laws of heredity are presented in Brunn and then overlooked
1865-1900 · Brunn (now Brno, Czech Republic)
After years of experiments crossing garden peas, the Augustinian monk Gregor Mendel presented his results on the inheritance of traits to the Brunn Natural Science Society in 1865, and they were published in 1866 in the society's relatively obscure proceedings. The work, which laid the mathematical foundation of genetics, remained largely overlooked during his lifetime. It was rediscovered around 1900 by Carl Correns, Hugo de Vries and Erich Tschermak, some fifteen years after Mendel's death.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One of the most important discoveries in biology was offered to listeners and readers who did not grasp its value, and it lay unused for a generation.
The kural compares speaking before those who cannot share one's understanding to nectar spilled into a gutter, not spoiled but wasted, much as Mendel's insight lay unused for thirty-five years.
