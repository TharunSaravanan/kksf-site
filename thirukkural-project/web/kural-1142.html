Kural 1142

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

மலரன்ன கண்ணாள் அருமை அறியாது அலரெமக்கு ஈந்ததிவ் வூர்.
malaranna kaNNaaL arumai aRiyaadhu alaremakku eendhadhiv voor

Meaning

This town does not know how rare and hard to win the girl with flower-like eyes is. By spreading a rumor about us, it has done me a favor.

Elementary

Suppose there is a very special girl in your class whom everyone thinks is out of reach. One day people start saying, ‘Oh, she likes you!’ They are only gossiping, but the gossip makes her seem a little closer. The young man in this verse thanks the town for gossiping, because the town has no idea how precious she really is.

University

Parimelazhagar marks this as ‘itu um atu’, the same situation as 1141, the hero speaking to the tōḻi. Arumai (அருமை) here means ‘difficulty of attainment’, which he explains as caused by failed trysts (alla kuṟi) and obstacles (iṭaiyīṭu); the town, unaware of it, has made her seem ‘easy’ (eḷiyaḷ) and so has ‘given’ (īntatu) him the rumour, a word chosen because the rumour is his support. Malar aṉṉa kaṇṇāḷ, ‘she whose eyes are like flowers’ (Puliyur Kesikan specifies the kuvaḷai or blue water-lily), is a conventional epithet. Manakkudavar reads arumai as the nobility of her birth (iṟpiṟappu), so that the gossips speak of her as of a common girl. The text agrees on ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net and thirukkural.com.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Uttara Kanda, Rama asks his companion Bhadra what the citizens are saying, and learns that the townspeople are gossiping about Sita, questioning how he could take her back after she lived in Ravana’s house.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda (Bhadra’s report) (paraphrase) [4]
The people of Ayodhya talk about Sita without understanding her proven purity and worth.
Like the Kural’s town, Ayodhya talks without knowing the true rarity of the woman it discusses, though in the Ramayana the gossip brings sorrow, not help.
Christianity
“My dove, my undefiled is but one; she is the only one of her mother, she is the choice one of her that bare her. The daughters saw her, and blessed her; yea, the queens and the concubines, and they praised her.”
— Song of Solomon 6:9 (KJV) [5]
The lover in the Song insists his beloved is unique, and pictures everyone who sees her recognising it.
The Kural’s lover also knows his beloved is beyond compare; his complaint and his comfort is that the gossiping town has not seen it.
Islam
“So when she heard of their scheming, she sent for them and prepared for them a banquet and gave each one of them a knife and said [to Joseph], ‘Come out before them.’ And when they saw him, they greatly admired him and cut their hands and said, ‘Perfect is Allāh! This is not a man; this is none but a noble angel.’”
— Qur’an 12:31 (tr. Saheeh International) [6]
The women who gossiped about Zulaikha’s love change their tune the moment they see how extraordinary Joseph is.
This is exactly the Kural’s point: gossips talk about a love without knowing the rare worth of the beloved.
Judaism
Proverbs asks who can find a woman of valour, and answers that her price is far above rubies.
— Proverbs 31:10 (paraphrase; cf. JPS 1917) [7]
The closing poem of Proverbs celebrates a woman whose worth is rare and hard to find.
The Kural’s hero prizes his beloved for the same rarity (arumai) that the gossiping town fails to see.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that there never was, never will be and is not now anyone who is wholly blamed or wholly praised.
— Dhammapada 228 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The verse warns that public opinion is fickle and never a true measure of a person.
The town in the Kural misjudges the heroine’s worth, which illustrates exactly how unreliable public talk is.
Jainism
When Arishtanemi’s brother Rathanemi sees Rajimati alone and invites her to enjoy pleasures with him, she firmly rebukes him and recalls him to his monastic vows.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [9]
Rathanemi treats Rajimati as easily approachable and discovers her steadfastness and worth.
Parimelazhagar says the gossips make the heroine seem ‘easy’ (eḷiyaḷ) without knowing her rarity; Rathanemi makes the same mistake about Rajimati.
Sikhism
“One who slanders me is my friend; the slanderer is in my thoughts.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 339, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir turns the slanderer into a friend, because the talk against him serves his spiritual good.
In the Kural the town, not knowing how hard the girl is to win, hands the lover a rumour and so does him a favour; Kabir likewise counts the one who spreads talk about him a friend.
Greek Mythology
As Helen walks to the wall of Troy, the old men of the city murmur that it is no cause for blame that Trojans and Achaeans should suffer so long for such a woman, so like is she to the immortal goddesses, though they wish she would go home.
— Homer, Iliad 3.156-160 (paraphrase) [11]
The elders of Troy gossip about Helen, and in the same breath admit her extraordinary worth.
The Kural’s town gossips without seeing the heroine’s rarity; Homer’s elders gossip and do see it.
Philosophy
Montaigne argues that the judgment of the common crowd is too uncertain and ill-informed to be a measure of anyone’s true worth, and that a person should not live by it.
— Michel de Montaigne, Essays 2.16, ‘Of Glory’ (paraphrase) [12]
In his essay on glory, Montaigne doubts that public reputation ever tracks real merit.
The gossiping town in the Kural proves his point by talking about a woman whose real value it has not grasped.

Real Life Example

Tennyson and Emily Sellwood part, and a story spreads to explain it
1836-1850 · Lincolnshire and Shiplake, England
Alfred Tennyson became engaged to Emily Sellwood in the late 1830s, but in 1840 the engagement was broken off. Accounts differ on why: one says her family ended it after he lost money in a bad investment, while another says Tennyson himself broke it off over money and fears about his family's health, and that a story was put about that her father had forbidden the match in order to spare Emily embarrassment. They were reunited and married quietly in 1850, shortly after In Memoriam was published.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Talk about a broken engagement could shape how a couple was seen, yet their bond survived ten years apart.
The kural's lover is grateful that the town's rumour has linked him with the girl it does not know is so hard to win; Tennyson and Emily's story shows how what people said about a courtship could serve a purpose of its own.
