Kural 1087

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

கடாஅக் களிற்றின்மேற் கட்படாம் மாதர் படாஅ முலைமேல் துகில்.
Kadaaak kalitrinmer katpadaam maathar padaaa mulaimel thukil.

Meaning

The cloth over the young woman's chest is like the cloth placed over the eyes of a wild, angry elephant. It holds back a dangerous power.

Elementary

At festivals, big temple elephants sometimes wear a decorated cloth over their faces. It looks beautiful, but it also helps keep the elephant calm. The young man thinks the scarf the girl wears works the same way, both lovely and protective.

University

Kadaaak kalitru (கடாஅக் களிறு) is a bull elephant in must (mada), and katpadaam (கட்படாம்) the cloth hung over its face and eyes to keep it from charging. Thukil (துகில்) is fine cloth worn by the maathar (மாதர், young woman). The lengthened forms kadaaa and padaaa are alapedai, vowel extension for metre. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read the comparison as protective: the covering stops her beauty from 'killing' the onlooker, just as the face-cloth restrains the elephant. Pope: 'As veil o'er angry eyes / Of raging elephant that lies, / The silken cincture's folds invest / This maiden's panting breast.' Identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Bhagavad Gita says that a person of steady wisdom draws the senses back from their objects as a tortoise draws its limbs into its shell.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.58 (paraphrase) [3]
Restraint is pictured as a protective covering that keeps the senses from being dragged away.
The Kural's cloth is also a protective covering that keeps a dangerous force in check.
Christianity
“In like manner also, that women adorn themselves in modest apparel, with shamefacedness and sobriety; not with broided hair, or gold, or pearls, or costly array;”
— 1 Timothy 2:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul's letter recommends modest clothing over showy adornment.
The Kural, too, sees the covering garment as a modest restraint on beauty's power.
Islam
“...and to wrap [a portion of] their headcovers over their chests and not expose their adornment...”
— Qur'an 24:31 (Sahih International; excerpt) [5]
The Qur'an asks believing women to draw their head-coverings over their chests as a sign of modesty.
The Kural's image is of exactly such a covering cloth, which it treats as a restraint on beauty's force.
Judaism
When Rebekah first sees Isaac coming toward her across the field, she takes her veil and covers herself.
— Genesis 24:64-65 (paraphrase; cf. JPS Tanakh on Sefaria) [6]
In Genesis, covering oneself at a first meeting is a sign of modesty and dignity.
The Kural's covering cloth likewise marks modesty and calms the power of first sight.
Buddhism
“Formerly this mind wandered about as it liked, where it wished and according to its pleasure, but now I shall thoroughly master it with wisdom as a mahout controls with his ankus an elephant in rut.”
— Dhammapada 326 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada pictures the untrained mind as an elephant in rut that must be kept in check.
The Kural uses the same raging elephant and the means of restraining it.
Jainism
“Having heard these well-spoken words of the virtuous lady, he returned to the Law like an elephant driven by the hook.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.46 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
After Rajimati rebukes him, the monk Rathanemi is brought back under control like an elephant guided by the goad.
The Kural, too, pictures the restraint of a dangerous elephant-like power, which here is the stirred heart.
Sikhism
“The mind is the elephant, the Guru is the elephant-driver, and knowledge is the whip. Wherever the Guru drives the mind, it goes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 516, Raag Gujri, Gujri ki Vaar (salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Gujri ki Vaar, Guru Amar Das compares the unruly mind to an elephant that obeys only when the Guru, as its driver, controls it with knowledge.
The Kural compares the cloth on a maiden's bosom to the face-cloth that restrains a raging elephant; Guru Amar Das also uses the restrained elephant, here the mind held in check by the Guru.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Aphrodite lends Hera her embroidered band, in which love, desire and persuasive charm are woven, and its power overcomes even Zeus.
— Homer, Iliad 14.214-221 (paraphrase) [10]
Greek myth imagines a garment that holds the full power of attraction.
The Kural's garment holds back the same power that Aphrodite's band releases.
Philosophy
“If you see an attractive person, you will find that self-restraint is the ability you have against your desire.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 10 (tr. Elizabeth Carter, as revised on classics.mit.edu) [11]
Epictetus teaches that every situation calls out a matching inner power, and attraction calls out self-restraint.
The Kural describes an outward covering that restrains; Epictetus points to the inner one.

Real Life Example

Draperies are painted over Michelangelo's Last Judgment
1564 · Sistine Chapel, Vatican City
In 1564, after the Council of Trent, church authorities decided that some figures in Michelangelo's Last Judgment fresco in the Sistine Chapel were improper and should be covered. The work of painting draperies over them was given to Michelangelo's follower Daniele da Volterra, who was nicknamed Il Braghettone, the breeches-maker. Encyclopaedia Britannica dates his commission to 1559 under Pope Paul IV.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode is a well-known example of cloth being added to cover a powerful image whose effect authorities feared.
The kural compares a garment over the beloved to the cloth over a raging elephant's face, a covering meant to hold a dangerous power in check; the draperies were added for the same kind of reason.
