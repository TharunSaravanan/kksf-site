Kural 1154

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

அளித்தஞ்சல் என்றவர் நீப்பின் தெளித்தசொல் தேறியார்க்கு உண்டோ தவறு.
Aliththanjal endravar neeppin theliththasol thaeriyaarkku undo thavaru.

Meaning

If the one who lovingly said 'Do not be afraid' goes away, is it the fault of the one who believed his comforting words?

Elementary

Imagine a parent says, ‘Don’t worry, I’ll be right here,’ and then leaves the room. Would it be your fault for believing them? Of course not. The wife in this couplet asks the same question: her husband promised she need not fear, so how can she be blamed for trusting him?

University

Aḷittu (அளித்து) is ‘having shown grace, having loved’ and añcal (அஞ்சல்) is the prohibitive ‘do not fear’; together they recall the husband’s reassurance at the start of their life together, which Solomon Pappaiah places at the time of marriage. Nīppiṉ (நீப்பின்) is ‘if he leaves’, and teḷitta col tēṟiyārkku (தெளித்த சொல் தேறியார்க்கு) ‘to those who trusted the words that made them clear (free of doubt)’. The question uṇṭō tavaṟu (உண்டோ தவறு, ‘is there any fault?’) is rhetorical: the fault lies with the one who promised. Commentators agree on this reading; Pope: ‘If he depart, who fondly said, “Fear not,” what blame’s incurred / By those who trusted to his reassuring word?’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Dushyanta marries Shakuntala in the hermitage, gives her his signet ring and promises to send for her, but when she later comes to his court he does not recognise her and rejects her.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 5 (paraphrase) [3]
In Kalidasa’s play, Shakuntala is publicly shamed for having trusted the king’s word, though the fault lies with his forgetting (caused by a curse).
Shakuntala’s position is the Kural’s question acted out: is the one who trusted a lover’s promise to blame when he fails her?
Christianity
“Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid.”
— John 14:27 (KJV) [4]
On the night before he leaves them, Jesus tells his disciples not to be afraid, and he promises to come again to them.
Jesus’ ‘neither let it be afraid’ answers to the Kural’s añcal (‘fear not’); where the Kural fears a reassurance broken, the Gospel presents one meant to be kept.
Islam
“So We restored him to his mother that she might be content and not grieve and that she would know that the promise of Allah is true.”
— Qur’an 28:13 (Sahih International) [5]
Moses’ mother sets her baby adrift on God’s assurance that he will be returned to her, and the Qur’an stresses that the promise was kept.
The verse contrasts with the Kural: God’s ‘do not fear’ proves true, while the human lover’s reassurance is what the heroine fears will be broken.
Judaism
In Hosea, God promises to betroth Israel to Himself forever, in righteousness, justice, lovingkindness, compassion and faithfulness, so that the bride may trust that this time she will not be abandoned.
— Hosea 2:21-22 (Hebrew numbering; 2:19-20 in English Bibles) (paraphrase) [6]
The prophet uses the language of marriage to describe a faithfulness that does not break its word.
Hosea offers the kind of promise the Kural’s heroine longs to trust, and shows by contrast how much hurt a broken ‘fear not’ can do.
Buddhism
In Ashvaghosha’s life of the Buddha, Yashodhara, waking to find that Siddhartha has left the palace in the night, laments that he abandoned her while she slept and did not take her with him as ancient kings took their wives into the forest.
— Ashvaghosha, Buddhacarita, canto 8 (paraphrase) [7]
Yashodhara’s grief is sharpened by the sense that she trusted a husband who then left without warning.
She asks, in effect, the Kural’s question: what fault was it of mine, who trusted him, that he should go?
Jainism
Princess Rajimati is promised in marriage to Arishtanemi, but on the wedding day, seeing animals penned for the feast, he gives away his ornaments and leaves to become a monk; she is left overwhelmed with grief.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.6-28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana tells how a betrothal everyone trusted ended on the wedding day itself.
Rajimati, who trusted the match, is left in the Kural’s position, though the Jain text treats the groom’s departure as an act of compassion rather than a broken promise.
Sikhism
“In separation, He is not separated from us; He is pervading and permeating amongst all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 46, Sri Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan teaches that the divine Beloved never truly leaves, because He pervades everything, even when the soul feels parted from Him.
The Kural's heroine asks whether she is at fault for trusting the one who said 'Do not fear' and then left; Gurbani answers that the divine Beloved who reassures the soul never actually departs, so trust in Him is never misplaced.
Greek Mythology
When Dido learns that Aeneas is secretly preparing his fleet to leave Carthage, she confronts him, reproaching him for hoping to slip away in silence and appealing to the pledges and union they had shared.
— Virgil, Aeneid 4 (paraphrase) [10]
In Virgil, Dido treats Aeneas’s departure as a betrayal of everything she had believed from him.
Dido’s anguish asks the Kural’s question: is the one who trusted to blame when the lover leaves?
Philosophy
Kant argues that making a promise one does not intend to keep cannot be made a universal law, because if everyone did it, no one would believe a promise at all.
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, section II (paraphrase) [11]
For Kant, promising depends on trust, and breaking one’s word harms the very practice of promising.
The Kural’s heroine shows the cost on a small scale: a broken ‘fear not’ damages her ability to trust any promise.

Real Life Example

John Donne writes A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning
1611 or 1612 (published 1633) · London, England
John Donne's poem A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning, first published in 1633, is a farewell in which the speaker tells his beloved not to grieve at their parting because their love cannot be divided by distance. According to Donne's early biographer Izaak Walton, Donne gave the poem to his wife shortly before setting out on a journey to France in 1611 or 1612.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem is one of the most famous reassurances ever written by a departing lover, telling the one left behind that there is no cause for fear or tears.
The kural asks whether any blame falls on the one who trusted the lover who said do not be afraid and then went away, and Donne's poem is that very do-not-fear spoken on the eve of a departure.
