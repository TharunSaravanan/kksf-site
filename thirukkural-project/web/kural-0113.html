Kural 113

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

நன்றே தரினும் நடுவிகந்தாம் ஆக்கத்தை அன்றே யொழிய விடல்.
Nandre tharinum naduvikandhaam aakkaththai Andre yozhiya vidal.

Meaning

Even if it seems to bring only good, give up any gain that comes from being unfair, that very same day.

Elementary

Suppose you find out you won a prize only because the judge was your uncle and cheated for you. Even though the prize is nice, the right thing is to give it back right away.

University

The couplet anticipates the objection that injustice might sometimes pay: நன்றே தரினும் (“even if it yields only good”). Valluvar’s answer is categorical. Gain acquired by நடு இகந்து (“transgressing the middle/the just mean”) must be relinquished அன்றே (“that very day”), with no waiting to see whether it turns out well. The emphasis on immediacy suggests a psychological insight: ill-gotten advantage becomes harder to renounce the longer one holds it. The ethic parallels Socrates’ insistence in the Apology that wealth pursued at the cost of the soul is shameful, the Qur’anic ban on consuming others’ property unjustly, and Hesiod’s warning that seized wealth stays only “for a little time.” Philosophically it is a deontological stance: the injustice of the means cannot be redeemed by the goodness of the outcome.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Bound by a network of hundreds of thousands of desires and absorbed in lust and anger, they secure money by illegal means for sense gratification.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.12 [4]
In describing the “demoniac” nature, Krishna names gathering wealth by unjust means (anyāyena, literally “by non-justice”) as a defining mark.
The Gita condemns exactly what the Kural tells us to drop: wealth gathered by departing from justice.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [5]
Jesus asks what any gain is worth if it costs a person their soul.
Both say that no gain, however great or “good,” is worth the moral loss involved in getting it wrongly.
Islam
“And do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly or send it [in bribery] to the rulers in order that [they might aid] you [to] consume a portion of the wealth of the people in sin, while you know [it is unlawful].”
— Qur’an 2:188 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an forbids taking others’ wealth unjustly, including bribing judges to rule in one’s favour.
Gain won by corrupting fair judgment is exactly the “gain beyond the middle” the Kural says to reject.
Judaism
“Better is a little with righteousness than great revenues without right.”
— Proverbs 16:8 (KJV) [5]
The proverb says a small income gained justly is worth more than a large one gained unjustly.
Same trade-off as the Kural: prefer less with justice over more without it.
Buddhism
“He is indeed virtuous, wise, and righteous who neither for his own sake nor for the sake of another (does any wrong), who does not crave for sons, wealth, or kingdom, and does not desire success by unjust means.”
— Dhammapada 84 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada defines the righteous person as one who does not seek success by unjust means, whether for himself or for others.
Both refuse success that comes through injustice, however attractive it looks.
Jainism
“The consequences of violence, falsehood, stealing, unchastity and attachment are calamity and reproach in this world and in the next birth.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.9 (English rendering on jainworld.com) [8]
Umāsvāti teaches that whatever is gained by breaching the vows, including taking what is not rightly one's own, brings disaster and disgrace both now and hereafter.
Valluvar says gain won by abandoning fairness must be given up that very day even if it seems to bring only good, and the Tattvārtha Sūtra explains why: such gain carries calamity in this life and the next.
Sikhism
“To take what rightfully belongs to another, is like a Muslim eating pork, or a Hindu eating beef.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh, Maajh ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak makes taking another's due, haq paraia, as abhorrent as the strongest taboo of each community.
The Kural tells us to give up at once any gain won by abandoning fairness, however good it looks; Guru Nanak treats such gain as utterly forbidden food.
Greek Mythology
“Wealth should not be seized: god-given wealth is much better; for if a man take great wealth violently and perforce, or if he steal it through his tongue, as often happens when gain deceives men’s sense and dishonour tramples down honour, the gods soon blot him out and make that man’s house low, and wealth attends him only for a little time.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 320–326 (trans. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod warns that wrongly seized wealth, “when gain deceives men’s sense,” is soon destroyed by the gods.
The phrase “gain deceives men’s sense” matches the Kural’s “even if it seems to give good”: the benefit is an illusion that will not last.
Philosophy
“are you not ashamed of heaping up the greatest amount of money and honour and reputation, and caring so little about wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul, which you never regard or heed at all?”
— Plato, Apology 29d–e (trans. Jowett) [11]
At his trial Socrates says his mission is to shame Athenians who pile up money and reputation while neglecting their souls.
Both put the integrity of the self above material gain.

Real Life Example

Eugenio Monti lends his bolt to his rivals
1964 · Igls, near Innsbruck, Austria (Winter Olympic Games)
During the two-man bobsleigh competition at the 1964 Innsbruck Winter Olympics, a bolt on the sled of British rivals Tony Nash and Robin Dixon broke, threatening to put them out of the event. Italy's Eugenio Monti, one of the world's leading drivers, gave them a replacement bolt from his own equipment, and Nash and Dixon went on to win the Olympic title. Monti also had his team help repair the sled of the Canadian four-man crew, who won that event, and the International Olympic Committee later honoured him with its first Pierre de Coubertin fair-play award.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Monti could have gained a medal simply by staying silent, but he refused to profit from a rival's misfortune.
The kural says any gain that comes from abandoning fairness should be given up at once; Monti gave up a likely advantage on the spot rather than win by his rivals' accident.
