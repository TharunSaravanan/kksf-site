Kural 697

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

வேட்பன சொல்லி வினையில எஞ்ஞான்றும் கேட்பினும் சொல்லா விடல்.
Vetpana solli vinaiyila egngnaandrum Ketpinum sollaa vidal.

Meaning

Tell the king things that are useful and good for him. Never talk about useless things, even if he asks you to.

Elementary

If your friend asks you to help them copy homework, a good friend says no, even though the friend asked. A good helper suggests things that really help, and stays quiet about bad ideas, even when invited to share them.

University

Vetpana means ‘what is desirable’, which in Parimelazhagar’s reading are things beneficial to the king, not merely things he would like to hear. Vinaiyila means ‘what has no deed’, that is, fruitless or unprofitable matters. The concessive ketpinum (‘even if he asks’) is the moral centre of the couplet: the courtier’s integrity must hold even against a direct royal request, and egngnaandrum (at no time whatever) rules out exceptions. Commentators differ here. Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as ‘speak only what the king desires’, while Solomon Pappaiah reads ‘speak useful things even unasked’. Pope’s ‘speak pleasant things’ softens the point. The couplet thus protects 696 from being read as a licence to flatter. The older text reads “வினையில வெஞ்ஞான்றுங் / கேட்பினுஞ் சொல்லா விடல்.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A person should set forth before the king those topics that are both profitable and pleasant; but should a subject be profitable without being pleasant, he should still communicate it, despite its disagreeableness. ... and not to indulge in speech that is alike unpleasant and profitless.”
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva, Section 4 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Dhaumya ranks the courtier’s speech by usefulness. Say what is useful and pleasant, say what is useful even if unpleasant, and never say what is both unpleasant and useless.
Like the Kural, Dhaumya makes benefit, not the king’s wish, the test of what a courtier should say.
Christianity
“But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment.”
— Matthew 12:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that empty, useless words carry moral weight and will be judged.
The Kural bans useless speech even when the king asks for it. Jesus makes every idle word answerable before God.
Islam
“Anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should talk what is good or keep quiet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6018 [5]
The Prophet makes useful, good speech or else silence a mark of faith.
Speak what is good or say nothing is the Kural’s rule, and the courtier follows it even before the king.
Judaism
“As the LORD liveth, what the LORD saith unto me, that will I speak.”
— 1 Kings 22:13-14 (KJV) [4]
When King Ahab’s four hundred prophets all flatter him with promises of victory, the prophet Micaiah is told to agree with them. He refuses and speaks only the unwelcome truth.
Micaiah refuses to say empty, harmful things even when the king’s court demands them, which is the Kural’s ‘even if he asks, do not say it’.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, unbeneficial, but endearing & agreeable to others, he does not say them.”
— Abhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 58 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha will not say even true and pleasant things if they do no good.
The Buddha’s refusal of pleasing but useless speech is exactly the Kural’s refusal to say unprofitable things, whatever the king wants.
Jainism
“He should not tell anything sinful or meaningless or hurtful, neither for his own sake nor for anybody else's, nor without such a motive.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The sutra forbids sinful, idle or harmful speech whatever the reason, whether for oneself or for another.
The Kural says never to speak of useless or harmful things even if the king asks; the Jain rule bars meaningless or hurtful speech even when it is for someone else's sake.
Sikhism
“Nanak speaks the Word of Truth; he proclaims the Truth at this, the right time.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 723, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Closing a hymn on the violence of Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak insists on telling the truth about rulers and events, whatever the powerful might prefer to hear.
The Kural tells the counsellor to say only what is truly good for the king and never what is useless, even if the king asks for it; Guru Nanak's resolve to speak only truth at the decisive moment rests on the same integrity.
Greek Mythology
“O Solon, our converse with kings should be either as rare, or as pleasing as is possible.” “No indeed!” said Solon, “but either as rare or as beneficial as possible.”
— Plutarch, Life of Solon 28 (trans. Bernadotte Perrin, Loeb) [9]
At King Croesus’s court, the fable-writer Aesop advises Solon to speak to kings pleasantly or not at all. Solon corrects him: speak rarely, or speak what is truly beneficial.
This exchange sets 696 against 697. Aesop voices the ‘pleasing’ side, and Solon voices Valluvar’s final word: benefit over pleasure.
Philosophy
“When asked, he should tell the king what is both good and pleasing, but not what is bad though pleasing; if the king is pleased to listen, he may secretly tell what though unpleasant, is good.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 5.4, The Conduct of a Courtier (trans. R. Shamasastry) [10]
Kautilya tells the courtier to offer what is good and pleasing, to refuse what is bad even if pleasing, and to say hard truths privately.
‘Not what is bad though pleasing’ is the Kural’s refusal to speak unprofitable things even when asked.

Real Life Example

Bismarck persuades King Wilhelm I to make a lenient peace at Nikolsburg
July 1866 · Nikolsburg (now Mikulov, Czech Republic)
After Prussia's victory over Austria at Koniggratz in July 1866, King Wilhelm I wanted to take territory from Austria and from Saxony, which he regarded as a legitimate war trophy, and senior officers pressed to cross the Danube. Bismarck, who had no interest in taking land from Austria and did not want to make it a lasting enemy, opposed these demands; he later claimed he came close to resigning and was saved when the Crown Prince persuaded his father to accept Bismarck's terms. The preliminary peace signed at Nikolsburg on 26 July 1866 took no Austrian territory for Prussia and left Saxony intact.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Bismarck refused to serve up the trophies his king wanted, and the moderate peace kept the door open to later friendship with Austria.
The kural says a counsellor should speak only of what truly benefits the king and never of what is useless or harmful, even when the king himself asks for it, and Bismarck held to that line against his own sovereign.
