Kural 1088

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

ஒண்ணுதற் கோஒ உடைந்ததே ஞாட்பினுள் நண்ணாரும் உட்குமென் பீடு.
Onnuthar koo udainthathe gnaatpinul nannaarum utkumen peedu.

Meaning

Oh no! My strength, which made even my enemies shake on the battlefield, has broken in front of her shining forehead alone.

Elementary

Imagine a champion wrestler who has never lost, but who becomes shy and tongue-tied when he meets someone he really admires. The young man is a brave warrior, yet one look at the girl's shining face makes all his toughness melt away.

University

On-nuthal (ஒண்ணுதல்) is a 'bright forehead', a standard metonymy for the heroine. Koo (கோஒ) is an exclamation of dismay lengthened by alapedai. Gnaatpu (ஞாட்பு) is the battlefield, nannaar (நண்ணார்) foes (literally 'those who do not draw near', i.e. are not friends), utkum (உட்கும்) 'fear', and peedu (பீடு) 'valour, prowess'. Mu. Varadarasanar reads: 'my strength, which made even foes fear in battle, has been defeated by her bright forehead'; Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah take nannaar as including those who only heard of his fame. The hero (talaivan) of Tamil akam poetry is ideally a warrior, so the defeat is sharpened. Pope: 'my might, / That awed my foemen in the fight, / By lustre of that beaming brow / Borne down, lies broken now!' Identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As a boat on the water is swept away by a strong wind, even one of the senses on which the mind focuses can carry away a man's intelligence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.67 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, 1972) [3]
Krishna warns that a single sense the mind fixes on can overpower even a strong person.
The Kural's warrior is carried away in exactly this way by a single sight.
Christianity
“And she made him sleep upon her knees; and she called for a man, and she caused him to shave off the seven locks of his head; and she began to afflict him, and his strength went from him.”
— Judges 16:19 (KJV) [4]
Samson, the mightiest warrior of Israel, loses his strength through his love for Delilah.
Like the Kural's hero, a famous warrior's strength is broken through love.
Islam
“She said, ‘That is the one about whom you blamed me. And I certainly sought to seduce him, but he firmly refused...’”
— Qur'an 12:32 (Sahih International; excerpt) [5]
The Qur'an presents Joseph as the one whose strength of will did not break before attraction.
The Kural shows a strong man's will breaking; Joseph is the Qur'an's contrasting model of firmness.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city.’”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah defines real strength as mastering one's own desires, not defeating enemies.
The Kural's warrior beats his foes but cannot master his heart, which is exactly Ben Zoma's point.
Buddhism
“Just as a storm throws down a weak tree, so does Mara overpower the man who lives for the pursuit of pleasures, who is uncontrolled in his senses...”
— Dhammapada 7 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita; excerpt) [7]
The Dhammapada says the person whose senses are uncontrolled is knocked down by Mara like a tree in a storm.
The Kural's warrior, strong in battle but unguarded in sense, is knocked down in the same way.
Jainism
“When Râgîmatî perceived that Rathanêmi's strength of will was broken, and temptation had got the better of him, she did not lose her presence of mind and defended her Self on that occasion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.39 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The prince and monk Rathanemi sees Rajimati unexpectedly and his strength of will breaks, until she brings him back to his vows.
The Kural's 'my might is broken' is told from the inside; the Uttaradhyayana describes the same collapse from outside.
Sikhism
“Give up your selfhood, and so obtain your Husband Lord; what other clever tricks are of any use?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 722, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak tells the soul-bride that only giving up her ego wins the Husband Lord, and that no cleverness can take its place.
The Kural's warrior, whose strength made enemies tremble, finds his might broken before one girl's bright forehead; Guru Nanak also teaches that pride and strength must give way in love.
Greek Mythology
“...Eros (Love), fairest among the deathless gods, who unnerves the limbs and overcomes the mind and wise counsels of all gods and all men within them.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 120-122 (tr. Hugh G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod makes Eros the power that defeats the strength and judgment of gods and men alike.
The Kural's warrior, whose valour breaks at one sight, is one of Eros's victims.
Philosophy
Lucretius opens his poem with Mars, the lord of war, lying in the lap of Venus, defeated by the eternal wound of love and gazing up at her.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 1.31-40 (paraphrase) [11]
The Epicurean philosopher pictures the god of battle conquered by love.
This is the Kural's image on a cosmic scale: martial might surrendering to beauty.

Real Life Example

Napoleon's love letters to Josephine from Italy
March–May 1796 · Italy and Paris, France
Napoleon Bonaparte married Josephine de Beauharnais in March 1796 and days later left to command the French army in Italy. While winning battles there, he wrote her frequent, passionate letters begging her to join him and complaining that she rarely wrote back. Josephine preferred to stay in Paris for several months.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters show the general who was conquering Italy as helpless and pleading in love.
The kural's warrior laments that strength which made enemies tremble has broken before one woman's brow; Napoleon, victorious in the field, wrote to Josephine as a man at her mercy.
