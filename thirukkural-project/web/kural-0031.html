Kural 31

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

சிறப்புஈனும் செல்வமும் ஈனும் அறத்தினூஉங்கு ஆக்கம் எவனோ உயிர்க்கு.
Sirappu eenum selvamum eenum araththinooungu, Aakkam evano uyirkku.

Meaning

Doing what is right brings both respect and riches. So what could be better for any living being than doing right?

Elementary

Imagine a tree that gives you both shade and fruit — you would not chop it down looking for something better. Doing the right thing is like that tree: it gives you a good name and it gives you good things in life, so there is nothing better to plant.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a rhetorical question rather than a command, staking out the claim that அறம் (aram, virtue/dharma) is not merely one good among many but the most productive of all. The pairing சிறப்பு (sirappu, 'distinction', glossed by the classical commentator Parimelazhagar as வீடுபேறு, liberation) with செல்வம் (selvam, wealth) collapses the usual opposition between otherworldly merit and worldly gain: virtue delivers both. This anticipates the tripartite scheme of aram–porul–inbam (dharma–artha–kama) that structures the Kural itself, and mirrors the classical Indian doctrine that dharma is the root from which artha and kama grow. The unusual archaic form அறத்தினூஉங்கு, with its alapedai (lengthened vowel), is metrically marked and gives the line its emphatic weight.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“With uplifted arms I am crying aloud but nobody hears me. From Righteousness is Wealth as also Pleasure. Why should not Righteousness, therefore, be courted?”
— Mahabharata 18.5, Svargarohanika Parva (Bharata Savitri), trans. Kisari Mohan Ganguli [3]
This is Vyasa's despairing summary of the entire Mahabharata — the so-called Bharata Savitri — insisting that both material gain and enjoyment are downstream consequences of dharma, not rivals to it.
It makes exactly Valluvar's argument: since virtue is the source of both honour and wealth, nothing else can be a greater good.
Christianity
“But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.”
— Matthew 6:33 (KJV) [4]
In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus tells anxious listeners that food, drink and clothing follow on from putting righteousness first, rather than being secured by chasing them directly.
Like the Kural, it ranks righteousness above material gain precisely because material gain follows from it.
Islam
“So be mindful of Allah to the best of your ability, hear and obey, and spend in charity—that will be best for you. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful.”
— Qur'an 64:16, At-Taghabun (Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The verse frames God-consciousness and generosity as what is genuinely 'best for you' (khayran li-anfusikum), redefining self-interest around righteous conduct.
Both texts argue that righteous conduct is the truly profitable course for a human being, not a sacrifice of profit.
Judaism
“The following are the things for which a man enjoys the fruits in this world while the principal remains for him in the world to come: Honoring one’s father and mother; The performance of righteous deeds; And the making of peace between a person and his friend; And the study of the torah is equal to them all.”
— Mishnah Peah 1:1 (trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah classifies certain good deeds as paying a 'dividend' that can be enjoyed immediately while the capital is preserved for the world to come.
It matches the Kural's double yield precisely: righteous action produces both present prosperity and ultimate distinction.
Buddhism
“The doer of good rejoices here and hereafter; he rejoices in both the worlds. He rejoices and exults, recollecting his own pure deeds.”
— Dhammapada 16, Yamakavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
One of the Dhammapada's 'twin verses', contrasting the evil-doer who grieves in both worlds with the good-doer who rejoices in both.
Same claim that virtue pays out in this life and the next, leaving no greater good available to a living being.
Jainism
“Religion is supremely auspicious; non-violence, selfcontrol and penance are its essentials. Even the gods bow down before him whose mind is ever preoccupied with religion.”
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 1.1, as Saman Suttam v. 82, Dharmasutra (tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; text via jainworld.com) [8]
The Dasavaikalika Sutra opens by declaring dharma, made up of non-injury, restraint and austerity, the highest good (mangala), and says even celestial beings honour the person whose mind rests always in it.
Like the Kural's opening question, 'what greater good than virtue?', this text places dharma above every other good and says it wins the highest honour. The Kural adds wealth to the list of virtue's rewards; the Jain verse does not.
Sikhism
“Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 62, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a Sri Raag ashtapadi Guru Nanak ranks sach aachaar, truthful conduct, above even the knowledge of Truth itself; the same hymn says that the companionship of the Naam brings the most excellent honour.
Like Valluvar's rhetorical question, it names right living as the highest good a person can possess, the source of true honour.
Greek Mythology
“But they who give straight judgements to strangers and to the men of the land, and go not aside from what is just, their city flourishes, and the people prosper in it… The earth bears them victual in plenty… They flourish continually with good things.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 225-237, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White [10]
Hesiod describes the city of the just as materially blessed by Zeus with peace, fertility and abundance, in direct contrast to the famine and ruin visited on the unjust city.
This is the Kural's claim in mythic-agricultural form: righteousness itself is what generates prosperity.
Philosophy
“I tell you that virtue is not given by money, but that from virtue come money and every other good of man, public as well as private.”
— Plato, Apology 30b, trans. Benjamin Jowett [11]
At his trial Socrates defends his life's work of urging Athenians to care for the soul before possessions, claiming that goods flow from virtue rather than the reverse.
This is almost a direct Greek restatement of Kural 31: virtue is the source of wealth and every other good, so nothing exceeds it.

Real Life Example

The Cadbury brothers move their works to Bournville
1879 · Bournville, Birmingham, England, United Kingdom
In 1861 the brothers George and Richard Cadbury took over their father's failing cocoa business, and in 1879 they moved their factory four miles out of industrial Birmingham to a rural site they named Bournville. There they introduced a private social security programme and working conditions far in advance of their time, and from 1893 George Cadbury built a model village of houses with large gardens, which he handed to the Bournville Village Trust in 1900. The firm grew into the highly prosperous Cadbury Brothers company, and Bournville became a model for later garden cities and suburbs.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Cadburys showed that a business run on ethical principles could become both rich and widely respected, its decent treatment of workers adding to rather than draining its success.
The kural says virtue yields both honour and prosperity, and the Cadburys' firm gained both the wealth and the lasting good name that their humane practices earned.
