Kural 978

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

பணியுமாம் என்றும் பெருமை சிறுமை அணியுமாம் தன்னை வியந்து.
Paniyumaam endrum perumai sirumai aniyumaam thannai viyandhu.

Meaning

Greatness always bows and stays modest. Smallness dresses itself up and admires itself.

Elementary

A tree heavy with fruit bends its branches low, while an empty stalk sticks straight up. Valluvar says truly great people are like the fruit tree: they stay humble, while small people keep showing off.

University

The couplet sets two verbs in parallel, paniyum (பணியும், 'bends, submits') and aniyum (அணியும், 'adorns'), with the expletive -aam (ஆம்) giving the tone of a settled maxim and endrum (என்றும், 'always') applying to both. Thannai viyandhu (தன்னை வியந்து, 'admiring itself') explains the adorning: littleness dresses itself up in its own praise. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses 'greatness walks in humility at all times, littleness praises itself', and Kalaignar adds that the small lie puffed up (இறுமாந்து). Pope's 'Greatness humbly bends, but littleness always / Spreads out its plumes' turns aniyum into a peacock image not in the Tamil. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Humility; pridelessness; nonviolence; tolerance; simplicity; approaching a bona fide spiritual master; cleanliness; steadiness; self-control…”
— Bhagavad Gita 13.8-12 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna's list of the marks of true knowledge begins with humility and pridelessness (amānitvam, adambhitvam).
Like the Kural, it puts humility first among the marks of the truly great.
Christianity
“For whosoever exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.”
— Luke 14:11 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that self-exaltation leads down and humility leads up.
Both texts set the humble bow of greatness against the self-promotion of littleness.
Islam
“Charity does not decrease wealth, no one forgives another except that Allah increases his honor, and no one humbles himself for the sake of Allah except that Allah raises his status.”
— Sahih Muslim 2588 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that humility raises a person's standing.
Like the Kural, it makes humble bowing the true sign and source of greatness.
Judaism
“…anyone who humbles himself, the Holy One, Blessed be He, exalts him, and anyone who exalts himself, the Holy One, Blessed be He, humbles him. Anyone who seeks greatness, greatness flees from him, and, conversely, anyone who flees from greatness, greatness seeks him.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 13b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud explains that Beit Hillel's rulings prevailed because they were humble, and draws a general lesson about greatness.
This mirrors the Kural's pairing of humble greatness with self-exalting smallness.
Buddhism
“‘Let both laymen and monks think that it was done by me. In every work, great and small, let them follow me’ — such is the ambition of the fool; thus his desire and pride increase.”
— Dhammapada 74 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada portrays the fool who wants credit for everything.
This is the Kural's littleness, decking itself out in self-admiration.
Jainism
After defeating his elder brother Bharata, Bahubali renounced the world and stood in motionless meditation for a year, but omniscience would not come because pride kept him from going to bow to his younger brothers, who had become monks before him; when his sisters made him see this and he resolved to go and bow, he attained kevala jnana.
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacaritra I (Life of Rishabha) (tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
In Jain tradition even the greatest austerity is blocked by pride and completed by humility.
Bahubali's greatness is fulfilled only when it bows, just as the Kural says.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak! Are the essence of virtue and goodness. Everyone bows down to himself; no one bows down to another. When something is placed on the balancing scale and weighed, the side which descends is heavier.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
The salok begins with the simmal tree, tall and showy but with useless fruit, and contrasts it with humility. On a scale the heavier pan sinks lower, so true worth shows itself by bowing.
Valluvar says greatness always bows while littleness admires and adorns itself, and Guru Nanak's image of the heavier pan sinking makes the same point.
Greek Mythology
Niobe boasted that with her many children she was more worthy of worship than the goddess Leto, who had only two; Apollo and Artemis killed her children, and the grieving Niobe was turned to weeping stone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.146-312 [10]
The myth makes self-glorifying boast the classic Greek form of fatal pride.
Niobe is the Kural's littleness that adorns itself with its own praise.
Philosophy
In the Apology Socrates explains the oracle's verdict by saying that the reputedly wise man he questioned knew nothing yet thought he knew, while he himself neither knew nor thought he knew, and so was wiser only in that small way.
— Plato, Apology 21d [11]
Socrates' wisdom consists in an honest humility that refuses to claim more than it has.
Socrates is the Kural's greatness that bows, and his self-satisfied interlocutors are the littleness that admires itself.

Real Life Example

Hokusai, at seventy-five, declares that nothing he drew before seventy was worth noticing
1834 · Edo (now Tokyo), Japan
By 1834 Katsushika Hokusai was already famous for his print series Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji, including the Great Wave, yet in a postscript to the first volume of his illustrated book One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji, written at the age of seventy-five, he declared that nothing he had drawn before seventy was worthy of notice. He wrote that only at seventy-three had he begun to grasp the structure of plants, birds, animals, insects and fish, and that he hoped to keep improving through eighty, ninety and a hundred until, at a hundred and ten, every dot and stroke would be alive. He signed himself the Old Man Mad About Painting, and on his deathbed in 1849 he still wished for a few more years in which to become a true artist.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Japan's most celebrated artist, at the height of his fame, judged his own work with humility and saw himself as still a learner.
The kural says that greatness always bows in humility while littleness decks itself out in self-admiration, and the great Hokusai dismissed his own famous work as only a beginning.
