Kural 867

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

கொடுத்தும் கொளல்வேண்டும் மன்ற அடுத்திருந்து மாணாத செய்வான் பகை.
Koduththum kolalvendum mandra aduththirundhu maanaadha seyvaan pakai.

Meaning

If someone stays close to you but does bad things, you should certainly make them your enemy, even if it costs you something.

Elementary

Suppose someone on your own team keeps quietly messing up every play. It would honestly be better if he went over to the other team, even if you had to give up something to make that happen. A clear opponent is safer than a teammate who harms you from inside.

University

The commentators divide on aduththirundhu (அடுத்திருந்து). Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it as ‘staying close by’: one who remains near you as an associate yet does unworthy things (maanaadha, மாணாத) should be turned into an open enemy, even at the cost of wealth. The older line represented by Parimelazhagar, followed by Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesigan and by the classic English version (‘having begun a work, does what is not conducive’), reads it as ‘having undertaken’ an action: the enmity of a foe who mismanages his own undertakings is so advantageous that it is worth buying. Mandra (மன்ற) means ‘certainly’. Either way the paradox is that some enmity is a gain worth paying for. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata war, Shalya agrees to serve as Karna’s charioteer but undermines him throughout with discouraging and belittling speech, weakening him from within his own chariot.
— Mahabharata, Karna Parva (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
An ally placed at Karna’s side becomes a source of harm rather than help.
Shalya is the close companion who does unworthy things, of whom the Kural says an open enemy would be better.
Christianity
Judas, one of the twelve, leads the arresting party to Gethsemane and identifies Jesus with a kiss; Jesus asks whether he betrays the Son of man with a kiss.
— Luke 22:47-48 (KJV) [4]
The betrayal comes from inside the closest circle, under the sign of friendship.
Judas is the archetype of the one who stays close yet does harm.
Islam
“Had they gone forth with you, they would not have increased you except in confusion, and they would have been active among you, seeking [to cause] you fitnah [i.e., chaos and dissension]. And among you are avid listeners to them.”
— Qur’an 9:47 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says it was better that the hypocrites stayed behind, since inside the army they would only have spread disorder.
Like the Kural, it judges a harmful insider to be worse than his absence.
Judaism
“For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it: neither was it he that hated me that did magnify himself against me; then I would have hid myself from him: But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance.”
— Psalms 55:12-13 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist says an open enemy can be endured or avoided, but harm from a close companion cannot.
This is the heart of the Kural’s logic: an open foe is easier to deal with than a harmful intimate.
Buddhism
Devadatta, the Buddha’s cousin and a monk within the Sangha, plotted against the Buddha and caused a schism by leading away a group of monks.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Cullavagga VII [6]
The most dangerous opposition to the Buddha arose from inside his own community.
Devadatta is the insider whose ‘unworthy deeds’ did more harm than any outside foe.
Jainism
“Now this driver (viz. Garga), who had to deal with bad bullocks, thought: 'What have I to do with bad pupils? I am disheartened. As are bad pupils, so are bad bullocks; I shall leave these lazy donkeys, and shall practise severe austerities.'”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 27.15–16 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The elder Garga, worn out by disciples who stayed close to him yet behaved unworthily, breaks with them completely and goes on alone, accepting the loss of his following.
The Kural says one should break even at a cost with someone who stays close yet acts unworthily, and Garga does this by giving up his whole following rather than keep company with bad pupils.
Sikhism
“Farid, there are dozens who say they are friends; I search, but I cannot find even one.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 87), Bhagat Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Farid laments that many claim friendship, but a true friend is hard to find.
The Kural warns against one who stays close as a friend yet does unworthy things; Farid likewise exposes the gap between those who call themselves friends and real friendship.
Greek Mythology
The Trojans bring the great wooden horse inside their walls as a gift, and the Greek warriors hidden within open the gates by night, so Troy falls.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.492-520 [9]
What the Trojans welcomed into their city destroyed them from within.
The horse is the danger the Kural warns of: harm lodged close by is worse than an enemy outside the walls.
Philosophy
Machiavelli warns that auxiliary troops borrowed from another power are useless and dangerous, because if they lose you are ruined and if they win you are their captive.
— Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. 13 (tr. W. K. Marriott) [10]
Forces that stand beside you without real loyalty are more dangerous than open enemies.
Valluvar likewise prefers a clear enemy to a companion whose conduct harms you.

Real Life Example

Truman relieves General MacArthur of his commands
11 April 1951 · Washington, D.C., United States
During the Korean War, General Douglas MacArthur publicly called for a wider war, despite a December 1950 directive requiring that statements on military and foreign policy be cleared first. After a letter of his criticising the administration's limited-war policy was read in Congress in April 1951, President Harry Truman relieved him of his commands, stating that MacArthur could not give wholehearted support to the policies of the United States and the United Nations. The public and congressional reaction against Truman was overwhelming, with some calling for his impeachment.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Truman chose open conflict with a celebrated subordinate who kept acting against policy, accepting a heavy political price to restore civilian control.
The kural says the enmity of one who stays close to you yet acts unworthily should be taken on even at a cost, and Truman paid in popularity to make that break.
