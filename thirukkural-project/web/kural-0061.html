Kural 61

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

பெறுமவற்றுள் யாமறிவது இல்லை அறிவறிந்த மக்கட்பேறு அல்ல பிற.
Perumavatrul Yaamarivadhu Illai Arivarindha, Makkatperu Alla Pira.

Meaning

Of all the good things a person can have, we know nothing greater than children who have learned what is worth knowing.

Elementary

Imagine someone gives you a huge pile of presents — toys, money, a big house. This kural says that if you also had a child who grows up kind and clever, that child would be the best present of all, better than everything else in the pile put together.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter on progeny by ranking it above every other பேறு (pēṟu, "gain, acquisition") a householder can obtain, and the qualifier is decisive: not merely மக்கட்பேறு (makkaṭ-pēṟu, the getting of children) but அறிவறிந்த மக்கட்பேறு — children who have "known what is to be known." The reduplicative phrase அறிவு அறிந்த is characteristically Kural-like compression: it makes discernment, not mere biological succession, the thing being praised. Parimēlaḻakar, the fourteenth-century commentator, frames the whole adhikāram through the pan-Indic doctrine of the three debts (ṛṇa-traya) owed by the twice-born — to the sages, discharged by study; to the gods, by sacrifice; and to the ancestors (தென்புலத்தார், ten-pulattār), discharged only by begetting offspring — which is why the chapter follows immediately on marriage and the worth of a wife. Notably, Valluvar himself never invokes that ritual apparatus: he argues purely from the sweetness and social worth of the thing itself, which is part of why the text reads as equally at home in Jain, Śaiva and secular frameworks. The use of the first-person plural யாம் ("we know of none") is also worth marking — it is the voice of considered human judgement rather than revelation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Vedic doctrine of the three debts (ṛṇa-traya) holds that every person is born owing three debts — to the sages, discharged by study of scripture; to the gods, discharged by sacrifice; and to the ancestors, which can be discharged in one way only, by begetting offspring to continue the line.
— Taittirīya Saṃhitā 6.3.10.5 (the doctrine of the three debts, ṛṇa-traya) [5]
In this framework a childless life leaves a structural obligation permanently unpaid, so progeny is not one good among many but a settling of accounts with the whole chain of generations behind you.
Both traditions place children at the very top of the ledger of what a householder can acquire — and Valluvar's own classical commentator Parimelalagar reads this exact kural through this doctrine.
Christianity
“Lo, children are an heritage of the LORD: and the fruit of the womb is his reward.”
— Psalm 127:3 (King James Version) [6]
The psalmist interrupts a poem about the futility of human striving — building houses, guarding cities, rising early to eat the bread of sorrows — to say that children are not something you earn but something given, and that they are the real return on a life.
Like the Kural, the psalm sets children above every other acquisition a person labours for.
Islam
“Wealth and children are [but] adornment of the worldly life. But the enduring good deeds are better to your Lord for reward and better for [one's] hope.”
— Qur'an 18:46, Sūrat al-Kahf (Sahih International) [7]
The verse concedes that children are genuinely among life's beauties, but ranks them below the lasting righteous deeds that outlive this world.
Both texts put children at the summit of worldly goods, though the Qur'an then relativises even that summit in a way Valluvar's couplet does not.
Judaism
“And Abram said, Lord GOD, what wilt thou give me, seeing I go childless, and the steward of my house is this Eliezer of Damascus?”
— Genesis 15:2 (King James Version) [6]
God has just promised Abram an exceedingly great reward, and Abram's reply is essentially that no reward counts for anything while he has no child of his own.
Abram states the Kural's thesis negatively: every other gain is worthless set beside the gain of a child.
Buddhism
“Considering these five reasons / The Wise wish for a child, thinking: / Supported he will support us, / He will do his duties for us, / He will maintain his heritage, / And practice according to wealth, / Also he will make offerings / To relatives who pass away.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.39, Puttasutta, vv. 506–507 (trans. Ānandajoti Bhikkhu, Buddhist Wisdom Verses) [8]
The Buddha here lays out, without disapproval, exactly why householders rationally want children — mutual support, continuity of the family line, and the merit-offerings made for the dead.
Like the Kural, this is an unembarrassed householder's-eye accounting of why a child is the most valuable thing a family can obtain.
Jainism
“And this boy, after having passed his childhood, and, with just ripened intellect, having reached the state of youth, will repeat, fully understand, and well retain (in his mind) the four Vedas … and in many other brahmanical [and monastic] sciences besides.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahâvîra §§9–10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
Interpreting Devânandâ's fourteen dreams, her husband foretells that the child she carries will grow into a youth of ripened intellect who masters every branch of learning; the prophecy is the reason the dreams are called fortunate.
Like Valluvar, the Kalpa Sutra counts it the highest blessing of a pregnancy that the child will become one who knows what is worth knowing.
Sikhism
“That family, whose son has no spiritual wisdom or contemplation - why didn’t his mother just become a widow? That man who has not practiced devotional worship of the Lord - why didn’t such a sinful man die at birth?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 328, Raag Gauri, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir puts the point in its harshest negative form: a son is worth having only if he has spiritual wisdom; without it, his birth brings his family nothing.
Kabir says from the other side what Valluvar says positively: the true gain in children lies in their knowing what is worth knowing, not in simply having them.
Greek Mythology
Aegeus, king of Athens, having no child by either of his two wives, travelled to Delphi to ask the oracle how he might obtain a son; the god gave him a riddling answer about not loosening the wineskin's mouth until he reached home, and his pursuit of an heir eventually produced Theseus.
— Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 3.15.6 [11]
The story presumes a king who has everything a king can have, and for whom the single unobtained good — a son — is worth a journey to the god himself.
Aegeus enacts the Kural's ranking: throne, city and wealth already in hand, yet the one gain still worth seeking is a child.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that parents are fond of their children as of something of themselves, while children are fond of parents as of something they came out of — and that parents know their offspring to be theirs more surely than offspring know their parentage.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.12, 1161b (Bekker) [12]
For Aristotle the parent–child bond is the most natural of all friendships precisely because a child is, in a real sense, an extension of the parent's own being.
This grounds philosophically why the Kural can call children the supreme acquisition: what one acquires in a child is a second self.

Real Life Example

Aage Bohr follows his father Niels Bohr to a Nobel Prize in Physics
1975 · Copenhagen, Denmark
Aage Bohr, born in Copenhagen in 1922 as the fourth son of the physicist Niels Bohr, studied physics at the University of Copenhagen and from 1943 to 1945 worked as his father's assistant and secretary. After Niels Bohr's death in 1962 he became director of the institute his father had founded, serving until 1970. In 1975 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Ben Mottelson and James Rainwater for work on the structure of the atomic nucleus.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Niels Bohr's son did not simply inherit his father's name; he mastered the same science and carried it into a new field, becoming a laureate in his own right.
The kural ranks children who have learned what is worth knowing above every other possession, and the Bohr household's greatest legacy was a son whose own learning matched his father's.
