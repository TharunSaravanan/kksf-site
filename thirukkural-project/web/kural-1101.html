Kural 1101

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

கண்டுகேட்டு உண்டுயிர்த்து உற்றறியும் ஐம்புலனும் ஒண்தொடி கண்ணே உள.
Kandukettu unduyirththu utrariyum aimpulanum ondhodi kanne ula.

Meaning

All the joys of the five senses, seeing, hearing, tasting, smelling and touching, are found together in this one woman with bright bangles.

Elementary

Think of the best day of a holiday: you see the lights, hear the music, taste the sweets, smell the flowers and feel a warm hug. Usually each good thing comes from a different place. The lover in this verse says that, for him, every one of those good feelings comes from one single person he loves.

University

Chapter 111 belongs to the kalaviyal (the section on premarital, secret love) and follows the exchange of glances in ch. 110; in the commentary tradition that runs through Parimelazhagar it voices the hero’s (talaivan’s) joy after the lovers’ first union, and nearly every couplet is his speech. The five verbs kaṇṭu (seeing), kēṭṭu (hearing), uṇṭu (eating, tasting), uyirttu (breathing in, smelling) and uṟṟu (touching) name the aimpulaṉ (ஐம்புலன், the five senses or sense-pleasures) in their classical order, and kaṇṇē uḷa means ‘are located in her’ (kaṇ as a locative). Oṇtoṭi (ஒண்தொடி, ‘she of the bright bangle’) is a standard metonymy for a young woman; the commentators on both source sites (Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar, Puliyur Kesikan) gloss the line as all five sense-joys being present in her alone. Pope renders: ‘All joys that senses five- sight, hearing, taste, smell, touch- can give, / In this resplendent armlets-bearing damsel live!’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the sung version printed on thirukkural.net writes ஒண்டொடி, an ordinary sandhi spelling of the same word.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“She seems a flower whose fragrance none has tasted, / A gem uncut by workman’s tool, / A branch no desecrating hands have wasted, / Fresh honey, beautifully cool.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act II (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
King Dushyanta describes Shakuntala by piling up images for smell, sight, touch and taste, as if every kind of sweetness were gathered in her.
Like the Kural, the lover finds the delights of all the senses summed up in one woman.
Christianity
“How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! how much better is thy love than wine! and the smell of thine ointments than all spices! Thy lips, O my spouse, drop as the honeycomb: honey and milk are under thy tongue; and the smell of thy garments is like the smell of Lebanon.”
— Song of Solomon 4:10-11 (KJV) [4]
The bridegroom of the Song of Songs praises his bride through sight, taste and smell at once.
As in the Kural, the beloved alone satisfies several senses together.
Islam
“In this world, women and perfume have been made dear to me, and my comfort has been provided in prayer.”
— Sunan an-Nasa’i 3939 (graded hasan by Darussalam) [5]
The Prophet names the love of women and of fragrance as good things of this world that God made dear to him, while placing his deepest comfort in prayer.
Islam affirms, as the Kural does, that the beloved is a rightful source of delight, but it ranks prayer above all sense-joys.
Judaism
“Let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; For sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.”
— Song of Songs 2:14 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover asks to see the beloved’s face and hear her voice, because both are sweet to him.
Sight and hearing, two of the Kural’s five senses, both find their joy in the one beloved.
Buddhism
In the opening suttas of the Anguttara Nikaya the Buddha says he knows of no other single sight, sound, smell, taste or touch that so overpowers a man’s mind as those of a woman, and the same of a man’s for a woman.
— Anguttara Nikaya 1.1-10, Rupadi Vagga (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha lists the same five senses as the Kural and says that the beloved’s form, voice, scent, taste and touch are the strongest pull on the mind.
Buddhism agrees with the Kural that all five sense-delights can meet in one person, but treats this as a danger to be understood, not a joy to be celebrated.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of ‘colours,’ will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24, with 32.22-99 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira’s lecture takes the five senses one by one (colour, sound, smell, taste, touch) and warns that passion for the objects of each sense ruins the soul.
Jainism shares the Kural’s map of the five senses but sees their gathering in one beloved as a snare, the opposite of the lover’s delight.
Sikhism
“O my eyes, the Lord has infused His Light into you; do not look upon any other than the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 922, Raag Ramkali, Anand Sahib (pauri 36), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Anand Sahib, Guru Amar Das speaks in turn to the body, the eyes and the ears, telling each sense that its true fulfilment lies in the Lord.
The Kural finds the joys of all five senses in one woman; Anand Sahib directs the senses one by one to the one Beloved, God.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Aphrodite lends Hera an embroidered girdle in which every charm is woven: love, desire and the sweet persuasion that steals the wits even of the wise.
— Homer, Iliad 14.214-217 [10]
The girdle gathers every power of attraction into one object, and whoever wears it becomes irresistible.
The Kural likewise imagines every delight of love gathered into one woman.
Philosophy
“I know not how I can conceive the good, if I withdraw the pleasures of taste and withdraw the pleasures of love and those of hearing and sight.”
— Epicurus, quoted in Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers X.6 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
Epicurus says he cannot even imagine the good without the pleasures of taste, love, hearing and sight.
Like the Kural’s lover, Epicurus counts the pleasures of all the senses, including love, as part of the good.

Real Life Example

The Lady and the Unicorn tapestries show the five senses
c. 1500 · Woven probably in Flanders; now in the Musée de Cluny, Paris, France
Around 1500 a set of six large tapestries known as The Lady and the Unicorn was woven, probably in Flanders, for a patron whom historians identify from its coats of arms as a member of the Le Viste family. Five of the tapestries are understood to show the five senses, sight, hearing, taste, smell and touch, each with a lady accompanied by a lion and a unicorn. The sixth carries the inscription À mon seul désir, meaning to my only desire, and its meaning is still debated; the set now hangs in the Musée de Cluny in Paris.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The set is one of the best-known works of medieval art, and it gathers all five senses around a single figure before naming one only desire beyond them.
The kural says that all the joys of the five senses are found together in one beloved woman, and the tapestries likewise gather sight, hearing, taste, smell and touch around one lady and crown them with a panel addressed to an only desire.
