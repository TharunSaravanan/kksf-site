Kural 599

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

பரியது கூர்ங்கோட்டது ஆயினும் யானை வெரூஉம் புலிதாக் குறின்.
Pariyadhu koorngottadhu aayinum yaanai Veruum pulidhaakkurin.

Meaning

An elephant is huge and has sharp tusks, yet it gets scared when a tiger attacks.

Elementary

A big dog can run away from a small, brave cat that hisses and charges. The Kural says size and weapons don’t decide who wins; the fierce spirit does, like a tiger that can scare an elephant.

University

The couplet is a pure illustration (eduththukkaattu uvamai) with the moral left implicit: ‘pariyadhu’ (bulky) and ‘koorng kottadhu’ (having sharp tusks) list the elephant’s physical advantages, and ‘veruum’ (fears) is an alapedai (vowel-lengthened) form of verum, which heightens the sense of sudden fright. Parimelazhagar explains that the tiger prevails because it has ookkam, and that the verse teaches that energy, not bulk or weaponry, decides contests; Kalaignar makes this explicit, calling ookkam stronger than physique. The pairing with 597, where the elephant is the image of steadfastness, shows Valluvar using the same animal both ways: wounded but energetic it stands, huge but unenergetic it flees. The text is identical in the base sources; transliterations differ only in rendering the alapedai (veru-um / veruum).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya records a debate on whether energy (utsāha-śakti) or material power (prabhāva-śakti) is superior: his teacher argues that an energetic king, brave and skilled, can overcome even a stronger one, while Kautilya himself ranks power, and above both good counsel, higher.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 9.1 [3]
The Arthashastra weighs energy against sheer resources as sources of victory.
The teacher’s view is the Kural’s tiger-and-elephant lesson; Kautilya’s own view is a candid counterpoint.
Christianity
The young shepherd David, without armour and armed only with a sling, faces the giant Philistine champion Goliath, clad in bronze with a massive spear, and fells him with one stone.
— 1 Samuel 17 [4]
The small but fearless fighter overcomes the huge, heavily armed one.
Like the Kural’s tiger, David shows that spirit beats size and weapons.
Islam
“How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the patient.”
— Qur’an 2:249 (Saheeh International) [5]
Before facing Goliath’s army, Saul’s few steadfast soldiers declare that small, determined forces often defeat larger ones.
This is the Kural’s tiger-versus-elephant lesson applied to armies.
Judaism
“Be strong as a leopard, and swift as an eagle, and fleet as a gazelle, and brave as a lion, to do the will of your Father who is in heaven.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:20 (tr. Joshua Kulp) [6]
Judah ben Tema calls for the fierce energy of predators in the service of God.
His leopard and lion are the Jewish counterpart of the Kural’s tiger, symbols of energetic spirit.
Buddhism
“Heedful among the heedless, wide-awake among the sleepy, the wise man advances like a swift horse leaving behind a weak jade.”
— Dhammapada 29 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The alert, energetic person pulls far ahead of those who are sluggish.
Like the Kural, it teaches that alert energy, not bulk, decides who prevails.
Jainism
“As a strong and irresistible elephant of sixty years, surrounded by his females, (has no equal), neither has a very learned monk. … As a proud lion with sharp fangs, who brooks no assault, is superior to all animals, so is a very learned monk (superior to all men).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.18 and 11.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In a chain of similes the Uttaradhyayana praises the accomplished monk through the mightiest animals, setting the lion that brooks no assault above all other beasts.
The Kural's point is that the great elephant still fears the attacking tiger, so courage outweighs size; the Jain verses use the same animal world of strength, with the fearless big cat at the top.
Sikhism
“The Lord is like sugar, scattered in the sand; the elephant cannot pick it up. Says Kabeer, the Guru has given me this sublime understanding: become an ant, and feed on it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1377, Salok Bhagat Kabir (238), Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says the great elephant, an image of pride, cannot gather the sugar of the Lord from the sand, while a tiny ant can.
The Kural's huge, sharp-tusked elephant takes fright before a tiger, showing that bulk does not decide a contest; Kabir also shows the great elephant beaten by something small, though what wins for him is humility, not energy.
Greek Mythology
Trapped in the cave of the giant Cyclops Polyphemus, the far smaller Odysseus blinds him with a sharpened stake and escapes with his men under the giant’s sheep.
— Homer, Odyssey 9 [10]
The small but bold hero overcomes a monstrous opponent many times his size.
The episode matches the Kural’s point that bulk does not guarantee victory over a spirited foe.
Philosophy
Rallying the Greek officers after their generals had been seized by the Persians, Xenophon tells them that it is neither numbers nor strength that wins victories in war: whichever side advances with the stronger spirit, its enemies as a rule will not stand against it.
— Xenophon, Anabasis, Book III, ch. 1, §42 (tr. H. G. Dakyns) [11]
Xenophon urges the officers to restore the courage of their troops, since morale, not size, decides battles.
Like the tiger routing the huge, tusked elephant, the side with the stronger spirit prevails over sheer bulk.

Real Life Example

The Athenians defeat the Persian army at Marathon
490 BCE · Marathon, Attica, Greece
In 490 BCE a Persian expeditionary force sent by King Darius I landed at the bay of Marathon, northeast of Athens. About 10,000 Athenians and 1,000 Plataeans, without Spartan help, faced a larger Persian army and, under generals including Miltiades, attacked with a thinned centre and strengthened wings that routed the Persian flanks and then closed on the centre. The Persians withdrew to their ships and sailed away.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The smaller force won because of its boldness and determination, not its numbers.
The kural says that the huge, sharp-tusked elephant still takes fright when a tiger attacks, meaning that spirit and energy defeat sheer size, as the Athenians showed at Marathon.
