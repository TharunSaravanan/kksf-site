Kural 1297

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

நாணும் மறந்தேன் அவர்மறக் கல்லாஎன் மாணா மடநெஞ்சிற் பட்டு.
Naanum marandhaen avarmarak kallaaen maanaa madanenjir pattu.

Meaning

My silly heart has no dignity and cannot forget him. Stuck with such a heart, I have even forgotten my modesty.

Elementary

Sometimes when we miss someone very much, we do things we would normally feel shy about, like crying in front of others or running to hug them in public. The wife says her heart missed her husband so much that she forgot to be shy.

University

Nāṇ is the feminine modesty or sense of shame that classical Tamil poetics treats as a woman’s guarding virtue; avar maṟakkallā is ‘unable to forget him’, and māṇā maṭa neñcu ‘my undignified, foolish heart’ (māṇā, lacking excellence; maṭam, simplicity/folly). Paṭṭu, ‘having fallen in with’, makes the heart the bad company that led her astray. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan add that nāṇ is ‘something that should not be forgotten’, while Solomon Pappaiah reads the heart as ‘unsteady’ and the husband as one ‘who has forgotten me’. Pope renders ‘Fall’n ’neath the sway of this ignoble foolish heart, / Which will not him forget, I have forgotten shame.’ The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the cowherd women of Vraja hear Krishna’s flute on the autumn night, they leave their work and families and rush to him in the forest, setting aside the propriety expected of them.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana shows love for Krishna so strong that the gopis forget ordinary restraint.
As with the gopis, the wife’s longing has overridden the modesty she would normally keep.
Christianity
“And stood at his feet behind him weeping, and began to wash his feet with tears, and did wipe them with the hairs of her head, and kissed his feet, and anointed them with the ointment.”
— Luke 7:38 (KJV) [4]
A woman at a Pharisee’s dinner, ignoring what the guests think, pours out her love at Jesus’ feet.
Like the wife, she is carried past ordinary decorum by the depth of her feeling.
Islam
The Prophet taught that faith has many branches and that haya, modesty or a sense of shame, is one branch of faith.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 9, Book of Belief (paraphrase) [5]
Islam treats modesty as part of faith itself, not merely good manners.
The Kural assumes the same high value for modesty, which is why the wife is dismayed to have lost it.
Judaism
“Oh that thou wert as my brother, that sucked the breasts of my mother! When I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; yea, and none would despise me.”
— Song of Songs 8:1 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The woman in the Song wishes she could show her love openly without being scorned.
Both poems feel the tension between love’s urge to show itself and the modesty society expects.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that two bright qualities protect the world, a sense of shame (hiri) and fear of wrongdoing (ottappa); without them, people would not respect even the bonds of family.
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.9 (paraphrase) [7]
Shame and conscience are treated as the guardians that keep people within bounds.
The wife laments that love has made her lose nāṇ, a guardian Buddhism also prizes.
Jainism
“A Sramana, engaged in penance, should not allow himself to watch the shape, beauty, coquetry, laughter, prattle, gestures, and glances of women, nor retain a recollection of them in his mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana tells monks not even to keep the memory of attraction in their minds.
The Kural’s heart does the opposite, unable to stop remembering the beloved, and so loses its restraint.
Sikhism
“If you desire to play this game of love with Me, then step onto My Path with your head in hand. ... give Me your head, and do not pay any attention to public opinion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaraan Thay Vadheek, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that true love for God means giving up concern for what people say, even giving one's head.
The Kural's speaker has lost her modesty to a heart that cannot forget her beloved; Guru Nanak says the path of love requires leaving behind the shame of public opinion.
Greek Mythology
Medea, falling in love with Jason, struggles through a sleepless night between shame (aidos) before her father and her passion; in the end love overcomes her shame and she goes to help him.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.C. Seaton) [10]
Apollonius shows a young woman whose modesty gives way before love.
Like Medea, the wife finds that her heart’s love has overpowered her sense of shame.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates pictures the soul as a charioteer with two horses; when the beloved comes into view, the unruly horse, deaf to shame, drags the charioteer and the modest horse toward the beloved.
— Plato, Phaedrus 253d-254b (paraphrase) [11]
Plato shows passion pulling the soul past its own sense of shame.
The wife’s heart is the unruly horse that has dragged her past her modesty.

Real Life Example

Lady Caroline Lamb’s open pursuit of Lord Byron
1812 · London, United Kingdom
Early in 1812 Lady Caroline Lamb, wife of William Lamb (a future prime minister), read Lord Byron’s new poem Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage and wrote him an anonymous admiring letter. Their relationship that year became a public scandal: Byron’s friend John Cam Hobhouse said all London agreed she had set her heart on him, and Hobhouse barely prevented the two from eloping. In 1816 she published the novel Glenarvon, a thinly veiled account of the affair.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Caroline Lamb’s feelings for Byron overrode every rule of reserve expected of a married woman of her class, and she paid for it with lasting notoriety.
The kural’s speaker says that, caught up with a heart that cannot forget her beloved, she has forgotten even her modesty, which is how London saw Caroline Lamb’s conduct in 1812.
