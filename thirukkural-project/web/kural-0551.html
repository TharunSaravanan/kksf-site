Kural 551

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

கொலைமேற்கொண் டாரிற் கொடிதே அலைமேற்கொண்டு அல்லவை செய்தொழுகும் வேந்து.
Kolaimerkon daarir kodithe alaimerkondu Allavai seythozhukum vendhu.

Meaning

A king who keeps troubling his people and doing wrong to them is crueler than a murderer.

Elementary

Picture a playground bully who knocks you over once, and then picture a class monitor who is supposed to look after everyone but pushes kids around every single day. The monitor is worse, because the hurt never stops and the one person meant to protect you is the one hurting you. Valluvar says a cruel king is like that monitor, only much bigger.

University

The chapter is the mirror image of the preceding one, செங்கோன்மை (the straight sceptre, ch. 55): கொடுங்கோல் is the ‘bent’ rod of a ruler who governs by oppression. The key word அலை means harassing or tormenting, and Parimelazhagar glosses அலைமேற்கொண்டு as taking up, out of covetousness for wealth (பொருள்வெஃகி), the business of harassing the subjects. His reason for the comparison is that the murderer’s harm lasts a moment, whereas the tyrant’s is lifelong, so ‘harassment is crueller than killing’. He also notes the grammatical point that வேந்து, a neuter-class noun, is used here of a person (திணை/பால் மயக்கம்). Manakkudavar gives substantially the same reading, while Solomon Pappaiah stresses greed for the people’s property. The text is uniform across ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net and thirukkural.com; the older continuous text on thirukkural-thamizh.org shows the unsplit sandhi forms யலைமேற்கொண் / டல்லவை.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kamsa, king of Mathura, imprisoned his own father, persecuted the Yadus and had the infant children of his sister Devaki put to death one after another, until Krishna, the child he feared, grew up and killed him.
— Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10, chapters 1-4 and 44 [4]
In the Bhagavata Purana, Kamsa is the classic tyrant whose power is used against the very people and kin he should protect.
Like Valluvar’s cruel king, Kamsa does more harm than an ordinary killer because the whole kingdom lives under his fear.
Christianity
“As a roaring lion, and a ranging bear; so is a wicked ruler over the poor people.”
— Proverbs 28:15 (KJV) [5]
The proverb compares a wicked ruler to beasts of prey loose among the helpless.
Like the Kural, it ranks an unjust ruler alongside the most dangerous killers.
Islam
“Indeed, Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and made its people into factions, oppressing a sector among them, slaughtering their [newborn] sons and keeping their females alive. Indeed, he was of the corrupters.”
— Qur’an 28:4 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an’s portrait of Pharaoh is its central image of a ruler who turns state power into ongoing oppression of his own subjects.
Pharaoh is the Kural’s tyrant exactly: a king whose rule is itself a form of killing.
Judaism
Ezekiel says that the princes of Jerusalem have become like wolves tearing their prey, shedding blood and destroying lives for the sake of dishonest gain.
— Ezekiel 22:27 (JPS 1917) [7]
The prophet accuses the rulers themselves, not outside enemies, of preying on the people for profit.
It matches Parimelazhagar’s reading that the tyrant harasses his subjects out of greed for their wealth.
Buddhism
“The whole nation sleeps badly, when the king is unprincipled.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 4.70, Adhammika Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [8]
The Buddha teaches that when the king goes astray, his officials and then the whole people follow, just as a herd follows its lead bull.
The whole nation suffering day and night echoes Parimelazhagar’s point that the tyrant’s harm never stops, unlike a killer’s single act.
Jainism
“Be without fear, O king; but grant safety to others also; in this transient world of living beings, why are you addicted to cruelty?”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 18.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The monk Gardabhāli answers King Sañjaya, who has been hunting terrified deer in the royal park, by asking him to give other beings the same safety he wants for himself.
The Kural calls a ruler who torments his subjects crueler than a murderer, and the Jain sage likewise confronts a king directly with his cruelty toward helpless beings.
Sikhism
“Those who act like tyrants are accepted and approved - recognize that this is the sign of the Dark Age of Kali Yuga.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 902, Raag Raamkalee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak lists the marks of a degenerate age, and one of them is that oppression by the powerful comes to be treated as lawful and normal.
The Kural calls the ruler who habitually harasses his people crueller than a professional killer; Guru Nanak likewise refuses to let accepted tyranny pass as legitimate and names it a sign of moral darkness.
Greek Mythology
King Lycaon of Arcadia, a cruel ruler, served human flesh to Zeus to test him; Zeus destroyed his house and turned him into a wolf whose savage nature matched his reign.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses I.209-243 [11]
Ovid tells the story as the first example of human wickedness that moves the gods to punish the world.
A king who becomes a literal wolf pictures the Kural’s claim that a tyrant is worse than an ordinary killer.
Philosophy
Socrates describes how the people’s ‘protector’ becomes a tyrant: once he has tasted the blood of his own citizens through unjust trials and killings, he turns into a wolf, as in the Arcadian legend.
— Plato, Republic VIII, 565d-566a [12]
For Plato the tyrant is the worst of men because he turns the power of the state against those he rules.
Plato and Valluvar agree that the ruler who preys on his own people is worse than a common criminal.

Real Life Example

Idi Amin expels Uganda's Asian community
4 August 1972 · Uganda
On 4 August 1972 Uganda's military ruler Idi Amin ordered the country's Asian population, most of them of Indian and Pakistani descent, to leave within 90 days, claiming they were sabotaging the economy. Tens of thousands were forced out, allowed to take only what they could carry, and received no compensation for their homes and businesses. The community had played a central role in Uganda's commerce, and the expulsion contributed to the country's economic decline in the 1970s.
— Sources: [13] [14]
A ruler used the power of the state to strip a whole community of its livelihood and property, causing suffering that lasted for years.
The kural says a king who makes a practice of harassing his people and doing injustice is crueller than a murderer, and Amin's expulsion order harmed tens of thousands for the rest of their lives.
