Kural 561

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

தக்காங்கு நாடித் தலைச்செல்லா வண்ணத்தால் ஒத்தாங்கு ஒறுப்பது வேந்து.
Thakkaangu naadith thalaichchellaa vannaththaal Oththaangu oruppadhu vaendhu.

Meaning

A true king looks into a wrong fairly and carefully, then punishes it in a way that fits the crime, so that it does not happen again.

Elementary

Imagine a teacher who finds a broken window. A good teacher first finds out calmly who did it and why, and then gives a fair consequence, not too big and not too small, so it won't happen again. This kural says a good ruler works the same way: find out the truth first, then correct fairly.

University

The chapter title வெருவந்த செய்யாமை means ‘not doing things that cause dread (veru)’, and Valluvar opens it not by forbidding punishment but by defining its right form. Three steps are packed into the couplet: தக்காங்கு நாடி (investigating as is fitting, i.e., impartially), ஒத்தாங்கு ஒறுத்தல் (punishing in a measure that matches the offence), and the purpose clause தலைச்செல்லா வண்ணத்தால் (so that the wrong does not advance or recur). The traditional commentators, with Parimelazhagar at their head, read this as the positive standard against which the tyranny of the previous chapter (கொடுங்கோன்மை, ch. 56) is measured, and modern glosses (Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar) all stress neutrality in inquiry and proportion in penalty. The definition-style ending ‘… வேந்து’ (‘that is a king’) makes proportionate justice constitutive of kingship itself. Text agrees across ytamizh.com and thirukkural-thamizh.org, the latter printing the older sandhi-joined form (வண்ணத்தா / லொத்தாங் கொறுப்பது) with no change in wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If (punishment) is properly inflicted after (due) consideration, it makes all people happy; but inflicted without consideration, it destroys everything.”
— Manusmriti 7.19 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu teaches that danda (punishment) is a blessing only when applied after careful examination; used rashly, it ruins the whole order it is meant to protect.
Both texts insist that investigation must come before punishment, exactly the Kural’s ‘தக்காங்கு நாடி’.
Christianity
“Doth our law judge any man, before it hear him, and know what he doeth?”
— John 7:51 (KJV) [4]
Nicodemus reminds the council that the law forbids condemning anyone before a hearing and a proper inquiry into what he has done.
Nicodemus’s principle of hearing before judging is the first step the Kural requires of a king.
Islam
“And if you punish [an enemy, O believers], punish with an equivalent of that with which you were harmed. But if you are patient - it is better for those who are patient.”
— Quran 16:126 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran allows retribution only in strict equivalence to the harm done, and praises restraint above even that.
The Kural’s ‘ஒத்தாங்கு ஒறுத்தல்’ (punishing in matching measure) is the same principle of equivalence.
Judaism
“And the judges shall make diligent inquisition … And those which remain shall hear, and fear, and shall henceforth commit no more any such evil among you.”
— Deuteronomy 19:18, 20 (KJV) [4]
Deuteronomy requires judges to investigate diligently, and gives the purpose of the penalty as preventing the evil from being repeated.
Diligent inquiry plus a penalty aimed at non-recurrence is almost exactly ‘தக்காங்கு நாடித் தலைச்செல்லா வண்ணத்தால்’.
Buddhism
“It is my desire that there should be uniformity in law and uniformity in sentencing.”
— Ashoka, Pillar Edict IV (trans. S. Dhammika) [6]
The Buddhist emperor Ashoka ordered his judicial officers (rajjukas) to judge consistently and even granted a three-day stay to those sentenced to death so appeals could be heard.
Ashoka’s even-handed, reviewable sentencing is a royal program for the Kural’s fair, fitting punishment.
Jainism
“Men frequently apply punishment wrongly: the innocent are put in prison, and the perpetrator of the crime is set at liberty.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
When Indra, disguised as a Brahmin, urges King Nami to punish thieves and robbers to prove himself a true Kshatriya, Nami answers that human punishment is often misdirected.
The Kural requires a king to investigate impartially before he punishes; Nami's reply names the injustice that follows when that inquiry is skipped.
Sikhism
“The Lord and Master is True, and True is His justice. He issues His Commands according to His judgement.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 723, Raag Tilang (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Closing his hymn on Babar's invasion, Guru Nanak affirms that the Lord's commands follow from true judgement, so that what befalls each person answers to justice.
The Kural says a true king first inquires fairly and then punishes in due measure; Guru Nanak's model of justice is likewise a command that issues only from true judgement.
Greek Mythology
In Greek myth Rhadamanthys, famed for his strict fairness, judges the dead; Plato’s Gorgias imagines souls judged stripped of rank and finery so that the judge sees each soul exactly as it is.
— Plato, Gorgias 523a–524a (myth of judgment) [9]
The myth makes the ideal judge one who examines the true character of each case without being swayed by status.
The Kural’s demand for impartial investigation before punishment is personified in Rhadamanthys.
Philosophy
“But he who desires to inflict rational punishment does not retaliate for a past wrong which cannot be undone; he has regard to the future, and is desirous that the man who is punished, and he who sees him punished, may be deterred from doing wrong again.”
— Plato, Protagoras 324a–b (trans. B. Jowett) [10]
Protagoras argues that rational punishment looks forward, aiming to prevent repetition rather than to vent anger over the past.
This is the Kural’s purpose clause ‘தலைச்செல்லா வண்ணத்தால்’, punishing so that the wrong does not recur.

Real Life Example

John Adams defends the British soldiers after the Boston Massacre
October-December 1770 · Boston, Massachusetts (then a British colony), United States
After British soldiers fired into a crowd in Boston on 5 March 1770, Captain Thomas Preston and eight soldiers were indicted, and John Adams agreed to serve on their unpopular defence team. Preston was acquitted on 30 October 1770 after a trial lasting longer than a day, and in the soldiers' trial, decided on 5 December 1770, six men were acquitted and two were found guilty of manslaughter rather than murder. The two convicted men avoided the death penalty by pleading benefit of clergy and were branded on the hand instead.
— Sources: [11] [12]
In a town furious at the army, the case was settled by testimony heard in court, and the verdicts distinguished between the men according to what each had actually done.
The kural says a true ruler first inquires impartially and then punishes in measure with the offence, and the Boston trials show inquiry before judgement and a penalty matched to the proven wrong rather than to public anger.
