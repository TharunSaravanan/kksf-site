Kural 1247

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

காமம் விடுஒன்றோ நாண்விடு நன்னெஞ்சே யானோ பொறேன்இவ் விரண்டு.
Kaamam viduondroa naanvidu nannenjae yaanoa poraen-iv virandu.

Meaning

My good heart, either let go of your love or let go of your modesty. I cannot carry both of them at once.

Elementary

Imagine wanting very badly to tell someone how much you miss them, but feeling too shy to say it. Holding both feelings at once is hard, so you tell yourself, “Either stop missing them, or stop being shy and just tell them!”

University

This is the chapter’s best-known dilemma: kāmam viṭu oṉṟō (‘either let go of desire’) nāṇ viṭu (‘or let go of modesty’), for yāṉō poṟēṉ iv iraṇṭu (‘I cannot bear these two together’). Nāṇ (modesty, shame) is a defining virtue of the Tamil heroine, which normally forbids her from going to the man or declaring her longing; kāmam here means love-desire, not merely physical lust. The heart is called naṉṉeñcu (‘good heart’), a coaxing address. Kalaignar makes the conflict explicit (desire drives her to speak, modesty stops her), and Pappaiah stresses that the two pull in opposite directions; Pope renders it ‘Or bid thy love, or bid thy shame depart’. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the gopis of Vraja hear Krishna’s flute at night, they abandon their household duties and the restraints of family and propriety, and hurry to meet him in the forest.
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.29.4-8 (paraphrase) [3]
In the Bhagavata’s account, love for Krishna overpowers the gopis’ sense of social shame.
The Kural’s heroine faces the same choice between love and modesty; the gopis resolve it by letting modesty go.
Christianity
“For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I.”
— Romans 7:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes a self torn between two opposing pulls that he cannot reconcile.
The Kural’s heroine is likewise torn between two forces, desire and modesty, and finds she cannot bear both.
Islam
The Prophet, passing a man who was admonishing his brother for being too modest, told him to leave him, for modesty (haya’) is part of faith.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 24 (Book of Belief) (paraphrase) [5]
Islamic teaching holds modesty to be an essential virtue, not a weakness to be overcome.
The Kural’s heroine feels modesty as a real value even while love presses her to abandon it.
Judaism
“Oh that thou wert as my brother, That sucked the breasts of my mother! When I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; Yea, and none would despise me.”
— Song of Songs 8:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The woman of the Song wishes she could show her love openly without incurring public shame.
This is exactly the Kural’s dilemma: love wants to show itself, but modesty and fear of reproach hold it back.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that two bright qualities, hiri (moral shame) and ottappa (fear of wrongdoing), protect the world; without them, people would lose all restraint.
— Anguttara Nikaya 2.9 (paraphrase) [7]
In Buddhism, a sense of shame is a guardian virtue that keeps desire in check.
The Kural’s heroine feels the pull of this guardian, modesty, against her desire, and cannot bear both.
Jainism
“…all these manifold passions arise in him who is attached to pleasures; and so do other emotions produced by those (before mentioned) arise in him who is to be pitied, who (ought to be) ashamed of himself, and who is hateful.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.102-103 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Jain text lists the many passions, including fear and sorrow, that spring up in one attached to pleasures, leaving him a figure of shame.
The Kural’s heroine feels desire and shame at war within her; the Jain answer would be to let go of desire, not modesty.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, when you are in love with the One Lord, duality and alienation depart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1365, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says that a heart wholly given to the One Lord is no longer torn between rival pulls.
The Kural's wife tells her heart to give up either desire or modesty, because she cannot bear both at once; Kabir offers the Sikh answer to a divided heart, single-minded love that ends the inner tug-of-war.
Greek Mythology
Medea, falling in love with Jason, spends a sleepless night torn between shame (aidōs) and love: her modesty and loyalty to her father hold her back, while love drives her to help the stranger.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.616-824 (paraphrase) [10]
Apollonius gives a famous portrait of a young woman divided between modesty and passion.
This is precisely the Kural’s dilemma: the heroine cannot carry both love and modesty and begs her heart to drop one.
Philosophy
In Plato’s image of the soul as a chariot, one horse is restrained by modesty and shame, while the other is driven by desire toward the beloved, and the charioteer is pulled painfully between them.
— Plato, Phaedrus 253c-254e (paraphrase) [11]
Plato pictures the lover’s soul as divided between a horse of shame and a horse of desire.
The Kural’s heroine experiences exactly this divided pull between modesty and desire, and finds it unbearable.

Real Life Example

Clara Wieck goes to court against her father to marry Robert Schumann
1837-1840 · Leipzig, Saxony (now Germany)
The pianist Clara Wieck and the composer Robert Schumann became secretly engaged in 1837, but her father Friedrich Wieck rejected Robert's written request for her hand and did everything he could to keep them apart. Rather than give Robert up, Clara joined him in taking her father to court for permission to marry; her father disowned her, the court gave them permission in 1840, and they married on 12 September 1840 at Schönefeld near Leipzig.
— Sources: [12] [13]
For a young woman of her time, suing her own father was a startling break with the duty of obedience expected of a daughter.
The kural's heroine says she cannot bear both her desire and her modesty and must give up one, and Clara Wieck, caught between a daughter's duty and her love, finally chose love.
