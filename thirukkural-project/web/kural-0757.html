Kural 757

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

அருளென்னும் அன்பீன் குழவி பொருளென்னும் செல்வச் செவிலியால் உண்டு.
Arulennum anbeen kuzhavi porulennum selvach cheviliyaal undu.

Meaning

Kindness is the child born to love, and it is brought up by a rich nanny called money.

Elementary

Picture a baby born to a loving mother, cared for by a kind nanny who has plenty of food and warm blankets. Valluvar says kindness is like that baby: love gives birth to it, and money helps it grow, because it is easier to help others when you have something to give.

University

This is one of the Kural’s finest extended metaphors. Arul (compassion for all beings) is the kuzhavi, the infant, born of anbu (love for one’s own; anbeen is anbu plus eenra, ‘borne by love’), and porul is the selvach cheviliyal, the rich wet-nurse or foster-mother (cevili) who raises it. The genealogy reverses the usual worry that wealth corrupts: love begets compassion, but compassion cannot grow into practical kindness without means. It links back to chapter 8, Anbudaimai, which says love begets affection and friendship, and it answers 755 by showing the right relation of wealth to arul and anbu. The sandhi-joined text reads அருளென்னு மன்பீன் குழவி பொருளென்னுஞ், which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Taittiriya Upanishad’s parting counsel to graduating students tells them to give with faith, to give in plenty, to give with modesty, with awe, and with sympathy.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.3 [3]
The teacher links generous giving to an attitude of reverence and fellow-feeling.
Giving ‘in plenty’ joined to sympathy echoes the Kural’s picture of wealth nursing compassion.
Christianity
“But whoso hath this world’s good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him?”
— 1 John 3:17 (KJV) [4]
John says that love is real only when a person who has means opens both heart and purse to someone in need.
John links love, compassion and ‘this world’s good’ in the same chain as the Kural.
Islam
“[True] righteousness is [in] one who believes in Allah... and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask [for help], and for freeing slaves.”
— Qur’an 2:177 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an defines true righteousness as faith shown in the giving of wealth to those in need, even though one loves that wealth.
In both texts, compassion takes practical form through wealth given away.
Judaism
“Where there is no bread, there is no Torah; where there is no Torah, there is no bread.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:17 (trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah teaches that spiritual life and material sustenance depend on each other.
As the Kural’s compassion needs a well-provided nurse, Torah needs bread in order to flourish.
Buddhism
“And they’d hold on to friends by dividing their wealth in four.”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha tells the young householder Sigalaka that a wise person keeps friendships alive by managing wealth well and sharing it.
Here too, well-kept wealth nourishes bonds of love and care.
Jainism
“Yathavat atithau sadhau dine cha pratipatti-krt : Diligent in succoring the ascetics, the righteous and the needy.” … “Sadaya : Compassionate.”
— Hemacandra, Yogaśāstra, thirty-five qualities of an ideal śrāvaka, nos. 19 and 31, as rendered in Vilas A. Sangave, ‘Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code’ (jainworld.com) [8]
Hemacandra's ideal householder, who first earns wealth honestly, is to be compassionate and to use his means in supporting ascetics, good people and the poor.
The Kural pictures compassion as a child born of love and nursed by wealth, and the Jain layman's code likewise expects the compassionate householder to sustain the needy and the holy from his resources.
Sikhism
When Guru Nanak left his post as storekeeper to the Nawab of Sultanpur, complaints were made of his extravagance; but on investigation the storehouse was found full and his accounts correct, and money was even found due to him from the State, which the Guru refused, asking the Nawab to spend it in relieving the wants of the poor.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion, vol. 1 (1909), Life of Guru Nanak, pp. 42-43 [9]
Macauliffe, following the Janamsakhi tradition, tells how Nanak's honestly kept wealth, far from being lost, became a means of care for the poor.
The Kural pictures compassion as a child nursed by the rich foster-mother called wealth; in this story honestly earned wealth is turned at once into the nourishment of compassion.
Greek Mythology
Athens set up Kephisodotos’ statue of Eirene (Peace) holding the infant Ploutos (Wealth) in her arms, and Pausanias describes a similar statue at Thebes of Tyche carrying the child Ploutos.
— Pausanias, Description of Greece 1.8.2 and 9.16.2 [10]
Greek art pictured wealth as a baby nursed and carried by a goddess.
The Greek image uses the same nurse-and-infant figure as the Kural, but reversed: in Athens Peace nurses Wealth, while in the Kural Wealth nurses Compassion.
Philosophy
In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle defines liberality as the virtue of giving to the right people, in the right amounts, at the right time, and notes that the liberal person must also take from the right sources so as to have something to give.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV, Chapter 1 [11]
For Aristotle, generosity is a real virtue, but it needs property to work with.
Aristotle, like Valluvar, sees kindness in action as needing wealth to grow.

Real Life Example

Zubaydah funds wells and rest stations on the pilgrim road to Mecca
late 8th - early 9th century · Darb Zubaydah, from Kufa (Iraq) to Mecca (Saudi Arabia)
Zubaydah bint Jafar, wife of the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid, supported charitable works along the pilgrimage road linking Kufa and Baghdad with Mecca. As growing numbers of pilgrims strained the water and food of the Arabian desert, she personally paid for many new intermediate rest stops for pilgrims travelling on foot, along with wells and reservoirs. The road became known as the Darb Zubaydah, and its Iraqi and Saudi sections are on UNESCO's World Heritage tentative list.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A royal fortune was turned into water and shelter for ordinary travellers, and the whole route came to bear her name.
The kural calls compassion the child of love, raised by the wealthy foster-mother called wealth, and Zubaydah's riches gave her care for pilgrims the means to save lives across the desert.
