Kural 830

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

பகைநட்பாம் காலம் வருங்கால் முகநட்டு அகநட்பு ஒரீஇ விடல்.
Paganatpaam kaalam varungaal muganattu aganatpu oreei vidal.

Meaning

When the time comes that you must treat an enemy like a friend, be friendly on your face but keep them out of your heart, and drop them when you can.

Elementary

Sometimes you have to work on a group project with someone who has been unkind to you. You can be polite and work together, but you don’t have to hand them your diary. Be friendly on the outside and careful on the inside.

University

Pagai-naṭpaam kaalam (பகை நட்பாம் காலம்) is ‘a time when enmity turns into friendship’, for instance an alliance forced by circumstances. The couplet resolves the chapter with the face/heart pair already used in Kural 824: muga-naṭṭu (முகநட்டு, befriending with the face) but aga-naṭpu oreei (அகநட்பு ஒரீஇ, removing friendship from the heart). Oreei (ஒரீஇ) is the alapeḍai-lengthened form of oree, ‘leaving’, and viḍal (விடல்) means ‘one should give it up’. Varadarasanar reads the final word as giving up even the outward friendship when the chance comes, Pappaiah as letting go of it in the mind, and Pope follows the first reading with ‘give up (even the former)’. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A weak person having made a compact with a stronger one when both are threatened by foes, should (when that common danger passes away) conduct himself heedfully and by considerations of policy. Having gained his object, the weaker of the two parties should not again repose confidence on the stronger.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section 138, the cat and the mouse (Ganguli translation) [3]
The mouse Palita, who made a temporary alliance with the cat Lomasa to escape a hunter, refuses the cat’s invitations to continued friendship once the danger has passed.
This is exactly the Kural’s counsel: accept a necessary friendship with a foe, but never let it into the heart.
Christianity
“If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.”
— Romans 12:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul urges believers to keep the peace with everyone so far as it depends on them.
The Kural too keeps outward peace with a former enemy, though it adds a caution Paul does not.
Islam
“And if they incline to peace, then incline to it [also] and rely upon Allah. Indeed, it is He who is the Hearing, the Knowing. But if they intend to deceive you - then sufficient for you is Allah.”
— Qur’an 8:61-62 (Saheeh International) [5]
Believers are to accept a hostile party’s offer of peace, while recognising it may be a deception.
Accept the enemy’s turn to friendship outwardly, but keep your reliance elsewhere: the Kural’s balance.
Judaism
When Jacob meets Esau after twenty years of enmity, they embrace and weep, and Esau offers to travel with him. Jacob graciously declines, sending Esau on ahead, and then settles in a different direction at Succoth.
— Genesis 33:1-17 [6]
Jacob accepts the reconciliation outwardly but does not put himself in his brother’s power.
Friendly in face, guarded in heart: Jacob’s conduct matches the Kural’s advice.
Buddhism
“He who is friendly amidst the hostile, peaceful amidst the violent, and unattached amidst the attached — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada 406 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada praises one who stays friendly and unattached in the midst of hostility.
Friendliness among the hostile without attachment loosely mirrors the Kural’s friendly face with an uncommitted heart.
Jainism
“Benevolence (maitrī) towards all living-beings (sattva), joy (pramoda) at the sight of the virtuous (guṇādhika), compassion and sympathy (kāruṇya) for the afflicted (kliśyamāna), and tolerance (mādhyasthya) towards the insolent and ill-behaved (avineya) are the other observances.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.11 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Tattvārtha Sūtra prescribes four attitudes to support the vows; toward the ill-disposed the Jain response is mādhyasthya, a detached equanimity free from both attachment and aversion.
Valluvar advises being friendly in face but withholding friendship in the heart when a foe must be treated as a friend; the Jain answer is also inner detachment from the hostile, but grounded in goodwill to all beings rather than political calculation.
Sikhism
During the siege of Anandpur in 1704, the Mughal commanders and the allied hill chiefs offered Guru Gobind Singh safe passage, backed by an assurance sworn on the Quran and an oath by the cow. The Guru had advised staying, but under starvation the Sikhs left the fort, and they were attacked near the Sarsa river with all the oaths broken.
— Wikipedia, 'Second siege of Anandpur' (citing Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion, vol. 5, and J. S. Grewal, Guru Gobind Singh, 2019) [9]
Sikh historical memory treats the broken oaths of Anandpur as the classic case of enemies offering friendship that was never meant, and Guru Gobind Singh's Zafarnama later rebuked Aurangzeb for them.
The Kural says that when an enemy turns friendly one may be friendly in face but must keep friendship out of the heart; the Guru's distrust of the oaths at Anandpur shows why.
Greek Mythology
After their single combat ends undecided at nightfall, Hector and Ajax exchange gifts, a sword and a belt, and part as men who fought in hatred but separated in friendship, yet they remain on opposite sides of the war.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 7 [10]
Enemies show each other the courtesies of friendship while remaining enemies.
A friendly face between foes without real alliance is the situation the Kural addresses.
Philosophy
“they neither love warmly nor hate bitterly, but following the hint of Bias they love as though they will some day hate and hate as though they will some day love.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.13 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle describes the guarded attitude of the experienced, following the sage Bias of Priene.
Loving ‘as though they will some day hate’ is the inner reserve the Kural advises toward a foe turned friend.

Real Life Example

Alexander I's alliance with Napoleon after Tilsit
1807-1812 · Tilsit, East Prussia (now Sovetsk, Russia), and Russia
After Russia's defeat at Friedland, Tsar Alexander I met Napoleon on a raft in the Neman River, and in July 1807 the Treaty of Tilsit made Russia France's ally and committed it to Napoleon's Continental Blockade against British trade. Russia's economy depended heavily on British trade, and its support for the blockade was half-hearted. On 31 December 1810 Alexander issued a decree reopening Russian ports to neutral shipping, which in effect readmitted British trade, and in June 1812 Napoleon invaded Russia.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
An alliance forced on Alexander by defeat was kept up in public for a time and then allowed to lapse once Russia was ready to stand on its own.
The kural says that when an enemy must for a time be treated as a friend, one should be friendly in face, keep friendship out of the heart, and let it go when one can, which is the course the Tilsit alliance followed.
