Kural 359

Knowledge of the True · Meyyunarthal · மெய்யுணர்தல்

சார்புணர்ந்து சார்பு கெடஒழுகின் மற்றழித்துச் சார்தரா சார்தரு நோய்.
Saarbunarndhu saarbu keda ozhukin matrazhiththuch Saardharaa saardharu noi.

Meaning

Whoever understands what truly supports all things, and lives so that all other attachments fall away, will never be touched by the sorrows that would otherwise cling to them.

Elementary

If you’re holding a hundred balloons, the wind can drag you anywhere. But if you let go of them and hold on to one strong railing, you stay safe. This kural says: know what is truly worth holding on to, let the other things go, and troubles can’t grab hold of you.

University

The couplet is built on a remarkable pun: the root saar- (‘to lean on, cling to, attach’) appears four times: saarbu unarndhu (knowing the true support), saarbu keda (so that attachments perish), saardharaa (will not cling) and saardharu noi (the afflictions that would cling). Commentators read the first saarbu as the Supreme Truth (semporul), the support of all things, and the second as worldly attachment (pattru). The wordplay thus contrasts one legitimate ‘leaning’ with many illegitimate ones. The logic, that attachment is the channel through which suffering ‘attaches’, is shared with Buddhist teaching on upadana (grasping) and with the Gita’s call to cut the world-tree ‘with the weapon of detachment’ while taking refuge in its root. Islam’s divine name as-Samad, the One on whom all depend, offers an especially close lexical parallel to the first saarbu.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But with determination one must cut down this tree with the weapon of detachment. So doing, one must seek that place from which, having once gone, one never returns, and there surrender to that Supreme Personality of Godhead from whom everything has begun and in whom everything is abiding since time immemorial.”
— Bhagavad Gita 15.3–4 [3]
Krishna pictures worldly existence as a deep-rooted tree to be cut with detachment, while the seeker takes refuge in the Supreme in whom everything rests.
The same double movement as the Kural: cut false attachments (saarbu keda) and rest in the true Support of all (saarbu unarndhu).
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal … For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.”
— Matthew 6:19–21 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns against clinging to perishable possessions and tells his hearers to anchor their hearts in heaven instead.
Like the Kural, what we cling to decides whether loss and sorrow can reach us.
Islam
“Allāh, the Eternal Refuge.”
— Qur’an 112:2 (Sahih International) [5]
The Arabic as-Samad describes God as the One on whom all things depend and to whom everyone turns, while He depends on nothing.
As-Samad is a close equivalent of the Kural’s first ‘saarbu’, the true Support of all things, which the wise must recognize.
Judaism
“Put not your trust in princes, nor in the son of man, in whom there is no help. … Happy is he that hath the God of Jacob for his help, whose hope is in the LORD his God.”
— Psalm 146:3, 5 (KJV) [4]
The Psalmist warns against leaning on mortal powers that fail, and blesses the one whose support is God alone.
Same contrast as the Kural: false supports that fail versus the one true Support.
Buddhism
“From attachment springs grief, from attachment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from attachment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 214 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha traces grief and fear to attachment: remove the attachment and they have nothing to hold on to.
Exactly the Kural’s logic: when attachment perishes, the afflictions that would cling can no longer cling.
Jainism
“Thus the objects of the senses and of the mind cause pain to passionate men, but they never in the least cause any pain to the dispassionate.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.100 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 190) [7]
After surveying all six sense-fields, the sermon concludes that pain can attach only where passion gives it something to hold. The dispassionate person offers no foothold.
The Kural's wordplay on saar- (clinging) says that afflictions which would cling will not cling to one whose attachments have perished. The Jain verse states the same: objects that hurt the passionate cannot touch the dispassionate.
Sikhism
“Meditate on the One, O my mind, who is the Support of all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 51, Siree Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan tells the mind to meditate on the One God, the support of all beings.
The Kural says that whoever understands the true Support of all things will be free of the sorrows that cling; Guru Arjan names that Support and asks the mind to rest on it.
Greek Mythology
Warned by Circe, Odysseus has his crew plug their ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast so that he can hear the Sirens’ song without being pulled to destruction by it.
— Homer, Odyssey 12 [9]
Knowing in advance what the lure really is, Odysseus holds fast to something firm and passes by the danger unharmed.
Like the Kural, knowledge plus refusal to be seized by attraction keeps ruin from taking hold.
Philosophy
Epictetus opens his Handbook by dividing everything into what is up to us (our judgments, desires and aversions) and what is not (body, property, reputation), teaching that suffering comes from attaching ourselves to what is not ours.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 [10]
For the Stoics, clearly knowing what one’s good actually depends on, and detaching from everything else, makes a person unshakable.
Parallel to the Kural: know the true support, drop false dependencies, and misfortune can no longer grip you.

Real Life Example

Kamo no Chomei writes An Account of My Hut
1212 · Hino, near Kyoto, Japan
Kamo no Chomei, a poet from a family of Shinto shrine officials, became a Buddhist monk at about fifty and withdrew from the capital, living first at Ohara and then in a tiny hut he built in the hills of Hino, southeast of Kyoto. In 1212 he wrote the Hojoki, An Account of My Hut, named for the hut's size of ten feet square. The work first describes the fires, whirlwind, famine and earthquake that had struck Kyoto in his lifetime, then contrasts them with the quiet of his simple life in the hills.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Chomei set the instability of worldly things beside the peace he found once he had let go of house, rank and possessions, and the Hojoki became a classic of Japanese literature on that theme.
The kural says that one who knows the true support and lets lesser attachments fall away is no longer gripped by sorrows, and Chomei's record of disasters followed by contentment in a ten-foot hut illustrates that loosening of attachment.
