Kural 1191

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

தாம்வீழ்வார் தம்வீழப் பெற்றவர் பெற்றாரே காமத்துக் காழில் கனி.
Thaamveezhvaar thamveezhap petravar petraare kaamaththuk kaazhil kani.

Meaning

Those who are loved back by the very one they love have won love's best prize. It is like a fruit with no hard seed inside, all sweetness.

Elementary

Imagine you save half your sandwich for your best friend, and your best friend has saved half of theirs for you. That happy feeling of sharing both ways is like biting into a fruit with no pit at all: nothing hard to spoil it.

University

Chapter 120, tanippaṭar mikuti (‘the excess of solitary suffering’), belongs to the karpiyal (married love) sequence on separation; in the traditional reading associated with Parimelazhagar the heroine, left alone, fears that her longing is not matched by her absent husband’s and speaks to her confidante (tōḻi). The chapter opens by defining what she lacks: tām vīḻvār tam vīḻap peṟṟavar, those whom the ones they desire (vīḻ, ‘to desire, fall for’) desire in turn. Kāḻ il kaṉi is ‘a fruit without a stone (kāḻ)’, i.e. pleasure without any hard residue of pain; Varadarasanar and Pappaiah both gloss it as the seedless fruit that is the yield (payaṉ) of married love. Pope renders: ‘The bliss to be beloved by those they love who gains, / Of love the stoneless, luscious fruit obtains.’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“In that family, where the husband is pleased with his wife and the wife with her husband, happiness will assuredly be lasting.”
— Manusmriti 3.60 (tr. Georg Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Manu’s household code makes lasting happiness depend on the delight being mutual, the husband pleased with the wife and the wife with the husband.
The Kural’s ‘stoneless fruit’ is exactly this two-way pleasure, which Manu calls the secure root of a happy home.
Christianity
“My beloved is mine, and I am his: he feedeth among the lilies.”
— Song of Solomon 2:16 (KJV) [4]
The bride in the Song of Solomon sums up her joy in a single line of perfect reciprocity.
‘Mine, and I am his’ is the Kural’s condition for the seedless fruit: the one I love loves me.
Islam
“There is nothing like marriage, for two who love one another.”
— Sunan Ibn Majah 1847 (graded hasan by Darussalam) [5]
The Prophet says that for two people who already love each other, nothing compares with marriage.
Both texts single out love that runs both ways as the best thing married life can give.
Judaism
“I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine, That feedeth among the lilies.”
— Song of Songs 6:3 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Shulammite states the reciprocity of love as a balanced formula, her belonging to him matched by his to her.
The Kural’s happy lovers are those for whom this formula is true in both directions.
Buddhism
The householders Nakula’s father and Nakula’s mother tell the Buddha that since their youthful marriage neither has been unfaithful even in thought, and that they wish to see each other in this life and the next; the Buddha teaches that a couple matched in faith, virtue, generosity and discernment will meet again.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Samajivina discourse treats a wholly mutual marriage as a real good worth wishing to prolong.
Nakula’s parents are the Buddhist picture of the Kural’s fortunate pair whose love is returned in full.
Jainism
Among the lay vows, the vow of chastity for a householder is framed as svadāra-santoṣa, contentment with one’s own spouse, which channels desire into a single faithful marriage.
— Ratnakaranda Shravakachara, on the vow of brahmacarya-anuvrata (paraphrase; cf. tr. Champat Rai Jain) [8]
Jain householder ethics do not praise love’s sweetness, but they do honour a marriage in which each partner’s desire rests contentedly on the other alone.
The closest Jain counterpart to the Kural’s fortunate lovers is the householder couple content in each other, though Jainism ranks even this below renunciation.
Sikhism
“She alone is the happy soul-bride, who is pleasing to God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 391, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that the true sohagan, the fortunate wedded soul-bride, is the one her Lord loves in return.
The Kural says that those loved back by the one they love have won love's stoneless fruit; Gurbani likewise names the soul-bride who pleases her Beloved as the truly blessed one.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus wishes the young princess Nausicaa a husband and a home of one mind, saying there is nothing better or nobler than when husband and wife keep house together in harmony, a grief to their enemies and a joy to their friends.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.180-185 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer has his hero name like-mindedness between husband and wife as the greatest blessing of life.
The Kural’s seedless fruit and Homer’s best of all gifts are the same thing: two hearts that agree.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that the friendship of husband and wife is natural and can include usefulness, pleasure and, where both are good, virtue, each delighting in the other’s goodness.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.12, 1162a (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle the best marriage is a friendship in which the good and the pleasure flow both ways.
The Kural’s happiest lovers are Aristotle’s married friends, whose delight is returned in kind.

Real Life Example

Robert Schumann and Clara Wieck win the right to marry
12 September 1840 · Schönefeld, near Leipzig, Germany
Clara Wieck's father, Friedrich Wieck, opposed her marriage to the composer Robert Schumann, rejected his written proposal and kept the couple apart. Robert and Clara took Wieck to court, and in 1840 the court ruled in their favour. They married on 12 September 1840 in the parish church at Schönefeld, near Leipzig.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Because the love ran equally in both directions, the couple were willing to go to law together to win it, and their marriage became one of the great partnerships in music.
The kural calls love that is returned by the very one you love a seedless, all-sweet fruit, and the Schumanns fought for exactly that kind of matched love.
