Kural 594

Energetics · Ookkamudaimai · ஊக்கமுடைமை

ஆக்கம் அதர்வினாய்ச் செல்லும் அசைவிலா ஊக்க முடையா னுழை.
Aakkam adharvinaaich chellum asaivilaa Ookkam udaiyaan uzhai.

Meaning

Good fortune itself asks for directions and goes to the home of a person whose drive never weakens.

Elementary

Picture good luck as a little traveller with a map, going around asking, ‘Where does the hardworking kid live?’ The Kural says success goes looking for people who never give up.

University

Valluvar personifies aakkam (prosperity, increase) as a traveller who ‘adhar vinaai’, asks the road (adhar = path), to reach the dwelling (uzhai) of the man of unwavering (asaivilaa) energy. Parimelazhagar notes that prosperity need not be sought by such a person; it seeks him, a reversal of the usual image of the striver chasing fortune. Solomon Pappaiah’s modern rendering, ‘wealth learns his address and goes to him’, keeps the playful personification. The idea closely matches the Sanskrit subhāṣita ‘udyoginaṁ puruṣa-siṁham upaiti lakṣmīḥ’ (Lakshmi approaches the lion-like man of effort), found in the Hitopadesha prologue, showing a shared Indian topos. The two base sources agree on the text; thirukkural.net joins ‘ookka mudaiyaa nuzhai’ as in the metrical manuscripts, which is simply the sandhi form of ‘ookkam udaiyaan uzhai’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Lakshmi, goddess of fortune, approaches the lion-like man of effort; only cowards say that fortune is simply handed out by fate, so set fate aside and exert yourself with your own strength.
— Hitopadesha, Introduction, verse 33 [3]
The Hitopadesha’s famous verse says prosperity comes to the energetic, not to those who wait on destiny.
This is almost the Kural’s own image: fortune travels to the unwavering man of energy.
Christianity
“His lord said unto him, Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the joy of thy lord.”
— Matthew 25:21 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the talents, the servants who actively traded with their money are given far more, while the one who buried his talent loses it.
As in the Kural, increase flows to the one who acts energetically with what he has.
Islam
“And that there is not for man except that [good] for which he strives”
— Qur’an 53:39 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an states that what a person gains corresponds to the effort he puts in.
The Kural similarly ties prosperity to unwavering effort.
Judaism
Proverbs teaches that the one who works with a slack hand becomes poor, while the hand of the diligent brings riches.
— Proverbs 10:4 [6]
The sages of Proverbs connect wealth directly to diligent, energetic effort and poverty to slackness.
The Kural says the same, adding the image of prosperity actively seeking out the diligent.
Buddhism
“Ever grows the glory of him who is energetic, mindful and pure in conduct, discerning and self-controlled, righteous and heedful.”
— Dhammapada 24 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that the energetic, careful person steadily grows in honour and standing.
Like the Kural, it promises increase to the one whose energy does not flag.
Jainism
“Fields and houses, gold, cattle, slaves and servants: where these four goods, the causes of pleasure, are present, in such families he is born. He will have friends and relations, be of good family, of fine complexion, healthy, wise, noble, famous, and powerful.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 3.17–18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana says that one who strenuously fulfils the Law and leaves this body rises to heaven and is later reborn into a family where prosperity and every advantage are already present.
The Kural pictures prosperity finding its own way to the door of the steadfast; here too prosperity arrives unsought for the one who has striven, though in Jainism it comes through karmic merit and rebirth.
Sikhism
“Make the effort, and you shall live; practicing it, you shall enjoy peace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 522, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Gujri ki Vaar, Guru Arjan promises that sustained effort brings life and that its practice brings peace.
The Kural says prosperity itself asks the way to the home of the person of unwavering energy; Guru Arjan likewise makes good fortune follow from effort.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod tells his brother Perses that the gods have set sweat before excellence, that work is no disgrace, and that if he works the idle will soon envy him as he grows rich, for fame and renown attend on wealth.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 289-313 [10]
Hesiod’s farming poem teaches that prosperity and honour follow the hardworking person.
This matches the Kural’s promise that prosperity comes to the one of unfailing energy.
Philosophy
In Terence’s comedy Phormio, a character declares that fortune helps the brave (fortes fortuna adiuvat), a proverb later repeated by Pliny and Cicero.
— Terence, Phormio, line 203 [11]
The Latin proverb says good fortune sides with the bold and energetic.
It is a Roman version of the Kural’s image of fortune seeking out the unwavering.

Real Life Example

Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita found the company that became Sony
1946 · Tokyo, Japan
In 1946, in a Tokyo still recovering from the war, Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita founded Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo (Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering Corporation), which began by making devices such as voltmeters. Early consumer products, including an electric rice cooker and Japan's first tape recorder, sold poorly, but the founders licensed the transistor from Bell Laboratories in the early 1950s and built transistor radios. The company renamed itself Sony in 1958 and in 1961 became the first Japanese company to list its shares on the New York Stock Exchange.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Two engineers with little capital kept working through failures, and prosperity followed their persistence rather than preceding it.
The kural says prosperity finds its own way to the home of the person whose energy never wavers, and Sony's growth from a small postwar workshop shows it arriving after years of steady effort.
