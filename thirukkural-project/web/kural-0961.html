Kural 961

Honour · Maanam · மானம்

இன்றி அமையாச் சிறப்பின ஆயினும் குன்ற வருப விடல்.
Indri amaiyaach chirappina aayinum Kundra varupa vidal.

Meaning

Even if something seems so important that you cannot live without it, give it up if doing it would lower your self-respect.

Elementary

Imagine you could win the biggest prize at school, but only by copying someone else's answers. The prize feels like something you cannot live without, but this kural says: if getting it makes you smaller inside, walk away from it.

University

The chapter on maanam (மானம், honour, self-respect) follows Kudimai (Nobility of Family) and is read in the Parimelazhagar tradition as the quality of never sinking below one's station. The opening couplet sets the bar at its highest: indri amaiyaa (இன்றி அமையா, 'that without which one cannot exist') describes acts that seem indispensable, and kundra varupa (குன்ற வருப) are acts that bring about a 'lowering' (kundru, to diminish), which must be let go (vidal, விடல்). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan both gloss the lowering as a fall in kudi-p-perumai (the honour of one's lineage), tying the chapter to the preceding one; Kalaignar generalises it to one's own dignity. The Pope translation quoted on ytamizh.com renders it 'Reject each act that may thine honour's clearness stain.' The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Bharata returns to Ayodhya and learns that his mother Kaikeyi has won him the throne by having Rama exiled, he refuses the kingdom, rebukes her, and goes to the forest to beg Rama to return, later ruling only as Rama's regent with Rama's sandals on the throne.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (Bharata's refusal of the throne) [3]
Bharata is offered the greatest prize a prince can have, but because it came through a shameful scheme, he will not take it.
Exactly the Kural's case: something of the highest value is refused because accepting it would stain one's honour and family.
Christianity
“By faith Moses, when he was come to years, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter; Choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season;”
— Hebrews 11:24-25 (KJV) [4]
Moses gives up a royal position and its comforts rather than keep them on terms that would betray who he is.
Like the Kural, it praises giving up great advantages when keeping them would cost one's integrity.
Islam
“Say, ‘Not equal are the evil and the good, although the abundance of evil might impress you.’ So fear Allah, O you of understanding, that you may be successful.”
— Quran 5:100 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran warns that a large gain from wrong should not tempt a person, because good and evil are never equal however much the evil seems to offer.
The Kural likewise says that even a great, seemingly necessary gain must be dropped when it comes by a degrading route.
Judaism
“And Abram said to the king of Sodom: ‘I have lifted up my hand unto the LORD, God Most High, Maker of heaven and earth, that I will not take a thread nor a shoe-latchet nor aught that is thine, lest thou shouldest say: I have made Abram rich;’”
— Genesis 14:22-23 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
After a victory, Abraham refuses the spoils he was entitled to, so that no one could claim to have made him rich.
A near one-to-one match: a lawful and valuable gain is refused because accepting it would diminish his standing.
Buddhism
“He is indeed virtuous, wise, and righteous who neither for his own sake nor for the sake of another (does any wrong), who does not crave for sons, wealth, or kingdom, and does not desire success by unjust means.”
— Dhammapada 84 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha praises the person who will not do wrong for any gain, even for family, wealth or a kingdom.
Both texts say that even the greatest goods must not be pursued by a lowering act.
Jainism
“The tiger of the forest having slain an elk, will not eat it, but will leave it if it fall on the left side. In like manner, could the excellent by a sacrifice of principle obtain all the wealth that exists under the wide-extended heavens, they would not even desire it.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 30 (மானம், 'Honour'; titled 'Innocence' in this translation), stanza 10 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
This Jain Tamil quatrain from the Naladiyar's chapter on honour says the noble would not want all the world's wealth if it cost them their principles.
Nearly the Kural's own claim: even what seems indispensable is refused when it would lower one's honour.
Sikhism
When the Mughal emperor Jahangir ordered Guru Arjan to remove supposedly offensive passages from the Adi Granth, the Guru refused and was tortured to death at Lahore in 1606, becoming the first Sikh martyr.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Arjan' (paraphrase) [9]
Guru Arjan could have saved his life by altering the scripture he had compiled, but he would not buy his survival at that price.
The Kural says to give up even what seems indispensable, life itself included, if keeping it would lower one's honour; Guru Arjan gave up his life rather than corrupt the Guru's word.
Greek Mythology
During Odysseus' twenty-year absence, Penelope is pressed by many suitors to remarry, which would end the strain on her household; she delays them for years with the trick of weaving and unweaving Laertes' shroud rather than give up her loyalty.
— Homer, Odyssey 2 and 19 (Penelope and the suitors) [10]
Penelope refuses an easy way out of her troubles because it would betray her husband and her house.
Like the Kural's noble person, she gives up relief that seems necessary because taking it would lower her honour.
Philosophy
“a man who is good for anything ought not to calculate the chance of living or dying; he ought only to consider whether in doing anything he is doing right or wrong”
— Plato, Apology 28b (tr. Benjamin Jowett; classics.mit.edu) [11]
Socrates tells his jury that a good person weighs only whether an act is right, not whether it saves his life.
The Kural also says that even what seems necessary for survival must be dropped if it is degrading.

Real Life Example

Muhammad Ali refuses induction into the United States Army
28 April 1967 · Houston, Texas, United States
On 28 April 1967 the heavyweight boxing champion Muhammad Ali appeared for induction into the United States Army in Houston and refused to step forward when his name was called, citing his religious beliefs as a conscientious objector. Boxing authorities suspended his licence and stripped him of his title, and he was convicted of refusing induction and sentenced to five years in prison, though he remained free on appeal. He was kept out of the ring for more than three years before the Supreme Court unanimously overturned his conviction in Clay v. United States in June 1971.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ali gave up his title and his best years as a boxer rather than act against what he held to be his conscience.
The kural says that even acts one seems unable to live without must be given up if doing them would lower one's dignity, and Ali sacrificed the career that was his livelihood rather than do what he believed was wrong.
