Kural 1056

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

கரப்பிடும்பை யில்லாரைக் காணின் நிரப்பிடும்பை எல்லாம் ஒருங்கு கெடும்.
Karappidumbai yillaaraik kaanin nirappidumbai ellaam orungu kedum.

Meaning

Just seeing people who never hide what they have is enough to make all the pains of being poor go away at once.

Elementary

When a hungry child sees a kind lunch lady who always shares, the worry disappears right away. Just seeing someone generous makes the scary feeling go.

University

The kural plays on two compounds built on idumbai (இடும்பை, 'affliction'): karappidumbai, the affliction (or 'disease', in Pappaiah's gloss நோய்) of concealment that belongs to the miser, and nirappidumbai, the affliction of want that belongs to the poor. The miser's karappu is thus treated as a sickness of its own, the counterpart of the beggar's poverty. Kaanin (காணின், 'if one but sees') shows that the mere sight of such a giver ends ellaam orungu (எல்லாம் ஒருங்கு, 'all together') the beggar's distress. Puliyur Kesikan adds that this holds for one who begs without losing self-respect (மானம் விடாமல்), and Kalaignar calls concealment an இழிநிலை (base state). The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree, both printing the sandhi form யில்லாரைக்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Rantideva, after fasting for forty-eight days, gives away his food and water in turn to a brahmin, a shudra, a man with dogs and finally an outcaste, praying only to take on the sufferings of all beings so that they may be free of pain.
— Bhagavata Purana 9.21 (story of Rantideva) (paraphrase) [3]
Rantideva's giving is so complete that every hungry person who comes to him leaves relieved.
A giver with no trace of withholding ends the misery of those who come to him, as the Kural says.
Christianity
“Then Peter said, Silver and gold have I none; but such as I have give I thee: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth rise up and walk.”
— Acts 3:6 (KJV) [4]
The lame beggar asks Peter for alms; Peter gives what he has, healing, and the man leaps up praising God.
One meeting with an unstinting giver ends all of the beggar's affliction, the Kural's ellaam orungu kedum.
Islam
The Prophet taught that whoever relieves a believer of one of the hardships of this world, God will relieve him of one of the hardships of the Day of Resurrection, and that God helps a servant as long as the servant helps his brother.
— Sahih Muslim 2699 (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith treats removing another's distress as one of the most rewarded acts.
It stresses, as the Kural does, that a generous person can lift a needy person's affliction.
Judaism
“For I saved the pauper who cried out, / The orphan who had no helper. / I received the blessing of the lost; / I gladdened the heart of the widow.”
— Job 29:12-13 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Job remembers his days of prosperity, when the poor who cried to him were rescued and the widow's heart was made glad.
Job is the giver without the 'disease of withholding' whose presence ended the distress of those who turned to him.
Buddhism
The householder Sudatta was so constant in feeding the destitute that he became known as Anathapindika, 'feeder of the unprotected', and the tradition counts him the foremost lay donor among the Buddha's followers.
— Pali tradition on Anathapindika (e.g., SN 10.8; AN 1 'foremost' lists) (paraphrase) [7]
His very name came to mean relief for the helpless who came to him.
A giver known never to hold back, whose name alone promises relief, fits the Kural's image well.
Jainism
Jain householder ethics lists four kinds of gift: ahara-dana (food), aushadha-dana (medicine), abhaya-dana (protection from fear) and jnana-dana (knowledge), which together aim to remove every kind of want.
— V. A. Sangave, Jaina ethics (jainworld.com), on the four kinds of dana (paraphrase) [8]
Taken together, the four gifts address hunger, sickness, fear and ignorance, the whole range of a needy person's distress.
The fourfold gift is a Jain picture of how a giver free of withholding can end all of a seeker's afflictions at once.
Sikhism
“The Langar - the Kitchen of the Guru's Shabad has been opened, and its supplies never run short.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 967, Raag Ramkali, Ramkali ki Vaar, Satta and Balwand (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Satta and Balwand praise the langar of Guru Angad's court, a free kitchen open to all whose stores never run out, alongside the spiritual food of the Guru's word.
The Kural says that at the mere sight of those free from withholding, the afflictions of want vanish; the Guru's langar, whose supplies never fail, is the Sikh institution where the hungry find their want relieved.
Greek Mythology
“the other suitors all gave him something, and filled his wallet with bread and meat”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Most of the suitors give to the disguised Odysseus and fill his wallet.
Among those who did not withhold, the beggar's want was met at once, a partial picture of the Kural's claim.
Philosophy
Seneca urges that a benefit be given promptly and without hesitation, since a gift that is long delayed and grudgingly given loses much of its value to the one who receives it.
— Seneca, On Benefits, Book II (tr. Aubrey Stewart) (paraphrase) [11]
For Seneca, the giver's readiness is itself part of the benefit.
A giver with no impulse to hold back relieves need at once, which is the effect the Kural describes.

Real Life Example

Operation Manna drops food to the starving Dutch
29 April - 8 May 1945 · Western Netherlands
With the occupied western Netherlands starving at the end of the Hunger Winter, the Allies negotiated with the German command so that their aircraft could fly unharmed along agreed corridors. From 29 April 1945 RAF Lancaster bombers flew very low over Dutch drop zones releasing food instead of bombs, and the American Operation Chowhound joined on 1 May; by the German surrender on 8 May the combined missions had dropped more than 10,000 tons of food. Dutch civilians waved at the low-flying aircraft and spelled out messages of thanks in tulips.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The sight of the bombers arriving with food became one of the most remembered moments of deliverance at the end of the war in Europe.
The kural says that the mere sight of givers free from the vice of withholding ends at once all the afflictions of want, and for starving Dutch families the sight of the food planes overhead meant that their ordeal was ending.
