Kural 761

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

உறுப்பமைந்து ஊறஞ்சா வெல்படை வேந்தன் வெறுக்கையுள் எல்லாம் தலை.
Uruppamaindhu ooranjaa velpadai vendhan verukkaiyul ellaam thalai.

Meaning

An army that has every part it needs and wins without fear of getting hurt is the greatest treasure a king can own.

Elementary

Think of a school sports team where every position is filled, everyone has practised, and nobody is scared of a hard game. A coach would say that team is worth more than any trophy cupboard. Valluvar says a king’s well-prepared army is like that: his most precious possession.

University

The chapter opens the Padai-iyal (the section on the army), and its first couplet ranks the army above every other form of veṟukkai (வெறுக்கை, ‘wealth, treasure’). Uṟuppu (உறுப்பு, ‘limb’) is read by Parimelazhagar as the traditional four limbs of an Indian army, elephants, chariots, horse and foot, so uṟuppamaintu means ‘complete in its divisions’; ūṟu (ஊறு) is ‘wound’ or ‘harm’, and ūṟañcā ‘not dreading injury’. The logic echoes kural 381, which lists the army (படை) first among the six limbs of a kingdom, and the Arthashastra’s list of seven elements of sovereignty. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss the line almost identically; Solomon Pappaiah modernizes the ‘limbs’ as land, sea and air forces and police. The ytamizh and thirukkural-thamizh texts agree, differing only in sandhi (உறுப்பமைந் தூறஞ்சா).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“THE king, the minister, the country, the fort, the treasury, the army and the friend are the elements of sovereignty.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book VI, Chapter 1 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya lists the army as one of the seven constituent elements without which a state cannot stand, and then describes the qualities of the best army in detail.
Valluvar goes a step further, ranking a complete, fearless army first among a king’s treasures.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand?”
— Luke 14:31 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses a king weighing the strength of his army as an image of counting the cost before committing to discipleship.
The parable assumes what the Kural states: a king’s real security rests on the army he can actually field.
Islam
“And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allah and your enemy and others besides them whom you do not know [but] whom Allah knows.”
— Qur’an 8:60 (Saheeh International) [5]
The community is told to keep its forces as fully equipped as it can, both cavalry and every other kind of strength.
Like the Kural, it treats a fully equipped, ready force as a primary duty and asset of the polity.
Judaism
“And Uzziah prepared for them, even for all the host, shields, and spears, and helmets, and coats of mail, and bows, and stones for slinging.”
— 2 Chronicles 26:14 (JPS 1917, Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The Chronicler presents Uzziah’s trained army of over three hundred thousand, fully equipped, as a mark of his strength while he sought God.
A complete, well-armed host is pictured as a king’s chief strength, as in the Kural.
Buddhism
“Excellent are well-trained mules, thoroughbred Sindhu horses and noble tusker elephants. But better still is the man who has subdued himself.”
— Dhammapada 322 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha grants that the trained animals of a royal army are excellent, then points to self-mastery as the higher prize.
It shares the Kural’s admiration for well-trained army ‘limbs’, while redirecting the idea of the greatest treasure inward.
Jainism
“As a universal monarch with his fourfold army and great power, the possessor of the fourteen attributes of a king, (has no equal), neither has a very learned monk.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana takes the cakravartin, whose fourfold army is the sign of his unmatched power, and uses him as a measure for the learned monk.
The 'fourfold army' is the same four-limbed force (elephants, chariots, horse, foot) that the Kural calls complete in its parts and ranks first among a king's treasures, though the Jain verse in the end prizes the monk above the monarch.
Sikhism
At his succession in 1606, after his father Guru Arjan's martyrdom, Guru Hargobind is said to have worn two swords, one for temporal authority (miri) and one for spiritual authority (piri). He built up a standing Sikh army and gave the faith a military side it had not had before.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Hargobind' (paraphrase) [9]
Sikh tradition remembers Guru Hargobind as the Guru who made an organised armed force part of the community's life, with the Akal Takht as the seat of temporal authority facing the Harmandir Sahib.
The Kural ranks a complete, fearless army first among a ruler's treasures; Guru Hargobind's reform is the moment Sikhism decided that the community needed such an army to protect itself, though always beneath the authority of piri, the spiritual sword.
Greek Mythology
Herodotus describes Xerxes’ ten thousand Persian ‘Immortals’, a corps kept always at full strength: whenever a man dropped out, another was chosen to take his place.
— Herodotus, Histories 7.83 [10]
The elite unit’s prestige came from never being incomplete, and it was the pride of the Persian king’s host.
It illustrates the Kural’s uṟuppamaintu, an army complete in all its parts, as a king’s prized possession.
Philosophy
“These are: (1) The Moral Law; (2) Heaven; (3) Earth; (4) The Commander; (5) Method and discipline.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 1.4 (trans. Lionel Giles) [11]
Sun Tzu lists the five constant factors by which a ruler should judge the strength of an army before going to war.
Like Valluvar, Sun Tzu treats a complete, well-ordered army as something to be assessed and valued with care.

Real Life Example

Gustavus Adolphus's reformed Swedish army wins at Breitenfeld
17 September 1631 · Breitenfeld, near Leipzig, Germany
At Breitenfeld, the Swedish-Saxon army under King Gustav II Adolf of Sweden met the imperial and Catholic League army under Count Tilly. When the Saxon contingent fled, the Swedes reordered their lines to form a new left flank, outgunned the enemy in the artillery exchange, and destroyed Tilly's army. It was the first major Protestant victory of the Thirty Years' War and established Sweden as a great power.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Britannica credits the victory to Sweden's flexible, well-drilled linear tactics, the product of Gustavus's reforms of infantry, cavalry and artillery working together.
The kural ranks first among a king's treasures an army complete in all its parts that wins without fearing harm, and at Breitenfeld a well-integrated Swedish army held firm even when its allies ran.
