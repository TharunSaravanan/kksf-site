Kural 36

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

அன்றறிவாம் என்னாது அறஞ்செய்க மற்றது பொன்றுங்கால் பொன்றாத் துணை.
Andrarivaam ennaadhu aranjeyka matradhu, Pondrungaal pondraath thunai.

Meaning

Do good now, and do not say, ‘I will do it later.’ When you die, the good you did is the friend that does not die with you.

Elementary

If you keep saying you will pack your bag tomorrow, one day the bus leaves without you. This kural says do good things today, because they are the only things that stay with you when everything else is gone.

University

The idiom அன்றறிவாம் — literally 'we shall know on that day' — is the voice of procrastination personified; Parimelazhagar expands it as the young person's reasoning, 'we are young now, we will do it at the hour of death.' Against this Valluvar sets the wordplay that gives the couplet its force: பொன்றுங்கால் பொன்றாத் துணை, 'at the time of perishing, the unperishing companion', built on repetition of the root பொன்று- in opposite senses. The image of virtue as துணை (companion, escort) that accompanies the soul past the body's dissolution is shared with Jain and Buddhist merit-doctrine, and Manakkudavar reads it explicitly as support in the next birth; the Dravidian rationalist commentator Kalaignar Karunanidhi instead reads it as imperishable fame. The verse is one of the clearest places where the Kural's deliberate theological reticence allows two very different readings of the same line.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: Son of Pṛthā, a transcendentalist engaged in auspicious activities does not meet with destruction either in this world or in the spiritual world; one who does good, My friend, is never overcome by evil.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.40 (Bhaktivedanta, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Answering Arjuna's fear that an unfinished spiritual effort is simply wasted, Krishna assures him that accumulated good is never destroyed and carries across the break of death.
It is the Kural's promise in reverse: because good done is indestructible, there is no reason to defer it and every reason to begin.
Christianity
“And I heard a voice from heaven saying unto me, Write, Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord from henceforth: Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours; and their works do follow them.”
— Revelation 14:13 (KJV) [4]
The vision declares that a person's deeds accompany them past death — the labours cease but the works travel on with the doer.
'Their works do follow them' is almost a translation of பொன்றுங்கால் பொன்றாத் துணை, the companion that does not perish.
Islam
“When a man dies, his acts come to an end, but three, recurring charity, or knowledge (by which people) benefit, or a pious child, who prays for him (for the deceased).”
— Sahih Muslim 1631, narrated by Abu Hurayra [5]
The hadith of ṣadaqa jāriya identifies the specific forms of good whose benefit continues to accrue to a person after the account of their deeds has otherwise closed.
It names exactly what the Kural means by the unperishing companion — the good done in life that outlives the doer.
Judaism
“Rabbi Eliezer said: Let the honor of your friend be as dear to you as your own; And be not easily provoked to anger; And repent one day before your death.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:10 (trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria); cf. b. Shabbat 153a [6]
The Talmud records that Rabbi Eliezer's students objected that no one knows the day of their death, to which he answered: all the more reason, then, to repent today — lest one die tomorrow.
This is the Kural's first line almost word for word: do not tell yourself you will attend to it when the time comes.
Buddhism
“As kinsmen welcome a dear one on arrival, even so his own good deeds will welcome the doer of good who has gone from this world to the next.”
— Dhammapada 220, Piyavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
The Dhammapada pictures a person's accumulated merit waiting for them on the far side of death like family gathered to greet a returning traveller.
It is the Kural's 'companion that does not perish', rendered as a homecoming rather than an escort.
Jainism
“He who can call Death his friend, or who can escape him, or who knows that he will not die, might perhaps decide: this shall be done tomorrow.” … ‘Whenever you die, O king, and leave all pleasant things behind, the Law alone, and nothing else in this world, will save you, O monarch.’”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.27 and 14.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The two young sons of the priest Bhrigu refuse their father's advice to put off renunciation until old age, since no one has a pact with death. Later Queen Kamalavati tells the king that at death dharma alone goes with a person and protects them.
This covers both halves of the Kural. One should not say 'we will see to it later', and the Law is the one companion that does not perish when the body does.
Sikhism
“Kabir! That which you have to do tomorrow - do it today instead; and that which you have to do now - do it immediately! Later on, you will not be able to do anything, when death hangs over your head.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir 138 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In one of his saloks preserved in the Guru Granth Sahib, the bhagat Kabir attacks procrastination, insisting that good be done at once because death leaves no time for it later.
It is almost exactly Valluvar's rebuke of 'we will see to it when the time comes': do good now, before death arrives.
Greek Mythology
“Do not put your work off till to-morrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work: industry makes work go well, but a man who puts off work is always at hand-grips with ruin.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 410-413, trans. Hugh G. Evelyn-White [10]
Hesiod's farming advice to his brother Perses treats deferral itself as the decisive cause of poverty and disaster, independent of ability or circumstance.
It is the Kural's first line transposed to the farm — the one who says 'later' is already losing.
Philosophy
“While we are postponing, life speeds by.”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 1 (On Saving Time), trans. Richard M. Gummere [11]
Seneca opens his first letter to Lucilius by urging him to gather and hold his time, since time is the only thing truly ours and the only loss that cannot be made good.
It supplies the reason the Kural forbids 'we shall know on that day': the day one is reserving for virtue is being spent right now.

Real Life Example

Terry Fox's Marathon of Hope
12 April - 1 September 1980 · St. John's, Newfoundland, to Thunder Bay, Ontario, Canada
Terry Fox, who had lost his right leg to cancer at 18, set out from St. John's on 12 April 1980 to run across Canada and raise money for cancer research, covering 5,373 kilometres in 143 days, nearly a marathon a day. He was forced to stop near Thunder Bay on 1 September 1980 when his cancer returned, and he died on 28 June 1981. Before his death his goal of one dollar for every Canadian had been reached, and the annual Terry Fox Run held in his name has since raised hundreds of millions of dollars for cancer research.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Fox did not wait for a better time to act; he ran while he could, and the good he began has continued long after his death.
The kural warns against saying we will do good later and calls virtue the companion that does not perish when we perish, and Fox's run, made in the time he had, lives on in the yearly runs and research funded in his name.
