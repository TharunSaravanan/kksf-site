Kural 537

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

அரியஎன்று ஆகாத இல்லைபொச் சாவாக் கருவியால் போற்றிச் செயின்.
Ariyaendru aakaadha illaipoch chaavaak Karuviyaal potrich cheyin.

Meaning

No job is too hard, however difficult people say it is, if you do it carefully, with a mind that never forgets or slips.

Elementary

Building a huge Lego castle sounds impossible. But if you keep the picture in your head, follow each step carefully, and don't lose track, you can finish it. A mind that doesn't forget is like a super-tool that makes hard jobs possible.

University

அரிய என்று ஆகாத இல்லை is a double negative: ‘there is nothing that, being called difficult, cannot be done’. Parimelazhagar takes பொச்சாவாக் கருவி to mean ‘the unforgetting mind’. On his reading, பொச்சாவாத has lost its final syllable by poetic licence, and the mind is called கருவி (‘instrument’) because it is the inner instrument (அந்தக்கரணம்); போற்றிச் செயின் means ‘if one does it with careful thought’. His summary is that people with unbroken attention and sound planning accomplish everything easily. Pavanar calls this gloss over-learned and prefers to read பொச்சாவாக் கருவி as an appositional compound, ‘the instrument that is unforgetfulness’. Manakkudavar likewise glosses it as மறவாமையாகிய கருவி. Modern commentators such as Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah reflect both readings.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Lord Śrī Kṛṣṇa said: O mighty-armed son of Kuntī, it is undoubtedly very difficult to curb the restless mind, but it is possible by suitable practice and by detachment.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.35 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is, A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
Arjuna protests that the mind is as hard to hold as the wind. Krishna grants that it is hard but insists that disciplined practice (abhyāsa) makes it possible.
Like the Kural, the Gita says that what is called ‘too hard’ becomes achievable through a steadily disciplined mind.
Christianity
“Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation: the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.”
— Matthew 26:41 (KJV) [5]
In Gethsemane Jesus gives watchfulness joined to prayer as the means by which a weak human nature can hold firm.
Vigilance here is the tool that overcomes weakness, as the Kural's ‘instrument of unforgetfulness’ overcomes difficulty.
Islam
“And those who strive for Us - We will surely guide them to Our ways. And indeed, Allah is with the doers of good.”
— Qur'an 29:69 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur'an promises that sustained striving (jihād in its broad sense) will be met with guidance to the way through.
Like the Kural, it says that persistent, careful effort opens paths that seemed closed.
Judaism
The Talmud records Rabbi Yitzḥak's maxim: if someone tells you they laboured and did not find, do not believe them; if they say they found without labouring, do not believe them; but if they say they laboured and found, believe them.
— Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 6b [7]
The teaching insists that real attainment, especially in Torah, comes only through sustained, careful effort, and that such effort does not fail.
The Kural too holds that diligent, attentive work makes even the ‘impossible’ achievable.
Buddhism
“The monk who delights in heedfulness and looks with fear at heedlessness advances like fire, burning all fetters, small and large.”
— Dhammapada 31, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [8]
The Dhammapada pictures heedfulness as a fire that consumes every obstacle, large or small.
Like the Kural's unforgetting instrument, heedfulness here overcomes every difficulty in its way.
Jainism
“By conquering his will, (a monk) reaches liberation, as a well-broken horse which is clad in harness (goes to battle). Be watchful in your young years; for thereby a monk quickly obtains liberation.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Watchfulness and self-control are the means by which even the hardest goal, liberation, is quickly reached.
The Kural says nothing is too hard for one who works with an unforgetting mind as his tool; Mahavira says watchfulness wins even liberation, the hardest goal of all.
Sikhism
“Remembering God, one's works are accomplished.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 263, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sukhmani Guru Arjan lists the fruits of simran, among them that one's undertakings are brought to completion.
The Kural says nothing is too hard for one who works carefully with an unforgetting mind as his tool; Guru Arjan says that remembrance brings one's works to completion.
Greek Mythology
Warned by Circe, Odysseus plugs his crew's ears with wax and has himself bound to the mast, ordering that his bonds be tightened if he begs to be freed. In this way he alone hears the Sirens' song and survives it.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.37-200 [11]
Odysseus knows his own mind will lapse under the song's spell, so he builds a device that keeps his resolve in force when his memory of it fails.
The mast and ropes are literally an ‘instrument of unforgetfulness’, and with them he achieves what no one had survived before.
Philosophy
“Every moment think steadily as a Roman and a man to do what thou hast in hand with perfect and simple dignity, and feeling of affection, and freedom, and justice; and to give thyself relief from all other thoughts.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.5 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus urges full, steady attention to whatever task is in hand, putting away carelessness and distraction.
This is the Kural's ‘doing with care through an unforgetting mind’, turned into a daily discipline.

Real Life Example

Amundsen sails the Gjoa through the Northwest Passage
1903-1906 · Arctic waters of northern Canada, from Norway to Nome, Alaska
Many earlier expeditions had sought a sea route through the Arctic waters north of Canada without completing it. After careful preparation Roald Amundsen bought the small 47-ton sloop Gjoa, built in 1872, and left Kristiania (Oslo) on 16 June 1903 with six companions, spending two winters at Gjoa Haven on King William Island, where the party made magnetic observations, and a third winter near Herschel Island. The Gjoa reached Nome, Alaska, in August 1906, the first ship to sail the whole Northwest Passage.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
A small, well-prepared party patiently completed over three years a voyage that larger and better-funded expeditions had failed to achieve.
The kural says no task is too hard if it is done carefully with an unforgetting mind as one's tool, and Amundsen's methodical voyage conquered a route long thought impassable.
