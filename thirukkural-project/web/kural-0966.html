Kural 966

Honour · Maanam · மானம்

புகழ்இன்றால் புத்தேள்நாட்டு உய்யாதால் என்மற்று இகழ்வார்பின் சென்று நிலை.
Pugazhindraal puththelnaattu uyyaadhaal enmatru Ikazhvaarpin sendru nilai.

Meaning

Running after people who look down on you brings you no fame here and no place in heaven. So what good is it?

Elementary

Some children follow around a bully who laughs at them, hoping to be liked. This kural asks: what do you get from that? Nobody admires it, and it does not make you a better person. Better to keep your self-respect.

University

The couplet is a rhetorical question built on the two goods of classical Tamil ethics: pugazh (புகழ், fame in this world) and puththel naadu (புத்தேள் நாடு, the land of the gods, i.e. heaven, reward in the next). Ikazhvaar pin sendru nilai (இகழ்வார்பின் சென்று நிலை), 'the state of going after those who scorn one', yields neither, hence en matru (என்மற்று), 'what else then?'. Solomon Pappaiah reads it as abandoning honour in order to live by following one's despisers. Kalaignar's gloss pointedly adds 'a heaven that does not exist', a modern rationalist reading; the older commentators take heaven at face value. Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The Supreme Personality of Godhead said: My dear Arjuna, how have these impurities come upon you? They are not at all befitting a man who knows the value of life. They lead not to higher planets but to infamy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.2 (tr. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada; vedabase.io) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that his collapse of spirit is unworthy and leads neither to heaven nor to fame.
The Gita's 'not to higher planets but to infamy' matches the Kural's 'no fame, no heaven' almost word for word.
Christianity
“For do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ.”
— Galatians 1:10 (KJV) [4]
Paul refuses to live by pleasing people at the cost of his true calling.
The Kural likewise rejects living by currying favour with others.
Islam
“Those who take disbelievers as allies instead of the believers. Do they seek with them honor [through power]? But indeed, honor belongs to Allah entirely.”
— Quran 4:139 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran asks why anyone would look for honour by attaching themselves to people who do not share or respect their faith.
Like the Kural, it says honour cannot be found by following those who hold you in contempt.
Judaism
“And all the king’s servants, that were in the king’s gate, bowed down, and prostrated themselves before Haman; for the king had so commanded concerning him. But Mordecai bowed not down, nor prostrated himself before him.”
— Esther 3:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Mordecai alone refuses to bow before the arrogant Haman, though everyone else does.
He embodies the Kural's refusal to bow and follow after those who hold one in contempt.
Buddhism
“Do not associate with evil companions; do not seek the fellowship of the vile. Associate with the good friends; seek the fellowship of noble men.”
— Dhammapada 78 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha advises seeking the company of the noble, not the base.
The Kural adds that attaching oneself to those who despise one brings no good at all.
Jainism
“Leave off the causes of sin, acquire fame through patience! (A man who acts up to this) will rise to the upper regions after having left this body of clay.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 3.13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira's teaching names the true way to both goods the Kural mentions: fame in this life and a higher world after death.
The Kural says fawning on scorners wins neither fame nor heaven; the Jain verse says patience and abandoning sin win both.
Sikhism
“One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else - says Nanak, listen, mind: call him spiritually wise.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahala 9 (salok 16), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks, Guru Tegh Bahadur defines the wise person as one who neither intimidates others nor bows to anyone out of fear.
The Kural scorns those who trail after people who despise them; Guru Tegh Bahadur's ideal neither fears nor fawns on anyone.
Greek Mythology
After Agamemnon publicly dishonours him by taking Briseis, Achilles refuses the embassy's offer of vast gifts, cities and a royal marriage, saying he will not be won over by the man who insulted him.
— Homer, Iliad 9.307-429 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Achilles will not return to serve a king who scorned him, whatever the reward.
He refuses exactly what the Kural condemns: going after those who held one in contempt.
Philosophy
Epictetus says an invitation to a great man's dinner has a price: flattery and attendance at his door. If you will not pay it, do not complain of going without; you keep instead the dignity of not having flattered.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 25 [11]
Courting the powerful costs one's self-respect, and Epictetus advises declining the bargain.
The Kural asks what such following gains; Epictetus answers that refusing it keeps something more valuable.

Real Life Example

Samuel Johnson's letter to Lord Chesterfield
7 February 1755 · London, England
Lord Chesterfield had accepted the dedication of Samuel Johnson's plan for a Dictionary of the English Language in 1747 but gave the project almost no further help during the long years Johnson spent compiling it. Shortly before the Dictionary appeared in 1755, Chesterfield published two magazine articles praising it, creating the impression that he had been its patron all along. On 7 February 1755 Johnson wrote to him rejecting his belated patronage, and copies of the letter circulated among Johnson's circle and were later printed by James Boswell.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter became famous as a declaration of an author's independence from aristocratic patronage.
The kural asks what is gained by trailing after those who scorn you, and Johnson refused to attach himself to a patron who had neglected him once his success made the patron eager.
