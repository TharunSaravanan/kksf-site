Kural 440

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

காதல காதல் அறியாமை உய்க்கிற்பின் ஏதில ஏதிலார் நூல்.
Kaadhala kaadhal ariyaamai uykkirpin Edhila edhilaar nool.

Meaning

If you can enjoy what you love without letting others know what you want, your enemies’ plans against you will fail.

Elementary

If everyone knows exactly what you want most, people who wish you harm can use that to trick you. This kural says a wise leader keeps their wishes private, so enemies have nothing to use against them.

University

The couplet uses doubled words for effect: காதல காதல் (‘the things one loves, [and] one’s love’) and ஏதில ஏதிலார் (‘come to nothing, [the plans of] strangers/enemies’). ஏதிலார் means those unrelated or hostile, and நூல் here means their ‘science’ or stratagems, the craft by which they plot. உய்க்கிற்பின் (‘if one is able to experience/carry out’) makes the condition discretion, not renunciation: the ruler may pursue his desires, but must not let them be known. The logic ties the chapter together. A known weakness or appetite is itself a fault (குற்றம்) in the strategic sense, a gap in the armour. By closing the chapter this way, Valluvar moves from moral self-correction to political self-guarding: the ruler must hide from enemies the very desires that could be used to exploit him.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya instructs that the king’s deliberations must be held in secret and guarded so that no outsider learns his plans; others should come to know of an undertaking only once it has begun or been completed.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book I, ch. 15 (tr. R. Shamasastry) [3]
The ancient Indian manual of statecraft teaches that a king must keep his intentions secret so enemies cannot counter them.
Same political logic as the Kural: hidden intentions defeat enemies’ schemes.
Christianity
“A fool uttereth all his mind: but a wise man keepeth it in till afterwards.”
— Proverbs 29:11 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs contrasts the fool who says everything he thinks with the wise person who holds back.
Both praise keeping one’s inner mind to oneself as a mark of wisdom.
Islam
A widely cited hadith (al-Tabarani) says: seek help in accomplishing your needs through keeping them concealed, for everyone who has a blessing is envied.
— Al-Tabarani, al-Mu‘jam al-Kabir (hadith on concealment of needs) [5]
The hadith advises keeping your plans and needs private because others may envy and work against them.
Very close to the Kural: concealing one’s desires protects them from those who would spoil them.
Judaism
“That he told her all his heart, and said unto her, There hath not come a razor upon mine head; for I have been a Nazarite unto God from my mother's womb: if I be shaven, then my strength will go from me, and I shall become weak, and be like any other man.”
— Judges 16:17 (KJV) [4]
Samson finally tells Delilah the secret of his strength, and the Philistines use it to capture him.
A negative example of the Kural: once his enemies know what he holds dearest, their scheme succeeds.
Buddhism
In the Kumma Sutta, a tortoise draws its four legs and neck into its shell when a jackal approaches; the jackal waits for any limb to come out, finds no opening, and leaves. The Buddha tells monks to guard their senses the same way so that Māra finds no opening.
— Samyutta Nikaya 35.199 (CDB 35.240), Kumma Sutta [6]
By keeping everything tucked away, the tortoise gives its enemy nothing to seize.
Same strategy as the Kural: by keeping desires hidden and guarded, one gives the enemy no handle, and their schemes fail.
Jainism
“Just as an impassioned fish which is eager to swallow the bait, has its body transfixed by a hook.” … “Just as an impassioned elephant who is inflamed by carnal desires, is turned from his way by a female elephant (and is captured and at last killed in battle).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.63, 32.89 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The lecture on carelessness shows how each creature is trapped through its own known craving: the fish through its appetite for bait, the elephant through desire. Whoever knows the craving can use it to catch the creature.
Valluvar warns that desires known to enemies become the means of their schemes; the Jain similes show the same mechanism, though Jainism's remedy is to give up the craving, not to hide it.
Sikhism
“They are close to the Lord, but they do not reveal their secret to anyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sheikh Farid describes true devotees who keep their inner love for God private rather than displaying it.
The Kural advises enjoying what one loves without others knowing one's desires; Farid praises a similar reticence about one's deepest longing, though for spiritual rather than strategic reasons.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus escapes the Cyclops Polyphemus by calling himself ‘Nobody’; but as he sails away he boasts his real name, and Polyphemus uses it to call down Poseidon’s curse on him.
— Homer, Odyssey 9 [9]
Hiding his identity saved Odysseus; revealing it gave his enemy what he needed to strike back.
Both sides of the Kural in one story: concealment defeats the enemy, and self-revelation lets the enemy’s plan succeed.
Philosophy
Francis Bacon distinguishes secrecy, dissimulation and simulation, praising secrecy as a virtue that protects a person’s affairs, and advising openness in reputation but secrecy in habit.
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Simulation and Dissimulation’ [10]
Bacon argues that a wise person keeps their inner intentions private, because revealing them exposes them to others.
Bacon’s ‘secrecy in habit’ is the Kural’s enjoying one’s desires without others knowing them.

Real Life Example

Henry Kissinger's secret journey to Beijing
9-11 July 1971 · Pakistan and Beijing, China
During an official visit to Pakistan in July 1971, United States National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger let it be known that he was unwell and resting at a hill resort, while in fact he flew secretly from Pakistan to Beijing. There he held long talks with Premier Zhou Enlai to prepare a visit by President Richard Nixon. The trip stayed hidden until 15 July, when both governments announced Nixon's planned visit to China.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Because the American aim was kept from rivals and from the press until it was achieved, no one had the chance to obstruct the opening to China before it happened.
The kural says that if one can pursue what one desires without others knowing, the schemes of rivals come to nothing, and Kissinger's concealed journey achieved its goal unopposed.
