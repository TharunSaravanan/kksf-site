Kural 412

Hearing · Kelvi · கேள்வி

செவிக்குணவு இல்லாத போழ்து சிறிது வயிற்றுக்கும் ஈயப் படும்.
Sevikkunavu illaadha pozhdhu siridhu Vayitrrukkum eeyap padum.

Meaning

Only when there is nothing to feed the ears, no good teaching to listen to, should you give a little food to the stomach as well.

Elementary

Imagine you are hungry but a wise teacher is speaking. Valluvar says: keep listening first! Feed your ears with good words, and only when there is nothing more to hear, give your tummy a little food.

University

The kural introduces the governing metaphor of the chapter — hearing as food (செவிக்கு உணவு) — and inverts the ordinary priority of bodily needs. The ‘உம்’ in வயிற்றுக்கும் (‘to the stomach too’) and the qualifier சிறிது (‘a little’) make the stomach a secondary and moderate claimant. Commentators read this not as a call to starve but as a hierarchy: instruction, once lost, may not recur, whereas food can be taken any time. The metaphor links this chapter to the Indian notion of the body as sustained by food and the mind by speech, and anticipates 413 (hearers as those who eat ‘ear-food’) and 420 (those who know only the mouth’s taste).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
King Parikshit, cursed to die in seven days, sits by the Ganga and listens to the sage Shuka recite the Bhagavata Purana, taking neither food nor water for the entire seven days.
— Bhagavata Purana 1.19; 10.1.13 [4]
The Bhagavata frames its entire narration as a listening-fast: the king forgoes food to hear the teaching, and later says the nectar of these narrations removed his hunger and thirst.
A dramatic enactment of the Kural: food for the ear first, the stomach only afterward, if at all.
Christianity
“It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God.”
— Matthew 4:4 (KJV) [5]
Fasting in the wilderness, Jesus rejects the tempter’s suggestion to turn stones into bread, placing the word of God above physical food.
Both texts rank ‘the word’ as nourishment superior to bread — the Kural’s ear-food over stomach-food.
Islam
During the Friday sermon, a trade caravan arrived and many listeners rushed out to it, leaving the Prophet standing; the Qur’an rebukes them: what is with God is better than diversion and trade, and God is the best of providers.
— Qur’an 62:11 (Sahih International), with tafsir [6]
Commentators report the caravan carried food during a time of scarcity; the verse censures those who left the sermon for provisions.
The Qur’an criticises exactly the reverse of the Kural’s order — abandoning ear-food for stomach-food.
Judaism
“Behold, the days come, saith the Lord GOD, that I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the LORD:”
— Amos 8:11 (KJV) [5]
The prophet Amos threatens a deprivation worse than hunger: the absence of divine words to hear.
Amos treats hearing as a form of food whose lack is a true famine — the Kural’s ‘food for the ear’ in prophetic form.
Buddhism
“The gift of Dhamma excels all gifts; the taste of Dhamma excels all tastes; the delight in Dhamma excels all delights.”
— Dhammapada 354 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The verse ranks the flavour of the teaching above every other taste, including that of food.
Like the Kural, it relocates ‘taste’ from the tongue to the teaching received — ear-food over stomach-food.
Jainism
“In the first Paurushî he should study, in the second he should meditate, in the third he should go on his begging-tour, and in the fourth he should study again.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 26.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Jain monk's daily routine puts study first. Looking for food comes only in the third quarter of the day, after study and meditation, and the fourth quarter is again for study.
The Kural tells us to give the stomach a little only when there is no food for the ear. The Jain monk's day, in which the alms round waits until study and meditation are done, puts the same order into practice.
Sikhism
“Upon this Plate, three things have been placed: Truth, Contentment and Contemplation. The Ambrosial Nectar of the Naam, the Name of our Lord and Master, has been placed upon it as well; it is the Support of all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1429, Mundavani, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Mundavani at the close of the Granth, Guru Arjan describes the scripture as a plate of spiritual food: truth, contentment, contemplation and the Name.
The Kural ranks food for the ear above food for the stomach; Guru Arjan also offers truth, contemplation and the Name as the food that truly sustains.
Greek Mythology
At the Phaeacian feast, Odysseus tells King Alcinous that there is nothing more gracious than a banquet where the whole company sits listening to a bard while tables stand full of bread and meat.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.2–11; 11.333–334 [10]
The Homeric ideal feast couples listening with eating — and in the Phaeacian episode the guests stay spellbound by stories deep into the night.
Homer places ear-food beside stomach-food at the feast; the Kural goes further and ranks it above.
Philosophy
Socrates warns young Hippocrates that teachings are food for the soul, and unlike food bought at market they cannot be carried home in a separate vessel and inspected first — one must take them straight into the soul by listening.
— Plato, Protagoras 313c–314b [11]
Plato explicitly calls instruction the nourishment of the soul (psychē trephetai mathēmasi) and compares teachers to food merchants.
Plato and Valluvar share the metaphor of heard instruction as food, and both treat it as more consequential than bodily food.

Real Life Example

Marie Sklodowska studies in Paris on a meagre diet
1891-1894 · Paris, France
In the autumn of 1891 the young Polish student Maria Sklodowska, later Marie Curie, enrolled at the Sorbonne, and after a few months moved to a cold garret in the Latin Quarter to be close to the university. She had enough money for tuition, her room and the cheapest food, and by her own later account her meals were often reduced to bread with chocolate, eggs or fruit while she attended lectures and studied far into the night. Though she arrived less well prepared than her classmates, she finished first in the physics degree examinations in 1893 and second in mathematics in 1894.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her student years are remembered as an example of someone who put instruction ahead of comfort and even of a proper diet.
The kural says the stomach should be given only a little, when there is no food for the ear, and the young Sklodowska fed her mind on lectures and study while giving her body as little as she could manage.
