Kural 882

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

வாள்போல பகைவரை அஞ்சற்க அஞ்சுக கேள்போல் பகைவர் தொடர்பு.
Vaalpola pakaivarai anjarka anjuka kelpol pakaivar thotarpu.

Meaning

Do not fear enemies who come at you openly like a drawn sword. Fear enemies who pretend to be your relatives or friends.

Elementary

A bully who shouts at you from across the playground is easy to spot and avoid. The trickier problem is someone who pretends to be your friend while secretly trying to get you in trouble, so that is the person to be careful about.

University

The couplet sets two kinds of foe against each other: vaal-pola pakaivar (வாள்போல பகைவர், foes like a sword), whose hostility is open and therefore manageable, and kel-pol pakaivar (கேள்போல் பகைவர், foes who appear as kel, 'kin' or intimates). The imperative pair anjarka / anjuka (அஞ்சற்க / அஞ்சுக, 'fear not' / 'fear') gives the verse its antithetical snap. What is to be feared is their thodarpu (தொடர்பு), the ongoing association, since closeness is the very channel through which the hidden enemy works. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses the second group as those who behave like relatives while harbouring utpakai, and Pope renders them 'foes who seem like kinsmen'. The text agrees in ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Duryodhana, outwardly welcoming his Pandava cousins, has a mansion of lac built at Varanavata for them, planning to burn them inside it.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Jatugriha sub-parva (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The plot hides lethal enmity behind the forms of family hospitality; the Pandavas escape only through Vidura's warning.
This is exactly the Kural's 'enemy who appears as kin', more dangerous than any open swordsman.
Christianity
“He that hateth dissembleth with his lips, and layeth up deceit within him; When he speaketh fair, believe him not: for there are seven abominations in his heart.”
— Proverbs 26:24-25 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that the hater often hides behind gracious speech.
Both texts tell us to fear the pleasant-faced foe more than the open one.
Islam
“And of the people is he whose speech pleases you in worldly life, and he calls Allah to witness as to what is in his heart, yet he is the fiercest of opponents.”
— Qur'an 2:204 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the person whose agreeable words mask the most bitter hostility.
Like the Kural, it ranks the smooth-tongued hidden opponent as the most dangerous.
Judaism
“For it was not an enemy that reproached me; then I could have borne it ... But it was thou, a man mine equal, my guide, and mine acquaintance.”
— Psalm 55:12-13 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist says he could have endured or hidden from an open enemy, but betrayal by a close companion is unbearable.
This is the Kural's contrast exactly: the open foe is bearable, the intimate foe is the one to fear.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha teaches young Sigala to recognize four kinds of foes who come in the guise of friends: the taker, the flatterer, the one who only talks, and the companion in ruin.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (trans. Narada Thera) [6]
The discourse gives practical signs for detecting enemies who present themselves as friends.
It matches the Kural's warning to fear foes who wear the face of a friend or relative.
Jainism
“A little monkey breaking into a fruit with its finger, will strike and seize its own father … the friendship of those who are without unity (of mind) is not pleasant.”
— Nāladiyār 24.7 (English translation serialised in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Jain-authored Tamil Nāladiyār, in its chapter on improper friendship, pictures the young monkey that turns on its own father as he comes to meet it, and says that closeness without real unity of mind brings no pleasure.
The Kural tells us to fear the foe who comes in the guise of kin more than the open swordsman, and the quatrain's monkey striking the father who comes to greet it is exactly that danger inside an intimate bond.
Sikhism
“Bronze is bright and shiny, but when it is rubbed, its blackness appears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak contrasts a gleaming outward surface with the darkness it hides; in the Janamsakhi tradition this shabad is linked to his meeting with Sajjan, a host who welcomed travellers with a show of piety and then robbed them.
The Kural warns that the foe who looks like a friend is more to be feared than an open enemy, and Guru Nanak likewise exposes the friendly surface that hides harm.
Greek Mythology
Demodocus sings of the wooden horse: the Trojans, deceived, drag the gift into their own city, and the Greeks hidden inside sack Troy from within.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.499-520 [9]
A city that withstood ten years of open siege falls to an enemy it welcomed inside its walls.
The open army was survivable; the enemy taken in as a friend proved fatal, as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
“There are no intrigues more difficult to guard against than those which are concealed under a pretence of duty, or under the name of some intimate connection. For you can easily avoid one who is openly an adversary, by guarding against him; but this secret, internal, and domestic evil not only exists, but even overwhelms you before you can foresee it or examine into it.”
— Cicero, Against Verres (In Verrem), II.1.39 (tr. C. D. Yonge) [10]
Accusing Verres of betraying the consul he served as quaestor, Cicero argues that the treachery of a trusted associate is far more dangerous than open hostility.
Cicero makes the Kural's own contrast: an open enemy can be guarded against, while an enemy in the guise of a friend or kinsman strikes before he is detected.

Real Life Example

Kim Philby is exposed as a Soviet agent inside British intelligence
January 1963 · Beirut, Lebanon
Kim Philby became a Soviet agent while at Cambridge in the 1930s, joined Britain's Secret Intelligence Service (MI6) in 1940, rose to head its counter-espionage section, and in 1949 became its chief liaison officer in Washington, passing detailed information about British and American intelligence to Moscow. Suspected after two fellow agents fled in 1951, he was eventually confronted in Beirut in January 1963 by his old friend and MI6 colleague Nicholas Elliott and gave a partial confession. Within days he fled to the Soviet Union, where he lived until his death in 1988.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The most damaging enemy of British intelligence was not a foreign agent kept at arm's length but a trusted colleague and friend at its heart.
The kural says to fear not the open enemy like a drawn sword but the one who poses as one's own, and Philby did his harm precisely through the trust of his colleagues.
