Kural 225

Philanthropy · Eekai · ஈகை

ஆற்றுவார் ஆற்றல் பசிஆற்றல் அப்பசியை மாற்றுவார் ஆற்றலின் பின்.
Aatruvaar aatral pasi aatral appasiyai Maatruvaar aatralin pin.

Meaning

People who train themselves by going hungry are strong. But they are less strong than people who take away the hunger of others.

Elementary

Going without food on purpose takes a lot of strength. But this kural says there is an even greater strength: giving food to someone who is hungry. Helping others beats showing how tough you are.

University

The couplet plays on the word ஆற்றல் (power, strength) and the verb ஆற்று (to endure, to perform): the ascetics’ power is பசி ஆற்றல், the power to endure hunger, whereas the philanthropist’s power is to மாற்று (change, remove) hunger. Valluvar deliberately ranks the householder’s charity above the ascetic’s self-mortification — a notable claim in a culture that deeply honoured tapas, and consistent with the Kural’s high regard for the householder (Chapter 5). The same contrast between self-denial and feeding the hungry is made by Isaiah, who says the fast God wants is to ‘deal thy bread to the hungry’. The couplet effectively redefines spiritual strength in social terms.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I do not pray to the Supreme Personality of Godhead for the eight perfections of mystic yoga, nor for salvation from repeated birth and death. I want only to stay among all the living entities and suffer all distresses on their behalf, so that they may be freed from suffering.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 9.21.12 (Prabhupada translation) [3]
King Rantideva, after fasting for forty-eight days, gives away his food and water to hungry visitors and declares that he wants neither yogic powers nor liberation, only to relieve others’ suffering.
Rantideva puts relieving others’ hunger above mystic attainments won by austerity — the same ranking as the Kural.
Christianity
“For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink... Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.”
— Matthew 25:35, 40 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the Last Judgement, Jesus identifies himself with the hungry and says feeding them is serving him.
Feeding the hungry is treated as the highest religious act, as in the Kural.
Islam
“But he has not broken through the difficult pass. And what can make you know what is [breaking through] the difficult pass? It is the freeing of a slave Or feeding on a day of severe hunger An orphan of near relationship Or a needy person in misery.”
— Qur'an 90:11-16 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an calls feeding the hungry in a time of famine ‘the difficult pass’, a steep and demanding climb.
Like the Kural, it frames relieving hunger as the true test of strength.
Judaism
“Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke? Is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house?”
— Isaiah 58:6-7 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah tells people who fast that God prefers them to share their bread with the hungry.
Near-identical ranking to the Kural: feeding the hungry is valued above going hungry oneself.
Buddhism
In the Sasa Jataka, the Bodhisatta is born as a hare who, having no food to offer a hungry brahmin visitor (the god Sakka in disguise), leaps into a fire so that his own body may be eaten. Sakka honours the gift by painting the hare’s image on the moon.
— Jataka No. 316, Sasa Jataka [6]
The hare offers the ultimate gift, its own body, to relieve a stranger’s hunger.
Relieving hunger is shown as the highest act of virtue, beyond any feat of self-control.
Jainism
“By austerities he cuts off the Karman.” … “By doing service he acquires the Karman which brings about for him the nâman and gôtra of a Tîrthakara.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.27 and 29.43 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
In Mahāvīra's list of the fruits of religious practice, austerity (tapas) wears away karma. Service to others (vaiyāvṛttya) is said to earn the karma that makes one a future Tīrthaṅkara, the highest rank Jain tradition recognises.
Valluvar ranks relieving others' hunger above the ascetic's power to endure hunger. This passage likewise gives service to others a spiritual fruit that austerity alone does not bring.
Sikhism
“Without this grain, time does not pass in peace. Forsaking this grain, one does not meet the Lord of the World.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 873, Raag Gond, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a hymn that opens by calling those who give up food hypocrites, Bhagat Kabir insists that food sustains life and that abandoning it does not bring anyone to God.
Valluvar ranks relieving hunger above the ascetic's power to endure it, and Kabir in the Guru Granth Sahib likewise refuses to treat fasting and self-starvation as a path to God.
Greek Mythology
Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to mortals so that they can cook food and survive, and is punished by Zeus by being chained to a rock where an eagle eats his liver.
— Hesiod, Theogony 561-616 [9]
Prometheus uses his power not for himself but to meet human need, at great cost.
Like the Kural, it honours power used to relieve others’ want above power used for oneself.
Philosophy
“If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it.”
— Peter Singer, “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” (1972) [10]
Peter Singer argues that people who can relieve starvation at modest cost are morally obliged to do so.
Like the Kural, Singer makes the relief of hunger a central moral demand and a measure of moral strength.

Real Life Example

The Ramakrishna Mission's monks begin famine relief at Mahula
May 1897 · Mahula, Murshidabad district, Bengal, India
In 1897 Swami Vivekananda founded the Ramakrishna Mission near Calcutta, with the twofold aim of spreading Vedanta and improving social conditions in India. That May, Swami Akhandananda, a monk of the order who had found famine-stricken villages in Murshidabad district, opened the Mission's first famine relief centre at Mahula, obtaining rice and distributing it to the hungry, with fellow monks and funds sent to help. The relief work lasted about a year.
— Sources: [11] [12]
An order of renunciant monks made the feeding of the hungry a central part of their religious life rather than devoting themselves only to personal austerity.
The kural ranks the strength of those who relieve others' hunger above the ascetic's strength to endure hunger, and here ascetics themselves turned to relieving hunger.
