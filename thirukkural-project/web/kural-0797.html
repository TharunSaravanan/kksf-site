Kural 797

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

ஊதியம் என்பது ஒருவற்குப் பேதையார் கேண்மை ஒரீஇ விடல்.
Oodhiyam enbadhu oruvarkup pedhaiyaar kenmai oreei vidal.

Meaning

The real gain for a person is to give up friendship with fools completely.

Elementary

If your kite is tied to a heavy rock, it can never fly. Cutting the string to the rock is not a loss; it is how the kite goes up. Letting go of a friend who keeps pulling you into silly trouble is the same kind of win.

University

Oodhiyam (ஊதியம்) is ‘profit’ or ‘gain’, a deliberately commercial word in a chapter of the Porutpal. Pedhaiyaar (பேதையார்) are fools in the specific Kural sense defined in chapter 84 (Pedhaimai): people who take up what harms them and drop what helps them. Oreei (ஒரீஇ) is an alapedai (sound-lengthened) form of oruvi, ‘having abandoned’, and with vidal (விடல், ‘letting go’) it makes ‘to abandon and let go entirely’. Parimelazhagar and Puliyur Kesigan read the couplet as applying to a friendship already formed with a fool unknowingly: leaving it as soon as the folly is recognised is itself a gain. The sandhi-joined text reads ஊதிய மென்ப தொருவற்குப் … யொரீஇ, the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Do not keep company with a fool for as we can see he is a two-legged beast. Like an unseen thorn he pierces the heart with his sharp words.”
— Chanakya Niti 3.7 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya warns that a fool’s company wounds you in ways you do not see coming.
Both texts say that staying away from fools is to your benefit.
Christianity
“He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed.”
— Proverbs 13:20 (KJV) [5]
The proverb contrasts the gain of walking with the wise with the loss suffered by the friend of fools.
Both texts frame leaving fools’ company as profit and keeping it as loss.
Islam
“The example of a good companion (who sits with you) in comparison with a bad one, is like that of the musk seller and the blacksmith’s bellows (or furnace); from the first you would either buy musk or enjoy its good smell while the bellows would either burn your clothes or your house, or you get a bad nasty smell thereof.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2101 [6]
The Prophet compares a good companion to a perfume seller whose fragrance you share, and a bad one to a blacksmith whose sparks burn you.
Both texts weigh companions in terms of gain and loss.
Judaism
“Keep your distance from a dull man, For you will not learn wise speech.”
— Proverbs 14:7 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, Sefaria) [7]
The proverb advises walking away from a fool, since nothing wise can be gained from him.
Both say that leaving the fool is the sensible, profitable choice.
Buddhism
“Should a seeker not find a companion who is better or equal, let him resolutely pursue a solitary course; there is no fellowship with the fool.”
— Dhammapada 61 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha says it is better to walk alone than to keep company with a fool.
Like the Kural, the verse treats leaving the fool, even for solitude, as the better bargain.
Jainism
“This is the road to it: to serve the Gurus and the old (teachers), to avoid throughout foolish people, to apply oneself earnestly to study, and to ponder zealously on the meaning of the Sûtras.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahāvīra lists keeping away from foolish people as one of the essential steps on the road to final deliverance.
The Kural calls giving up friendship with fools a person's true gain, and the Jain canon likewise puts avoiding fools among the requirements of the path to liberation.
Sikhism
“Kabir! Do not associate with the faithless cynics; run far away from them. If you touch a vessel stained with soot, some of the soot will stick to you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir 131 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir uses the image of a soot-blackened pot: however careful you are, contact leaves a mark, so the only safe course with a bad companion is to get well away.
The Kural says a person's real gain is to give up completely the friendship of fools; Kabir likewise counsels a clean break, since even slight contact with the wrong company leaves its stain.
Greek Mythology
“The Farmer laughed aloud and said, ‘It may be all as you say, I only know this: I have taken you with these robbers, the Cranes, and you must die in their company.’”
— Aesop, The Farmer and the Stork (trans. G. F. Townsend) [11]
A stork caught in a net with crop-stealing cranes pleads its good character, but the farmer judges it by the company it was keeping.
The fable shows the cost of not leaving bad company in time, the loss the Kural tells us to avoid.
Philosophy
“But wicked men have no steadfastness (for they do not remain even like to themselves), but become friends for a short time because they delight in each other’s wickedness.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.8 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle says friendships among bad people are short and unstable because they rest on shared vice.
Aristotle explains why such a friendship is worth nothing and losing it is no loss.

Real Life Example

Gandhi breaks with his boyhood companion Sheikh Mehtab
1890s · Durban, Natal (now South Africa)
As a schoolboy in Rajkot, Mohandas Gandhi formed a close friendship with Sheikh Mehtab, entering it, as he later wrote, in the spirit of a reformer, and he described it in his autobiography in two chapters titled A Tragedy. Mehtab later lived in Gandhi's household in Durban, where Gandhi discovered serious misconduct by him and learned that he had turned Gandhi against an honest clerk with false suspicions. Gandhi told him he would have nothing more to do with him and made him leave the house, later writing that in trying to reform the man he had nearly ruined himself and that the break let him begin a more detached and disciplined life.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Gandhi came to see the end of this friendship not as a loss but as a rescue that freed him for his later work.
The kural says the real gain for a person is to give up completely the friendship of fools, and Gandhi himself described his break with Mehtab as a deliverance.
