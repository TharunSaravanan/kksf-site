Kural 1300

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

தஞ்சம் தமரல்லர் ஏதிலார் தாமுடைய நெஞ்சம் தமரல் வழி.
Thanjam thamarallar aedhilaar thaamudaiya nenjam thamaral vazhi.

Meaning

When your own heart does not act like your family, it is no surprise that strangers do not either.

Elementary

If even your own feelings won’t be kind to you, you can’t be surprised when people you hardly know aren’t kind either. The wife says that since her heart has left her, she can hardly blame others for not standing by her.

University

Tañcam here is glossed by the commentators as ‘easy, a slight matter’ (eḷitu): Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read ‘it is easy (natural) for strangers (ētilār) not to be kin (tamar allar) when one’s own heart (tām uṭaiya neñcam) is not kin’. Puliyur Kesikan agrees, calling it ‘natural’ (iyalpāṉatē). Sivayogi Sivakumar instead takes tañcam in its other sense, ‘refuge’: ‘there is no kin to give refuge if one’s own heart is not one’s own’, a variant reading of the same text. Pope renders ‘A trifle is unfriendliness by aliens shown, / When our own heart itself is not our own!’ The couplet closes the chapter by turning the wife’s quarrel into a general truth about the self; it is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For him who has conquered the mind, the mind is the best of friends; but for one who has failed to do so, his very mind will be the greatest enemy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.6 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna teaches that one’s own mind can be the closest friend or the worst enemy.
The Kural says the same: when one’s own heart is not kin, it is a greater blow than any stranger’s coldness.
Christianity
“A double minded man is unstable in all his ways.”
— James 1:8 (KJV) [4]
James warns that a person divided within cannot stand firm in anything.
The wife, at odds with her own heart, finds she can expect little steadiness from anyone else.
Islam
“And know that Allah intervenes between a man and his heart and that to Him you will be gathered.”
— Qur’an 8:24 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that a person does not fully command even his own heart.
The Kural’s speaker has found exactly this: her heart is not wholly her own.
Judaism
“The heart knoweth its own bitterness; and with its joy no stranger can intermeddle.”
— Proverbs 14:10 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs observes that each heart lives its own inner life that outsiders cannot share.
The Kural draws on the same contrast between one’s own heart and strangers.
Buddhism
“Neither mother, father, nor any other relative can do one greater good than one’s own well-directed mind.”
— Dhammapada 43 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha ranks one’s own well-directed mind above any kinsman as a source of good.
The Kural makes the same comparison: one’s heart should be closer kin than any relative or stranger.
Jainism
“A cut-throat enemy will not do him such harm as his own perversity will do him; the man without pity will feel repentance in the hour of death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that no outside enemy harms a person as much as his own misdirected self.
The Kural likewise finds one’s own heart a worse betrayer than any stranger.
Sikhism
“See the brotherhood of all mankind as the highest order of Yogis; conquer your own mind, and conquer the world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 6, Japji Sahib (Pauri 28), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that mastering one's own mind is the key that sets right one's relation to the whole world.
The Kural says that when one's own heart is not kin, strangers will not be either; Japji likewise makes the mind the starting point for everything outside it.
Greek Mythology
Euripides’ Medea, before her terrible act, says that she understands the evil she is about to do, but her passion (thumos) is stronger than her reasoning.
— Euripides, Medea 1078-1080 (paraphrase) [10]
Medea’s own heart overrules her and becomes her worst enemy.
As in the Kural, when one’s own heart is not on one’s side, nothing outside can make up for it.
Philosophy
Augustine marvels that the mind commands the body and is obeyed at once, but when the mind commands itself it is resisted.
— Augustine, Confessions VIII.9 (paraphrase) [11]
Augustine finds the strangest disobedience not in others but within his own will.
The wife discovers the same: the one who will not obey her is her own heart, so strangers’ coldness is no surprise.

Real Life Example

Beethoven writes the Heiligenstadt Testament
6 October 1802 · Heiligenstadt, near Vienna, Austria
On 6 October 1802, in the village of Heiligenstadt outside Vienna, Ludwig van Beethoven wrote a long letter to his brothers Carl and Johann that has become known as the Heiligenstadt Testament. In it he explained that people who thought him unfriendly, stubborn or misanthropic misjudged him, because they did not know the secret cause of his behaviour, his growing deafness. He wrote that such experiences had brought him to the edge of despair.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Beethoven’s own failing hearing cut him off from people, and the people around him, not knowing why, turned against him in their judgements.
The kural says that when even one’s own heart will not act like kin, it is no wonder strangers do not, and Beethoven found that when his own body failed him, outsiders also failed to understand him.
