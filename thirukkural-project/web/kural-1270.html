Kural 1270

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

பெறின்என்னாம் பெற்றக்கால் என்னாம் உறினென்னாம் உள்ளம் உடைந்துக்கக் கால்.
Perin-ennaam petrakkaal ennaam urinennaam ullam udaindhukkak kaal.

Meaning

If her heart breaks with sorrow and she dies, what good will it be that she gets me back, or has me, or even holds me close?

Elementary

The husband worries: “If I stay away too long, she might become so sad that her heart breaks. Then what good would it be to come home? I must hurry back while there is still time.” It is like a promise that is only worth something if you keep it in time.

University

Most commentators, following the tradition associated with Parimelazhagar, give this closing couplet to the husband, who fears for his wife: Solomon Pappaiah reads ‘if her heart breaks… what use is it that she gets me?’, and Pope translates ‘If she heart-broken lie, with all her life poured forth’. The triple question peṟiṉ eṉ ām / peṟṟakkāl eṉ ām / uṟiṉ eṉ ām moves from ‘should she obtain [me]’ to ‘having obtained’ to ‘having embraced’, and uḷḷam uṭaintu ukkakkāl means ‘when the heart has broken and spilled away’, a euphemism for dying of grief. Mu. Varadarasanar keeps the first person plural (நம்மை) and Kalaignar a neutral ‘each other’, so the speaker is not unanimous, but the sense of urgency is shared. Text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Queen Indumati dies suddenly, King Aja laments that without her his kingdom, his pleasures and his very life have lost their meaning, and he lives on only for his son’s sake.
— Kalidasa, Raghuvamsha, canto 8, Aja’s lament for Indumati (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa shows that every good a husband possesses becomes empty once the beloved wife is gone.
This is the fear behind the Kural: if she dies of grief, no reunion, victory or embrace will be worth anything.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus asks what any gain is worth if the one thing that matters is lost.
The Kural’s husband asks the same kind of question: what use is any homecoming if she is lost?
Islam
In Nizami’s romance, Majnun, kept apart from Layla all his life, learns of her death and goes to her grave, where he dies; their union comes only in death.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (paraphrase) [5]
The great Persian love story shows separation lasting until it is too late for any earthly reunion.
This is the outcome the Kural’s husband dreads: a heart broken by separation, after which meeting means nothing.
Judaism
“The spirit of a man will sustain his infirmity; but a broken spirit who can bear?”
— Proverbs 18:14 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The proverb says the spirit can carry bodily illness, but nothing can carry a broken spirit.
The Kural’s husband fears precisely a broken spirit (uḷḷam uṭaintu) that nothing afterward could mend.
Buddhism
“Therefore hold nothing dear, for separation from the dear is painful. There are no bonds for those who have nothing beloved or unloved.”
— Dhammapada 211 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that separation from loved ones is inherently painful and counsels non-attachment.
The Kural’s couple know that pain of separation all too well; the husband’s answer is to hurry home, the Buddha’s is to loosen the bond.
Jainism
“Shame upon my life, that I have been forsaken by him! it is better I should turn nun.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Forsaken by Arishtanemi, Rajimati feels that her life as it was has lost its worth, and turns to renunciation.
Rajimati is the heart-broken wife the Kural’s husband fears to leave behind, though in Jainism her grief opens onto a new path.
Sikhism
“My Beloved has not come home, and I am dying of the sorrow of separation. The lightning flashes, and I am scared.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1108, Raag Tukhaari, Barah Maha, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha Guru Nanak's soul-bride says that while her Beloved stays away she is dying of the grief of separation.
The Kural's husband fears his wife will die of the grief of waiting, so that his return will come too late; Guru Nanak's soul-bride voices that very danger of dying while the Beloved is away.
Greek Mythology
Theseus, sailing home victorious from Crete, forgets to raise the white sail; his father Aegeus, watching from the cliff and seeing the black sail, throws himself into the sea, so that Theseus’ triumphant return comes too late.
— Plutarch, Life of Theseus 22; Catullus 64 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth shows a victory made empty because the one waiting at home did not survive the wait.
This is the Kural’s warning in story form: if the one at home is lost to grief, the return and the victory are worth nothing.
Philosophy
Seneca urges his friend to hold every hour fast, since everything else belongs to others and time alone is truly ours, and once lost it cannot be recovered.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 1 (tr. R.M. Gummere; paraphrase) [11]
Seneca treats time as the one irreplaceable possession, which delay squanders.
The Kural’s husband sees the same danger: delay could cost what no later return can restore.

Real Life Example

Mumtaz Mahal dies on Shah Jahan's Deccan campaign
1631 · Burhanpur, India
In 1631 Mumtaz Mahal, wife of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, accompanied him while pregnant on a military campaign in the Deccan. She died at Burhanpur after giving birth to the couple's fourteenth child. Her body was moved to Agra in January 1632, and work began there on the Taj Mahal as her tomb.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The emperor's campaign went on, but the loss of his wife turned his energies to building one of the world's most famous monuments of grief.
The kural's husband, away on campaign, asks what use any reunion would be if his wife's heart broke and she died; Shah Jahan's campaign ended with exactly such a loss.
