Kural 1308

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

நோதல் எவன்மற்று நொந்தாரென்று அஃதறியும் காதலர் இல்லா வழி.
Noadhal evanmatru nondhaarendru aqdhariyum kaadhalar illaa vazhi.

Meaning

What use is it to feel sad when there is no loved one who will understand, "They are hurting because of me"?

Elementary

If you sulk in your room, it only helps if someone who loves you notices and comes to ask what is wrong. If nobody notices at all, the sulking just makes you lonely.

University

Nōtal evaṉ maṟṟu, ‘what then is the use of grieving,’ when there are no kātalar, ‘lovers,’ who know it (aḵtu aṟiyum) as nontār eṉṟu, ‘she has grieved (because of us).’ Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar take the lover’s grief as worthwhile only when the beloved recognizes himself as its cause; Kesikan adds that such a lover would then try to remove it. The verse states the precondition of the whole pulavi convention: a sulk is a message, and it needs a receiver. Pope’s prose explanation, ‘What avails sorrow when I am without a wife who can understand the cause of my sorrow?’, puts the words in the husband’s mouth, whereas the Tamil commentators leave the speaker general. ytamizh.com prints நொந்தாரென்று அஃதறியும் and thirukkural.net the sandhi form நொந்தாரென் றஃதறியும்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, the gopis of Vraja pour out their grief at Krishna’s absence to his messenger Uddhava, addressing a passing bee and asking what use their longing is when the one who caused it has gone away to the city.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47, Bhramara Gita (paraphrase) [3]
The gopis feel their grief is wasted unless the beloved knows and answers it.
The Kural states the same complaint: sorrow means little when the beloved does not recognize that it is for him.
Christianity
“Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep.”
— Romans 12:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks believers to share in one another’s feelings, so no one grieves alone.
The Kural assumes the same need: grief has meaning only when someone who loves us shares and understands it.
Islam
“He said, ‘I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know.’”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob, grieving for Joseph, directs his complaint only to the One who truly knows and cares.
The Kural likewise holds that grief should be addressed to one who will understand it; without such a listener, it is wasted.
Judaism
“Look on my right hand, and see, For there is no man that knoweth me; I have no way to flee; No man careth for my soul.”
— Psalms 142:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist laments that no one recognizes him or cares about his distress.
This is the Kural’s situation exactly: sorrow with no one who knows it is for them, and so no one to answer it.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches that one does not gain peace by weeping and lamenting; grieving only increases one’s pain and harms the body, and does nothing for the one mourned.
— Sutta Nipata 3.8, Salla Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
Buddhism sees lamentation as useless suffering that should be pulled out like an arrow.
The Kural also asks what grief accomplishes, though it answers that it helps only when a loving partner recognizes it.
Jainism
When Prince Neminatha turns back from his own wedding and renounces the world, his bride Rajimati grieves for the one who has left her, then follows him into the ascetic life.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22 (paraphrase; tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati’s grief has no one to answer it, so she transforms it into renunciation.
The Kural asks what grief is for when the beloved will not acknowledge it; Rajimati’s answer is to let it lead beyond worldly love.
Sikhism
“Without water, she cannot live, even for an instant. God knows the suffering of her mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 60, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak compares the soul to a fish that cannot live without water, and says God knows its inner suffering.
The Kural asks what use grieving is when no lover knows it is for him; Gurbani answers that the soul's grief is never unseen, because God knows the pain of the mind.
Greek Mythology
Abandoned by Theseus on the shore of Naxos, Ariadne pours out her lament to the empty sea and the winds, with no one to hear her complaint.
— Catullus 64 (paraphrase) [10]
Her grief is all the more bitter because the one who caused it is gone and cannot hear it.
The Kural’s grief without a knowing lover is exactly Ariadne’s situation on the deserted shore.
Philosophy
Francis Bacon writes that friendship doubles joys and cuts griefs in half, since no one shares a sorrow with a friend without grieving the less.
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Friendship’ (paraphrase) [11]
Bacon sees grief as lightened only when another person receives it.
The Kural’s question rests on the same insight: sorrow has its use only when someone who loves us understands it.

Real Life Example

Eleanor Roosevelt discovers the Lucy Mercer letters
1918 · Washington, D.C., United States
In 1918 Eleanor Roosevelt discovered that her husband, Franklin D. Roosevelt, was romantically involved with her social secretary, Lucy Mercer. She offered him a divorce, but he refused, for reasons that included his political career and his mother’s financial support, and he agreed to stop seeing Mercer. He later resumed contact with Mercer, who was with him when he died at Warm Springs, Georgia, in 1945.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Eleanor’s grief produced a promise, but whether her husband truly understood the hurt he had caused is doubtful, since political reasons weighed heavily and the promise was not kept.
The kural asks what use grieving is when the beloved does not truly understand that the sorrow is his doing; Eleanor Roosevelt’s experience shows how hollow a reconciliation can be when that understanding is missing.
