Kural 1213

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

நனவினால் நல்கா தவரைக் கனவினால் காண்டலின் உண்டென் உயிர்.
Nanavinaal nalkaa thavaraik kanavinaal kaantalin unten uyir.

Meaning

He is not kind to me while I am awake. But I see him in my dreams, and that is what keeps me alive.

Elementary

Think of a plant on a windowsill that only gets a little sunlight each morning. It is just enough to keep it alive. For this woman, seeing her husband in dreams is that little bit of sunlight that keeps her going until he comes home.

University

The couplet sets up the chapter’s governing antithesis of naṉavu (waking) and kaṉavu (dream), each in the instrumental (naṉaviṉāl, kaṉaviṉāl, ‘by waking’, ‘by dream’). Nalkātavar, ‘he who does not grant grace’, from nalku (to show favour, bestow love), names the absent husband by his failure to come; the word recurs through the chapter (1214, 1217, 1219) and functions almost as an epithet. Kāṇṭaliṉ, ‘because of seeing’, gives the cause, and uṇṭu eṉ uyir, ‘my life exists’, makes the dream the slender thread on which her life hangs. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all gloss it as ‘my life has not yet left me only because I see him in dreams’. Pope: ‘Him, who in waking hour no kindness shows, / In dreams I see; and so my lifetime goes!’ The text is identical in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Bhasa’s play, King Udayana, believing his queen Vasavadatta dead, murmurs to her in his sleep; she, actually alive and in disguise nearby, gently touches him and slips away, and on waking he cannot tell whether he has dreamed her or truly seen her.
— Bhasa, Svapnavasavadatta, Act 5 (paraphrase) [3]
The ‘Vision of Vasavadatta’ makes a dream the only place where a grieving husband can still meet his wife.
Udayana, like the Kural’s heroine, is kept going by seeing in dreams the spouse whom waking life denies him.
Christianity
“It was but a little that I passed from them, but I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go.”
— Song of Solomon 3:4 (KJV) [4]
After a night of fruitless searching, the bride in the Song of Solomon at last finds her beloved and clings to him; many interpreters read the scene as a night-vision.
Her finding the beloved after vain searching is the Kural’s dream-meeting, the encounter that keeps the seeker alive.
Islam
“Whoever sees me in a dream will see me in his wakefulness, and Satan cannot imitate me in shape.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6993 [5]
The Prophet assures believers who long for him that seeing him in a dream is a true sight, a promise of meeting him in waking.
For the Kural’s heroine too, the dream-sight of the absent beloved is real enough to sustain her until waking union returns.
Judaism
“I sleep, but my heart waketh; Hark! my beloved knocketh: ‘Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled.’”
— Song of Songs 5:2 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Shulammite, asleep, hears her beloved come to her door in what reads as a dream-visit.
As in the Kural, the beloved comes to the sleeper when he cannot be had by day, and that visit keeps the heart awake and alive.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that from what is dear springs grief and from what is dear springs fear; for one wholly free from endearment there is no grief.
— Dhammapada 212 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhism sees the heart’s dependence on a loved one as the very source of sorrow.
The Kural’s heroine stakes her life on dream-glimpses of the beloved; the Buddhist verse explains why such dependence brings both her sustenance and her pain.
Jainism
In Jain retellings of the Ramayana, Sita in Ravana’s captivity is sustained by unbroken fidelity to Rama and by the news of him that Hanuman brings.
— Vimalasuri, Paumacariya, Sita’s captivity (paraphrase) [8]
Jain narrative presents a separated wife whose constancy to an absent husband keeps her alive through captivity.
The Kural’s heroine likewise survives on the image of the husband who is not with her, though she finds it in dreams rather than in vows.
Sikhism
“In a dream, I was lifted up; why didn't I grasp the hem of His Robe?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1362, Phunhay, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan's soul-bride recalls rising to meet her Lord in a dream, the only place she glimpsed Him, and reproaches herself for not holding on.
The Kural's wife says that he who shows no grace while she is awake is still seen in her dreams, and that this keeps her alive; the Guru's bride too has her Lord only in a dream.
Greek Mythology
“Often you will gladden me, appearing in my dreams; for sweet it is to look on those we love in dreams, however brief the night.”
— Euripides, Alcestis 354-356 (tr. Richard Aldington, via the Internet Classics Archive) [10]
Admetus, about to lose his wife Alcestis, consoles himself that she will still come to him in dreams.
This is the Kural’s comfort almost word for word: when waking life withholds the beloved, dream-sight keeps the lover going.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that dream-images are residual movements left in the sense-organs by earlier perceptions, which persist after the perceived object has gone and present themselves in sleep.
— Aristotle, On Dreams (De Insomniis) 460b-461a, Parva Naturalia (paraphrase) [11]
On Aristotle’s account, the sight of an absent person survives within us after the person departs.
The beloved who no longer comes by day still reaches the Kural’s heroine at night because, as Aristotle explains, his image lingers in her.

Real Life Example

Ono no Komachi's dream poems enter the Kokinshu
9th century; anthology compiled 905 · Heian court, Kyoto, Japan
Ono no Komachi was a ninth-century poet of the Heian court, and eighteen of her poems were included in the imperial anthology Kokinshu, compiled in 905. Several are dream poems: in one, having seen the man dear to her while she napped, she says she has begun to trust the things called dreams; in another she says that had she known it was a dream, she would never have woken.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Komachi's poems are among the most famous in Japanese literature on meeting a lover only in dreams.
The kural's heroine says that though her husband shows no kindness while she is awake, seeing him in dreams keeps her alive, which is the very theme of Komachi's dream poems.
