Kural 759

Wealth Accumulation · Porul Seyalvakai · பொருள் செயல்வகை

செய்க பொருளைச் செறுநர் செருக்கறுக்கும் எஃகதனிற் கூரிய தில்.
Seyga porulaich cherunar serukkarukkum eqkadhanir kooriya thil.

Meaning

Build up your money. No blade is sharper than money for cutting down the pride of your enemies.

Elementary

Imagine a bully who brags that nobody can stand up to him. Then the whole class saves up to build a strong new fence and hire a coach, and suddenly the bully stops bragging. Valluvar says a well-stocked treasury is the sharpest sword against a proud enemy.

University

The verse opens with a blunt imperative, seyga porulai, ‘make wealth!’, which is rare in the Kural and gives the command its force. Cherunar are foes, serukku is their arrogance, and aRukkum (‘that cuts off’) sets up the image of eqku (எஃகு), steel or a sword, of which nothing is sharper (kooriyadhu il). Parimelazhagar explains that wealth lets a king raise and keep armies and allies, and so humbles enemies without his having to strike himself. Solomon Pappaiah addresses the verse to anyone who wants to achieve something. The sandhi-joined text reads செருக்கறுக்கு மெஃகதனிற், which divides into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kautilya’s Arthashastra holds that the army is rooted in the treasury (koshamulo hi dandah), so a king with a full treasury can raise and keep the force needed to subdue enemies.
— Kautilya, Arthashastra, Book VIII (on the elements of sovereignty), trans. R. Shamasastry [3]
Indian statecraft ranked the treasury as the source of military power.
For Kautilya as for Valluvar, wealth is the real weapon behind every other weapon.
Christianity
“For wisdom is a defence, and money is a defence: but the excellency of knowledge is, that wisdom giveth life to them that have it.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:12 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher places money beside wisdom as a protection, while ranking wisdom higher.
Money as a defence is the protective side of the Kural’s sharp steel.
Islam
“And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allah and your enemy... And whatever you spend in the cause of Allah will be fully repaid to you.”
— Qur’an 8:60 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an tells the community to prepare strength to deter enemies and promises that spending for this cause will be repaid.
In the verse, spending builds the strength that humbles an enemy, much as the Kural’s wealth cuts down a foe’s pride.
Judaism
“For to be in the shelter of wisdom is to be also in the shelter of money, and the advantage of intelligence is that wisdom preserves the life of the one who possesses it.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:12 (JPS Tanakh, Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet says money, like wisdom, gives shelter from life’s threats.
Kohelet’s shelter of money is the defensive side of the Kural’s weapon against enemies.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta, the Buddha tells how a king who failed to give wealth to the poor set off a slide into theft, weapons and violence, showing that neglecting material provision breeds conflict.
— Digha Nikaya 26, Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta [7]
The sutta traces social violence back to poverty the ruler failed to relieve.
Seen from the opposite side, wealth rightly used is what keeps hostility from arising, which is a gentler version of the Kural’s weapon.
Jainism
“Fight with your Self; why fight with external foes? He who conquers himself through himself, will obtain happiness.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.35 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Nami tells Indra, who urges him to subdue rival kings, that the real battle is against one's own passions, and in the next verse he names them: the senses, anger, pride, delusion and greed.
The Kural calls wealth the sharpest steel for cutting down the pride of one's enemies; Jainism points the blade inward, telling one to cut down one's own pride and greed instead of external foes.
Sikhism
“From the Guru, I have obtained the supremely powerful sword of spiritual wisdom.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1087, Raag Maaroo, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das names the Guru's wisdom as the sharpest of swords, the weapon with which the enemies within, the passions and ego, are cut down.
The Kural says there is no steel sharper than wealth for cutting down a foe's pride; Gurbani names a different sharpest blade, the Guru's wisdom, which cuts down one's own pride.
Greek Mythology
In Thucydides, the Spartan king Archidamus warns his people before the Peloponnesian War that war is a matter less of arms than of money, which is what makes arms useful.
— Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 1.83 [10]
Archidamus argues that Athens’ wealth makes it more dangerous than Sparta’s soldiers alone can handle.
Archidamus and Valluvar agree that wealth is the sharpest weapon in a conflict.
Philosophy
Cicero, urging war against Mark Antony, calls unlimited money the sinews of war (nervos belli, pecuniam infinitam), a phrase that became proverbial.
— Cicero, Philippics 5.5 [11]
Cicero argues that money is what holds a war effort together.
The sinews of war and the sharpest steel are the same idea: wealth is what defeats an enemy.

Real Life Example

Jakob Fugger finances the election of Charles V as Holy Roman Emperor
1519 · Augsburg and Frankfurt, Germany
After Emperor Maximilian I died in 1519, his grandson Charles of Spain and King Francis I of France competed to be elected Holy Roman Emperor, and the electors sought payments from both. Of election expenses of about 852,000 guilders spent on Charles's behalf, the Augsburg banker Jakob Fugger raised almost 544,000. Charles was elected, and Francis I's bid failed.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A banker's money, not an army, decided which of Europe's two most powerful kings would wear the imperial crown.
The kural says no steel is sharper than wealth for cutting down an enemy's pride, and Fugger's loans defeated the ambitions of the king of France without a blow being struck.
