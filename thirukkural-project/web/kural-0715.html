Kural 715

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

நன்றென்ற வற்றுள்ளும் நன்றே முதுவருள் முந்து கிளவாச் செறிவு.
Nandrendra vatrullum nandre mudhuvarul Mundhu kilavaach cherivu.

Meaning

Of all good things, the best is holding back from speaking first when you are among elders who are wiser than you.

Elementary

When your grandparents and their friends are talking about the old days, you might know a fun fact too. But the polite and smart thing is to listen first and let them speak. Waiting your turn shows you are growing up.

University

Mudhuvar are elders in knowledge, not just in age. In Parimelazhagar’s scheme this is the conduct suited to an assembly superior to oneself, just as 714’s first half suits an equal one. Mundhu kilavaa means not uttering (kila, to speak) first, not jumping ahead. Cherivu (literally density or closeness) here means self-restraint, what Mu. Varadarasanar calls adakkam, and it links this chapter to ch. 13, Adakkamudaimai. The superlative nandrendravatrullum nandre (good even among the things called good) is strong praise: such restraint is ranked above other virtues of the counsellor. Kalaignar’s paraphrase adds a vivid Tamil idiom, “like a cashew seed” (mundhiri kottai), for a person who pushes forward to speak. The text agrees across sources; the sandhi text reads “நன்றென்ற வற்றுள்ளு நன்றே முதுவருண்”.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For the vital airs of a young man mount upwards to leave his body when an elder approaches; but by rising to meet him and saluting he recovers them.”
— Manusmriti 2.120 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
Manu teaches that deference to elders is basic to right conduct, so much so that failing to show it is pictured as losing one’s life-breath.
The Kural’s restraint before elders, letting them speak first, comes from the same culture of respect for seniors.
Christianity
“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath.”
— James 1:19 (KJV) [5]
James tells believers to put listening first and to be slow to speak.
“Slow to speak” is the Kural’s mundhu kilavaa, not rushing to speak first.
Islam
“I wanted to say that it was the datepalm tree but as I was the youngest of all (of them) I kept quiet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 72 (narrated by Ibn Umar) [6]
The Prophet asked his companions a riddle. Young Ibn Umar knew the answer but stayed silent out of deference to the senior companions present.
Ibn Umar’s silence is almost a dramatization of this kural: knowing, yet not speaking first among elders.
Judaism
“A wise man does not speak before one who is greater than he in wisdom, And does not break into his fellow’s speech; And is not hasty to answer.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:7 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
The Mishnah lists seven marks of the wise, and the first is not speaking before someone greater in wisdom.
This is virtually the Kural’s wording: do not speak first before those greater in learning.
Buddhism
In the Tittira Jataka, a partridge, a monkey, and an elephant work out which of them is the eldest so that they can honour him. The Buddha tells the story when senior monks are left without lodging because younger monks took it first, and he rules that respect and precedence go to seniority.
— Tittira Jataka (Jataka No. 37), in The Jataka, vol. 1, ed. E. B. Cowell [8]
The Buddha makes deference to elders a rule of the monastic community.
The Kural’s restraint before elders applies the same principle of precedence to speech in council.
Jainism
“One should always be meek, and not be talkative in the presence of the wise; one should acquire valuable knowledge, and avoid what is worthless.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.8 (cf. 1.14) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Uttaradhyayana's discipline also tells the disciple 'He should not speak unasked' (1.14). Restraint of speech before the wise is part of vinaya, the humility that makes learning possible.
This is the Kural's restraint of not rushing to speak first among wiser elders, stated almost word for word as a Jain rule of conduct.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa, Asa ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Asa ki Vaar, Guru Nanak names sweetness and humility as the very essence of all good qualities.
The Kural calls the restraint that does not rush to speak first before wiser elders the best of all good things; Guru Nanak names humility the essence of virtue.
Greek Mythology
Arriving at Pylos, the young Telemachus hesitates to approach the aged king Nestor. He tells Athena, disguised as Mentor, that a young man is shy of questioning an elder.
— Homer, Odyssey 3.22-24 (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
Homer presents Telemachus’s reluctance to speak first before a revered elder as proper modesty, and Athena encourages him gently.
Telemachus’s restraint before Nestor is the Kural’s modesty among elders.
Philosophy
“In all cases, then, silence is a safe adornment for the young man, and especially so, when in listening to another he does not get excited or bawl out every minute, but even if the remarks be none too agreeable, puts up with them, and waits for the speaker to pause …”
— Plutarch, Moralia, On Listening to Lectures (De auditu), ch. 4 (tr. F. C. Babbitt) [12]
Plutarch advises a young man attending a philosopher's lecture to listen in silence, to wait until the speaker has finished, and not to rush in with objections, recalling the saying that nature gave us two ears and one tongue.
Plutarch, like the Kural, praises the restraint of a younger person who does not hurry to speak before those wiser than himself.

Real Life Example

Thomas Jefferson's silence in the Continental Congress
1775-1776 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
Thomas Jefferson, a young Virginia delegate in the Continental Congress, rarely spoke in its debates, and John Adams later recalled that during the whole time he sat with him in Congress he never heard him utter three sentences together. Jefferson was nonetheless known for a masterly pen, and in June 1776 he was named to the committee to prepare the Declaration of Independence alongside Adams, the elder Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman and Robert Livingston, which entrusted him with the draft. Adams reflected in his autobiography that eloquence in public assemblies was not the surest road to fame, pointing to the reserve of Washington, Franklin and Jefferson.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Jefferson let senior and more forceful members carry the floor, and his restraint left him trusted with the most important writing of all.
The kural praises the restraint that does not rush to speak first among wiser elders, and Jefferson's quiet in Congress earned him the confidence of his seniors.
