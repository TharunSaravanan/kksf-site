Kural 928

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

களித்தறியேன் என்பது கைவிடுக நெஞ்சத்து ஒளித்ததூஉம் ஆங்கே மிகும்.
Kaliththariyen enbadhu kaividuka nenjaththu oliththadhooum aange migum.

Meaning

A drinker should stop saying, 'I have never been drunk.' When they drink, whatever they hid in their heart comes out even more.

Elementary

It's like a shaken bottle of fizzy drink: open it and everything inside comes spraying out. The Kural says drink opens people up the same way, so pretending you never drink doesn't work. Your secrets fizz out the moment you do.

University

Kaliththariyen (களித்தறியேன்) is the drinker's own denial: 'I have never known intoxication.' Kaividuka (கைவிடுக, 'give it up') tells him to drop the lie. The reason is nenjaththu oliththadhooum (நெஞ்சத்து ஒளித்ததூஉம், 'even what was hidden in the heart'; the -ooum is an alapedai of -um, 'even') aange migum (ஆங்கே மிகும், 'there and then increases', that is, comes out all the more). Mu. Varadarasanar reads the hidden thing as the hidden fault; Puliyur Kesikan as the earlier concealment of drinking itself; Kalaignar as the truth that the drunk man blurts out. The idea is close to the Graeco-Roman proverb 'in wine, truth'. The text is identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the Prabhasa revel the drunken Yadava heroes bring up old, buried grievances: Satyaki's contempt for Kritavarma's night massacre, Kritavarma's taunt about Bhurishrava, and the affair of the Syamantaka gem. The quarrel turns into slaughter.
— Mahabharata, Mausala Parva, Section 3 (K. M. Ganguli trans.) [3]
Resentments hidden for years come out the moment wine loosens the heroes' tongues.
This is the Kural's warning in narrative form: drink brings out what was hidden in the heart.
Christianity
“Thine eyes shall behold strange women, and thine heart shall utter perverse things.”
— Proverbs 23:33 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that under wine the heart speaks out its distortions.
The ‘heart’ that ‘utters’ is the Kural's nenjaththu oliththadhu, what is hidden in the heart, coming out.
Islam
“Avoid Khamr for it is the mother of all evils. … He said: ‘Pour me a cup of this wine.’ So she poured him a cup. He said: ‘Give me more.’ And soon he had intercourse with her and killed the boy.”
— Sunan an-Nasa'i 5666 [5]
Uthman tells of a devout worshipper who chose wine as the ‘least’ of three sins, and drink then led him into the other two.
The cup lets out every evil the man had held back, as the Kural warns.
Judaism
“Wine [yayin] was given in seventy letters, … Similarly, the word secret [sod] was given in seventy letters, … Typically, when wine entered the body, a secret emerged.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Eruvin 65a (William Davidson ed., Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud notes that 'wine' and 'secret' have the same numerical value of seventy: when wine goes in, the secret comes out.
This is almost word for word the Kural's claim that drink brings out what the heart hid.
Buddhism
“Easily seen is the fault of others, but one's own fault is difficult to see. Like chaff one winnows another's faults, but hides one's own, even as a crafty fowler hides behind sham branches.”
— Dhammapada 252 (Buddharakkhita trans.) [7]
The Dhammapada describes the habit of hiding one's own faults.
The drinker who denies drinking is hiding his fault, which the Kural says cannot stay hidden.
Jainism
“Pride, fear, disgust, laughter, disliking, grief, sex-passion, and anger etc., are bywords for hiṃsā; and all these go together with wine.”
— Amṛtacandra, Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya v. 64 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2012), as quoted in the explanatory note to Ratnakaraṇḍaka-śrāvakācāra v. 66 on wisdomlib.org [8]
Amṛtacandra teaches that the passions (kaṣāya) and quasi-passions that make up inner violence all go along with wine and come out under its influence.
Valluvar says that once a man drinks, whatever he has hidden in his heart comes out all the more; Amṛtacandra likewise holds that wine brings to the surface the pride, anger, lust and other passions a person carries within.
Sikhism
“That which is within them, comes out; it cannot be concealed by concealment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1243, Raag Saarang, Saarang ki Vaar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak observes that whatever lies inside a person sooner or later shows itself, however hard they try to hide it.
The Kural tells the drinker to stop denying it, since whatever is hidden in his heart comes out the moment he drinks, and Guru Nanak states the same law in general form.
Greek Mythology
The lyric poet Alcaeus is credited with the saying that wine and truth go together, the origin of the Latin proverb in vino veritas.
— Alcaeus, fragment (via the proverb ‘in vino veritas’) [10]
Greek tradition held that wine strips away pretence and lets the truth out.
This is the Kural's point: whatever is hidden comes out with wine.
Philosophy
“When the strength of wine has become too great and has gained control over the mind, every lurking evil comes forth from its hiding-place. Drunkenness does not create vice, it merely brings it into view; …”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 83.20 (Gummere trans.) [11]
Seneca says drink does not create vices but exposes those already present.
This matches the Kural's nenjaththu oliththadhooum aange migum almost exactly.

Real Life Example

Cleitus speaks his grievances at a drinking party and is killed by Alexander
Autumn 328 BCE · Maracanda (now Samarkand, Uzbekistan)
At a banquet in Maracanda, the officer Cleitus, who had saved Alexander's life at the Granicus and had just been named governor of Bactria, heard courtiers flatter Alexander at the expense of his father Philip and the older generation of commanders. Heated with wine, Cleitus answered with insolent words, praising Philip and saying that soldiers win the victories for which generals take the glory. Alexander, also in a drunken rage, seized a weapon and killed him, and when sober again was overcome with remorse for days.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Wine drew out resentments that Cleitus had kept to himself and stripped away the restraint of both men, turning a feast into a tragedy that Alexander bitterly regretted.
The kural warns that the moment a man drinks, whatever he has hidden in his heart comes out all the more, as Cleitus's long-held grievances burst out at the drinking party.
