Kural 656

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

ஈன்றாள் பசிகாண்பான் ஆயினுஞ் செய்யற்க சான்றோர் பழிக்கும் வினை.
Eendraal pasikaanbaan aayinum seyyarka Saandror pazhikkum vinai.

Meaning

Even if you have to watch your own mother go hungry, do not do anything that wise people would call wrong.

Elementary

Suppose someone you love very much needs help. You would want to do anything for them, but stealing or lying still isn’t the right way. The Kural says even love for your own mother doesn’t make a wrong deed right. You must find an honest way to help.

University

This is the strongest statement in the chapter. Īṉṟāḷ, ‘she who bore (you)’, is the mother; pacikāṇpāṉ āyiṉum means ‘even if he should (have to) see her hunger’. Tamil ethics counts care of parents among the highest duties, so the verse deliberately picks the hardest case: not even that duty justifies deeds the cāṉṟōr (people of perfected character) condemn. Parimelazhagar and the modern commentators read the line without exception clauses, and Kalaignar sharpens it to ‘not even to relieve’ her suffering. This contrasts with the Sanskrit doctrine of āpad-dharma (law in emergencies), where the Mahabharata allows the sage Vishvamitra, in famine, to steal dog’s flesh to survive. The Kural here refuses that concession. The text is identical across sources apart from the sandhi form பசிகாண்பா னாயினுஞ் in thirukkural-thamizh.org. (Solomon Pappaiah’s printed gloss has பெற்றவனின், apparently a slip for பெற்றவளின்.)

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Kaikeyi demands Rama’s exile, his mother Kausalya begs him with tears to stay, yet Rama refuses to break his father’s pledged word, choosing dharma over his mother’s grief and his own claim to the throne.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (Rama and Kausalya) [4]
Rama feels his mother’s anguish deeply but holds that truth and duty cannot be given up even for her.
Like the Kural, the epic makes a mother’s suffering the hardest test, and has dharma win.
Christianity
“And not rather, (as we be slanderously reported, and as some affirm that we say,) Let us do evil, that good may come? whose damnation is just.”
— Romans 3:8 (KJV) [5]
Paul flatly rejects the idea that a good end can justify an evil act.
This is the principle behind the Kural: not even the good of feeding one’s mother permits a condemned deed.
Islam
“O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives.”
— Quran 4:135 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran requires justice even when it goes against one’s own parents and closest kin.
Both texts say that love for parents, however sacred, cannot override the demand of right action.
Judaism
The Talmud rules that for three sins, idolatry, forbidden sexual relations and murder, a person must let himself be killed rather than transgress, even to save his own life.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 74a [7]
Although saving life normally overrides almost every commandment, the rabbis set limits that not even survival can cross.
Like the Kural, the Talmud names deeds so condemned that no desperate need, not even life, can justify them.
Buddhism
“He is indeed virtuous, wise, and righteous who neither for his own sake nor for the sake of another (does any wrong), who does not crave for sons, wealth, or kingdom, and does not desire success by unjust means.”
— Dhammapada 84 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada defines the wise person as one who will not do wrong even on behalf of others.
‘Nor for the sake of another’ is the principle of the Kural, which uses one’s own mother as the hardest example.
Jainism
“'Mother, father, daughter-in-law, brother, wife, and sons will not be able to help me, when I suffer for my own deeds.'”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com); the verse recurs at Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.9.5 [9]
Jainism teaches that each soul bears the fruit of its own karma alone, and no relative, not even a mother, can share or lift that burden.
The kural forbids deeds the wise condemn even to relieve a starving mother; the Jain verse gives a reason in its own terms, since the doer alone will suffer for the wrong and the mother cannot help him then.
Sikhism
“To take what rightfully belongs to another, is like a Muslim eating pork, or a Hindu eating beef.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh (Majh ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak makes taking another's due (hak paraaia) as abhorrent as the strongest taboo of each community, a line no circumstance excuses.
The Kural says one must not do what the wise condemn even if it means watching one's own mother go hungry; Guru Nanak likewise treats wrongful gain as absolutely forbidden.
Greek Mythology
Stranded and starving on Thrinacia, Odysseus’ crew, urged on by Eurylochus, slaughter the forbidden cattle of Helios; Zeus wrecks their ship and all perish except Odysseus, who alone refused to eat.
— Homer, Odyssey 12.260-419 [11]
Hunger seemed to justify breaking a divine prohibition, but the forbidden meal destroyed those who ate it.
The episode shows the Kural’s point: dire hunger does not make a condemned deed safe or right.
Philosophy
Socrates argues against Polus that doing wrong is worse than suffering it, so that if he had to choose, he would rather suffer injustice than commit it.
— Plato, Gorgias 469b-c [12]
For Socrates, wrongdoing harms the soul of the doer, which is a worse evil than any loss he could suffer.
The Kural reaches the same conclusion: better to bear the pain of want than to do a deed the wise condemn.

Real Life Example

The plant scientists of Leningrad guard the seed bank while starving
1941-1944 · Leningrad (St Petersburg), Soviet Union
During the siege of Leningrad, staff of the plant-breeding institute founded by Nikolai Vavilov stayed on to protect its collection of about a quarter of a million seed samples, guarding it against rats, looters and their own hunger. Several of them, including the groundnut specialist Aleksandr Shchukin and the head of the rice section, died of starvation at the institute while surrounded by seeds and grain they had refused to eat. Most of the central collection survived the siege, and seeds from it were later used to breed crops grown on a large share of Russia's farmland.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The scientists believed that eating the collection, the irreplaceable work of many lives, would be a betrayal of their duty to the future, and they held to that even in the most extreme hunger.
The kural says not to do what the wise condemn even if one must watch one's own mother go hungry, and the Leningrad botanists kept to their duty even at the cost of their own lives by starvation.
