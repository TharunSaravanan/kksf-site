Kural 25

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

ஐந்தவித்தான் ஆற்றல் அகல்விசும்பு ளார்கோமான் இந்திரனே சாலுங் கரி.
Aindhaviththaan aatral akalvisumbu ulaarkoman, Indhirane saalung kari.

Meaning

Indra, the king of the gods in heaven, is proof enough of how powerful a person becomes by controlling the desires of the five senses.

Elementary

Imagine the strongest king in the world admitting that someone else is stronger than him. That would settle the argument, wouldn't it? This kural says the king of the sky-world himself is the living proof of how powerful a person becomes when they put out the fire of wanting.

University

“Aindhavith-thaan” is built on avi-, to extinguish or quench, so the ascetic has not merely restrained the five but put them out, as one puts out a lamp. Against this Valluvar sets a legal metaphor: Indra, “akal visumbu ulaar koman,” king of those in the wide sky, is the “kari” — the witness, the one whose testimony settles a case. The choice of Indra is doubly pointed. In Puranic narrative Indra's own sovereignty was won by tapas, so his throne is itself an exhibit of what austerity produces; and Indra is famously the god who panics when a human ascetic's penance grows strong enough to threaten his seat, which makes his testimony that of a reluctant expert witness. Some commentators, including Parimelazhagar, also read a darker allusion to the Ahalya episode, in which Indra's own failure to quench desire cost him everything — in which case the god testifies not by his glory but by his fall. Notably, the Kural cites Indra only as evidence, never as an object of worship.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana's account of Vishvamitra, the king-turned-ascetic's austerities grow so formidable that Indra, fearing for his own throne, sends the apsara Menaka to break the sage's concentration — and the heavenly court's alarm becomes the measure of what the ascetic had achieved.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda (the Vishvamitra and Menaka episode, sargas 63-64) [4]
The recurring Puranic pattern is that the gods intervene against human tapas not out of malice but out of well-founded fear: accumulated austerity is a real force that can rival theirs.
This is precisely the Kural's evidentiary move — Indra's own conduct is what proves the power of the man who has quenched the five.
Christianity
“Howbeit this kind goeth not out but by prayer and fasting.”
— Matthew 17:21 (KJV) [5]
Jesus explains his disciples' failure to cast out a spirit by pointing to a power that only sustained prayer and fasting confer — asceticism is presented as producing real, verifiable capacity.
Like the Kural, it treats ascetic self-denial as the source of a demonstrable power rather than as mere piety.
Islam
“By Him in Whose Hands my soul is, the smell coming out from the mouth of a fasting person is better in the sight of Allah than the smell of musk. (Allah says about the fasting person), ‘He has left his food, drink and desires for My sake. The fast is for Me. So I will reward (the fasting person) for it and the reward of good deeds is multiplied ten times.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1894 (Book of Fasting) [6]
In this hadith God Himself speaks about the faster, claiming the fast as His own and declaring that the physical evidence of hunger is more pleasing to Him than perfume.
As in the Kural, it is the highest authority in the heavens who furnishes the testimony about what self-denial is worth.
Judaism
The Talmud reports that a heavenly voice went out each day declaring that the whole world is sustained on account of Hanina ben Dosa — a sage so poor that he lived on a measure of carob pods from one Sabbath eve to the next.
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 17b [7]
The passage deliberately juxtaposes extreme material deprivation with cosmic significance, and puts the assessment in the mouth of a voice from heaven rather than of admiring humans.
A heavenly authority testifying to the power of a man who has renounced appetite is the Kural's exact structure.
Buddhism
“Even the gods hold dear the wise one, whose senses are subdued like horses well trained by a charioteer, whose pride is destroyed and who is free from the cankers.”
— Dhammapada 94, Arahantavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's chapter on the perfected states plainly that the arahant's standing is recognised by the devas themselves, who occupy a lower rung than the liberated human.
Same claim, same witnesses: the gods' regard is offered as the proof of what sense-mastery accomplishes.
Jainism
“Throwing off the guise of a Brahmana, and making visible his true form, Sakra saluted him respectfully and praised him with these sweet words” … “Here (on earth) you are the highest man, Reverend sir, and hereafter you will be the highest”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.6-58, esp. 9.55-59 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
When King Nami renounces his throne, Sakra (Indra), king of the gods, comes disguised as a Brahmin to test him with arguments for worldly duty. When Nami's resolve holds, Indra shows his true form, praises Nami for having conquered anger, pride, delusion and greed, and circles him in reverence.
The Kural names Indra as the witness to the power of the one who has quenched the five senses. In the Uttaradhyayana Indra literally takes that role, first testing the renouncer and then publicly testifying to his supremacy.
Sikhism
“Ahalyaa was the wife of Gautam the seer. Seeing her, Indra was enticed. When he received a thousand marks of disgrace on his body, then he felt regret in his mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1344, Raag Prabhati, Dakhni, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak retells the Puranic story of Indra, who failed to master desire and was disgraced for it. He uses it to show that only those who accept God's hukam stay free of such falls.
Some commentators, Parimelazhagar among them, read the Kural's Indra as a witness through his fall. Guru Nanak calls Indra up in exactly that role, as proof of what unmastered desire costs.
Greek Mythology
Heracles, after a life of imposed labours and endurance, is burned free of his mortal part on Mount Oeta and taken up to Olympus, where the gods who had set him his trials receive him as one of their own.
— Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.7.7 [11]
Greek myth's one clear case of a human ascending to divine rank makes endurance and hardship, rather than birth, the qualification — and the Olympians' acceptance is what certifies it.
Like Indra's testimony in the Kural, the divine court's own recognition is what establishes that a human being's self-conquest has reached superhuman force.
Philosophy
Alexander the Great, at the height of his power, sought out Diogenes the Cynic sunning himself and asked what he could do for him; Diogenes asked him only to stop blocking the sun, and Alexander afterwards told his companions that if he were not Alexander he would wish to be Diogenes.
— Plutarch, Life of Alexander 14; cf. Diogenes Laertius 6.32 [12]
The most powerful man in the world publicly concedes that the man who owns nothing occupies the more enviable position, and the concession is what gives the anecdote its force.
This is the Kural's evidentiary logic transposed to the human plane: the king himself is the sufficient witness to the ascetic's superior power.

Real Life Example

Emperor Akbar honours the hermit Salim Chishti at Sikri
1569-1572 · Sikri (Fatehpur Sikri), Uttar Pradesh, India
In 1568-69 the Mughal emperor Akbar visited the Sufi hermit Shaikh Salim Chishti, who lived on the ridge at Sikri, and Chishti foretold that Akbar would have an heir. Akbar's son Salim, the future emperor Jahangir, was born at Sikri in 1569. Akbar then moved his court there and built Fatehpur Sikri, whose Jama Masjid, completed in 1571-72, came to enclose the saint's tomb.
— Sources: [13] [14]
One of the most powerful emperors in the world built an entire capital beside a hermit's dwelling, a striking public acknowledgement of an ascetic's standing.
The kural says Indra, king of the heavens, is witness to the power of one who has quenched the senses, and here an earthly emperor bore that witness by building his capital in honour of a hermit.
