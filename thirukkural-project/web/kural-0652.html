Kural 652

Purity in Execution · Vinaithooymai · வினைத்தூய்மை

என்றும் ஒருவுதல் வேண்டும் புகழொடு நன்றி பயவா வினை.
Endrum oruvudhal vendum pugazhodu Nandri payavaa vinai.

Meaning

Always stay away from any action that brings neither a good name nor any real good.

Elementary

Before you do something, ask two questions: will people be proud of me for this, and will it truly help? If the answer to both is no, don’t do it, even if it looks easy or tempting.

University

The couplet gives the test that runs through the whole chapter: a deed must yield pukaḻ (fame, good name) together with naṉṟi (benefit, goodness), and a deed that yields neither is to be avoided (oruvutal, ‘setting aside, shunning’) at all times (eṉṟum). Parimelazhagar and later commentators such as Solomon Pappaiah read the pair as fame in this life and merit (aṟam) for the life to come, so a clean act must be both publicly honourable and morally fruitful. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses the rejected deeds as those ‘without purity’ (tūymai aṟṟa), tying the verse to the chapter title. The prose explanation printed with Pope’s translation (‘acts which, in addition to fame, yield no benefit’) reads pukaḻoṭu slightly differently, but all readings agree that honour alone is not enough without real good. The text is stable across all three sources consulted.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu tells the householder to avoid the pursuit of wealth and pleasure whenever they conflict with dharma, the sacred law.
— Manusmriti 4.176 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
The lawgiver ranks artha (wealth) and kāma (desire) below dharma and tells the householder to give up any gain that goes against it.
Like the Kural, Manu rules out any act that does not also serve the good, however profitable it looks.
Christianity
“whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things.”
— Philippians 4:8 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells the Philippians to fix their minds on what is both morally good and of good report.
Paul’s pairing of virtue and praise mirrors the Kural’s pairing of naṉṟi and pukaḻ.
Islam
“Wealth and children are [but] adornment of the worldly life. But the enduring good deeds are better to your Lord for reward and better for [one’s] hope.”
— Quran 18:46 (Sahih International) [6]
The Quran sets passing worldly gains against good deeds whose worth lasts.
The Kural too tells the doer to choose only deeds whose good lasts beyond the moment.
Judaism
“which is the straight path that a man should choose for himself? One which is an honor to the person adopting it, and [on account of which] honor [accrues] to him from others.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Rabbi Judah the Prince defines the right path as one that is honourable in itself and also brings honour from other people.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s double test: a deed must be good in itself and bring an honourable name.
Buddhism
In the Vanijja Sutta the Buddha names five trades a lay follower should not practise: dealing in weapons, in living beings, in meat, in intoxicants and in poisons.
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.177 (Vanijja Sutta) [8]
These livelihoods may be profitable, but they cause harm, so a disciple is told to give them up entirely.
Like the Kural, the sutta rules out whole kinds of action because they bring no real good, however much they earn.
Jainism
“An intelligent man who has learned (the sacred texts) takes his duties upon himself, and he becomes renowned in the world; as the earth is the dwelling of all beings, so he will be a dwelling of all duties.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.45 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The lecture on discipline presents renown as the natural result of taking up one's duties fully and rightly.
The kural requires a deed to bring both good name and real good; the Jain verse joins renown to faithful performance of duty in the same way.
Sikhism
“Fareed, those deeds which do not bring merit - forget about those deeds.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1381, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, tells the seeker simply to drop any action that yields no good.
The Kural says one must always give up any deed that brings neither honour nor real good; Farid gives almost the same rule.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod warns his brother Perses that the road to badness is smooth and close at hand, while the gods have set sweat before goodness, on a road that is long and steep at first but easy once the top is reached.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 287-292 [11]
The poet urges choosing the harder path that leads to real worth over the easy one that leads to ruin.
The Kural too asks for the discipline to turn down easy acts that bring no honour and no good.
Philosophy
Cicero argues that nothing can truly be expedient (utile) that is not also morally right (honestum), and that the appearance of profit in a wrong act is always a delusion.
— Cicero, De Officiis 3.7-3.19 [12]
Book 3 of On Duties is built on the claim that the useful and the honourable can never really conflict.
Cicero’s refusal to separate profit from honour matches the Kural’s demand for deeds that bring both good name and good.

Real Life Example

Lise Meitner refuses to work on the atomic bomb
1942 · Stockholm, Sweden
Lise Meitner, the Austrian physicist who with her nephew Otto Frisch first explained nuclear fission in 1939, was living in exile in Sweden when she was invited in 1942 to join the Manhattan Project. She refused, declaring that she would have nothing to do with a bomb. After the war she continued to avoid any connection between her research and the atomic bomb, and when Otto Hahn alone received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for fission, her own contribution went unrecognised by the prize.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Meitner turned away from the most consequential scientific project of the war because she judged the deed itself to be one she would not take part in, whatever prestige it might bring.
The kural says one must always give up a deed that brings neither honour nor real good, and Meitner, judging bomb-making to be such a deed, refused it outright.
