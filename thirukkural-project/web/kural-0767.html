Kural 767

The Excellence of an Army · Padaimaatchi · படைமாட்சி

தார்தாங்கிச் செல்வது தானை தலைவந்த போர்தாங்கும் தன்மை அறிந்து.
Thaarthaangich chelvadhu thaanai thalaivandha poarthaangum thanmai arindhu.

Meaning

A true army knows how to stop an attack, and it moves forward by pushing back the front line of the enemy.

Elementary

In a game of tug-of-war, the strongest kids stand at the front where the pull is hardest, and they know how to dig their heels in. Valluvar says a good army is like that: it knows how to take the first hard push and keep moving forward.

University

Tār (தார்) is the vanguard, called tūci (தூசி) in the commentaries, which Pope rendered ‘dust-van’ after the dust raised by an advancing force; tāṉai (தானை) is the army. Parimelazhagar reads the couplet as describing an army that, knowing the art of checking an oncoming assault (talaivanta pōr tāṅkum taṉmai), meets and bears the enemy’s vanguard. Solomon Pappaiah reads tār more broadly as the front-line foot soldiers, while Sivayogi Sivakumar takes it as an image of ranks arrayed like a banana bunch, an idiosyncratic reading. Both texts agree (சந்தி: போர்தாங்குந் தன்மை யறிந்து).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Now all of you must give full support to Grandfather Bhīṣma, standing at your respective strategic points in the phalanx of the army.”
— Bhagavad Gita 1.11 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Duryodhana orders his commanders to hold their positions in the formation so that the line is not broken.
Holding one’s place in the array to meet the oncoming assault is what the Kural asks of a true army.
Christianity
The apostle Peter urges believers to be watchful because the adversary prowls like a lion, and to resist him steadfast in the faith.
— 1 Peter 5:8–9 [4]
The faithful are told to stand their ground against an attack rather than give way.
Meeting the oncoming assault and withstanding it is the Kural’s description of a true army.
Islam
At Uhud the Prophet stationed a company of archers on a hill and ordered them not to leave their post; when most of them left it, the Muslim line was turned, and the Qur’an refers to this disobedience and dispute.
— Qur’an 3:152 and the sira accounts of Uhud [5]
The episode shows that the outcome depended on holding the assigned position against the enemy’s charge.
It illustrates by contrast the Kural’s army that knows how to bear the enemy’s attack.
Judaism
“And they stood every man in his place round about the camp; and all the host ran; and they shouted, and fled.”
— Judges 7:21 (JPS 1917, Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Gideon’s three hundred hold their positions around the Midianite camp, and the vast host breaks and flees.
Each man holding his place as the enemy surges is the Kural’s army that bears the assault and advances.
Buddhism
“There is the case of a warrior who, on seeing a cloud of dust [stirred up by the enemy army], falters, faints, doesn’t steel himself, can’t engage in the battle.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.75, Yodhajiva Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha ranks warriors by how far they can withstand the stages of an enemy advance, from the dust cloud to hand-to-hand combat.
The best warrior who bears the ‘cloud of dust’ is exactly the Kural’s army that bears the dust-van of the foe.
Jainism
“Suffering from insects a great sage remains undisturbed. As an elephant at the head of the battle kills the enemy, so does a hero (in self-control conquer the internal foe).”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the list of the twenty-two hardships, the monk who stands firm under stinging insects is compared to a war-elephant at the front of the battle. The next verse forbids him to harm even the insects that bite him (2.11).
The Kural's army meets and takes the full weight of the enemy's vanguard, and the Jain hero stands at the 'head of the battle' and bears the assault, though his enemy is inside him.
Sikhism
During the first siege of Anandpur on 1 September 1700, the hill chiefs sent a drunken, armour-plated elephant to batter down the gate of Lohgarh Fort. Guru Gobind Singh chose Bhai Bachittar Singh to meet it; he rode out and drove his spear through the plate into its forehead, and the wounded elephant turned back into the besiegers' ranks.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, 'Bachittar Singh, Bhai (d. 1705)', citing Gurbilas Patshahi 10 (paraphrase) [9]
The episode is celebrated in Sikh tradition as a single warrior meeting the head of the enemy's assault and turning it back.
The Kural's true army knows how to withstand the attack and bears the brunt of the enemy's vanguard; Bachittar Singh met the very spearhead of the assault at the gate and broke it.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Nestor arrays the Pylians with charioteers in front, a mass of brave foot soldiers behind as a bulwark, and the less reliable men placed in the middle so that they must fight.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 4 [10]
The wise old king shows how an army’s arrangement lets it absorb and repel an attack.
Knowing how to bear the oncoming assault through good order is the Kural’s point.
Philosophy
During the retreat of the Ten Thousand, the Greeks formed a hollow square with baggage in the centre and reorganised their rearguard so that they could keep marching while bearing the Persians’ harassing attacks.
— Xenophon, Anabasis 3.2–3.4 [11]
Xenophon describes the practical skill of an army that advances while absorbing pressure.
It is the Kural’s army that ‘knows how to bear the attack’ and keeps going.

Real Life Example

The 93rd Highlanders' Thin Red Line at Balaklava
25 October 1854 · Balaklava, Crimean Peninsula
During the Battle of Balaklava a body of Russian cavalry advanced toward the British base, and the 93rd Highlanders, about 500 men under Sir Colin Campbell, stood in its path. Instead of forming the usual four-deep square, Campbell drew them up in a line two deep, and their disciplined volleys turned the Russian horsemen back. The Times correspondent William Howard Russell's description of them gave rise to the phrase the thin red line.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single steady regiment took the full weight of the enemy's leading charge and stopped it.
The kural praises an army that knows how to withstand an oncoming attack and bears the brunt of the enemy's vanguard, which is precisely what the 93rd did at Balaklava.
