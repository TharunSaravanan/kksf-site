Kural 698

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

இளையர் இனமுறையர் என்றிகழார் நின்ற ஒளியோடு ஒழுகப் படும்.
Ilaiyar inamuraiyar endru ikazhaar nindra Oliyodu ozhukap padum.

Meaning

Do not look down on the king, thinking 'he is younger than me' or 'he is my relative.' Act with respect for the high place he holds.

Elementary

Suppose your older cousin becomes the team captain. At family dinners they are just your cousin, but on the field you listen to them as captain. The job deserves respect, whoever is doing it.

University

The couplet names two excuses for disrespect: ilaiyar (‘he is our junior’) and inamuraiyar (‘he stands in such-and-such a kinship relation to us’). Ikazhaar forbids the contempt that comes from both. The key phrase is nindra oli, ‘the radiance that abides [in him]’. Parimelazhagar interprets it as the divine light or majesty present in the king, and Pope accordingly translates ‘the glory kingly state doth bring’. Modern readers such as Solomon Pappaiah reduce it to respect for the office (padhavi) rather than the person. The idea that royal dignity belongs to the office, not the age or family of its holder, closely matches Manusmriti 7.8. The couplet is also a warning to royal kinsmen, whose familiarity is the most tempting route to contempt.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Even an infant king must not be despised, (from an idea) that he is a (mere) mortal; for he is a great deity in human form.”
— Manusmriti 7.8 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu teaches that a king’s youth is no reason to disrespect him, because kingship itself carries divine majesty.
This is almost the Kural’s own claim. ‘He is young’ is no excuse, because of the radiance (oli) that resides in the king.
Christianity
“Let no man despise thy youth; but be thou an example of the believers, in word, in conversation, in charity, in spirit, in faith, in purity.”
— 1 Timothy 4:12 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the young church leader Timothy that his youth should not be used to discount his authority.
Paul, like the Kural, rejects ‘he is young’ as a reason to despise someone who holds authority.
Islam
“O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you.”
— Quran 4:59 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Quran commands obedience to those who hold rightful authority, grounding it in the office rather than personal qualities.
Like the Kural, it directs respect to ‘those in authority’ as such, whatever their age or relation to us.
Judaism
“The LORD forbid that I should do this thing unto my master, the LORD’S anointed, to stretch forth mine hand against him, seeing he is the anointed of the LORD.”
— 1 Samuel 24:6 (KJV) [4]
David, hunted by King Saul, who is also his father-in-law, has the chance to kill him in a cave. He refuses because Saul still bears the anointing of kingship.
David honours the anointing, the office’s sacred radiance, over his personal relationship and grievance, which is the Kural’s nindra oli.
Buddhism
“The king does not count as a common person, the king must not be paired with anyone else; kings are easily vexed, as the eye is hurt if touched by a barley-awn.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 155 [6]
Vidhura warns courtiers never to treat the king as an equal or an ordinary man.
Both texts forbid bringing the king down to one’s own level, whether as a junior or as family.
Jainism
“If you, fearless at the thought, ‘He is my brother,’ do not come, that is not fitting. Kings, whose essence is command, are not restrained by relationship.”
— Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra 1.5, Part 1, 'Negotiations with Bahubali' (Hemachandra, tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS 1931) [7]
Suvega tells Bahubali that kinship with the Chakravartin is no excuse for failing to honour him as king, because royal authority is not set aside by family ties.
The Kural forbids looking down on the king as 'my relative' or 'younger than me'; Suvega's speech makes the same point that kinship does not cancel the respect owed to the throne.
Sikhism
Amar Das (born 1479) became the disciple of Guru Angad (born 1504), a man twenty-five years his junior, and after Guru Angad's death succeeded him as the third Guru at the age of 73.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Amar Das and Guru Angad (dates and succession) [8]
Sikh tradition remembers Amar Das for years of humble service to his younger Guru, to whose family he was also related by marriage, treating him with full reverence as Guru.
The Kural forbids slighting a ruler on the grounds that 'he is younger than me' or 'he is my relative', and asks respect for the dignity of the office; Amar Das honoured a younger Guru, and a kinsman, entirely for the light of the office he held.
Greek Mythology
When Achilles quarrels with Agamemnon, old Nestor tells him not to strive against a sceptred king. Achilles may be the stronger fighter and the son of a goddess, but the king whom Zeus has honoured holds the greater rank.
— Homer, Iliad 1.275-284 (Nestor to Achilles) [9]
Nestor argues that personal superiority in strength and birth does not cancel the honour that belongs to the king’s office.
The Kural says the same: personal factors such as age or family do not outrank the dignity of the throne.
Philosophy
“Duties are universally measured by relations. … In this manner, therefore, you will find, from the idea of a neighbor, a citizen, a general, the corresponding duties if you accustom yourself to contemplate the several relations.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion, ch. 30 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus teaches that what we owe someone follows from the role or relation in which they stand to us, such as father, brother, citizen or general, and not from what we think of them personally.
In the same way the Kural asks that the king be treated according to the office he holds, not according to his age or his kinship with oneself.

Real Life Example

Peter the Great removes his half-sister Sophia from power
1689 · Moscow, Russia
Sophia Alekseyevna ruled Russia as regent from 1682 for her brother Ivan V and her younger half-brother Peter, who were proclaimed joint tsars, and she used grand royal titles while the boy Peter lived away from the Kremlin. In July 1689 the seventeen-year-old Peter clashed with her publicly in Moscow's Kazan Cathedral, and when Sophia tried to turn the streltsy against his family, many of their colonels supported Peter instead. In September 1689 Peter overthrew her, she was denied her royal titles, and she was confined in the Novodevichy Convent in Moscow.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Sophia had treated the crowned tsar as a junior relative to be managed, and the young ruler's authority asserted itself the moment he came of age.
The kural warns against slighting a ruler because he is younger or a relative, and Sophia lost everything by treating the tsar as merely her younger half-brother.
