Kural 394

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

உவப்பத் தலைக்கூடி உள்ளப் பிரிதல் அனைத்தே புலவர் தொழில்.
Uvappath thalaikkoodi ullap piridhal Anaiththe pulavar thozhil.

Meaning

This is the sign of learned people: others are happy to meet them, and when they leave, others keep thinking of them and want to meet them again.

Elementary

Think of a teacher or grandparent whose stories you love. When they come, you are happy. When they leave, you wish they could stay longer. Valluvar says that is how learned people make everyone feel.

University

Here Valluvar moves from the value of learning to its social effect. The புலவர் (pulavar, the learned or poet-scholar) is defined by an experience they create in others: உவப்ப, gladness at meeting, and உள்ளப் பிரிதல், parting in a way that leaves people thinking of them. The word தொழில் (thozhil, occupation or characteristic work) suggests that bringing joy through conversation is the scholar's real 'profession'. Commentators such as Mu. Varadarasan gloss the second half as others wondering when they will see such a person again. The kural implicitly contrasts true learning, which is generous and delightful, with pedantry that makes people glad to leave. Greek tradition offers vivid parallels, from Odysseus spellbinding the Phaeacians to Alcibiades' account of being enchanted by Socrates' words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhaja Govindam, Adi Shankaracharya teaches that the company of the good (satsanga) leads to non-attachment, then freedom from delusion, then steadiness of mind, and finally liberation.
— Adi Shankaracharya, Bhaja Govindam, verse 9 [3]
The tradition of satsang holds that simply being in the company of wise people transforms and uplifts the listener.
Like the Kural, it treats time with the learned as a treasured, transforming experience. Shankara stresses spiritual effect rather than delight and longing.
Christianity
“Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend.”
— Proverbs 27:17 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs describes how the meeting of wise minds leaves each person brighter and sharper.
Like the Kural, it describes the benefit of meeting a wise person. The Proverb's emphasis is mutual improvement, while the Kural's is delight and longing.
Islam
“The example of a good pious companion and an evil one is that of a person carrying musk and another blowing a pair of bellows.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5534 [5]
The Prophet compares a good companion to a perfume seller. Even if you buy nothing, you leave carrying a pleasant fragrance.
The musk image matches the Kural well: time with a good, learned person leaves something pleasant that stays with you after you part.
Judaism
“If two sit together and there are words of Torah [spoken] between them, then the Shekhinah abides among them.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:2 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Hananiah ben Teradion teaches that when people meet and share words of learning, the Divine Presence rests among them.
Like the Kural, it makes a learned meeting a blessed, uplifting event. It focuses on sacred presence rather than personal delight.
Buddhism
“To be patient and obedient, to associate with monks and to have religious discussions on due occasions — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Mangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.4 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Mangala Sutta counts meeting and discussing the Dhamma with learned, disciplined people among life's highest blessings.
Like the Kural, it treats meeting the learned as a real joy and blessing.
Jainism
“Sramanas, skilled in the interpretation of sacred texts and rich in moral discipline, austerities and right knowledge, should be welcomed with a stand-up, should be served and be bowed down to by other ascetics.”
— Pravacanasara III.263 (Kundakunda, tr. A.N. Upadhye; jainworld.com) [8]
Kundakunda tells monks to rise to their feet, serve and bow when a learned ascetic arrives. Meeting someone rich in knowledge is treated as an occasion for warm and respectful welcome.
Valluvar describes the learned as those whose company gladdens others and whose parting leaves people longing to meet again. The Jain rule of joyful, reverent welcome expresses the same regard, although it does not mention the longing after they leave.
Sikhism
“The sight of them banishes my evil-mindedness; they are my only true friends.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 520, Raag Gujri (Gujri ki Vaar, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Gujri ki Vaar Guru Arjan says his true friends are those whose very presence drives away his bad thoughts.
The Kural defines the learned by the gladness people feel on meeting them and the longing they feel on parting; Guru Arjan likewise names his true friends by the good their mere presence works in him.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus pauses in telling his adventures at the Phaeacian court, his audience sits spellbound in silence. King Alcinous begs him to go on, saying they would listen until dawn.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.333–376 [10]
Odysseus' learning, gained through long travel and hardship, makes his company so delightful that his hosts cannot bear to let him stop.
This fits the Kural closely: the listeners rejoice in his company and do not want it to end.
Philosophy
In Plato's Symposium, Alcibiades says that when Socrates speaks, listeners are overwhelmed and enchanted. He compares Socrates to the satyr Marsyas, whose music held everyone spellbound, except that Socrates does it with bare words.
— Plato, Symposium 215b–216c [11]
Alcibiades testifies that Socrates' conversation stays in the soul long after it ends and keeps drawing him back.
Like the Kural's pulavar, Socrates makes others glad to meet him and unable to forget him afterward.

Real Life Example

Einstein's lecture tour of Japan
November-December 1922 · Kobe, Tokyo, Kyoto and other cities, Japan
At the invitation of the publisher Kaizosha, Albert Einstein toured Japan from 17 November 1922, having learned during the voyage that he had been awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics. Crowds and journalists met him at Kobe, and audiences packed auditoriums in several cities to hear him lecture on relativity for three or four hours at a time through an interpreter. At Keio University in Tokyo on 19 November about 2,000 people filled the lecture hall.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The tour shows how a great scholar's presence and talk could draw and delight ordinary people far beyond the circle of specialists.
The kural says the mark of the learned is that people rejoice to meet them and think of them after they part, and Japan's welcome to Einstein shows that gladness.
