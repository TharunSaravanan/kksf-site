Kural 179

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

அறனறிந்து வெஃகா அறிவுடையார்ச் சேரும் திறன்அறிந் தாங்கே திரு.
Aranarindhu vehkaa arivudaiyaarch serum Thiranarin thaange thiru.

Meaning

The goddess of good fortune knows the worth of wise people who understand what is right and do not want others' things, and she finds her own way to them.

Elementary

Imagine good luck as a friend who likes visiting kind, honest people. This kural says if you know what’s right and don’t try to take other people’s things, good fortune will find its way to your door by itself.

University

திரு (thiru), glossed by all traditional commentators as Lakshmi (Thirumagal), the goddess of fortune, is here personified as an agent with judgment: திறன் அறிந்து (thiran arindhu) means she ‘knows the proper way’ or ‘recognises their merit’ and arrives ஆங்கே (aange, ‘right there’). The kural thus reverses the covetous logic: the coveter reaches for wealth and loses it, while the one who does not reach is sought out by wealth itself. Parimelazhagar’s gloss emphasises that these people are அறிவுடையார் (arivudaiyaar) who know aram, so their restraint is a considered moral choice, not mere passivity. The idea that virtue attracts prosperity has close analogues in the Gita’s closing verse (where ‘sri’ accompanies righteousness), Socrates’ ‘from virtue come money and every other good’, and the Buddhist teaching that the righteous non-coveter may reappear among those of ‘great property’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Wherever there is Krsna, the master of all mystics, and wherever there is Arjuna, the supreme archer, there will also certainly be opulence, victory, extraordinary power, and morality.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.78 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita ends with Sanjaya declaring that where righteousness is, there Sri (fortune) will be too.
Both texts say Sri, fortune, goes to the side of righteousness; the Gita even uses the same goddess-word.
Christianity
“But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.”
— Matthew 6:33 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his listeners to seek righteousness first rather than chase material things, and those things will be given to them.
The Kural’s reversal: don’t grasp at wealth, pursue virtue, and provision will come to you.
Islam
“And will provide for him from where he does not expect. And whoever relies upon Allah - then He is sufficient for him.”
— Qur’an 65:2-3 (Sahih International; 65:3 quoted) [5]
Continuing 65:2 (‘whoever fears Allah, He will make for him a way out’), the Qur’an promises that the God-conscious will be provided for from unexpected sources.
As in the Kural, provision comes to the righteous of its own accord rather than through their grasping.
Judaism
“The blessing of the LORD, it maketh rich, and he addeth no sorrow with it.”
— Proverbs 10:22 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs says real wealth comes from God’s blessing, and that kind of wealth carries no sorrow with it.
Both texts say prosperity is given to the righteous rather than seized by them.
Buddhism
“Here someone is not covetous: he is not a coveter of another’s chattels and property thus: ‘Oh, that what is another’s were mine!’” … “If a householder who observes conduct in accordance with the Dhamma, righteous conduct, should wish: ‘Oh, that … I might reappear in the company of the warrior-nobles of great property!’ it is possible…”
— Majjhima Nikaya 41, Saleyyaka Sutta (tr. Ñanamoli) [6]
The Buddha names non-covetousness as part of righteous conduct and says such a righteous householder can gain rebirth among people of great wealth.
Exactly the Kural’s pairing: the one who does not covet another’s property is the one to whom prosperity comes.
Jainism
“After King Nami refused to multiply his treasury, Śakra (Indra) dropped his disguise and praised him: “Bravo! you have conquered anger; bravo! you have vanquished pride; bravo! you have banished delusion; bravo! you have subdued greed.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.55–56 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
The king of the gods, who had tested Nami by urging wealth and conquest on him, comes in his true form to honour the one who desired nothing, then pays him reverence and departs.
Valluvar pictures Fortune seeking out the wise who do not covet, and in the Jain story it is the king of gods himself who comes to honour the sage who has subdued greed.
Sikhism
“Maya is the slave of the Lord's devotees; it works for them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 231, Raag Gauree, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das teaches that worldly wealth, which enslaves the grasping, comes to serve those who are devoted to God.
The Kural says fortune seeks out, of her own accord, the wise who do not covet; Guru Amar Das says worldly wealth becomes the servant of the devoted.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor elderly couple, gladly shared their meagre food with disguised gods whom richer neighbours had turned away. Zeus and Hermes turned their hut into a temple, made them its priests, and granted their wish to die together.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 [9]
A poor couple who didn’t grasp at anything, and shared what little they had, were richly rewarded by the gods.
Like the Kural’s Thiru, divine favour sought out the humble and virtuous rather than those who grasped.
Philosophy
“I tell you that virtue is not given by money, but that from virtue come money and every other good of man, public as well as private.”
— Plato, Apology 30b (tr. Jowett) [10]
Socrates says virtue doesn’t come from wealth; it is the other way round, and every good, including wealth, comes from virtue.
The Kural’s claim in philosophical form: virtue comes first, and fortune follows it.

Real Life Example

American women give Marie Curie a gram of radium
20 May 1921 · The White House, Washington, D.C., United States
Marie Curie had deliberately chosen not to patent radium or its medical uses, and by 1920 its high price left her without enough for her own research in Paris. The journalist Marie Meloney organised a fund among American women that raised the money to buy a gram of radium for her. On 20 May 1921 President Warren G. Harding presented the gift to Curie at the White House on behalf of the women of America.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Curie had refused to profit from her discovery, and the resources she needed came to her unasked through the gratitude of others.
The kural says fortune recognises the worth of those who do not covet and seeks them out of her own accord, and the radium Curie would not profit from came to her as a gift.
