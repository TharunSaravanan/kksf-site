Kural 341

Renunciation · Thuravu · துறவு

யாதனின் யாதனின் நீங்கியான் நோதல் அதனின் அதனின் இலன்.
Yaadhanin yaadhanin neengiyaan nodhal Adhanin adhanin ilan.

Meaning

Whatever a person has let go of, whatever it may be, that thing can no longer cause them pain.

Elementary

If you stop caring about keeping a certain toy, you won't cry when it breaks. This kural says every single thing you stop clinging to is one less thing that can make you sad.

University

The doubled pronouns (yaadhanin yaadhanin … adhanin adhanin, “from whatever, whatever … from that, that”) set up an exact one-to-one ledger: each object released cancels the suffering tied to that particular object. Parimelazhagar reads the couplet as the gradualist justification for renunciation — one need not abandon everything at once to begin reaping relief, since each detachment pays its own dividend; Karunanidhi likewise glosses it as “whichever single attachment one gives up.” The chapter therefore opens not with an ascetic command but with a near-empirical observation about the mechanics of grief: pain travels along the line of attachment. The same causal claim underlies the Buddhist analysis of suffering as arising from clinging (upādāna) and the Stoic insistence that distress comes from our relation to externals rather than from the externals themselves.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who neither hates nor desires the fruits of his activities is known to be always renounced. Such a person, liberated from all dualities, easily overcomes material bondage and is completely liberated, O mighty-armed Arjuna.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.3 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna defines the true renunciate not by outward robes but by an inner state: someone who neither craves nor resents outcomes, and who therefore slips easily out of bondage.
The Kural says what you have let go of can no longer hurt you; the Gita says the one who no longer craves is freed from the bondage that craving creates.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal.”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that earthly treasure is always exposed to decay and theft, and urges his hearers to invest where nothing can be lost.
Both locate vulnerability in what we hold: release the earthly treasure and moth, rust and thief lose their power to wound you.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you. And Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful -”
— Quran 57:23 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran teaches that events are decreed so that believers neither collapse over what they lose nor grow proud over what they gain.
The same equanimity the Kural describes: loss cannot wound a person whose heart is not bound to the thing lost.
Judaism
“Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return thither: the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD.”
— Job 1:21 (KJV) [4]
Stripped in one day of his wealth and his children, Job answers that he arrived with nothing and will leave with nothing, and blesses God.
Job's un-possessive stance lets him face catastrophic loss without collapse — the Kural's principle tested at its most extreme.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha traces grief and fear directly to what we hold dear, and declares that one wholly free of such attachment has no grief at all.
Nearly identical logic to the Kural: grief follows attachment, so where attachment is removed, the grief tied to it is gone.
Jainism
“But a man who is indifferent to ‘colours,’ is free from sorrows; though still in the Saṃsâra, he is not affected by that long succession of pains, just as the leaf of the Lotus (is not moistened) by water.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895, p. 189) [7]
Mahāvīra's sermon on the causes of carelessness goes through the senses one by one. For each object (colours, sounds, smells, tastes, touches, feelings) it shows that passion for it brings pain and indifference to it ends that pain.
Valluvar's doubled 'from whatever, from that' sets up the same one-to-one ledger: each object a person becomes indifferent to stops causing that person pain.
Sikhism
“As long as I walked along, carrying the load, I continued to pay the fine. But I threw away that bundle, when I met the Perfect Guru; O Nanak, then I became fearless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 214, Raag Gauree (Gauree Maalaa), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes attachment to Maya as a bundle carried on the head: as long as it is carried, its penalty is paid, and once it is set down, fear and burden go with it.
The Kural says that from whatever one has let go, one suffers no more pain; Guru Arjan says the same of the bundle he threw down, whose fine he no longer pays.
Greek Mythology
“It would be absurd, if Manes can live without Diogenes, but Diogenes cannot get on without Manes.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 6.55 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [9]
When Diogenes the Cynic's slave Manes ran away and friends urged him to chase him, Diogenes refused with this reply.
Diogenes simply declines to let the loss hurt him, because he never made his contentment depend on Manes — the Kural's point made as a joke.
Philosophy
“Never say of anything, "I have lost it"; but, "I have returned it." Is your child dead? It is returned. Is your wife dead? She is returned. Is your estate taken away? Well, and is not that likewise returned?”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 11 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [10]
Epictetus trains his students to treat everything external as on loan, so that its loss is experienced as a return rather than a theft.
Once a thing is no longer held as 'mine', its departure causes no pain — exactly the Kural's claim.

Real Life Example

Gandhi adopts the loincloth at Madurai
22 September 1921 · Madurai, Tamil Nadu, India
During a tour of South India, Mohandas Gandhi was troubled by the poverty of peasants he saw working in loincloths and by the point that many people were too poor to buy enough khadi to replace the foreign cloth they were being asked to give up. On the night of 22 September 1921 in Madurai he decided to wear only a loincloth, at first until the end of October, and he appeared in it in public in Madurai shortly afterwards. The change became permanent and remained his dress for the rest of his life.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Gandhi gave up his customary clothing one step at a time, having considered and set aside the idea on earlier occasions before acting on it. The loincloth became a lasting symbol of his identification with India's poor.
The kural teaches that each single thing a person lets go of is one less source of suffering, and Gandhi's gradual shedding of possessions, of which the Madurai decision was one step, shows renunciation proceeding item by item.
