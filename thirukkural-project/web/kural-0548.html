Kural 548

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

எண்பதத்தான் ஓரா முறைசெய்யா மன்னவன் தண்பதத்தான் தானே கெடும்.
Enpadhaththaan Oraa Muraiseyyaa Mannavan Thanpadhaththaan Thaane Ketum.

Meaning

A king whom people cannot easily reach, and who does not look into their problems and give fair judgments, will fall low and be ruined, even without an enemy.

Elementary

Imagine a class monitor who locks herself away and never listens when kids come to her with a problem. Problems pile up, everyone gets upset, and soon nobody trusts her. She didn’t need an enemy to lose her job. She lost it all by herself.

University

எண்பதம் means ‘easy access’ (எண் = easy, பதம் = occasion or audience; Varadarasan: எளிய செவ்வி); தண்பதம் means a ‘low state’ (Parimelazhagar: தாழ்ந்த நிலை). The two rhyming compounds frame the couplet. Commentators take the negation of ஓரா (not examining) and முறைசெய்யா (not doing justice) as extending back over எண்பதத்தான், so the king is one who is not accessible, does not investigate, and does not give justice; Pope accordingly renders ‘Hard of access, nought searching out’. Pappaiah keeps the positive reading grammatically but gives the same sense. தானே கெடும் (‘perishes of himself’) is taken by Parimelazhagar and Varadarasan to mean ‘even without enemies’ (பகைவரில்லாமலும்): neglect of justice is self-destruction. Kalaignar departs and reads எண்பதம் as pomp or display. The couplet links accessibility to justice, a theme taken up again in வெருவந்தசெய்யாமை (Ch. 57). The text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Uttara Kanda of the Valmiki Ramayana, Rama tells Lakshmana the story of King Nriga, who left two brahmins disputing over a cow waiting at his palace gate for days without a hearing; in their anger they cursed him to live as a lizard in a pit, and Rama draws the lesson that a king must never keep those seeking justice waiting.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda (the story of Nriga, told by Rama) [3]
A king ruined not by an enemy but by his own failure to give petitioners a hearing.
Nriga is the Kural’s inaccessible king who ‘perishes of himself’.
Christianity
“Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard.”
— Proverbs 21:13 (KJV) [4]
The proverb warns that whoever refuses to hear the poor will one day cry out and not be heard himself.
The ruler who shuts out petitioners brings about his own downfall, as in the Kural.
Islam
“If Allah puts anyone in the position of authority over the affairs of the Muslims, and he secludes himself (from them), not fulfilling their needs, wants, and poverty, Allah will keep Himself away from him, not fulfilling his need, want and poverty.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 2948 (graded sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet warns that a ruler who shuts himself off from his people’s needs will find God shut off from his own; on hearing it, Mu‘awiya appointed a man to attend to people’s needs.
An almost exact counterpart of the Kural’s inaccessible ruler who ruins himself.
Judaism
“See, thy matters are good and right; but there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee.”
— 2 Samuel 15:3 (JPS 1917), with 15:2–6 [6]
Absalom stood at the city gate telling every litigant that King David had no one to hear his case, and so ‘stole the hearts’ of Israel and raised a revolt.
A king’s failure to hear cases nearly costs David his throne, without any foreign enemy, just as the Kural warns.
Buddhism
“Therefore your aim should be to act with impartiality. … Those who are bored with the administration of justice will not be promoted;”
— Ashoka, Kalinga Rock Edict I (trans. S. Dhammika) [7]
Ashoka warns his judicial officers that indifference, laziness and weariness lead to unjust imprisonment, and that those bored with justice will not advance.
Neglecting to examine cases and give justice is the fault the Kural condemns.
Jainism
“A cut-throat enemy will not do him such harm as his own perversity will do him; the man without pity will feel repentance in the hour of death.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The sage tells King Śreṇika that a person's own wrong conduct does more damage than any outside foe, and in the same sermon he says the self is ‘friend and foe’ (20.37).
The Kural's king, inaccessible and failing to do justice, is brought down by his own conduct ‘even without an enemy’, which is exactly the Jain claim that one's own perversity outdoes any enemy.
Sikhism
“The Dark Age of Kali Yuga is the knife, and the kings are butchers; righteousness has sprouted wings and flown away.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 145, Raag Maajh (Majh ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak indicts the rulers of his age, who preyed on their subjects instead of giving them justice.
The Kural says a king who is hard to reach and does not give justice sinks and perishes of himself; Guru Nanak paints that unjust rule, in which righteousness itself has fled.
Greek Mythology
Homer compares the rout of the Trojans to the autumn storm that Zeus sends against men who give crooked judgments in the assembly and drive out justice, heedless of the gods, so that the rivers flood and the works of men are ruined.
— Homer, Iliad 16.384–392 [10]
In Homer, a community’s neglect of justice brings destruction without any human enemy.
Ruin that comes from within, through crooked judgment, is the Kural’s தானே கெடும்.
Philosophy
“An old woman once assailed Demetrius as he was passing by, and demanded many times that he give her a hearing. ‘I have no time,’ said Demetrius. ‘Then don't be king,’ screamed the old woman.”
— Plutarch, Life of Demetrius, ch. 42 (tr. Bernadotte Perrin) [11]
Plutarch says Demetrius was hard to approach, kept embassies waiting for years and threw his subjects' petitions into a river, so that the Macedonians felt insulted rather than ruled; soon afterwards his army deserted him and he lost Macedonia.
The king who cannot be reached and will not hear complaints undoes his own kingdom, exactly as the Kural warns.

Real Life Example

The Wanli Emperor withdraws from government
1580s-1620 · Beijing, China
The Wanli Emperor of the Ming dynasty, who reigned from 1573 to 1620, increasingly withdrew from government after the death of his able Grand Secretary Zhang Juzheng in 1582. For years he neglected daily business, failed to appear at court audiences and refused to read the memorials his officials sent him, while factional quarrels among officials grew and more and more of his responsibilities passed to palace eunuchs. Strained by costly wars and an empty treasury, the Ming dynasty fell in 1644, less than a quarter of a century after his death.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Historians commonly trace the Ming dynasty's decline to Wanli's long withdrawal, when officials could no longer reach the throne and the empire's problems went unresolved.
The kural says a king whom his people cannot reach, and who does not look into their complaints and give justice, sinks low and perishes by his own doing, and the Wanli Emperor's closed court began the ruin of his dynasty.
