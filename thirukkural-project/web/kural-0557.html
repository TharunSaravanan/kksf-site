Kural 557

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

துளியின்மை ஞாலத்திற்கு எற்றற்றே வேந்தன் அளியின்மை வாழும் உயிர்க்கு.
Thuliyinmai gnaalaththirku etratre vendhan Aliyinmai vaazhum uyirkku.

Meaning

A king without kindness is to his people what a lack of rain is to the earth.

Elementary

When it doesn’t rain for a long time, plants wilt, rivers shrink, and animals go thirsty. A king without kindness makes his people wilt in just the same way, because his care is like rain to them.

University

துளி (a drop) stands for rain by synecdoche. Parimelazhagar says the word was chosen because a drop is the most telling part of rain. அளி means gracious, tender care, and உயிர் (life, living beings) here stands for the subjects. The construction எற்று… அற்றே (‘of what kind…? just so’) makes a rhetorical comparison. Parimelazhagar notes that this couplet puts negatively what kural 542 had said positively: the whole world lives looking to the sky for rain, and the subjects look to the king’s sceptre. He groups kurals 557-558 as stating the evils that fall on those who live in the tyrant’s land. Solomon Pappaiah reads அளி more broadly as ‘righteous governance’. The text is the same across the sources consulted.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu tells the king to act like Indra: as Indra sends plentiful rain in the four months of the rainy season, so the king should shower benefits on his kingdom.
— Manusmṛti 9.304 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
The ‘vow of Indra’ makes the king’s generosity the political counterpart of rain.
It is the positive form of the Kural’s image: the king’s kindness is rain to his people.
Christianity
“…for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”
— Matthew 5:45 (KJV) [5]
Jesus points to God’s rain, given freely to everyone, as the model for goodness that does not pick and choose.
The Kural calls a ruler’s kindness a kind of rain; here God’s rain is the pattern of such kindness.
Islam
The Prophet said that whoever is not merciful to people, God will not be merciful to him.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7376 [6]
Mercy toward others is made the condition of receiving God’s mercy.
The Kural’s merciless king cuts himself off from mercy, and his people from relief.
Judaism
“May he come down like rain upon the mown grass, as showers that water the earth.”
— Psalm 72:6 (JPS 1917) [7]
The psalm for the king prays that his just rule will refresh the land as rain does.
It is the same image as the Kural: a king’s gracious rule is rain for his people.
Buddhism
In the Lotus Sutra, the Buddha compares his teaching to a great rain cloud that pours down equally on every herb, shrub and tree, each drinking according to its kind.
— Saddharmapuṇḍarīka (Lotus Sutra), ch. 5 (trans. H. Kern) [8]
Compassion is pictured as rain that nourishes every living thing without discrimination.
The Kural’s ‘kindness as rain’ is the same image, applied to a king.
Jainism
“The customs, taxes, and confiscations were released, buying and selling prohibited, no policemen were allowed to enter houses, great and small fines were remitted, and debts cancelled.”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Mahāvīra §102 (with §100) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [9]
At Mahāvīra's birth King Siddhārtha also freed all prisoners, and the Kalpa Sūtra says the whole population of town and country rejoiced at this display of royal grace.
The Kural says a king's lack of kindness is like drought to his people. Siddhārtha's festival shows the reverse: a king's kindness falling on his subjects like relief.
Sikhism
“When it rains, there is happiness. Water is the key to all life.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 150, Raag Maajh (Maajh ki Vaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak celebrates rain as the source of joy and the sustainer of every living thing.
The Kural compares a king's lack of kindness to drought on the world; Guru Nanak's verse shows how completely all life hangs on the rain, which is what gives the Kural's comparison its force.
Greek Mythology
When Demeter withdrew in grief and anger, she made the earth bear no crops, and mankind would have perished of famine if Zeus had not brought her daughter back.
— Homeric Hymn to Demeter 305-312 [11]
When the goddess who sustains life holds back her care, the whole world goes hungry.
Like the Kural’s drought, the withdrawal of care from above leaves every living being in distress.
Philosophy
Answering Thrasymachus, Socrates argues that a true ruler, like a true shepherd, looks to the good of those in his care, not his own profit.
— Plato, Republic I, 345c-e [12]
Rule is defined as care for the ruled.
The Kural’s king without அளி (gracious care) fails the very definition of ruling.

Real Life Example

Haile Selassie's government and the Wollo famine
1972-1974 · Wollo province, Ethiopia
During a drought in 1972-73, famine struck the province of Wollo in northern Ethiopia, and tens of thousands of people died. The imperial government of Haile Selassie tried to conceal the famine, and its denial became an international scandal after it was exposed in a British television documentary. Famine, unemployment and political stagnation fed the unrest that led army officers to depose the emperor on 12 September 1974.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The lack of care from the ruler turned a natural drought into a human disaster and cost him his throne.
The kural says a king's lack of kindness is to his people what drought is to the world, and in Wollo the two came together.
