Kural 1113

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

முறிமேனி முத்தம் முறுவல் வெறிநாற்றம் வேலுண்கண் வேய்த்தோ ளவட்கு.
Murimaeni muththam muruval verinaatram vaelunkan vaeyththo lavatku.

Meaning

Her shoulders are like bamboo. Her skin is the color of young leaves, her smile is like pearls, she smells sweet, and her dark-lined eyes are as sharp as spears.

Elementary

This is like drawing a picture of someone you love using things from nature: new green leaves for skin, shiny pearls for teeth, a flower-garden smell, and smooth bamboo for shoulders. Every part gets its own beautiful match.

University

This kural is a compact catalogue of the heroine’s features, each paired with its classical Tamil upamaanam (standard of comparison): muri (முறி, tender sprout, esp. of mango) for her complexion, muththam (முத்தம், pearl) for her smiling teeth (muruval), veri naatram (வெறி நாற்றம், natural fragrance, not applied scent) for her body’s scent, vel (வேல், lance) for her un-kan (உண்கண், kohl-'eaten' eyes) and vey (வேய், bamboo) for her rounded shoulders. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan all parse it as 'for her of bamboo shoulders: tender shoot is the body, pearl the teeth, fragrance the scent, lance the eyes', which is the Parimelazhagar construction; Sivayogi Sivakumar alone reads muththam as 'kiss', an outlier. The printed division 'வேய்த்தோ ளவட்கு' (ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net) is a metrical split of வேய்த்தோள் அவட்கு and not a variant. Pope: 'As tender shoot her frame; teeth, pearls; around her odours blend; / Darts are the eyes of her whose shoulders like the bambu bend.'

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Her arms are tender shoots; her lips / Are blossoms red and warm; / Bewitching youth begins to flower / In beauty on her form.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act I (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
Watching Shakuntala among the hermitage trees, the king describes her body part by part in images from the garden.
The Kural’s 'tender shoot' (muri) and Kalidasa’s 'tender shoots' belong to the same pan-Indian catalogue of beauty.
Christianity
“thou hast doves' eyes within thy locks: thy hair is as a flock of goats, that appear from mount Gilead. Thy teeth are like a flock of sheep that are even shorn, which came up from the washing;”
— Song of Solomon 4:1-2 (KJV) [4]
The lover of the Song praises his beloved feature by feature, each with its own image from the land.
Both poems use the catalogue form, matching eyes, teeth and more to images from nature.
Islam
“And [for them are] fair women with large, [beautiful] eyes, The likenesses of pearls well-protected,”
— Qur’an 56:22-23 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the companions of Paradise with beautiful eyes and likens them to guarded pearls.
Eyes and pearls, two of the Kural’s images of beauty, appear together here too.
Judaism
“Thy teeth are like a flock of ewes, Which are come up from the washing; Whereof all are paired, And none faileth among them. Thy temples are like a pomegranate split open Behind thy veil.”
— Song of Songs 6:6-7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs continues its feature-by-feature praise of the beloved with images from flock and orchard.
Like the Kural, it praises the beloved’s teeth and face through careful natural comparisons.
Buddhism
“Like plaintain buds in their color, my teeth were once splendid. With age, they’re broken & yellowed. The truth of the Truth-speaker’s words doesn’t change.”
— Therigatha 13.1, Ambapali (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The former courtesan Ambapali lists her once-famous features with their classic comparisons and then shows what age has done to each.
Ambapali uses the very catalogue the Kural uses for praise, but turns it into a lesson on impermanence.
Jainism
“Shall I forsake asceticism because the senseless crowd would excite me, saying, Woman's eye is like the lotus in clear water, the Gyal fish, and the battle-spear?”
— Naladiyar 5.4 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet hears the very comparisons of love poetry and refuses to be moved, choosing to see the eye as it physically is.
The Kural’s lance-like eye (vel-un-kan) is exactly the battle-spear the Naladiyar names and then rejects.
Sikhism
“Your eyes are so beautiful, and Your teeth are delightful. Your nose is so graceful, and Your hair is so long. Your body is so precious, cast in gold.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 567, Raag Vadhans, Chhant, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a Vadhans chhant, Guru Nanak describes the beauty of the Lord feature by feature (eyes, teeth, nose, hair and a body like gold), while the Sikh commentators note that all beauty in creation is His.
The Kural lists the beloved's body, smile, fragrance, eyes and shoulders, each with its classic comparison; Guru Nanak gives the same kind of feature-by-feature praise of the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
“I can only compare you to a young palm tree which I saw when I was at Delos growing near the altar of Apollo”
— Homer, Odyssey 6 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus compares the princess Nausicaa to a young palm he once saw springing up at Delos.
Like the Kural’s 'tender shoot', the Greek hero finds the image for a young woman’s beauty in fresh young growth.
Philosophy
Diotima teaches that the lover begins by loving one beautiful body, then sees that the beauty of every body is akin, and rises from there to beauty of soul, of laws and of knowledge.
— Plato, Symposium 210a-d (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato treats admiration of bodily features as only the first step on a ladder toward beauty itself.
The Kural lingers lovingly on the first rung, the beloved’s body; Plato honours that step but urges the lover to climb beyond it.

Real Life Example

Kalidasa's Meghaduta describes the yaksha's beloved
c. 5th century CE · India
The Sanskrit poet Kalidasa, writing in about the fifth century CE, composed the Meghaduta (The Cloud Messenger), a lyric love poem of some 115 verses. In it an exiled yaksha, a nature spirit, asks a monsoon cloud to carry a message to his beloved in the Himalayan city of Alaka, and much of the poem describes the landscapes along the cloud's route. In its second part the yaksha describes his very beautiful wife and her lonely state at home.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Meghaduta is one of the most admired poems of classical India, and it shaped how later Indian poets wrote about longing and beauty.
The kural paints the beloved's beauty through images taken from nature, and the Meghaduta likewise carries its reader through the natural world before arriving at a lover's description of his wife's beauty.
