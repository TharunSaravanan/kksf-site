Kural 255

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

உண்ணாமை உள்ளது உயிர்நிலை ஊனுண்ண அண்ணாத்தல் செய்யாது அளறு.
Unnaamai ulladhu uyirnilai oonunna, Annaaththal seyyaathu alaru.

Meaning

Life is kept safe by not eating meat. If a person eats meat, a place of terrible suffering will swallow them and never let them out.

Elementary

Many animals stay alive because people choose not to eat them. But the Kural gives a warning too: eating flesh is like falling into a deep, sticky pit that closes its mouth and won’t let you climb back out.

University

The first line, உண்ணாமை உள்ளது உயிர்நிலை, is read by Parimelazhagar and Varadarasan as “the continued life of beings depends on not eating flesh,” while Karunanidhi reads it socially: because some refrain, many creatures are spared. The second line uses a vivid image: அளறு (literally “mire, bog,” conventionally “hell”) will not perform அண்ணாத்தல் — opening the mouth — to release the one it has swallowed. The mire-image suggests a moral trap from which there is no easy exit, a consequence proportional to the finality of the death inflicted. Comparable ideas of unending or long-lasting punishment for bloodshed appear in Manu’s “mamsah” etymology and in Empedocles’ exile of the blood-polluted daimon.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“'Me he (mam sah)' will devour in the next (world), whose flesh I eat in this (life); the wise declare this (to be) the real meaning of the word 'flesh' (mamsah).”
— Manusmriti 5.55 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu derives the word for meat (māṃsa) from “me he (will eat)”: the animal you eat now will eat you in the next life.
Both texts warn that eating flesh sets up a consequence that swallows the eater in return.
Christianity
“And beside all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed: so that they which would pass from hence to you cannot; neither can they pass to us, that would come from thence.”
— Luke 16:19-26 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the rich man, who “fared sumptuously every day” and ignored suffering at his gate, the rich man ends in torment and cannot cross back.
Shares the Kural’s image of a place of punishment that does not let its captive go, earned by self-indulgence without mercy.
Islam
“A lady was punished because of a cat which she had imprisoned till it died. She entered the (Hell) Fire because of it”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3482 [5]
The Prophet taught that cruelty to a single animal can lead a person into Hell.
Like the Kural, it ties the death of an animal at human hands to a hellish consequence for the human.
Judaism
“And while the flesh was yet between their teeth, ere it was chewed, the wrath of the LORD was kindled against the people, and the LORD smote the people with a very great plague.”
— Numbers 11:33 (KJV) [4]
When the Israelites in the wilderness demanded meat, they were struck down while still eating the quail; the place was named Kibroth-hattaavah, “graves of craving.”
The craving for flesh leads directly to a pit of death — close to the Kural’s image of the swallowing mire.
Buddhism
“One who, while himself seeking happiness, oppresses with violence other beings who also desire happiness, will not attain happiness hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 131 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says harming others for one’s own happiness blocks happiness in future lives.
Parallels the Kural’s warning that the flesh-eater’s future is closed off.
Jainism
“You like meat minced or roasted; I have been made to eat, ever so many times, poisoned meat, and red-hot to boot.” … “Always frightened, trembling, distressed, and suffering, I have experienced the most exquisite pain and misery.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 19.69, 19.71 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
Prince Mrigaputra recalls his past lives in hell for his parents, setting the meat they enjoy beside the poisoned, red-hot meat he was forced to eat there.
Valluvar warns that hell will swallow the flesh-eater and not let him go; Mrigaputra's account of the hells pairs the taste for meat with its endless repayment there.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, those mortals who consume marijuana, fish and wine - no matter what pilgrimages, fasts and rituals they follow, they will all go to hell.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1377, Salok Bhagat Kabir 233 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir warns that people who eat fish and take intoxicants are not saved by their pilgrimages, fasts or rituals, but go down to hell.
Valluvar says hell swallows the flesh-eater and does not let him go; Kabir's salok also sends the eater of flesh to hell, whatever religious merit he claims.
Greek Mythology
“for they perished through their own sheer folly in eating the cattle of the Sun-god Hyperion; so the god prevented them from ever reaching home.”
— Homer, Odyssey 1 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
Odysseus’ crew, starving, slaughtered and ate the sacred cattle of the Sun; as punishment they all died and never returned home.
A mythic picture of the Kural’s warning: eating forbidden flesh brings a doom from which there is no return.
Philosophy
“whenever one of the daemons, whose portion is length of days, has sinfully polluted his hands with blood… he must wander thrice ten thousand seasons from the abodes of the blessed”
— Empedocles, fr. 115 DK (trans. J. Burnet) [10]
Empedocles taught that a soul stained by bloodshed is exiled through countless lifetimes before it can return to bliss.
Both texts describe a long captivity for those who shed blood; the Kural’s mire “will not open its mouth.”

Real Life Example

Emperor Tenmu bans the eating of certain meats in Japan
675 · Asuka, Yamato, Japan
In 675 Emperor Tenmu, a devout Buddhist, issued a decree forbidding the eating of cattle, horses, dogs, monkeys and chickens, at first during the farming months of the year. Later rulers strengthened the restriction and it hardened into a broad taboo on meat that lasted in some form for about 1,200 years. The ban was formally ended in 1872 when Emperor Meiji publicly began eating meat.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A single act of restraint, rooted in Buddhist respect for life, spared animals across Japanese society for more than a thousand years.
One reading of the kural is that the life of creatures is preserved because people refrain from eating flesh, and Japan's long meat taboo is a large-scale example of that preservation.
