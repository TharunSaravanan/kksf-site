Kural 931

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

வேண்டற்க வென்றிடினும் சூதினை வென்றதூஉம் தூண்டிற்பொன் மீன்விழுங்கி அற்று.
Vendarka vendridinum soodhinai vendradhooum thoondirpon meenvizhungi atru.

Meaning

Do not go after gambling, even if you can win. Winning at gambling is like a fish swallowing bait with an iron hook hidden in it.

Elementary

A fish sees a tasty worm and gulps it, happy for one second, but the worm hides a sharp hook. Valluvar says winning at gambling is that worm: it feels like a prize, but it pulls you in and you cannot get away.

University

The chapter opens by closing the most tempting loophole: vendridinum (வென்றிடினும், 'even should you win') concedes the gambler's best case and still forbids the desire (vendarka, வேண்டற்க). The simile turns on thoondirpon (தூண்டிற்பொன்), where pon (பொன்), usually 'gold', is read by the commentators as 'metal, iron': Mu. Varadarasanar on both ytamizh and thirukkural.net glosses it as தூண்டில் இரும்பு, 'the iron of the hook', and Kalaignar speaks of the iron barb (இரும்பு முள்). The alapedai in vendradhooum (வென்றதூஉம்) gives emphasis: even the thing won. The tradition descending from Parimelazhagar reads this chapter, like the two before it (Varaivin Magalir and Kallunnaamai), as a list of pleasures a ruler and a householder must shun, and the opening couplet sets the theme that gambling's gains are themselves the trap. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Dice, verily, are armed with goads and driving-hooks, deceiving and tormenting, causing grievous woe. They give frail gifts and then destroy the man who wins, thickly anointed with the player's fairest good.”
— Rig Veda 10.34.7 (Gambler's Hymn), trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith [3]
In the Vedic Gambler's Hymn the speaker describes dice as hooked weapons that hand out small gifts only to ruin even the winner.
The hymn's hooks that destroy the man who wins match the Kural's hook hidden in the fish's bait.
Christianity
“For what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul?”
— Matthew 16:26 (KJV) [4]
Jesus asks what any gain is worth if it costs a person their very self.
Like the Kural, it says a win can be a loss in disguise.
Islam
“They ask you about wine and gambling. Say, ‘In them is great sin and [yet, some] benefit for people. But their sin is greater than their benefit.’”
— Qur'an 2:219 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an admits that gambling can bring some gain but judges the harm to be greater.
Both texts grant that one may win and still say: do not want it.
Judaism
“And as an asmakhta does not effect acquisition, the one who wins takes money that is not legally his, and is considered a robber.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 24b (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English) [6]
In the Talmud, Rami bar Hama argues that a dice-player's winnings are not truly his, so even the winner counts as a kind of robber.
The Talmud, like the Kural, denies that the gambler's win is a real gain.
Buddhism
“He bolts the die quite boldly,--knowing not / What burning poison thereon lurks unseen.”
— Litta Jataka (No. 91), The Jataka vol. I, ed. E. B. Cowell [7]
In this Jataka a cheating dice-player keeps swallowing a die to escape losing, not knowing the Bodhisatta has coated the dice with poison.
A gambler swallowing what hides poison is close to the Kural's fish swallowing what hides a hook.
Jainism
“Just as an impassioned fish which is eager to swallow the bait, has its body transfixed by a hook.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.63 (the comparison line of the verses on taste, 32.61–73) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In the lecture on the causes of carelessness, the man who is passionately attached to tastes is compared to a fish that, greedy for the bait, is pierced by the hook hidden inside it.
Valluvar warns that even gambling's winnings are like the bait a fish swallows with the iron hook inside; the Uttarādhyayana uses the same image of the baited hook for the pleasure that hides its own ruin.
Sikhism
“Trying to taste the flavor, you swallow the hook.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1252, Raag Saarang, Bhagat Namdev (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Namdev uses the old image of the fish: drawn by the taste of the bait, it swallows the iron hook (loh) hidden inside and is caught. For him the lure of the senses always hides the hook.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s simile, where the gambler’s winnings are the bait a fish swallows together with the iron hook.
Greek Mythology
“...because by a sad spite I had killed the son of Amphidamas- not of set purpose, but in childish quarrel over the dice.”
— Homer, Iliad 23 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
The ghost of Patroclus recalls that a boyhood quarrel over dice made him a killer and sent him into exile.
A small game that seemed harmless carried a hidden cost, as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
“But the gamester and the footpad (and the highwayman) belong to the class of the mean, since they have a sordid love of gain.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle classes the gambler with the robber because both love gain from the wrong source.
For Aristotle, as for Valluvar, even successful gambling gains are something not worth wanting.

Real Life Example

Francois Blanc turns big wins into bait at the Monte Carlo casino
1863 onwards · Monte Carlo, Monaco
Facing bankruptcy, Prince Charles III of Monaco in 1863 gave the casino concession to Francois Blanc, who had already run a successful casino at Bad Homburg. Since every casino game is weighted in favour of the house, most players lost in the long run, but Blanc made a public ceremony of any player who broke the bank, covering the table with a black cloth while fresh money was fetched, because he knew that news of a big win drew in crowds of new gamblers hoping to repeat it. The casino soon made Monaco rich, and the principality's own residents have ever since been forbidden to gamble in its casinos.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The business of Monte Carlo rested on the fact that a well-publicised win costs the house little and lures many more people into losing.
The kural says not to desire gambling even when you can win, because the winnings are like the bait a fish swallows with the hook inside, and Blanc deliberately used other people's winnings as bait.
