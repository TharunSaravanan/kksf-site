Kural 1079

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

உடுப்பதூஉம் உண்பதூஉம் காணின் பிறர்மேல் வடுக்காண வற்றாகும் கீழ்.
Uduppadhoovum unbadhoovum kaanin pirarmel vadukkaana vatraagum keezh.

Meaning

When mean people see others wearing nice clothes and eating good food, they become experts at finding faults in them.

Elementary

Imagine a classmate gets new shoes and a nice lunch. A jealous child might say, 'Those shoes are ugly' or 'Your lunch smells funny,' just because they feel envious. The Kural says base people do this: they invent faults in others because they cannot stand to see them happy.

University

The couplet describes envy (azhukkaaru, the subject of ch. 17) turned into fault-finding. Uduppadhum unbadhum (உடுப்பதூஉம் உண்பதூஉம், with alapedai lengthening: 'what they wear and what they eat') are the most ordinary signs of others' well-being; on seeing them, the keezh become vatru (வற்று, 'strong, capable') at vadu kaana (வடுக்காண, 'finding a blemish'). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read envy as the cause and deliberate fault-finding as the result; Puliyur Kesikan adds that the base may even try to create a blemish (வடு உண்டாக்கவும் முயல்வான்). Pope: 'If neighbours clothed and fed he see, the base / Is mighty man some hidden fault to trace.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Duryodhana returns from the Pandavas' splendid assembly hall at Indraprastha burning with envy at their prosperity, tells his father he cannot bear to see their wealth, and plots the dice game to ruin them.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva, Dyuta sub-parva (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Duryodhana's envy at the Pandavas' good fortune turns into scheming against them.
He is the Kural's keezh: the sight of others' well-being drives him to find a way to harm them.
Christianity
“Then the presidents and princes sought to find occasion against Daniel concerning the kingdom; but they could find none occasion nor fault; forasmuch as he was faithful, neither was there any error or fault found in him.”
— Daniel 6:4 (KJV) [4]
Envious officials, jealous of Daniel's high position, search his life for a fault to accuse him of.
They are the Kural's base men, roused by another's success to hunt for blemishes.
Islam
“Or do they envy people for what Allāh has given them of His bounty?”
— Qur'an 4:54 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an rebukes those who resent the blessings God has given to others.
This is the envy at others' well-being that, in the Kural, drives the base to look for faults.
Judaism
“envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ha-Kappar names envy as one of three passions that destroy a person.
The Kural shows envy at work in the base, poisoning their view of anyone who prospers.
Buddhism
“Easily seen is the fault of others, but one's own fault is difficult to see. Like chaff one winnows another's faults, but hides one's own, even as a crafty fowler hides behind sham branches.”
— Dhammapada 252 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes how readily people sift out others' faults while hiding their own.
The Kural's base man is this eager winnower of others' faults, set in motion by envy.
Jainism
“what are those wretches' tongues made of, who, standing in the presence of those persons, declare their faults, for the purpose of destroying their reputation?”
— Naladiyar 36.3, chapter 'Baseness or Envy' (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
In its chapter 'Baseness or Envy', the Jain Naladiyar condemns those who announce others' faults in order to ruin their good name.
This is the Kural's keezh, skilled at finding blemishes in others out of envy.
Sikhism
“Fareed, if you have a keen understanding, then do not write black marks against anyone else. Look underneath your own collar instead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 6) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid tells the clever person to stop listing other people's faults and to examine his own.
The Kural says the base become experts at finding fault when they see others well clothed and fed; Farid's salok tells the fault-finder to look at himself instead.
Greek Mythology
Ovid describes Envy (Invidia) as a figure who wastes away at the sight of others' success and is tormented by others' happiness; sent by Minerva, she poisons Aglauros with jealousy of her sister's good fortune.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.760-832 (tr. A. S. Kline) [10]
Envy in the myth cannot bear to see others thrive and turns that pain into malice.
Ovid's Envy is the Kural's base man made into a goddess: others' well-being is what torments her.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines envy as hatred that makes a person sad at another's good fortune and glad at another's misfortune.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Definitions of the Affects 23 [11]
For Spinoza, envy is a form of hatred, and it naturally leads one to belittle the person envied.
The Kural shows the same passion in action: the base, pained by others' comforts, look for faults to bring them down.

Real Life Example

Artists publish a protest against the Eiffel Tower
14 February 1887 · Paris, France
Soon after construction of Gustave Eiffel's tower began, a group of about forty well-known artists and writers, including Charles Gounod, Charles Garnier and Guy de Maupassant, published a protest on the front page of Le Temps. They called the tower useless and monstrous and said it would dishonour Paris. The authorities backed Eiffel, building continued, and the finished tower proved hugely popular.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The protest is remembered as a famous example of prominent critics finding fault with a bold new achievement that later became loved.
The kural describes people who become experts at finding faults when they see others' good fortune; the protesters poured out fault-finding at another man's conspicuous success.
