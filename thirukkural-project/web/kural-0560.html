Kural 560

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

ஆபயன் குன்றும் அறுதொழிலோர் நூல்மறப்பர் காவலன் காவான் எனின்.
Aapayan kundrum aruthozhilor noolmarappar Kaavalan kaavaan enin.

Meaning

If the ruler does not protect the land, the cows give less milk and the learned priests who do the six sacred duties forget their holy books.

Elementary

If a school has no one looking after it, the lunch kitchen stops working and the teachers stop teaching. Soon both food and learning are gone. Valluvar says that when a king doesn’t protect his land, food (milk from cows) and learning (the old books) both start to fade.

University

ஆபயன் is ‘the yield of the cow’ (ஆ, cow, and பயன், fruit), meaning milk and its products. அறுதொழிலோர் are ‘those of the six duties’, which Parimelazhagar lists as the Brahmin’s six duties: studying, teaching, sacrificing, officiating at sacrifice, giving and receiving (ஓதல், ஓதுவித்தல், வேட்டல், வேட்பித்தல், ஈதல், ஏற்றல்). He ties the couplet to 559: without milk there is no oblation (அவி), without recitation there are no mantras, so sacrifice stops, and so the rain fails. He groups 559-560 as the evils that befall the tyrant’s land. Modern commentators widen the reference: Solomon Pappaiah reads ‘the wise’ (ஞானியர்), and Kalaignar and Sivayogi Sivakumar read ‘productive work and the major occupations decline’. The older continuous text has the sandhi form நூன்மறப்பர்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The three kinds of Fire, the three Vedas, and sacrifices with Dakshina, all become lost when the king becomes heedless.” … “Elephants and steeds and kine … all lose their vigour when the king becomes unrighteous.”
— Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva, Section XCI (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Utathya warns Mandhatri that royal neglect brings down both the Vedic order and the vigour of cattle.
This matches both halves of the Kural, cattle failing and the Vedas forgotten, almost word for word.
Christianity
“But he that is an hireling, and not the shepherd, whose own the sheep are not, seeth the wolf coming, and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth: and the wolf catcheth them, and scattereth the sheep.”
— John 10:12 (KJV) [5]
Jesus contrasts the good shepherd with the hired hand who abandons the flock when danger comes.
The hireling is the Kural’s ‘guardian who does not guard’, and his flock is left exposed.
Islam
The Prophet said that every one of you is a shepherd and responsible for his flock: the ruler is a shepherd and responsible for his people.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 893 [6]
Rule is defined as guardianship, for which the ruler will be held to account.
The Kural’s காவலன் (guardian) is the Prophet’s shepherd, whose neglect harms everyone in his care.
Judaism
“Pray for the welfare of the government, for were it not for the fear it inspires, every man would swallow his neighbor alive.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:2 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Rabbi Hanina teaches that without a protecting authority, society collapses into violence.
It is the Kural’s point: when the guardian does not guard, the order that sustains everything else falls apart.
Buddhism
In the Buddha’s account of the origin of society, once theft and disorder break out the people choose one of themselves, the ‘Great Elect’ (Mahāsammata), to guard the fields and punish wrongdoers in return for a share of the rice.
— Dīgha Nikāya 27, Aggañña Sutta [8]
Kingship begins as a contract to protect the harvest and keep order.
A king who does not guard has broken the very agreement that, in the Buddhist story, created kings.
Jainism
“A dreadful dearth prevailing about this time forced the monks to emigrate as far as the seaside. During these unsettled times they neglected their regular studies, so that the sacred lore was on the point of falling into oblivion.”
— Hemacandra, Pariśiṣṭaparvan IX.55–76, as summarised by Hermann Jacobi; quoted in ‘The Jain Church After Mahavira’, jainworld.com [9]
Hemacandra tells how a great famine in Candragupta's time scattered the Jain monks so that the canon was nearly forgotten, until the Saṅgha met at Pāṭaliputra to gather the eleven Aṅgas again.
The Kural warns that when the land is not protected, the learned forget their scriptures, and Jain history records exactly that: in a time of public distress the monks almost lost their sacred texts.
Sikhism
“They tax the cows and the Brahmins, but the cow-dung they apply to their kitchen will not save them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 471, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak rebukes the officials of his day who levied taxes on cows and Brahmins while still clinging to outward rituals of purity.
The Kural says that when the guardian fails to protect, the cows yield less and the learned forget their scriptures; Guru Nanak also connects the plight of cows and learned men with failing custodians, though his concern is their hypocrisy rather than the loss of Vedic learning.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus compares Penelope’s fame to that of a blameless king who upholds justice: under such a king the earth bears wheat and barley, the trees are laden with fruit, the flocks bear young without fail, and the sea gives fish.
— Homer, Odyssey XIX.109-114 [11]
Homer ties the fertility of land and flocks to the king’s justice.
This is the positive side of the Kural: where the guardian guards, the herds are fruitful.
Philosophy
Hobbes argues that without a common power to keep order, there is no place for industry or agriculture, no arts, no letters and no society, and the life of man is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short.
— Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, ch. 13 [12]
For Hobbes, the sovereign’s protection is the precondition of all production and learning.
Both halves of the Kural are here: livelihood fails and ‘letters’ are lost when the guardian does not guard.

Real Life Example

The Nazi book burnings and the purge of German universities
April-May 1933 · Berlin and university towns across Germany
On 7 April 1933 the new Nazi government issued the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service, which led to the dismissal of Jewish and politically undesirable university teachers, over 1,100 of them by 1935. On 10 May 1933 pro-Nazi student groups burned books by Jewish, pacifist and left-wing authors in university towns, with some 20,000 volumes burned before a crowd of about 40,000 on Berlin's Opernplatz.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
When the state stopped protecting scholars and learning, Germany's universities lost much of the knowledge they had been built to keep.
The kural warns that when the guardian of the land fails to protect it the learned forget their texts, and the burnings and dismissals of 1933 drove learning out of German universities.
