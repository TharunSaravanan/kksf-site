Kural 1244

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

கண்ணும் கொளச்சேறி நெஞ்சே இவையென்னைத் தின்னும் அவர்க்காணல் உற்று.
Kannum kolachchaeri nenje ivaiyennaith thinnum avarkkaanal utru.

Meaning

My heart, when you go to him, take my eyes with you too. They want so badly to see him that they are eating me alive.

Elementary

Imagine your heart can fly to visit someone you miss, but your eyes are stuck at home and keep crying because they want to see that person too. So you say, “Heart, please take my eyes with you, they won’t stop bothering me!”

University

This couplet presupposes that the heart has already gone to the absent husband (a convention developed in neighbouring couplets), so the heroine asks it to take the eyes too: kaṇṇum koḷa (‘taking the eyes also’) cēṟi (‘you will go’). The eyes, left behind, ivai eṉṉait tiṉṉum (‘these devour me’) avar kāṇal uṟṟu (‘set on seeing him’); Mu. Varadarasanar glosses tiṉṉum as பிடுங்கித் தின்கின்றன (‘pluck and eat’), and Pappaiah softens it to ‘grieve as though eating me’. The image of a body divided against itself, heart travelling while eyes torment the one who stays, extends the Kāmattuppāl motif of personified eyes (compare chapter 118, kaṇ vitumpu aḻital). Pope renders the line ‘O rid me of these eyes, my heart’. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When You go off to the forest during the day, a tiny fraction of a second becomes like a millennium for us because we cannot see You.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.31.15 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The gopis tell Krishna that every moment their eyes cannot see him is unbearable.
The Kural’s heroine likewise suffers through her eyes, which torment her with their craving to see the absent beloved.
Christianity
“Mine eyes fail for thy word, saying, When wilt thou comfort me?”
— Psalms 119:82 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes his eyes wearing out with longing as he waits for a comfort that does not come.
In the Kural the eyes, too, are consumed by longing for one who stays away.
Islam
“For indeed, it is not eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts which are within the breasts.”
— Qur’an 22:46 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an distinguishes the eyes from the heart and locates true seeing in the heart.
The Kural also separates eyes and heart, imagining the heart going off while the eyes stay behind and suffer.
Judaism
“I am weary of my crying; my throat is dried; Mine eyes fail while I wait for my God.”
— Psalms 69:4 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist describes his eyes worn out with waiting and weeping for one who has not yet come.
The Kural’s eyes are similarly ‘eaten up’ by waiting to see the absent beloved.
Buddhism
The monk Vakkali loved simply to gaze at the Buddha’s body; when he was ill and longed to see him, the Buddha came and told him that there was no point in looking at this body, for whoever sees the Dhamma sees the Buddha.
— Samyutta Nikaya 22.87 (Vakkali Sutta) (paraphrase) [7]
The story treats the eyes’ craving to see a beloved form as something to be redirected inward.
The Kural’s eyes are consumed by the same craving to see the beloved, which the Buddha gently tells Vakkali to look beyond.
Jainism
“He who is passionately fond of ‘colours,’ will come to untimely ruin; just as an impassioned moth which is attracted by the light rushes into death.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In Jain teaching each sense, beginning with the eye, can destroy a person through its passionate attachment to its object.
The Kural’s eyes, ‘eating’ the heroine with their craving to see her husband, are just such a sense consuming its owner.
Sikhism
“My eyes long for You; when will I see Your Vision?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1094, Raag Maaroo, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan voices the soul's eyes as aching for a single sight of the Beloved.
The Kural's wife says her eyes, set on seeing him, are eating her alive and asks her heart to take them along; Guru Arjan likewise gives the eyes their own longing for the Beloved's vision.
Greek Mythology
Clytie, abandoned by the sun-god Helios, sat on the bare ground for nine days without food, turning her face and eyes to follow him across the sky, until she was changed into a flower that still turns toward the sun.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.256-270 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s story makes the abandoned lover’s gaze itself the thing that consumes her.
Like the Kural’s eyes, which ‘eat’ the heroine in their longing to see her husband, Clytie’s eyes follow her beloved until she wastes away.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus, Socrates describes how the lover’s soul, when parted from the beloved, becomes parched and frantic, unable to sleep or rest, and runs wherever it hopes to catch sight of the beautiful one, finding relief only in seeing him.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-e (paraphrase) [11]
Plato treats the sight of the beloved as what nourishes the lover’s soul, and its absence as torment.
The Kural’s eyes, tormenting the heroine in their need to see her husband, express the same dependence on sight.

Real Life Example

Abigail Adams crosses the Atlantic to rejoin John Adams
1784 · Boston, Massachusetts, to England and Auteuil, France
John Adams's long absences on public service, including diplomatic posts in Europe, left his wife Abigail to raise their children and run the farm and household in Massachusetts. In the summer of 1784, after nearly five years apart, she made a month-long voyage from Boston to England with their daughter and joined John in Europe, settling with him at Auteuil, near Paris.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her journey ended one of the longest separations of any founding American couple, and her record of the voyage survives in the Adams Papers.
The kural's heroine, longing to see her husband, wants her eyes carried to him, and Abigail Adams, after years of longing, went herself to see the husband her heart had followed.
