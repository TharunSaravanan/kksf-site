Kural 396

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

தொட்டனைத் தூறும் மணற்கேணி மாந்தர்க்குக் கற்றனைத் தூறும் அறிவு.
Thottanaith thoorum manarkeni maandharkkuk Katranaith thoorum arivu.

Meaning

A well dug in sand gives more water the deeper it is dug. In the same way, a person’s understanding grows the more they learn.

Elementary

When you dig a hole at the beach, water starts to seep in, and the deeper you dig, the more water comes. Your mind works the same way: the more you learn, the more wisdom flows out of you.

University

This is perhaps the most quoted kural of the chapter. It rests on a precise agricultural image from the Tamil landscape: the மணற்கேணி, a shallow well dug in sandy riverbed or soil, where groundwater seeps in more strongly the deeper one digs. The parallel verbs தொட்டனை ('as much as dug') and கற்றனை ('as much as learned') set up an exact proportion, and the shared verb ஊறும் ('springs, oozes up') links the two halves. The point is that wisdom (அறிவு, arivu) is not poured in from outside but springs up from within, released by the labour of study. It also implies that learning has no fixed end, since there is always more water below. Parallels range from Proverbs' 'wellspring of wisdom' and the Mishnah's 'ever-flowing spring' to Pope's 'Pierian spring'.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Taittiriya Upanishad, young Bhrigu asks his father Varuna to teach him Brahman. Each time, he is told to go and practise tapas (disciplined inquiry). He comes to understand Brahman step by step as food, breath, mind, understanding, and finally bliss.
— Taittiriya Upanishad 3.1–3.6 (Bhrigu Valli) [3]
Bhrigu's knowledge deepens in stages. Each round of effort 'digs' further and reveals a deeper layer of truth.
Like the Kural's sand-well, understanding grows exactly as far as the seeker keeps digging through sustained effort.
Christianity
“The words of a man's mouth are as deep waters, and the wellspring of wisdom as a flowing brook.”
— Proverbs 18:4 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs pictures wisdom as a spring that keeps flowing, deep water that wells up and never runs dry.
A strong image match: both texts picture wisdom as water welling up from a deep source.
Islam
“Say, “If the sea were ink for [writing] the words of my Lord, the sea would be exhausted before the words of my Lord were exhausted, even if We brought the like of it in [continual] supplement.”
— Qur'an 18:109 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an uses an ocean image to show that divine knowledge has no limit. There is always more to learn.
Like the Kural's sand-well, it pictures knowledge as a water source that is never used up.
Judaism
“Rabbi Eleazar ben Arach is like a spring that [ever] gathers force.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:8 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai praises his disciple as a spring whose flow keeps increasing, in contrast to another disciple who is 'a plastered cistern which loses not a drop' (retaining learning but not producing new insight).
A striking parallel: the ideal scholar is an ever-growing spring, just as the Kural's learner is a well whose water rises with every bit of digging.
Buddhism
“Wisdom springs from meditation; without meditation wisdom wanes. Having known these two paths of progress and decline, let a man so conduct himself that his wisdom may increase.”
— Dhammapada 282 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that wisdom 'springs up' in proportion to cultivation and dries up when practice stops.
Like the Kural, wisdom rises in proportion to effort. Here the effort is meditation rather than study.
Jainism
“By study he destroys the Karman which obstructs right knowledge.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 29.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In Jain doctrine, knowledge is the soul's own nature, only covered over by knowledge-obscuring karma (jnanavaraniya). Study wears that covering away, so understanding grows as more of the soul's latent knowledge is uncovered.
Valluvar's well in the sand yields water the deeper it is dug, and knowledge wells up in proportion to learning. In the Jain picture, too, knowledge is already present and comes out more fully the more one studies.
Sikhism
“The seeker comes forth, and the debater dies down; I am a sacrifice, a sacrifice to the Guru, the Creator Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1255, Raag Malaar, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that the one who keeps searching grows in spiritual life, while the one who only argues withers away.
The Kural says understanding wells up in proportion to how deeply one digs into learning; Guru Nanak likewise says that it is the persistent seeker, not the disputer, who comes to flourish.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod describes the Muses of Mount Helicon bathing in the Hippocrene, the 'Horse's Spring' said to have been struck from the rock by Pegasus' hoof. Along with the Pierian spring, it became the classical symbol of the source of poetic and intellectual inspiration.
— Hesiod, Theogony 1–8 [10]
In Greek myth, wisdom and inspiration flow from sacred springs, and poets 'drink' from them.
Like the Kural, it pictures knowledge as water rising from a spring, though Greek myth stresses divine gift more than human digging.
Philosophy
“A little learning is a dang'rous thing; / Drink deep, or taste not the Pierian spring: / There shallow draughts intoxicate the brain, / And drinking largely sobers us again.”
— Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism, Part II, lines 215–218 (1711) [11]
Pope warns that shallow learning breeds arrogance, while deep learning brings sober wisdom. The depth of study decides the quality of understanding.
An excellent match: like Valluvar, Pope ties the benefit of learning to its depth, using the image of a spring.

Real Life Example

Michael Ventris deciphers Linear B
1952 · London, England, United Kingdom
Michael Ventris became fascinated by the undeciphered Linear B script at the age of 14, after visiting a 1936 London exhibition of finds from Crete and meeting Sir Arthur Evans there. Though he trained as an architect, he kept studying the tablets for years and from 1949 worked intensively on them, using careful analysis of how signs recurred and combined. In June 1952 he announced on British radio that the script recorded a very early form of Greek, a result confirmed with the linguist John Chadwick.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Years of patient study let Ventris see what experts had missed, pushing written Greek back centuries before Homer.
The kural compares understanding to water in a sand well that rises as one digs deeper, and Ventris's insight came only after about sixteen years of steady digging into the problem.
