Kural 148

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

பிறன்மனை நோக்காத பேராண்மை சான்றோர்க்கு அறனொன்றோ ஆன்ற வொழுக்கு.
Piranmanai nokkaadha peraanmai saandrorkku, Aranondro aandra ozhukku.

Meaning

For good people, the great courage of not even looking at another man’s wife with desire is not just goodness. It is the fullness of right living.

Elementary

Being truly strong is not about big muscles. This kural says real strength is choosing not even to look at someone else's husband or wife in a wanting way. That kind of self-control is the strongest and best way to behave.

University

The striking term பேராண்மை ('great manliness' or 'supreme valour') redefines heroism: in a culture that prized martial courage, Valluvar says the greatest valour is the self-mastery not to look (நோக்காத) at another's wife with desire. The couplet moves the moral line from the act to the gaze, the same shift found in the Sermon on the Mount and in the Qur'anic command to lower the gaze. The second line says this restraint is not just one virtue (அறன் ஒன்றோ, 'is it only virtue?') but ஆன்ற ஒழுக்கு, complete or mature conduct, linking this chapter back to the preceding chapter on ஒழுக்கமுடைமை (right conduct). For the சான்றோர் (the noble or accomplished), then, this restraint is the crown of character.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, when Rama shows Lakshmana ornaments dropped by the kidnapped Sita, Lakshmana says he cannot recognise her bracelets or earrings, only her anklets, because he bowed at her feet every day and never raised his eyes to look at her.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda (narrative summary) [3]
Lakshmana, a great warrior, had lived beside his brother's wife for years and never once looked at her face with any desire.
This is the Kural's 'great manliness that does not look at another's wife' in its most famous Indian example.
Christianity
“Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery: But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart.”
— Matthew 5:27-28 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that the wrong begins with the desiring look, not only with the act.
Same move as the Kural: the line is drawn at the gaze itself.
Islam
“Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts. That is purer for them. Indeed, Allah is Acquainted with what they do.”
— Qur'an 24:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an commands believing men to lower their gaze and guard their chastity.
The Kural praises the man who does not look; the Qur'an commands exactly this lowering of the gaze.
Judaism
“I made a covenant with mine eyes; why then should I think upon a maid?”
— Job 31:1 (KJV) [4]
Job says he has made a solemn promise with his own eyes not to look at a woman with desire.
Job's 'covenant with the eyes' is the Kural's refusal to look, framed as a sacred vow.
Buddhism
“Come now, monks: with regard to women who are old enough to be your mother, establish the attitude you would have toward your mother. With regard to women who are old enough to be your sister, establish the attitude you'd have toward a sister.”
— Bharadvaja Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 35.127 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha teaches young monks to look at women as they would at their own mother or sister, so that desire does not arise.
The Kural praises not looking at another's wife with desire; the Buddha trains the eye to see such a woman as family.
Jainism
“While his loving wife dwells in his home, the tender one whom he espoused, — seeking (out an auspicious) day, and sounding the drum, for many folks to know,—and whom he guards as his own, what means a man's glance at another's wife?”
— Nālaṭiyār 86 (ch. 9, 'The Not Desiring Other Men's Wives'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [7]
The Nālaṭiyār asks why a man who has publicly married a loving wife should even look at another man's wife, so the restraint begins with the eyes.
Valluvar calls the man who does not even look (நோக்கா) at another's wife the possessor of 'great manliness', and the Nālaṭiyār likewise puts the moral line at the glance.
Sikhism
“whose eyes do not gaze upon the beauty of others' wives,”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 274, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 9, Pauri 1, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan lists not looking at other men's wives with desire among the marks of the rare, truly pure person.
The Kural moves the line from the act to the gaze and calls this restraint the fullness of conduct; Guru Arjan likewise makes the undesiring eye a mark of holiness.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad (Book 6), the hero Bellerophon stays as a guest with King Proetus. The king's wife Anteia desires him, but Bellerophon, being honourable, refuses her. She falsely accuses him, and he is sent on deadly quests, which he survives with the gods' help.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 6 (narrative summary) [9]
Bellerophon, famed for his courage, showed an even greater courage by refusing his host's wife.
This is the Kural's 'great manliness': heroism shown not in battle but in refusing another's wife.
Philosophy
“To-day when I saw a handsome person, I did not say to myself, I wish I could lie with her, and Happy is her husband; for he who says this says, Happy is her adulterer also. Nor do I picture the rest to my mind.”
— Epictetus, Discourses II.18 (trans. George Long) [10]
Epictetus describes the victory of seeing a beautiful woman and refusing even to let desire or fantasy begin.
Almost a commentary on the Kural: true strength is not letting the look turn into desire at all.

Real Life Example

Goethe leaves Wetzlar and Charlotte Buff
1772 · Wetzlar, Holy Roman Empire (now Germany)
In the summer of 1772 the young Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, then a trainee at the Imperial Chamber Court in Wetzlar, fell in love with Charlotte Buff, who was already engaged to Johann Christian Kestner. After a few months Goethe left Wetzlar abruptly in September 1772 without saying goodbye, and Charlotte married Kestner the following spring. Goethe drew on the experience for his novel The Sorrows of Young Werther, published in 1774, in which Lotte was modelled on Charlotte and her fiance on Kestner.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Rather than press his feelings on a woman promised to another man, Goethe withdrew, and the episode became the seed of one of the most influential novels of the age.
The kural calls it the greatest manliness not even to look with desire at another's wife; Goethe's departure from Wetzlar was a withdrawal from a woman who belonged to another.
