Kural 816

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

பேதை பெருங்கெழீஇ நட்பின் அறிவுடையார் ஏதின்மை கோடி உறும்.
Pedhai perungezheei natpin arivudaiyaar edhinmai kodi urum.

Meaning

Being a stranger, or even an enemy, to wise people is worth ten million times more than being a very close friend of a fool.

Elementary

If you’re building a sandcastle, a careful kid who doesn’t like you will still not knock it over, but a clumsy best friend might jump right on it while trying to help. Sometimes a sensible rival is safer than a silly friend.

University

The couplet sets pedhai perung kezheei natpu (பேதை பெருங்கெழீஇ நட்பு), ‘the greatly intimate friendship of a fool’, against arivudaiyaar edhinmai (அறிவுடையார் ஏதின்மை), and the reading of edhinmai divides commentators. Edhilaar means ‘strangers, outsiders’, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesigan take edhinmai as ‘the absence of friendship’, being unconnected to the wise; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar sharpen it to ‘enmity’ (பகை), giving the more striking proverb ‘a wise enemy is better than a foolish friend’. Kezheei (கெழீஇ) is the alapedai form of kezhu-, ‘to be closely joined’. ‘Kodi urum’ (கோடி உறும்) means ‘is worth a crore’, i.e., ten million times over. The texts agree; the sandhi-joined edition writes ‘நட்பி னறிவுடையா ரேதின்மை’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In a Panchatantra story, a king appoints his pet monkey as bodyguard; when a fly settles on the sleeping king, the monkey swings a sword at it and kills the king, and the tale is set beside one in which a wise thief saves a life, to teach that a wise enemy is better than a foolish friend.
— Panchatantra, Book I (Mitrabheda), the king and the foolish monkey [4]
The monkey’s devotion is real, but its foolishness makes its closeness deadly.
This is the Kural’s comparison in story form: the fool’s intimacy does more harm than the wise man’s enmity.
Christianity
“He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed.”
— Proverbs 13:20 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs contrasts the gains of walking with the wise against the ruin that comes from keeping company with fools.
Both texts judge friendship with fools as harmful and closeness to the wise as valuable.
Islam
“The example of a good pious companion and an evil one is that of a person carrying musk and another blowing a pair of bellows.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5534 [6]
The Prophet compares a good companion to a perfume seller whose scent rubs off on you, and a bad one to a blacksmith whose bellows burn your clothes or leave a foul smell.
As in the Kural, simply being near the wrong person damages you, however close the bond.
Judaism
King Rehoboam rejected the counsel of the elders who had served his father Solomon and followed the young men who had grown up with him; their advice to answer the people harshly led ten tribes to break away.
— 1 Kings 12:6-16 (Hebrew Bible) [5]
Rehoboam preferred the familiar intimacy of foolish peers to the counsel of the wise, and lost most of his kingdom.
This is the Kural’s warning in history: a fool’s close friendship costs far more than distance from the wise.
Buddhism
“Better it is to live alone; there is no fellowship with a fool. Live alone and do no evil; be carefree like an elephant in the elephant forest.”
— Dhammapada 330 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada declares that there is no real companionship to be had with a fool.
Valluvar and the Buddha agree that a fool’s intimacy is worth less than none.
Jainism
“This is the road to it: to serve the Gurus and the old (teachers), to avoid throughout foolish people, to apply oneself earnestly to study, and to ponder zealously on the meaning of the Sûtras.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana names avoiding foolish people, together with serving one's teachers and study, as part of the road to liberation.
The Kural ranks even distance from the wise far above close friendship with a fool, and the Jain canon likewise makes keeping away from fools part of the path itself.
Sikhism
“Friendship with a fool never works out right. As he knows, he acts; behold, and see that it is so.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad says that however close one becomes to a fool, the fool still acts only according to his own limited understanding, so the friendship can never come right.
The Kural says being a stranger to the wise is worth ten million times more than the closest friendship with a fool; Guru Angad gives the reason, since intimacy cannot change how a fool acts.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable, a Mouse ‘formed an intimate acquaintance with a Frog’, who tied their feet together and dragged him into the pond, where the Mouse drowned and a Hawk carried off both.
— Aesop, The Mouse, the Frog, and the Hawk (tr. G. F. Townsend) [10]
A tie of close friendship with a heedless partner brings death to one and ruin to both.
It dramatises the Kural’s perung kezheei natpu, the tightly bound friendship that destroys.
Philosophy
La Fontaine’s fable of the Bear and the Garden-Lover ends with the moral that nothing is as dangerous as an ignorant friend and that a wise enemy would be better: the bear, guarding his sleeping friend, kills him with a paving stone while trying to swat a fly.
— Jean de La Fontaine, Fables VIII.10, L’Ours et l’Amateur des jardins [11]
The bear’s loyalty is sincere; its stupidity is what kills.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s proverb: a fool’s close friendship is worse than a wise man’s enmity.

Real Life Example

Charles I shields his favourite, the Duke of Buckingham
1625-1628 · London, England
George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham, was the closest friend and chief minister of King Charles I, and he led England's war policy. A naval expedition against Cadiz in 1625 failed, and in 1626 the House of Commons blamed Buckingham and tried to impeach him, but Charles imprisoned one of the impeachment managers, Sir John Eliot, and dissolved Parliament rather than give up his favourite. Buckingham's landing on the Ile de Re in 1627 also ended in failure, and the king's quarrels with Parliament over money and his favourite deepened.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Charles's intimacy with an unsuccessful favourite cost him the cooperation of Parliament at the start of his reign, beginning the conflict that later led to civil war.
The kural says that distance, or even enmity, with the wise is worth far more than close friendship with a fool, and Charles chose his favourite over the counsel of Parliament at great cost.
