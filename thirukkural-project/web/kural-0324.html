Kural 324

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

நல்லாறு எனப்படுவது யாதெனின் யாதொன்றும் கொல்லாமை சூழும் நெறி.
Nallaaru enappaduvadhu yaadhenin yaadhondrum Kollaamai soozhum neri.

Meaning

If you ask what the good path is, it is the way of life that takes care never to kill any creature at all.

Elementary

When you walk in a garden, you watch where you step so you don’t squash the ants or crush the flowers. Valluvar says the “good road” in life is exactly that: always looking out so that you don’t hurt even the smallest living thing.

University

The couplet defines nallāṟu, “the good path,” not by a doctrine or ritual but by a disposition: the neṟi (way of conduct) that “deliberates upon” (cūḻum) not killing “anything at all” (yātoṉṟum). The verb cūḻ suggests active, careful forethought — non-killing is not passive but a vigilant attentiveness in every act. The phrase yātoṉṟum explicitly extends the scope to all living beings, however small, aligning the Kural with the rigorous ahiṃsā of Jain and Buddhist ethics. This conception of ethics as an attentive way of moving through the world anticipates Albert Schweitzer’s “reverence for life.”

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…austerity; simplicity; nonviolence; truthfulness; freedom from anger; renunciation; tranquillity; aversion to faultfinding; compassion for all living entities…” — these “belong to godly men endowed with divine nature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1–3 (As It Is) [4]
Krishna lists the divine qualities that lead toward liberation, including ahimsa and compassion for all beings.
Marks non-violence toward all creatures as a defining feature of the good, liberating path.
Christianity
“A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast: but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel.”
— Proverbs 12:10 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs identifies care for the life even of one’s animals as a mark of the righteous person.
Like the Kural, the good way is shown by concern for the lives of all creatures, not only humans.
Islam
“And there is no creature on [or within] the earth or bird that flies with its wings except [that they are] communities like you.”
— Qur’an 6:38 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an describes animals and birds as communities like human beings, all known to and gathered by God.
Grounds the Kural’s regard for “any creature whatsoever” in the dignity of all living communities.
Judaism
“Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace.”
— Proverbs 3:17 (KJV); cf. Talmud Gittin 59b [5]
Said of Wisdom, and applied by the rabbis to the Torah as a whole, which they held exists “for the sake of the ways of peace.”
Defines the good path, as the Kural does, as a way of non-harm and peace.
Buddhism
“And what, monks, is right action? Abstaining from taking life, abstaining from stealing, abstaining from unchastity: This, monks, is called right action.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 45.8, Magga-vibhanga Sutta (Thanissaro Bhikkhu trans.) [7]
In analysing the Noble Eightfold Path, the Buddha defines right action beginning with abstention from taking life.
Nearly identical to the Kural: the good path (magga / nallāṟu) is defined first by not killing.
Jainism
“A wise man should study them with all means of philosophical research. All beings hate pains; therefore one should not kill them. This is the quintessence of wisdom: not to kill anything.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.11.9–10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
In the lecture titled 'The Path', the Sūtrakṛtāṅga answers 'what is the path?' by enumerating all classes of living beings and concluding that the essence of wisdom is to kill nothing.
The Kural defines the good path as the way that takes thought to kill nothing at all; Mahāvīra's 'Path' lecture likewise makes 'not to kill anything' its core after careful study of every form of life.
Sikhism
“And when, O Nanak, he is merciful to all beings, only then shall he be called a Muslim.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 141, Raag Maajh, Maajh ki Vaar, Salok, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak redefines what makes a person truly religious: not outward forms but mercy towards every living being.
The Kural defines the good path as the way that takes care not to kill any creature; Guru Nanak likewise makes mercy to all beings the defining mark of the true religious path.
Greek Mythology
Plato recalls the so-called “Orphic life” of old, in which people abstained from animal sacrifice and from everything that has life, keeping to what is lifeless.
— Plato, Laws 6.782c [10]
Followers of the mythic singer Orpheus were remembered for a way of life defined by refusal to kill.
An ancient Greek “good path” defined, like the Kural’s, by abstaining from killing any creature.
Philosophy
“It is good to maintain and cherish life; it is evil to destroy and to check life.”
— Albert Schweitzer, Civilization and Ethics (The Philosophy of Civilization, Part II) [11]
Albert Schweitzer’s ethic of reverence for life holds that every living thing has a will-to-live that the ethical person must respect.
A modern restatement of the Kural’s good path: careful attention never to destroy any life.

Real Life Example

Shogun Tsunayoshi's Edicts on Compassion for Living Things
1680s-1709 · Edo (now Tokyo), Japan
Tokugawa Tsunayoshi, the fifth Tokugawa shogun, issued a long series of decrees known as the Edicts on Compassion for Living Things. They protected a wide range of creatures, including dogs, cats, horses, birds, fish, shellfish and even insects, with many edicts forbidding the abandonment or mistreatment of dogs. Because of these laws he became known as the Dog Shogun, and the edicts remained in force for more than two decades of his rule.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It was one of the most sweeping attempts by any government to make care for animal life a matter of law, and it remains famous and much debated in Japan.
The kural defines the good path as a way of life that carefully considers how to avoid killing anything at all, and Tsunayoshi's edicts tried to extend that care even to insects and shellfish.
