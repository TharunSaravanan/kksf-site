Kural 334

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

நாளென ஒன்றுபோற் காட்டி உயிர்ஈரும் வாளது உணர்வார்ப் பெறின்.
Naal ena ondru pol kaatti uyir eerum Vaal adhu unarvaar perin.

Meaning

A day looks like just a stretch of time. But people who understand know it is really a saw, cutting away at your life bit by bit.

Elementary

Each day feels like just another day: you wake up, play, and sleep. But every day that passes is one less day you have left, like a saw slowly cutting through a log. This kural says wise people notice this.

University

The couplet turns on an unmasking: “naal” (the day) displays itself (kaatti) as a neutral thing, an ordinary measure of time, but its real nature is a “vaal” (blade, commonly understood as a saw) that “eerum” (cleaves, saws through) life. The verb eer suggests the slow back-and-forth of a saw rather than a single cut, so life is shown being worn away in daily strokes. The conditional “unarvaarp perin” (if one finds those who understand) implies that such people are rare; most take the passing day at face value. Philosophically the kural anticipates Seneca's remark that we die daily and the Psalmist's prayer to ‘number our days’: time is not a container in which life takes place, but the very process by which life is used up.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari laments that with every rising and setting of the sun, life is shortened day by day, yet people, busy with their many affairs, do not notice time slipping away.
— Bhartrhari, Vairagyashataka (verse beginning ādityasya gatāgatair; numbering varies by edition) [3]
The poet observes that each passing day quietly takes something off a person's lifespan.
This is almost the Kural's own image: the ordinary day, marked by sunrise and sunset, is secretly cutting away at life.
Christianity
“Whereas ye know not what shall be on the morrow. For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away.”
— James 4:14 (KJV) [4]
James reminds his readers that life is a brief vapour and tomorrow is never guaranteed.
Both texts correct the illusion that time is plentiful: life is being used up day by day.
Islam
“By time, Indeed, mankind is in loss, Except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience.”
— Quran 103:1-3 (Sahih International) [5]
Surat al-Asr swears by time and declares that people are in loss as time passes, unless they fill it with faith and good deeds.
Time as a steady loss of life is the Kural's point; al-Asr adds the remedy the chapter goes on to give: good deeds.
Judaism
“So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.”
— Psalm 90:12 (KJV) [4]
The Psalm asks God to help people count their days so they grow wise.
The Kural's ‘those who understand’ are exactly those who have learned to number their days.
Buddhism
“Just as a cowherd drives the cattle to pasture with a staff, so do old age and death drive the life force of beings (from existence to existence).”
— Dhammapada 135 [6]
The Buddha pictures aging and death steadily driving life onward, as a herdsman drives cattle with a stick.
Both texts show time as an active force wearing life away, not a harmless backdrop.
Jainism
“Mankind is harassed by Death; it is taken hold of by Old Age; the days are called unfailing… The day that goes by will never return; the days elapse without profit to him who acts contrary to the Law.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.23–24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
Bhṛgu's sons explain that the days pass unfailingly while death and old age seize humanity, and each lost day is gone for ever.
Valluvar unmasks the ordinary day as a saw cutting away life; the Jain verses likewise reveal each passing day as irrecoverable life lost.
Sikhism
“Day by day, hour by hour, life runs its course, and the body withers away.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 692, Raag Dhanaasree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir describes life being whittled away unit by unit of time, days into watches and watches into hours, until the body is spent.
The Kural unmasks the day as a saw that cuts away at life; Kabir sees the same steady erosion of life in each passing day and hour.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod names the three Fates, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos, who spin, measure out and finally cut the thread of each mortal life.
— Hesiod, Theogony 217-219, 904-906 [9]
In Greek myth, every life is a thread that is spun, measured, and in the end cut by Atropos, ‘the one who cannot be turned’.
The Fates' shears and the Kural's saw both picture time as a blade working on the length of a life.
Philosophy
“What man can you show me who places any value on his time, who reckons the worth of each day, who understands that he is dying daily?”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 1.2 (Gummere translation) [10]
Seneca tells Lucilius that most people waste their days without noticing that each one is a part of their death already lived.
Seneca's ‘dying daily’ is the Kural's day-saw, and his rare person who ‘understands’ matches Valluvar's ‘those who understand’.

Real Life Example

Steve Jobs's Stanford commencement address on mortality
12 June 2005 · Stanford University, California, United States
Apple co-founder Steve Jobs gave the commencement address at Stanford University and devoted the third of his three stories to death. He told the graduates that for 33 years he had looked in the mirror each morning and asked whether he would want to do what he was about to do if it were the last day of his life, and that when the answer was no for too many days in a row he knew he needed to change something. He described a recent brush with a serious illness and urged them not to waste their limited time.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The speech became one of the most widely shared commencement addresses because it treated each ordinary day as a measured part of a limited life. It shows a practical habit of noticing that every day spent is a day used up.
The kural says the day only looks like a harmless unit of time, while the wise see it as a saw cutting away at life, and Jobs described a daily practice built on exactly that awareness.
