Kural 682

The Envoy · Thoodhu · தூது

அன்பறிவு ஆராய்ந்த சொல்வன்மை தூதுரைப்பார்க்கு இன்றி யமையாத மூன்று.
Anbarivu aaraaindha solvanmai thoodhuraippaarkku Indri yamaiyaadha moondru.

Meaning

Love for the king, good sense, and the skill to speak with carefully chosen words are three things an envoy must have.

Elementary

Imagine you are sent to ask the neighbours to give back your family’s ball. You need three things. You have to care about your family, you have to know exactly what to ask for, and you have to pick your words well so the neighbours say yes.

University

The couplet names three indispensable (iṉṟiyamaiyāta) qualities. Parimelazhagar makes each one specific: aṉpu is love for one’s own king, aṟivu is knowing what is good for him, and ārāynta colvaṉmai is careful speech before foreign kings. He explains the ‘examining’ (ārāytal) as choosing words the other side will agree to. From ‘indispensable three’ he infers that the ministerial virtues listed earlier are also needed. He also notes that 681 and 682 give the general marks common to both kinds of envoy the chapter goes on to separate. Solomon Pappaiah reads aṉpu as love of country, and Kalaignar gives a plain list. The couplet looks back to chapter 65 (Colvaṉmai, power of speech). On the text: the sandhi-joined text at thirukkural-thamizh.org runs the words together (அன்பறி வாராய்ந்த … தூதுரைப்பார்க் கின்றி யமையாத), with no change of wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
On first meeting Hanuman, Rama tells Lakshmana that someone who speaks like this must have mastered the Vedas and grammar, because in a long speech he made not one error, and his words would win over even an enemy with a raised sword.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda, sarga 3 [5]
Hanuman comes to Rama as Sugriva’s envoy. His devotion, knowledge and flawless, well-chosen speech make Rama trust him at once.
Hanuman has all three of the Kural’s essentials: love for his master, knowledge, and carefully chosen speech.
Christianity
“Now then we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God did beseech you by us: we pray you in Christ’s stead, be ye reconciled to God.”
— 2 Corinthians 5:20 (KJV) [6]
Paul describes apostles as ambassadors who speak on another’s behalf and plead with their hearers to be reconciled.
Like the Kural’s envoy, the ambassador is moved by love for the one who sends him (2 Cor 5:14) and has to speak persuasively.
Islam
“[Moses] said, ‘My Lord, expand for me my breast [with assurance] And ease for me my task And untie the knot from my tongue That they may understand my speech.’”
— Qur’an 20:25-28 (Sahih International) [7]
Before going to Pharaoh as God’s envoy, Moses asks for confidence and clear, understandable speech.
Moses treats power of speech as indispensable to the mission, just as the Kural does.
Judaism
Sent to Pharaoh, Moses protests that he is ‘slow of speech’. God appoints his brother Aaron to be his spokesman, so the message is delivered with both Moses’ knowledge and Aaron’s eloquence.
— Exodus 4:10-16 [6]
The Torah treats the mission to Pharaoh as needing a good speaker, and supplies one when the envoy himself lacks the skill.
It shows the Kural’s claim that speaking skill is indispensable, not optional, for an envoy.
Buddhism
“It’s a mendicant who learns and educates others. They memorize and remember. They understand and help others understand.”
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 8.16, Dūteyya Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [8]
The Buddha lists the qualities that make a monk fit to go on a mission, and names Sāriputta as having them.
Learning, understanding and the ability to make others understand match the Kural’s knowledge and discerning speech.
Jainism
“Then the King, after giving instructions, despatched a messenger, named Suvega, skilled in polity, eloquent, courageous, to Bāhubali.”
— Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra 1.5, Part 1, 'Negotiations with Bahubali' (Hemachandra, tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS 1931) [9]
When the Chakravartin Bharata sends an envoy to his brother Bahubali, Hemachandra's narrative picks a man described by exactly the qualities a messenger needs: knowledge of statecraft, eloquence and courage.
The Kural's three indispensables, love for the king, knowledge of what serves him and skill with words, closely match Suvega's knowledge of polity and eloquence, chosen for his master's mission.
Sikhism
Bhai Lehna, who became Guru Angad, is remembered above all for his loyalty to Guru Nanak; having understood his master's ideals, he gave them definite form and, as Guru, standardised the Gurmukhi script in which much of the scripture is written.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, Guru Angad [10]
Guru Angad's devotion to Guru Nanak, joined to an understanding of what his master intended and a gift for setting it down in words and script, made him Guru Nanak's chosen successor.
The Kural names love for one's master, knowledge of what serves him, and skill with words as the envoy's three essentials; Guru Angad's loyalty, understanding and work on the written word together show all three.
Greek Mythology
The Trojan elder Antenor recalls how Odysseus came to Troy as an envoy: he stood with his eyes fixed on the ground, but when he spoke, his words fell like winter snowflakes and no mortal could rival him.
— Homer, Iliad 3.203-224 [11]
Homer presents Odysseus as the model of an envoy’s eloquence.
It is the Kural’s ārāynta colvaṉmai, carefully weighed speech, at its best.
Philosophy
Aristotle says a speaker earns trust through three things: practical wisdom (phronēsis), virtue (aretē), and goodwill toward the audience (eunoia).
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.1 (1378a) [12]
For Aristotle, persuasion depends on the speaker’s character as well as the argument.
His goodwill, wisdom and skilled speech line up closely with the Kural’s love, knowledge and chosen words.

Real Life Example

Sergei Witte secures peace at Portsmouth without an indemnity
August to 5 September 1905 · Portsmouth, New Hampshire, United States
To end the Russo-Japanese War, President Theodore Roosevelt invited Russia's chief negotiator Sergei Witte and Japan's Baron Komura Jutaro to talks that were held at the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard. Under the treaty signed on 5 September 1905, Russia gave up its rights to Port Arthur and the southern half of Sakhalin Island but paid no indemnity to Japan. Witte was made a count for his efforts.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Witte came out of a lost war having protected the point his government cared about most, refusing any payment, while still finding terms the other side would sign.
The kural asks of an envoy loyalty to his ruler, knowledge of what serves him, and words chosen so that the other side will agree, and Witte's settlement at Portsmouth shows those three working together.
