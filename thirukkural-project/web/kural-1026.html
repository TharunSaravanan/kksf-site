Kural 1026

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

நல்லாண்மை என்பது ஒருவற்குத் தான்பிறந்த இல்லாண்மை ஆக்கிக் கொளல்.
Nallaanmai enbadhu oruvarkuth thaanpirandha illaanmai aakkik kolal.

Meaning

True manliness means taking charge of and caring for the family you were born into.

Elementary

Some people think being strong means winning fights. Valluvar says real strength is when you grow up and become the person your family can lean on, the one who takes care of things at home.

University

The couplet redefines nallaanmai (நல்லாண்மை, ‘good manliness, true valour’) by rhyming it with illaanmai (இல்லாண்மை, ‘mastery of the household’): real valour is aakkik kolal (ஆக்கிக் கொளல், ‘making one's own’) the governance of the home one was born in (thaan pirandha, தான் பிறந்த). Varadarasanar and Kesikan read it as taking on the ruling of one's birth-family; Pappaiah again extends it to house and country; Karunanidhi reverses the emphasis, saying the able ruler brings glory to the family of his birth. Pope gives ‘To him who gives his powers, the house from which he sprang to raise.’ The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is far better to discharge one’s prescribed duties, even though faultily, than another’s duties perfectly.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.35 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that a person's own duty, the one that falls to him by his station, is the one he must take up.
The Kural similarly asks a person to take up the duty of the house he was born into.
Christianity
“But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.”
— 1 Timothy 5:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul makes providing for one's own household a basic duty of faith.
Like the Kural, it treats taking charge of one's own family as the measure of a person.
Islam
“All of you are guardians and responsible for your wards and the things under your care. The Imam (i.e. ruler) is the guardian of his subjects and is responsible for them and a man is the guardian of his family and is responsible for them.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 893 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet teaches that every person is a shepherd responsible for those in his care, and a man for his family.
The Kural's ideal of ruling and caring for one's household is the same guardianship.
Judaism
After their father's death, Joseph reassures his frightened brothers that he will sustain them and their little ones, and he comforts them.
— Genesis 50:21 (Tanakh, via Sefaria) [6]
Joseph, now powerful, uses his position to provide for the whole family he was born into.
He embodies the Kural's ideal of making oneself the head and support of one's birth-family.
Buddhism
“Having supported me I shall support them, I shall do their duties, I shall keep the family tradition, I shall make myself worthy of my inheritance”
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (tr. Narada Thera, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha lists the duties a grown child takes on toward parents and the family line.
This is the Kural's ideal of taking charge of the household one was born into.
Jainism
The Kalpa Sutra tells that Mahavira, out of regard for his parents, resolved not to renounce the world while they lived, and became a monk only after their death.
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
Even the future Tirthankara honoured his duty to the family he was born into before leaving it.
It shows Jainism recognising the claim of one's birth-family that the Kural celebrates, though only as a stage before renunciation.
Sikhism
“He alone is a householder, who restrains his passions”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 952, Raag Ramkali, Ramkali ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Ramkali ki Vaar, Guru Nanak defines the true grihasti (householder) not by property or family but by self-mastery. Sikhism prefers the householder path to renunciation.
The Kural redefines true manliness as the mastery of one's own household; Guru Nanak redefines the true householder as one who first masters himself.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus returns to Ithaca after twenty years, drives out the suitors who are wasting his estate, and takes his place again as head of his father's house.
— Homer, Odyssey, Books 21-24 [10]
The hero's last and greatest labour is restoring order in his own household.
It dramatises the Kural's claim that true valour is mastery of one's own home.
Philosophy
“Thus it is clear that household management attends more to men than to the acquisition of inanimate things, and to human excellence more than to the excellence of property which we call wealth, and to the virtue of freemen more than to the virtue of slaves.”
— Aristotle, Politics I.12-13, 1259b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
After describing the head of the household as ruling his children like a king, by love and seniority (I.12), Aristotle concludes that running a household is chiefly about the excellence of its people, not about wealth.
Like the Kural, Aristotle treats leading and caring for one's household as a test of a man's character.

Real Life Example

Young Andrew Carnegie works to keep his family afloat
1848-1855 · Allegheny, Pennsylvania, United States
In 1848 the Carnegie family emigrated from Dunfermline, Scotland, to Allegheny, Pennsylvania, after mechanised looms ruined his father's handloom weaving. Young Andrew went straight to work as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill for $1.20 a week, then moved through better-paid jobs to the telegraph office; when his father died in 1855 he was the family's only breadwinner.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Carnegie took charge of the household he was born into while still a boy, the start of a career that later made him one of the world's richest men.
The kural calls true manliness making oneself the leader and support of the family one was born into, which is what young Carnegie did.
