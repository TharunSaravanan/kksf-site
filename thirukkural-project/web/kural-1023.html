Kural 1023

Family Maintenance · Kudiseyalvakai · குடிசெயல்வகை

குடிசெய்வல் என்னும் ஒருவற்குத் தெய்வம் மடிதற்றுத் தான்முந் துறும்.
Kudiseyval ennum oruvarkuth theyvam madidhatruth thaanmun thurum.

Meaning

When a person decides, 'I will lift up my family,' even the gods tuck in their clothes, ready for work, and hurry ahead to help.

Elementary

Have you noticed that when you really decide to clean your room, things suddenly seem to fall into place? Valluvar says that when someone truly decides to lift up their family, it is as if heaven rolls up its sleeves and runs ahead to help.

University

The striking image is madi tharru (மடிதற்று, ‘having tucked up the fold of its garment’), the gesture of someone girding up to work or run, so that theyvam (தெய்வம்) mun urum (முந்துறும், ‘comes forward first’). Mu. Varadarasanar glosses theyvam as oozh (ஊழ், ‘destiny, fate’), keeping with the Kural's chapter 38; Solomon Pappaiah reads it as a deity tightening its garment; Karunanidhi, rationalising, calls it the force of nature known as divine action. Pope gives ‘With vest succinct the goddess leads the way.’ The verb kudiseyval (‘I will make my family’) is a first-person vow, continuing 1021. The text is identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The demigods, being pleased by sacrifices, will also please you, and thus, by cooperation between men and demigods, prosperity will reign for all.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.11 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna describes a partnership in which human effort offered rightly draws divine help, and prosperity follows.
As in the Kural, the divine meets the person who first commits to the work.
Christianity
The Psalmist says that unless the LORD builds the house, those who build it labour in vain.
— Psalm 127:1 (KJV) [4]
The psalm teaches that human building succeeds only when God takes part in it.
It shares the Kural's sense that unseen help joins the family-builder, but it puts God first where the Kural puts the human vow first.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves.”
— Qur'an 13:11 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an ties God's change of a people's condition to the change they first make in themselves.
As in the Kural, divine help follows a human resolve.
Judaism
“do His will as though it were your will, so that He will do your will as though it were His.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 2:4 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabban Gamaliel teaches that a person who makes God's purposes his own will find God taking up his purposes in turn.
The Kural similarly promises that heaven runs ahead to help the one who commits to a worthy task.
Buddhism
“You yourselves must strive; the Buddhas only point the way.”
— Dhammapada 276 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha says no outside power does the work for you; teachers only show the road.
Buddhism agrees that everything starts with personal resolve, but unlike the Kural it promises no deity running ahead to help.
Jainism
“My own Self is the doer and undoer of misery and happiness; my own Self, friend and foe, according as I act well or badly.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The monk Anathi tells King Srenika that a person's own conduct, not any outside protector, makes happiness or misery.
Jainism, which has no creator God, reads the Kural's ‘theyvam’ as the fruit of one's own action, much as Varadarasanar glosses it as destiny.
Sikhism
“A disciple who walks one step towards Guru to take his refuge and goes to him with devotion and humility, Guru advances to receive him (devotee) by taking million steps.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabitt Savaiye (the kabitt beginning ‘Charan saran gur ek painda jai chal’), English translation as given in the BaniDB database on SikhiToTheMax [9]
Bhai Gurdas, the early Sikh scholar and scribe of the Adi Granth, says that the Guru comes a million steps forward to meet anyone who takes a single step toward him. His Kabitt Savaiye are early exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural says that when a person resolves to raise his family, fortune tucks up its garment and runs ahead to help; Bhai Gurdas says the Guru runs far ahead to meet the one who sets out.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop's fable of Hercules and the Waggoner, a carter whose wagon is stuck prays to Hercules without lifting a finger; the god tells him to put his shoulder to the wheel and whip his horses, and only then to call on him.
— Aesop's Fables, ‘Hercules and the Waggoner’ [10]
The god helps the one who is already working, not the one who waits.
It matches the Kural exactly: divine help comes running to the person who has started the work.
Philosophy
Terence's proverb that fortune helps the brave (fortes fortuna adiuvat), later repeated by Cicero and Seneca, holds that luck favours those who act boldly.
— Terence, Phormio 203 [11]
Roman moralists turned the line into a maxim that bold, committed action draws good fortune to it.
The Kural's fortune that hurries ahead of the committed family-builder is the same idea.

Real Life Example

Mary Anning's family finds the first complete ichthyosaur
1811-1812 · Lyme Regis, Dorset, England
After cabinetmaker Richard Anning died in 1810 leaving his family poor and in debt, his children Mary and Joseph kept collecting and selling fossils from the cliffs to support the household. In 1811 Joseph found a large fossil skull, and Mary later uncovered the rest of the skeleton; the specimen, recognised as an ichthyosaur, sold for 23 pounds and helped the family out of debt.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The find launched Mary Anning's career as one of the most important fossil collectors in the history of palaeontology.
The kural promises that when someone resolves to raise the family, fortune itself girds up and runs ahead to help, and the Annings' striking find came while they were working to keep their family afloat.
