Kural 1161

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

மறைப்பேன்மன் யானிஃதோ நோயை இறைப்பவர்க்கு ஊற்றுநீர் போல மிகும்.
Maraippaenman yaaniqdhoa noayai iraippavarkku ootruneer poala mikum.

Meaning

I would hide this pain of love from others, but like water in a spring that rises more the more you draw from it, it only grows the more I hide it.

Elementary

Think of a well in a garden: every bucket you pull out, the spring at the bottom fills it up again, even higher. The wife in this verse misses her husband, and she tries to keep her sadness a secret, but every time she pushes it down, it bubbles back up, bigger than before.

University

Chapter 117 belongs to the karpiyal (married love) and follows the husband’s departure in Chapter 116; its title joins padar (படர், the anguish of longing), melindhu (மெலிந்து, growing thin) and irangal (இரங்கல், lamenting), and commentators read most of its couplets as the heroine’s replies to her confidante (tōḻi), who urges her to bear the separation quietly. The image turns on iṟaippavarkku (இறைப்பவர்க்கு, ‘for those who bail or draw water’) and ūṟṟunīr (ஊற்றுநீர், spring water): concealment works like drawing from a spring, which only makes it flow faster. Man (மன்) is a particle of unrealised wish, ‘I would indeed hide it, but…’, and nōy (நோய், ‘sickness’) is the standard Kural word for love-pain. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both gloss ‘the more I hide it, the more it grows’, and Pope renders: ‘I would my pain conceal, but see! it surging swells, / As streams to those that draw from ever-springing wells.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In one of her best-known songs Mirabai cries that she has gone mad with love and that no one knows her pain, which only the one who has suffered such a wound can understand.
— Mirabai, pada ‘Hey ri main to prem diwani’ (paraphrase) [3]
Mira’s longing for Krishna overflows every attempt to keep it private, and she lets it pour out in song.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Mira finds that love-pain cannot be kept hidden; it rises and spills into the open.
Christianity
“Then I said, I will not make mention of him, nor speak any more in his name. But his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was weary with forbearing, and I could not stay.”
— Jeremiah 20:9 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah resolves to keep silent, but what he holds inside burns until he can no longer contain it.
The same failure of concealment as the Kural: the harder the speaker holds it in, the more it presses to come out.
Islam
“And he turned away from them and said, ‘Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,’ and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.”
— Qur’an 12:84 (Sahih International) [5]
Jacob holds in his grief for his lost son, yet it breaks out in his words and even whitens his eyes.
Jacob is a ‘suppressor’ of sorrow whose sorrow still shows, just as the Kural’s heroine hides a pain that keeps welling up.
Judaism
“I was dumb with silence; I held my peace, had no comfort; and my pain was held in check. My heart waxed hot within me; while I was musing, the fire kindled; Then spoke I with my tongue.”
— Psalms 39:3-4 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist tries to keep silent about his pain, but the restraint only makes it burn hotter until he must speak.
This is the Kural’s spring in another image: pain that is held in check grows until it breaks out.
Buddhism
“Whoever is overcome by this wretched and sticky craving, his sorrows grow like grass after the rains.”
— Dhammapada 335 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that sorrow fed by craving does not shrink on its own but keeps sprouting and spreading.
The Kural’s heroine finds her love-pain multiplying like spring water; the Dhammapada traces the same growth of sorrow to attachment itself.
Jainism
“O Gautama, in the innermost heart there grows a plant which brings forth poisonous fruit; how have you torn it out?”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 23.45 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the dialogue of Kesi and Gautama, the plant growing in the heart is ‘love of existence’, which Gautama has torn out by the roots.
The Kural’s heroine has a love growing in her heart that only spreads when hidden; the Jain image is the same hidden growth, though its cure is uprooting.
Sikhism
“That which is within them, comes out; it cannot be concealed by concealment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1243, Raag Saarang, Sarang ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak observes that whatever truly fills a person inside will surface, however hard they try to hide it.
The Kural's heroine tries to hide her love-pain, but it wells up like spring water the more she draws on it; Guru Nanak likewise says what is within cannot be hidden by hiding.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Hippolytus, Phaedra hides her love for her stepson until she is wasting away and refusing food, and her nurse finally draws the secret from her.
— Euripides, Hippolytus, opening scenes (paraphrase) [10]
Phaedra’s concealment does not quiet her passion; it consumes her body until it can no longer be kept in.
Phaedra lives out the Kural’s complaint: the love she hides grows stronger and shows itself despite her.
Philosophy
La Rochefoucauld observes that there is no disguise that can long hide love where it exists, nor feign it where it does not.
— La Rochefoucauld, Maxims, maxim 70 (paraphrase) [11]
For the moralist, love is one passion that resists every effort at concealment.
The heroine discovers exactly this: her attempt to hide love only reveals it.

Real Life Example

Emily Dickinson's unsent Master letters
c. 1858-1861 · Amherst, Massachusetts, United States
Emily Dickinson drafted three intensely emotional letters addressed only to an unnamed Master between about 1858 and 1861. The drafts were found among her papers after her death, and there is no evidence that finished versions were ever sent. Scholars still disagree about who the Master was, and some read the documents as a writing experiment rather than as real letters.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Feelings that Dickinson apparently never sent to anyone poured out on paper anyway, kept hidden in her own drawers.
The kural says that love-pain the heroine tries to hide only wells up the more, like a spring that rises as it is drawn, and Dickinson's secret drafts show strong feeling overflowing even while it was kept concealed.
