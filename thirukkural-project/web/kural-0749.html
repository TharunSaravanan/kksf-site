Kural 749

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

முனைமுகத்து மாற்றலர் சாய வினைமுகத்து வீறெய்தி மாண்ட தரண்.
Munaimukaththu maatralar saaya vinaimukaththu veereythi maanda tharan.

Meaning

A true fort becomes famous because its fighters are so skilled that they knock down the enemy right at the front line.

Elementary

A castle becomes famous not just because its walls are pretty but because of the brave defenders who stopped every attack at the gate. People tell stories about what they did there. Valluvar says a fort gets its greatness from the deeds done in its defense.

University

The couplet sets munaimukam (the battle-front) against vinaimukam (the field of action or operations): the enemy (maatralar) falls (saaya) at the front because of what the defenders do, and through those deeds the fort attains distinction (veeru eythi) and excellence (maanda). Pappaiah notes that the enemy is destroyed ‘as soon as battle begins’, and Kalaignar stresses attacks launched from within. Veeru, ‘pride, distinction, uniqueness’, suggests that a fort’s fame comes from the deeds done in its defense rather than from its stones. The couplet is thus the bridge between the structural couplets (742-747) and the conclusion in 750 that without vinai maatchi (excellence in action) no fort exists at all. The repetition of mukaththu (‘at the face of’) in both halves is a characteristic Kural device. The sandhi-joined text is identical in wording to the ytamizh.com reading.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Considering your specific duty as a kṣatriya, you should know that there is no better engagement for you than fighting on religious principles; and so there is no need for hesitation.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.31 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna reminds Arjuna that for a warrior, fighting in a righteous battle is the highest duty.
The Kural gives the fort’s glory to the warriors who fight at the front, and the Gita makes such fighting the warrior’s highest calling.
Christianity
“out of weakness were made strong, waxed valiant in fight, turned to flight the armies of the aliens.”
— Hebrews 11:34 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews praises the heroes of faith who became brave in battle and drove back foreign armies.
Like the Kural, it finds honor in defenders whose deeds in battle make the enemy flee.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah loves those who fight in His cause in a row as though they are a [single] structure joined firmly.”
— Qur’an 61:4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises believers who stand together in battle as solidly as a well-built wall.
The defenders themselves become the fortification, and their firm stand is what earns honor, as in the Kural.
Judaism
“As for the builders, each had his sword girded at his side as he was building.”
— Nehemiah 4:12 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition 2023, via Sefaria; KJV 4:18) [6]
Nehemiah’s builders worked on Jerusalem’s wall with swords at their sides, ready to fight.
The wall’s strength came from men ready to act at once, and it is their readiness that the Kural honors.
Buddhism
“Realizing that this body is as fragile as a clay pot, and fortifying this mind like a well-fortified city, fight out Mara with the sword of wisdom. Then, guarding the conquest, remain unattached.”
— Dhammapada 40 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha tells the practitioner to fortify the mind like a city and actively fight Mara from it.
As in the Kural, the fortified city is a base for active fighting, not just a place to hide.
Jainism
“Though a man should conquer thousands and thousands of valiant (foes), greater will be his victory if he conquers nobody but himself.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.34 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Nami answers Indra's call to subdue rival princes by ranking self-conquest above any number of battlefield victories.
The Kural praises a fort whose defenders win renown by felling the enemy at the battle-front; Jainism accepts that such victories happen but holds that the more glorious victory is won over oneself.
Sikhism
“He alone is known as a spiritual hero, who fights in defense of religion. He may be cut apart, piece by piece, but he never leaves the field of battle.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo (Salok), Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in Raag Maaroo, Kabir says that true heroism is recognised on the battlefield itself, in the one who fights for the right and never abandons his post.
The Kural says a fortress wins its fame from the deeds of its defenders at the very front of battle; Kabir likewise locates a warrior's renown in standing firm on the field.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Hector is the chief defender of Troy: his infant son is called Astyanax, ‘lord of the city’, because Hector alone protected Ilios, and his attacks from the city drive the Greeks back to their ships.
— Homer, Iliad 6.402-403 and Books 12-15 [10]
Troy’s glory in the epic rests on Hector’s deeds on the battlefield.
Hector is the Kural’s idea in a single hero: the city’s fame comes from its defender’s deeds at the front.
Philosophy
“A city will be well fortified which is surrounded by brave men and not by bricks.”
— Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 19.4 (trans. B. Perrin, LacusCurtius) [11]
In a saying attributed to Lycurgus, the Spartan lawgiver says that brave men are a better wall than bricks.
It anticipates the Kural’s conclusion that a fort’s worth lies in its defenders’ deeds.

Real Life Example

The defence of Rorke's Drift
22-23 January 1879 · Rorke's Drift, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa
During the British invasion of the Zulu Kingdom, after the British defeat at Isandlwana on 22 January 1879, the small garrison at the mission station of Rorke's Drift, led by Lieutenants John Chard and Gonville Bromhead, chose to stay and fight rather than retreat. They built barricades from bags of maize, biscuit boxes and other stores and held off attacks by a Zulu force several thousand strong from late afternoon until dawn. Eleven Victoria Crosses were awarded to the defenders.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The improvised post became one of the most celebrated actions in British military memory, remembered far more widely than the larger defeat that came just before it.
Valluvar says a fortress wins its fame through the deeds of its defenders at the battle front, and Rorke's Drift, not even a true fort, became famous solely for the stand made there.
