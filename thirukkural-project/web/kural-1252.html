Kural 1252

Reserve Overcome · Niraiyazhithal · நிறையழிதல்

காமம் எனவொன்றோ கண்ணின்றென் நெஞ்சத்தை யாமத்தும் ஆளும் தொழில்.
Kaamam enavondroa kannindren nenjaththai yaamaththum aalum thozhil.

Meaning

This thing called love has no pity. Even at midnight, it keeps giving orders to my heart.

Elementary

Think of a strict boss who makes you work even in the middle of the night while everyone else is asleep. The heroine says love is like that boss: it never lets her heart rest, not even at midnight.

University

The heroine personifies kāmam as a harsh ruler: kaṇ iṉṟu means ‘without kaṇṇōṭṭam’ (without kindly regard, pitiless), as Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it (கண்ணோட்டம் இல்லாதது), and yāmattum āḷum toḻil is ‘the work of ruling even at yāmam’, the dead of night. Solomon Pappaiah draws out the image of a master who makes a servant toil at midnight while all others sleep, which sharpens the chapter’s theme: niṟai cannot survive a power that never rests. The phrase eṉa oṉṟō (‘this one thing called…’) carries a note of exasperated contempt in the traditional commentary associated with Parimelazhagar. Sivayogi Sivakumar alone reads kaṇ iṉṟu as ‘standing in the eye’ (கண்ணில் நின்று), a minority interpretation. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
On a bright autumn night the gopis of Vraja hear Krishna’s flute and, leaving whatever they were doing, run to him through the dark, unable to stay at home.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.29 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Bhagavata shows love for Krishna taking command of the gopis precisely at night, overriding every household duty.
Like the Kural’s heroine, the gopis find that love rules their hearts at the hour when all others rest.
Christianity
“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh.”
— Song of Solomon 5:2 (KJV) [4]
The bride is asleep, yet her heart stays awake, listening for her beloved at night.
The Kural’s heroine also finds that her heart cannot rest at night; love keeps it at work.
Islam
Nizami tells how Qays, separated from Layla, roams the desert by night and day, so mastered by his love that he loses his name and is called Majnun, the madman.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (paraphrase) [5]
In the Persian romance, love becomes a ruler that leaves the lover no rest and no command over himself.
The Kural’s heroine says love rules her heart even at midnight; Majnun’s love rules him so completely that it costs him his reason.
Judaism
“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth; I sought him, but I found him not.”
— Song of Songs 3:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
In the night the woman of the Song cannot sleep; her longing sends her searching for the one she loves.
The Kural’s heroine likewise finds love at work in her heart in the middle of the night.
Buddhism
“Lord, as I was leaving home, a Sakyan girl — the envy of the countryside — glanced up at me, with her hair half-combed, and said, ‘Hurry back, master.’ Recollecting that, I don’t enjoy leading the holy life.”
— Udana 3.2, Nanda Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
Nanda, the Buddha’s half-brother, tells the Buddha that the memory of his betrothed has such a hold on him that he cannot keep up his monastic life.
Nanda’s heart, like the heroine’s, is governed by love even when everything around him calls for calm.
Jainism
“If you fall in love with every woman you see, you will be without hold like the Haṭha-plant, driven before the wind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati warns the monk Rathanemi that a mind ruled by desire has no anchor and is pushed wherever passion blows it.
The Kural’s heroine describes exactly such a mind: love rules it at every hour, and she has lost her hold.
Sikhism
“I am thirsty, thirsty for the Lord; the soul-bride gazes upon Him all through the night.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1232, Raag Saarang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes a bride so possessed by longing that she keeps watch the whole night instead of sleeping.
The Kural's wife complains that love is a pitiless master that rules her heart even at midnight when everyone else rests; Guru Nanak's bride is likewise kept awake all night by her thirst for the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
After first seeing Jason, Medea lies awake through the night: sleep will not come to her, her heart throbs like sunlight flickering on water in a pail, and she turns her choices over and over until dawn.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.744-801 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.C. Seaton) [10]
Apollonius gives one of ancient literature’s fullest portraits of a young woman whose love keeps her awake and torments her at night.
Medea’s sleepless heart is the Kural’s heart ruled by love even at midnight.
Philosophy
Socrates says the lover, parted from the beautiful one, is in such distress that he can neither sleep by night nor stay quietly in one place by day, but runs wherever he hopes to see the beloved.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato describes love as a restlessness that takes away the lover’s sleep and composure.
The Kural’s heroine feels the same: love will not let her heart rest even at midnight.

Real Life Example

Pliny the Younger writes to Calpurnia of his sleepless nights
early 2nd century CE · Rome, Italy
In a short letter to his wife Calpurnia, who was away from him, the Roman senator and author Pliny the Younger wrote that he missed her badly, partly from love and partly because they were not used to being apart. He told her that he lay awake much of the night thinking of her, and that at the hours when he used to visit her, his feet carried him to her room, where he turned away sad from the empty doorway. He said he found relief only in the hard work of pleading cases in court.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter is one of the best-known personal records from ancient Rome of a husband's longing during a separation, and scholars note how it borrows the language of Latin love poetry.
The kural says pitiless love keeps working on the heart even at midnight while others rest; Pliny describes exactly this, lying awake at night and drawn by longing to an empty room.
