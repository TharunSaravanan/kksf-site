Kural 1175

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

படலாற்றா பைதல் உழக்கும் கடலாற்றாக் காமநோய் செய்தஎன் கண்.
Padalaatraa paidhal uzhakkum kadalaatraak kaamanoi seydha-en kan.

Meaning

My eyes brought on a love-sickness bigger than the sea. Now they suffer the pain of not being able to sleep.

Elementary

Have you ever been so worried about something that you couldn’t fall asleep, even though you were tired? The lady’s eyes feel that way every night. Her missing-him feeling is so big, bigger than the ocean, that her eyes just can’t close.

University

The couplet sets two ‘cannot’ compounds against each other: paṭal āṟṟā, ‘unable to lie down (sleep)’, and kaṭal āṟṟā, ‘which the sea cannot hold’, so that the eyes are punished by sleeplessness for a love-sickness larger than the ocean. Paital uḻakkum is ‘are tormented by affliction’. Puliyur Kesikan adds that the eyes suffer ‘through this evil deed’, at night, while Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar stress that the sickness was caused by the eyes and they now cannot sleep. Pope: ‘The eye that wrought me more than sea could hold of woes, / Is suffering pangs that banish all repose.’ No variant readings were found between ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
During the monsoon at Kishkindha, Rama, parted from Sita, cannot rest; the clouds, the rain and the night only sharpen his longing, and he passes the season in sleepless grief.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda, Rama’s lament in the rainy season (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki depicts Rama’s separation from Sita as a sorrow that keeps him awake through the rainy nights.
Rama, like the Kural’s heroine, suffers a separation so vast that his eyes find no sleep.
Christianity
“Thou holdest mine eyes waking: I am so troubled that I cannot speak.”
— Psalm 77:4 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist describes a distress so deep that his eyes are held open through the night.
The Kural’s eyes are held waking in the same way, troubled beyond rest.
Islam
“He said, ‘I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know.’”
— Qur’an 12:86 (Sahih International) [5]
Jacob, rebuked for endlessly remembering Joseph, answers that he lays his unbearable grief before God alone.
His grief, like the heroine’s, is too large to be contained or put to rest; it can only be poured out.
Judaism
“Mine eye is poured out, and ceaseth not, Without any intermission,”
— Lamentations 3:49 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The poet of Lamentations describes eyes that weep without pause, day and night.
The Kural’s eyes likewise know no intermission: they cannot even lie down to sleep.
Buddhism
“Which is greater, the tears you have shed while transmigrating & wandering this long, long time — crying & weeping from being joined with what is displeasing, being separated from what is pleasing — or the water in the four great oceans?”
— Assu Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 15.3 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells his monks that the tears shed over separation from the beloved across countless lives exceed all the oceans.
The Kural’s love-sickness is likewise more than the sea can hold; the Buddha measures the grief of separation by the same ocean.
Jainism
“Desiring happiness and being submerged in the ocean of delusion, he forms many plans for warding off misery; and for their sake an impassioned man exerts himself.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.105 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana pictures the passionate person as sunk in an ocean of delusion, restlessly scheming to escape misery.
The Kural’s heroine is likewise sunk in a sea-sized love-sickness, too restless to sleep.
Sikhism
“My eyes long for the Blessed Vision of Your Darshan; the night passes, but I cannot sleep.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 542, Raag Bihaagraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes eyes that thirst for the sight of the Lord and so stay open through the whole night.
The Kural says the eyes that brought on a love-sickness greater than the sea are now tormented by being unable to sleep; Guru Arjan's eyes likewise long for the Beloved and cannot close all night.
Greek Mythology
After Eros’s arrow strikes her, Medea lies awake through the night, torn by love for Jason and fear for him, while all the city sleeps around her.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 3.744-824 (paraphrase) [10]
Apollonius shows Medea’s new love as a torment that will not let her sleep.
Like the Kural’s eyes, Medea’s are kept open by a love too great for her to contain.
Philosophy
Spinoza calls human weakness in moderating and restraining the emotions ‘bondage’: a person subject to the emotions is not his own master but is at the mercy of fortune.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part IV, Preface (paraphrase) [11]
On Spinoza’s view, strong passions overpower the mind and leave it unable to rest in its own power.
The heroine’s eyes are in exactly this bondage: a passion larger than the sea keeps them from sleep.

Real Life Example

Juliette Drouet's fifty years of daily letters to Victor Hugo
1833-1883 · Paris, France, and the Channel Islands
From 1833 until her death in 1883, the actress Juliette Drouet wrote to the writer Victor Hugo every day, producing about 22,000 letters over fifty years. Around 17,000 of them are now held by the Bibliotheque nationale de France and others by institutions such as the University of Leeds. Scholars describe her as Hugo's soulmate, collaborator, first reader and copyist, and her letters as a kind of journal of their relationship.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her letters are a record of a love so large that it filled a page or more every day for half a century.
The kural speaks of a love-sickness too great even for the sea to hold, and Drouet's ocean of letters, written day after day for fifty years, gives that size a measurable form.
