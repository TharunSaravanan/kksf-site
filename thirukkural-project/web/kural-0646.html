Kural 646

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

வேட்பத்தாஞ் சொல்லிப் பிறர்சொல் பயன்கோடல் மாட்சியின் மாசற்றார் கோள்.
Vetpaththaanj sollip pirarsol payankodal Maatchiyin maasatraar kol.

Meaning

Speaking so that others want to listen, and picking out the useful points from what others say, is the way of people with no faults.

Elementary

A good friend talks in a way you enjoy hearing, and when you talk, they listen for what you really mean instead of picking on small mistakes. This kural says the best people do both: speak well and listen well.

University

The couplet balances two actions: vēṭpat tām colli (oneself speaking so as to be desired) and piṟar col payan kōṭal (taking the payan, the fruit or meaning, of others’ words). Parimelazhagar adds that in hearing others one should grasp the substance while overlooking faults in their wording, and Solomon Pappaiah follows him. Māṭciyin mācaṟṟār, “those without stain in their excellence”, and kōḷ, “principle or settled practice”, present this as the rule of the most accomplished counsellors. It widens sol-vanmai from speaking to include skilled listening. The older edition reads பிறர்சொற் பயன்கோடன் in sandhi form.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Sanskrit tradition praises the haṃsa (swan) for its legendary ability to separate milk from water, and uses the haṃsa-kṣīra-nyāya as a maxim for taking the essence of what one hears and leaving the rest.
— Haṃsa-kṣīra-nyāya (Sanskrit maxim), as collected in Wisdom Library [3]
The swan is the model of a discerning listener who keeps the worth in others’ words and lets the rest go.
Taking the fruit of others’ speech while ignoring its faults is the swan’s skill applied to listening.
Christianity
“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath.”
— James 1:19 (KJV) [4]
James puts eager listening before speech as the mark of a mature believer.
Being swift to hear matches the Kural’s call to draw the meaning from others’ words.
Islam
“Who listen to speech and follow the best of it. Those are the ones Allah has guided, and those are people of understanding.”
— Qur’an 39:18 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those who hear what is said and take up the best of it.
Following the best of what one hears is almost the Kural’s piṟar col payan kōṭal.
Judaism
“A wise man does not speak before one who is greater than he in wisdom, And does not break into his fellow’s speech; And is not hasty to answer; He asks what is relevant, and he answers to the point.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:7, trans. Joshua Kulp [6]
The Mishnah lists the habits of a sage, which combine patient listening with relevant, well-aimed speech.
Listening without interrupting and answering to the point is the double skill the Kural credits to the flawless.
Buddhism
“That monk who has control over his tongue, is moderate in speech, unassuming and who explains the Teaching in both letter and spirit — whatever he says is pleasing.”
— Dhammapada 363, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
The Dhammapada describes a monk whose restrained, clear speech is welcome to all who hear him.
Speech that is pleasing because of its restraint and meaning matches the first half of the Kural.
Jainism
“Will those who are not born of a good family, no matter how much learning they have acquired, will they become sufficiently wise to pass over, without censuring, the faults which occur in the speech of others?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 32 'Experience in (conducting of) assemblies', stanza 10 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Naladiyar's chapter on conduct in assemblies treats overlooking the faults in other people's speech as a mark of the truly cultivated.
Parimelazhagar reads the kural's 'taking the fruit of others' words' as grasping their substance while passing over faults in their wording; the Naladiyar names exactly that generous listening.
Sikhism
“One who has a basket of fragrant virtues, should enjoy its fragrance. If my friends have virtues, I will share in them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 765, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak urges drawing out the fragrance of virtue wherever it is found and sharing in the good qualities of one's companions.
The Kural praises those who speak so others long to hear and who take the useful meaning from others' words; Guru Nanak likewise asks us to take in the good that others hold.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus pauses his tale of wanderings at the Phaeacian court, King Alcinous praises how gracefully and wisely he tells it, like a skilled bard, and begs him to keep going through the night.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.333-376 [10]
Odysseus speaks so well that his hosts want more, and he in turn has listened carefully to their customs and wishes.
A hearer begging for more is exactly vēṭpa, speech that makes people want to hear.
Philosophy
In his essay On Listening, Plutarch advises the young to listen to lectures by looking past a speaker’s style and faults of diction to take hold of what is useful, like a bee drawing honey.
— Plutarch, Moralia, De recta ratione audiendi (On Listening) [11]
Plutarch treats good listening as a skill: taking the benefit and ignoring the flaws.
This is almost exactly Parimelazhagar’s gloss: take the fruit of others’ words, overlook their faults.

Real Life Example

Mahalia Jackson's call and King's dream
28 August 1963 · Lincoln Memorial, Washington, D.C., United States
Martin Luther King Jr. was the final official speaker at the March on Washington, before a crowd of some 250,000 people, and he began by reading his prepared text. As he neared the end, the singer Mahalia Jackson called out to him to tell the crowd about the dream, a theme he had used at earlier events. King turned aside from his manuscript and spoke extemporaneously about his dream, and these improvised passages became the most celebrated part of the speech.
— Sources: [12] [13]
King was able both to hold a huge audience and to take in a single call from a friend nearby, seize its value and use it at once.
The kural praises those who speak so that others long to hear and who also grasp the useful meaning of what others say, and King's response to Jackson's call shows both skills in one moment.
