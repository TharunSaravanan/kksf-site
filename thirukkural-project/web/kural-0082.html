Kural 82

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

விருந்து புறத்ததாத் தானுண்டல் சாவா மருந்தெனினும் வேண்டற்பாற் றன்று.
Virundhu puraththadhaath thaanundal saavaa, Marundhu eninum vendarpaatru andru.

Meaning

Eating alone while a guest waits outside is not right, even if the food were a medicine that stops death.

Elementary

Imagine you were handed a magic sweet that would let you live forever. But your friend is waiting outside the door, hungry, and you shut the door and eat it alone. This kural says: then the magic sweet isn't worth having. What you eat matters far less than who you eat it with.

University

Valluvar builds the verse on a deliberately extreme hypothetical: சாவா மருந்து, the 'deathless medicine' (the amṛta of pan-Indian myth, the elixir gods themselves fight over). The rhetorical structure is a fortiori — if even immortality is not worth eating alone, then plainly nothing else is. புறத்ததா ('being outside') is doing precise ethical work: the fault is not refusing the guest outright but the far commoner domestic evasion of simply not noticing that someone is out there. Manakkudavar's tenth-century gloss defines the whole chapter as the rule that 'when strangers arrive at mealtime, one must divide and eat,' locating hospitality at the specific moment of consumption. The verse belongs to a widespread ancient horror of solitary eating, found equally in the Vedic yajña-remnant doctrine and in Greek sympotic ethics, but Valluvar states it with unusual economy: the guest's presence, not the food's value, determines whether a meal is worth taking.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The foolish man in whose house a Brahmana guest remains without food, all his hopes and expectations, all the merit gained by his association with the holy, by his good words and deeds, all his sons and cattle, are destroyed.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.1.8 (trans. Swami Paramananda) [4]
The Katha Upanishad opens with the boy Nachiketa left standing three nights at Death's door unfed; Death is warned that an unfed guest burns away everything a household has accumulated.
Both texts say that the guest left outside while the host eats annihilates the value of the household's goods.
Christianity
Jesus tells of a rich man who feasted sumptuously every day while a starving beggar, Lazarus, lay at his gate covered in sores, longing for the crumbs from the table; after death their positions are reversed and the gulf between them is fixed.
— Luke 16:19–31 (King James Version) [5]
The parable's whole moral weight rests not on any cruelty the rich man commits but on the gate — he ate well while someone hungry was just outside it.
This is the Kural's exact scenario: the fault is eating one's fill while another waits outside the door.
Islam
“And they give food in spite of love for it to the needy, the orphan, and the captive,”
— Qur'an 76:8 (Sahih International) [6]
Surah al-Insān praises the righteous precisely for handing over food they themselves want — the merit lies in the fact that they were hungry for it too.
Both verses locate virtue at the moment of the meal, when what one wants to eat is what one gives away.
Judaism
“Let all who are hungry come and eat.”
— Passover Haggadah, Ha Lachma Anya [7]
The Ha Lachma Anya declaration, recited in Aramaic near the very start of the Passover seder, has the household throw its door open before anyone eats a bite of the festival meal.
Ritually enacts the Kural's rule — the meal may not begin while someone hungry is still outside.
Buddhism
“If beings knew, as I know, the results of giving and sharing, they would not eat without having given, nor would the stain of selfishness overcome their minds. Even if it were their last bite, their last mouthful, they would not eat without having shared, if there were someone to receive their gift.”
— Itivuttaka 26 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha describes what ordinary people would do if they could actually see the karmic consequences of generosity — they would find solitary eating unthinkable.
Almost a restatement of the Kural: nothing on the plate, not even one's last mouthful, is worth eating alone while someone is there to receive.
Jainism
“How can a householder be not called greedy who does not offer food etc. to an ascetic, who is full of virtues and accepts gift (food) like a honeybee without causing harm to others, when he (the ascetic) visits his home?”
— Amṛtacandra, Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya v. 173 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2012), as quoted in the explanatory note to Ratnakaraṇḍaka-śrāvakācāra v. 113 on wisdomlib.org [9]
Amṛtacandra counts failing to feed a visiting ascetic as plain greed, and so as a form of himsa (harm). The next verse says that feeding him from the family's own meal loosens greed.
Like Valluvar, who says no food is worth eating alone while a guest stands outside, Amṛtacandra condemns the householder who keeps his meal while a worthy visitor is at the door.
Sikhism
Guru Amar Das made it a rule that anyone who wished to see him had first to eat in the langar, sitting alongside everyone else; tradition holds that even the Mughal emperor Akbar ate in the casteless langar before meeting him.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Guru Amar Das' (paraphrase) [10]
The third Guru made shared eating the precondition of every meeting, so that no one, however powerful, could be received while others went unfed or were kept apart.
It enacts the Kural's refusal to eat alone while a guest waits outside: at the Guru's court no one dined privately while others were left out.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus and his men enter the Cyclops' cave and claim the guest-right of Zeus; Polyphemus answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus, and proceeds to eat his guests instead of feeding them, gorging himself on milk and raw flesh while his visitors cower.
— Homer, Odyssey 9.105–542 [11]
Homer makes Polyphemus the definitional anti-host of Greek literature — solitary, herding alone, consuming everything himself and sharing with no one.
Polyphemus is the Kural's warning made monstrous: the man who eats while the guest stands unfed before him.
Philosophy
Seneca relays Epicurus's advice that one should consider very carefully with whom one eats and drinks before considering what one eats and drinks, since a meal of meat without a friend present is the life of a lion or a wolf.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 19.10, quoting Epicurus [12]
For the Epicureans, the value of a meal was set by the company; solitary feasting was regarded as a bestial, not a luxurious, condition.
Both hold that the presence of another at the table, not the quality of the food, is what makes a meal worth taking.

Real Life Example

The villagers of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon shelter refugees
1940-1944 · Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, France
Led by Pastor Andre Trocme, his wife Magda and Pastor Edouard Theis, the people of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon and the surrounding plateau sheltered an estimated 5,000 people between 1940 and 1944, about 3,000 to 3,500 of them Jews. Refugees who arrived at the door were housed in private homes, farms, hotels and schools, and residents forged identity and ration cards for them; Pastor Trocme and Pastor Theis were arrested and held for 28 days in 1943. In 1990 Israel recognised the inhabitants collectively as Righteous Among the Nations.
— Sources: [13] [14]
In a time of scarcity and danger, an entire community refused to keep its food and shelter to itself while strangers were left outside.
The kural says eating alone while a guest stands outside is not worth wanting even if the food were the elixir of life, and Le Chambon chose to share even its rations rather than turn strangers away.
