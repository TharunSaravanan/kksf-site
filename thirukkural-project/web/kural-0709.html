Kural 709

The Knowledge of Signals · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

பகைமையும் கேண்மையும் கண்ணுரைக்கும் கண்ணின் வகைமை உணர்வார்ப் பெறின்.
Pakaimaiyum kenmaiyum kannuraikkum kannin Vakaimai unarvaarp perin.

Meaning

To people who understand the different ways eyes look, the eyes alone will show if someone is an enemy or a friend.

Elementary

A dog can tell if you are friendly by the way you look at it. Soft eyes mean ‘friend’, a hard stare means ‘watch out’. Wise people learn to read eyes the same way, and the eyes tell them who is a friend and who is not.

University

Pakaimai (enmity) and kenmai (friendship, intimacy) are the two things kan uraikkum, ‘the eye will declare’. The condition is kannin vakaimai unarvaar, ‘those who perceive the varieties (vakai) of the eye’, i.e., the different kinds of look. Parimelazhagar applies it to foreign policy: if the king has ministers who can read the movements of the eye, the eyes of other kings and their envoys will themselves reveal hostility or friendship. Drew’s translation, following him, adds ‘(of foreign kings)’. Commentators link the verse with the Kural’s treatment of hidden enmity (ch. 89, Utpakai) and false friendship (ch. 83), where a smiling face may cover hostility: the eye is harder to disguise than the mouth. Sandhi text: பகைமையுங் கேண்மையுங் கண்ணுரைக்குங் கண்ணின் / வகைமை யுணர்வார்ப் பெறின்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Angry appearance when the courtier is in sight; evading or refusal to hear his speech; no inclination to give him a seat or to see him; change in syllables and accents while talking to him; seeing with one eye; brow-beating ...”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 5.5 (‘Time-Serving’), trans. R. Shamasastry [3]
Kautilya lists the signs by which a courtier can tell that the king has turned against him, many of them in the eyes and brows.
Kautilya catalogues the ‘varieties of the eye’ that, as the Kural says, reveal enmity to the trained observer.
Christianity
“And the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter. And Peter remembered the word of the Lord, how he had said unto him, Before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice.”
— Luke 22:61 (KJV) [4]
After Peter’s third denial, a single look from Jesus tells Peter everything, and he goes out and weeps.
A look carries a whole message of relationship without words, as the Kural says eyes speak friendship and estrangement.
Islam
“Hatred has already appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater. We have certainly made clear to you the signs, if you will use reason.”
— Qur’an 3:118 (Saheeh International) [5]
Believers are warned that hostile outsiders give away their enmity through outward signs, if one pays attention.
Enmity shows through outward signs to the attentive, which is the Kural’s claim about the eye.
Judaism
“And Jacob beheld the countenance of Laban, and, behold, it was not toward him as beforetime.”
— Genesis 31:2 (JPS 1917) [6]
Jacob reads in his father-in-law’s face that friendship has turned to hostility, and decides to leave.
Jacob does exactly what the Kural describes: he reads enmity in a face and acts on it.
Buddhism
The Buddha teaches Sigala how to tell false friends, such as the flatterer and the one who only talks, from true friends, by the signs in their behaviour.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta [7]
Friendship and enmity in disguise can be recognised by those who know what to look for.
Like the Kural, the sutta teaches that friend and foe can be told apart from their signs.
Jainism
Hemacandra tells how the serpent Caṇḍakauśika, reborn as a snake whose very look was poisonous because of anger carried over from former lives, turned its deadly glare on the wandering Mahavira. Mahavira's calm, unhostile gaze did not change, and the serpent was pacified and awakened.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 10 (Mahāvīra-caritra), ch. 3, part 10, 'Episode of Caṇḍakauśika' (tr. Helen M. Johnson; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The episode sets the look of hatred against the look of compassion and shows that anger carried in the soul comes out through the eyes.
It dramatises the Kural's claim that the eyes declare enmity or friendship to anyone who can read them.
Sikhism
“Bronze is bright and shiny, but when it is rubbed, its blackness appears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Suhi, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, Guru Nanak sang this hymn to Sajjan, a welcoming host who secretly robbed and killed travellers; the shining surface hid an inner blackness, and Sajjan repented.
The Kural says those who understand glances can tell an enemy from a friend; Guru Nanak saw the enemy beneath Sajjan's friendly surface.
Greek Mythology
“whereon Minerva went up to Ulysses and prompted him to beg pieces of bread from each one of the suitors, that he might see what kind of people they were, and tell the good from the bad”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Disguised as a beggar, Odysseus goes round the suitors to learn from their looks and conduct which are friendly and which hostile.
Odysseus is the Kural’s trained observer, sorting friend from foe by watching them.
Philosophy
“It is a point of cunning, to wait upon him with whom you speak, with your eye; as the Jesuits give it in precept: for there be many wise men, that have secret hearts, and transparent countenances.”
— Francis Bacon, Essays, ‘Of Cunning’ [11]
Bacon advises watching the eyes and face of the person you negotiate with, because even guarded people give themselves away there.
Bacon’s ‘secret hearts, transparent countenances’ is the Kural’s point that the eye tells what the tongue hides.

Real Life Example

Margaret Thatcher judges Gorbachev a man she can do business with
16-17 December 1984 · Chequers and London, England, United Kingdom
On 16 December 1984, British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher held long talks at Chequers with Mikhail Gorbachev, then a rising Soviet leader who had been mentioned as a possible successor in Moscow. In a BBC interview with John Cole the next day she said: I like Mr Gorbachev. We can do business together. She explained that he had been ready for full, detailed discussion rather than prepared statements, and her assessment became international news, a first Western verdict on the man who became Soviet leader in 1985.
— Sources: [12] [13]
From a face-to-face meeting Thatcher judged that a Soviet adversary could be dealt with as a partner, a judgement that proved important as the Cold War began to thaw.
The kural says that to those who understand the different kinds of glances, the eyes themselves reveal friend or foe, and Thatcher read from a personal encounter that Gorbachev could be a partner rather than simply an enemy.
