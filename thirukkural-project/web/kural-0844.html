Kural 844

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

வெண்மை எனப்படுவ தியாதெனின் ஒண்மை உடையம்யாம் என்னும் செருக்கு.
Venmai enappaduva dhiyaadhenin onmai udaiyamyaam ennum serukku.

Meaning

What is stupidity? It is the proud belief that says, “We are the wise ones.”

Elementary

A kid who says “I already know everything, I don’t need to listen” stops learning right there. The poet says that feeling of being sure you are the smartest is exactly what being foolish means.

University

The kural defines folly by a pair of light-words: venmai (வெண்மை, literally ‘whiteness’, i.e. blankness, emptiness of mind) is set against onmai (ஒண்மை, ‘brightness’, i.e. keen intelligence). Folly is not mere blankness but the serukku (செருக்கு, conceit, intoxicated pride) that claims brightness: ‘udaiyam yaam’, ‘we possess it’, with the royal or self-inflating plural. Mu. Varadarasanar equates venmai with pullarivu, the chapter’s key term; Pappaiah calls it a ‘delusion’ (mayakkam) and Kalaignar ‘arrogance’ (aanavam). Structurally the kural mirrors 831 in the preceding chapter (‘pedhaimai enbadhondru yaadhenin…’), giving a definition in question-and-answer form. Text identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Fools dwelling in darkness, wise in their own conceit, and puffed up with vain knowledge, go round and round, staggering to and fro, like blind men led by the blind.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.5 (Max Müller) [3]
Yama describes those who think themselves learned while remaining in ignorance.
‘Wise in their own conceit’ is precisely Valluvar’s definition of folly.
Christianity
“Seest thou a man wise in his own conceit? there is more hope of a fool than of him.”
— Proverbs 26:12 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs ranks the self-satisfied ‘wise man’ below even the plain fool.
Both texts locate the worst folly in thinking oneself wise.
Islam
“[They are] those whose effort is lost in worldly life, while they think that they are doing well in work.”
— Qur’an 18:104 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes people who are confidently wrong about the worth of their own deeds.
Their false certainty is the same conceit the kural names as folly.
Judaism
“Ah, / Those who are so wise— / In their own opinion; / So clever— / In their own judgment!”
— Isaiah 5:21 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition) [6]
Isaiah pronounces woe on those whose wisdom exists only in their own eyes.
Self-assessed brilliance is exactly the serukku Valluvar condemns.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says that self-awareness redeems a fool, but the conceit of wisdom seals his folly.
This is the same definition: real folly is believing oneself wise.
Jainism
“Clever talking will not work salvation; how should philosophical instruction do it? Fools, though sinking lower and lower through their sins, believe themselves to be wise men.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 6.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahavira mocks people who rely on fine words and doctrine without right conduct, and names as the mark of the fool his belief that he is wise.
Valluvar defines folly as the conceit that says 'we are wise', and the Uttarādhyayana gives the same definition: the fool sinks while he believes himself wise.
Sikhism
“O Nanak! Everyone speaks of Him, each one wiser than the rest. … O Nanak! One who claims to know everything shall not be decorated in the world hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib (pauri 21), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib Guru Nanak notes that everyone holds forth as wiser than the next, and warns that whoever claims to know everything will not be honoured hereafter.
The Kural defines folly as the conceit that says 'we are the wise ones'; Guru Nanak likewise names the claim to know everything as the mark that disgraces.
Greek Mythology
Arachne, a gifted weaver, boasts that her skill surpasses Minerva’s and refuses the goddess’s warning to be humble; she loses the contest of pride and is turned into a spider.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6 [10]
Her downfall comes from overrating herself and dismissing counsel.
The pride that says ‘we are the wise ones’ is the folly Valluvar defines.
Philosophy
“For he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know.”
— Plato, Apology 21d (Jowett) [11]
Socrates finds that his only advantage over a reputed wise man is that he does not mistake ignorance for knowledge.
The kural’s folly is the reputed wise man’s condition: claiming a wisdom he lacks.

Real Life Example

General Braddock spurns Native allies and is defeated at the Monongahela
9 July 1755 · Monongahela River, near present-day Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, United States
In 1755 General Edward Braddock led a British army trained in European parade-ground drill against the French at Fort Duquesne. He had very few Native American guides, a shortage sources link to his own treatment of the Native leaders he met, and he appears to have ignored intelligence on the French position offered through a Mohawk ally. On 9 July 1755 his advance column was attacked near the Monongahela River by a smaller French and Native force, more than half his men were killed or wounded, and Braddock was mortally wounded.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The defeat kept the Ohio Valley in French hands for three more years and made Braddock a lasting example of a commander undone by confidence in his own methods.
The kural defines folly as the arrogance that says we are the wise ones, and Braddock's reliance on his own European ways over local knowledge led his army into disaster.
