Kural 1051

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

இரக்க இரத்தக்கார்க் காணின் கரப்பின் அவர்பழி தம்பழி அன்று.
Irakka iraththakkaark kaanin karappin avarpazhi thampazhi andru.

Meaning

If you meet people it is right to ask, you may ask them for help. If they hide what they have and say no, the blame is theirs, not yours.

Elementary

Imagine you are really hungry and you politely ask a kind neighbour who has plenty of food for a little. If that neighbour pretends to have nothing, you did nothing wrong by asking. The person who hid the food is the one who acted badly.

University

The chapter on iravu (இரவு, 'asking, mendicancy') follows Nalkuravu (ch. 105, poverty) and is balanced by Iravachcham (ch. 107, dread of begging); together they permit asking only in real need and only of fitting givers. The key word irakka iraththakkaar (இரத்தக்கார், 'those of whom it is fit to ask') limits the permission: one may ask those able and willing to give. Karappin (கரப்பின், 'if they conceal') names the vice of hiding what one has, a word the chapter returns to again and again (karappu, karaththal); the commentarial line that follows Parimelazhagar, as reflected in Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan, takes the blame (pazhi) as falling wholly on the one who conceals. Pope renders it 'Theirs is the sin, not yours, if they the gift deny.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The man with food in store who, when the needy comes in miserable case begging for bread to eat, / Hardens his heart against him-even when of old he did him service-finds not one to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.2 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Vedic hymn 'Liberality' condemns the well-stocked man who hardens his heart against a hungry beggar and warns that he will himself be left without comfort.
Like the Kural, the hymn puts the fault on the one who withholds, not on the one who asks.
Christianity
“Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it. Say not unto thy neighbour, Go, and come again, and to morrow I will give; when thou hast it by thee.”
— Proverbs 3:27-28 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs forbids putting off a neighbour in need when one actually has what he asks for.
The Kural's karappu, pretending to have nothing while having it, is exactly what Proverbs forbids.
Islam
“And withhold [simple] assistance.”
— Qur'an 107:7 (Sahih International) [5]
Surah al-Ma'un ends its list of those it rebukes with people who refuse even small acts of help.
The Qur'an, like the Kural, places the fault on those who hold back help from someone who asks.
Judaism
“Beware lest you harbor the base thought, ‘The seventh year, the year of remission, is approaching,’ so that you are mean and give nothing to your needy kindred—who will cry out to God against you, and you will incur guilt.”
— Deuteronomy 15:9 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Torah warns that whoever refuses a needy kinsman out of meanness incurs guilt when the poor person cries out against him.
Deuteronomy, like the Kural, says the guilt falls on the one who refuses, not on the one who asks.
Buddhism
“Speak the truth; yield not to anger; when asked, give even if you only have a little. By these three means can one reach the presence of the gods.”
— Dhammapada 224 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha lists giving when asked, even from little, as one of three practices that lead to the heavens.
Dhammapada 224 holds the person who is asked responsible for answering the request, which is where the Kural puts the duty.
Jainism
“The hand (of the giver) is not always kindly stretched out to a monk when he is on his begging tour; but he should not think that it would be better to live as a householder.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Among the twenty-two hardships a Jain monk must bear, Mahavira lists having to ask for everything and meeting givers whose hands are not always open.
The Jain text admits what the Kural assumes, that some who could give will hold back, and it does not count asking as a fault in the one who asks.
Sikhism
“Lentils, flour and ghee - these things, I beg of You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 695, Raag Dhanasri, Bhagat Dhanna (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his aarti hymn in Dhanasri, Bhagat Dhanna, a farmer, asks God plainly for the simple food and goods he needs. In Gurbani the one fully fitting giver to ask is God.
The Kural permits asking when one meets someone it is fitting to ask; Dhanna asks without shame because he addresses the one Giver who is always fit to be asked.
Greek Mythology
“Your looks, my fine sir, are better than your breeding; if you were in your own house you would not spare a poor man so much as a pinch of salt, for though you are in another man's, and surrounded with abundance, you cannot find it in you to give him even a piece of bread.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Disguised as a beggar, Odysseus rebukes the suitor Antinous, who refuses him while sitting amid plenty.
The epic, like the Kural, puts the disgrace on the man who hides behind excuses and refuses, not on the beggar.
Philosophy
“If you have already given to anyone else, give to me also; if not, begin with me.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.49 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
Diogenes the Cynic, who begged when he was poor, used this formula to ask for alms without apology.
Diogenes asked openly, as if asking a fitting person were no disgrace, which is the Kural's own position.

Real Life Example

Canada turns away the passengers of the Komagata Maru
23 May - 23 July 1914 · Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada
The steamship Komagata Maru arrived in Vancouver harbour on 23 May 1914 carrying 376 South Asian passengers who hoped to settle in Canada. Under the immigration laws of the day the government refused almost all of them entry, and after two months aboard the ship was escorted out of the harbour by the Canadian military on 23 July and forced to return to India. In May 2016 Prime Minister Justin Trudeau formally apologised in the House of Commons, saying the Canadian government had been responsible for the laws that kept the passengers out.
— Sources: [12] [13]
More than a century later the Canadian state accepted that the fault lay with the laws and officials who refused the passengers, not with the people who had come asking to be admitted.
The kural says one may ask those whom it is fitting to ask, and if they withhold, the blame is theirs and not the asker's, and Canada's formal apology placed the blame squarely on those who refused.
