Kural 892

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

பெரியாரைப் பேணாது ஒழுகிற் பெரியாரால் பேரா இடும்பை தரும்.
Periyaaraip penaadhu ozhukir periyaaraal peraa itumpai tharum.

Meaning

If you act without respect for great people, they will bring you trouble that never goes away.

Elementary

If you keep ignoring your coach and being rude, you might find yourself benched for the whole season. Not treating important people with respect can bring trouble that doesn’t go away quickly.

University

The couplet turns on the repeated periyaar (பெரியார், the great): conduct (ozhugal, ஒழுகல்) that does not penu (பேணு, cherish, honour) the great yields, through those same great ones, peraa idumbai (பேரா இடும்பை, affliction that does not depart). The adjective peraa (from pe-, to move away) stresses permanence, which Mu. Varadarasanar renders as நீங்காத துன்பம். Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar read it simply as disrespect to elders or great persons, while the traditional reading behind Pope’s explanation glosses periyaar as powerful rulers. The phrase periyaaraal (by the great) shows that the harm is not mere fate but the active response of the offended party. Both sources agree on the text, including the sandhi form ஒழுகிற்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s Abhijnanashakuntalam, Shakuntala, lost in thoughts of Dushyanta, fails to greet the visiting sage Durvasa, who curses her that her beloved will forget her; much of the play’s suffering follows.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 4 (Durvasa’s curse) [3]
A single lapse in honouring a great ascetic brings a long chain of sorrow that is only lifted much later.
This is the Kural’s peraa idumbai: lasting trouble that comes from the very great one who was neglected.
Christianity
“Whosoever therefore resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God: and they that resist shall receive to themselves damnation.”
— Romans 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul teaches that opposing rightful authority brings judgment upon the one who resists.
Like the Kural, it warns that defying those in power returns as heavy consequence on oneself.
Islam
“O you who have believed, do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet or be loud to him in speech like the loudness of some of you to others, lest your deeds become worthless while you perceive not.”
— Qur’an 49:2 (Saheeh International) [5]
Believers are warned that even careless disrespect toward the Prophet can quietly nullify their good deeds.
As in the Kural, failing to honour the great carries a serious and lasting loss, sometimes unnoticed until too late.
Judaism
“Curse not the king, no not in thy thought; and curse not the rich in thy bedchamber: for a bird of the air shall carry the voice, and that which hath wings shall tell the matter.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:20 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet advises against even private contempt for the powerful, since word of it will reach them.
The Kural likewise warns that disregard for the great returns as trouble from the great themselves.
Buddhism
“Not to associate with the foolish, but to associate with the wise; and to honor those who are worthy of honor — this is the greatest blessing.”
— Maha-mangala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.4 (trans. Narada Thera) [6]
The Buddha lists honouring those worthy of honour among the highest blessings of life.
The Kural states the reverse side: failing to honour the great invites lasting harm.
Jainism
“O lord of the fair hill-land resounding with streams! we should not, thinking they will forgive us, do what is hateful to the guiltless, for none can remove their anger when once they are provoked.”
— Nāladiyār 17.1 (English translation serialised in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The first quatrain of the Nāladiyār's chapter 'Against reproaching the great' warns against wronging the worthy on the assumption that they will forgive, because their anger once roused cannot be undone.
The Kural says disregard for the great brings affliction that will not leave, and the Jain Tamil quatrain names the same lasting, irremovable consequence.
Sikhism
“One who slanders the Saints, O Nanak! Shall be reincarnated over and over again.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa, as given on srigranth.org) [8]
The shalok opening the thirteenth ashtapadi of Sukhmani Sahib says that contempt for the holy brings suffering that keeps returning, birth after birth.
The Kural says disregard for the great brings lasting trouble that will not go away, and Guru Arjan likewise describes a penalty that keeps coming back.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Agamemnon rudely dismisses Chryses, priest of Apollo, who comes to ransom his daughter; Apollo answers the priest’s prayer with a plague on the Greek camp.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 1 [9]
Insulting a man under divine protection brought sustained disaster on the whole army until the wrong was put right.
Agamemnon’s contempt for a great one’s claim produced the Kural’s trouble that comes through the great.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Apology, Socrates tells the Athenian jurors that if they put him to death they will harm themselves more than him, and will not easily find another like him.
— Plato, Apology 30c-31a [10]
Socrates argues that a city which mistreats its wisest citizen injures itself most of all.
The Kural similarly holds that disregarding the great rebounds as lasting harm on the offender.

Real Life Example

Einstein signs the letter warning Roosevelt about atomic bombs
2 August 1939 · Long Island, New York, United States
Albert Einstein left Germany after the Nazis came to power in 1933 and settled in the United States, and the Hungarian-born physicist Leo Szilard also left Germany in 1933 because of rising anti-Semitism. In 1939, fearing that Germany might build an atomic bomb, Szilard drafted a letter dated 2 August 1939 that Einstein signed, warning President Franklin D. Roosevelt that uranium fission could lead to extremely powerful bombs. Delivered by Alexander Sachs in October, the letter led Roosevelt to set up the Advisory Committee on Uranium, a step towards the Manhattan Project.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Scientists whom Nazi Germany had driven out played a key part in starting the effort that gave its enemies the decisive weapon of the war.
The kural warns that disregarding the great brings lasting trouble at their hands, and Germany's treatment of its own scientists turned their talents to the service of its foes.
