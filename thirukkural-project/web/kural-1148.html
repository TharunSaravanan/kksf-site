Kural 1148

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

நெய்யால் எரிநுதுப்பேம் என்றற்றால் கௌவையால் காமம் நுதுப்பேம் எனல்.
neyyaal erinhudhuppaem endratraal kavvaiyaal Kaamam nudhuppeam enal

Meaning

Thinking that gossip can put out love is like thinking you can put out a fire by pouring ghee (melted butter) on it.

Elementary

If you pour water on a fire, it goes out. But if you pour oil or butter on it, it flares up even higher! The girl in this verse says people who try to stop love by gossiping are making the same mistake: they are pouring butter on the fire.

University

Parimelazhagar marks this ‘itu um atu’, continuing the heroine’s speech of 1147. Nutuppēm (நுதுப்பேம்) means ‘we shall extinguish’; ney (நெய்), ‘ghee, clarified butter’, is the classic accelerant of the sacrificial fire. He notes that the third-case endings (-āl) are instrumental and sums up: love cannot be extinguished by rumour, which is the very cause of its flaring up. Manakkudavar assigns the couplet to the tōḻi, speaking when the hero still wants to continue the secret affair. The comparison of desire to fire fed with ghee is widespread in Indian literature, notably in Manu and in the story of Yayati, which gives this couplet unusually direct parallels. The text agrees in all three sources apart from the கெளவை spelling, normalised here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Desire is never extinguished by the enjoyment of desired objects; it only grows stronger like a fire (fed) with clarified butter.”
— Manusmriti 2.94 (tr. Georg Bühler, SBE 25) [4]
Manu uses the image of ghee poured on fire to show that feeding desire only makes it burn hotter.
The Kural uses the very same image: trying to put out love with gossip is like trying to put out fire with ghee.
Christianity
“But sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence. For without the law sin was dead.”
— Romans 7:8 (KJV) [5]
Paul observes that a prohibition, instead of killing desire, can stir it up.
The Kural says the same of gossip: what was meant to stifle love only inflames it.
Islam
In Nizami’s Layla and Majnun, Majnun’s father takes him to the Kaaba to pray to be cured of his love, but Majnun instead prays that his love may grow ever stronger.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (1188), the pilgrimage to Mecca (paraphrase) [6]
The attempt to extinguish Majnun’s love only makes him ask for more of it.
This is the Kural’s point dramatised: efforts to put out love become fuel for it.
Judaism
“Many waters cannot quench love, Neither can the floods drown it; If a man would give all the substance of his house for love, He would utterly be contemned.”
— Song of Songs 8:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [7]
The Song of Songs insists that nothing outside love can put it out.
The Kural says the same with a sharper image: not water but ghee, gossip that only feeds the flame.
Buddhism
In the Fire Sermon the Buddha declares that the eye, its objects and everything experienced through the senses are burning, burning with the fire of passion, aversion and delusion.
— Samyutta Nikaya 35.28 (Adittapariyaya Sutta, the Fire Sermon) (paraphrase) [8]
The Buddha pictures sensual life as a fire that keeps burning on what the senses feed it.
The Kural, too, sees love as a fire that the town’s gossip only feeds; the Buddha’s remedy is to stop feeding it altogether.
Jainism
“The more you get, the more you want; your desires increase with your means. Though two mâshas would do to supply your want, still you would scarcely think ten millions sufficient.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 8.17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
Kapila, who began by wanting two pieces of gold for the girl he loved, discovers that each satisfaction only enlarges desire.
Like ghee poured on fire, what was meant to satisfy Kapila’s love-driven wish only made it blaze higher, which is the Kural’s point about love.
Sikhism
“as fire is not satisfied by fuel.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 280, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan compares the slanderer's restless envy to a fire that is never satisfied however much fuel is fed to it.
The Kural says hoping to quench love with gossip is like pouring ghee on a fire; Gurbani uses the same image of fire that only grows when fed, here for the talk of the slanderer.
Greek Mythology
Ovid’s Pyramus and Thisbe are forbidden by their fathers to marry, but the prohibition cannot stop their love: the more it is covered up, the more the hidden fire burns.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.55-64 (Pyramus and Thisbe) (paraphrase) [11]
Parental prohibition and concealment only make the young lovers’ passion fiercer.
The Kural says the same of gossip: what is meant to put out love only makes it burn harder.
Philosophy
La Rochefoucauld writes that absence lessens moderate passions and increases great ones, as the wind blows out candles and fans a fire.
— La Rochefoucauld, Maxims, no. 276 (paraphrase) [12]
The same obstacle can extinguish a small feeling and inflame a great one.
The town’s gossip is such a wind: on the Kural’s great love it acts like fuel on a fire, not like a breath on a candle.

Real Life Example

The Lovings' marriage outlasts banishment and reaches the Supreme Court
1958-1967 · Caroline County, Virginia, and Washington, D.C., United States
Richard and Mildred Loving married in Washington, D.C., on 2 June 1958, were arrested at home in Virginia under its ban on interracial marriage, and in January 1959 were sentenced to a year in prison, suspended on condition that they leave the state. They moved to Washington but kept fighting, and the American Civil Liberties Union took up their case. On 12 June 1967 the United States Supreme Court unanimously struck down Virginia's law.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Punishment and public condemnation did not end the marriage; instead the couple's struggle changed the law of the whole country.
The kural says that trying to put out love with gossip is like pouring ghee on a fire; every attempt to break the Lovings apart only made their marriage a larger cause.
