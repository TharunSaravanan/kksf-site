Kural 109

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

கொன்றன்ன இன்னா செயினும் அவர்செய்த ஒன்றுநன்று உள்ளக் கெடும்.
Kondranna innaa seyinum avarseydha Ondrunandru ullak kedum.

Meaning

Even if someone who once helped you later hurts you terribly, like a killing blow, the pain goes away when you remember the one good thing they did.

Elementary

Suppose a friend who once caught you when you were falling later does something that really hurts you. If you remember the time they caught you, the hurt shrinks. One good memory can melt a big hurt.

University

கொன்றன்ன இன்னா, 'an injury like killing', is the strongest possible provocation, and the verb கெடும் ('will perish') leaves its subject implicit: commentators take it to be the injury, or the pain and resentment it causes, that is destroyed merely by recalling (உள்ள) one (ஒன்று) earlier good (நன்று). The kural thus makes gratitude an active remedy rather than merely a debt: memory of a past benefit is prescribed as the means of dissolving present grievance. Valmiki's portrait of Rama in Ayodhya Kanda 2.1.11, gladdened by a single good deed and forgetting a hundred wrongs, is close enough to suggest a shared South Asian ethical ideal. A concrete legal-historical parallel is the Prophet Muhammad's pardon of Hatib ibn Abi Balta'a, whose betrayal was weighed against his earlier service at Badr.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That Rama, because of his good bent of mind, feels glad even by whatever way a good thing is done to him. He does not remember any number of bad things done to him.”
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda 2.1.11 (K. M. K. Murthy trans.) [3]
Valmiki describes Rama as someone who is made happy by even one good thing done for him and simply does not keep count of the wrongs done to him.
One remembered good cancelling many injuries is the very mechanism of this Kural.
Christianity
After Peter had three times denied knowing Jesus on the night of his arrest, the risen Jesus meets him by the Sea of Galilee, asks him three times whether he loves him, and restores him with the charge to feed his sheep.
— John 21:15-17 (KJV) [4]
Instead of punishing Peter for his betrayal, Jesus remembers Peter's love and loyalty and gives him back his place.
A painful betrayal is overcome by recalling the good bond that came before it, close to the Kural's remedy.
Islam
When Hatib ibn Abi Balta'a was caught sending a secret letter warning the Meccans of the Prophet's plans, Umar asked permission to execute him as a traitor; the Prophet refused, saying that Hatib had fought at Badr and that God may have looked upon the people of Badr and forgiven them.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3007 [5]
A companion committed a serious betrayal that could have cost lives, but the Prophet pardoned him because of the great service he had once given at the Battle of Badr.
One remembered good (Badr) outweighed an injury as grave as treason, exactly the Kural's teaching.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not abhor an Edomite; for he is thy brother: thou shalt not abhor an Egyptian; because thou wast a stranger in his land.”
— Deuteronomy 23:7 (KJV; 23:8 in Hebrew numbering) [4]
Even though Egypt later enslaved and oppressed the Israelites, the Torah forbids hating Egyptians, because Egypt once gave Jacob's family refuge in a famine.
The earlier good (shelter) outweighs even the later near-deadly harm (slavery), precisely the Kural's teaching.
Buddhism
“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”
— Dhammapada 5 (Acharya Buddharakkhita trans.) [6]
The Buddha teaches that you can never end hatred by hating back; only letting go of hatred ends it.
Like the Kural, it seeks to dissolve the pain of injury inwardly rather than answer it.
Jainism
“Even though they do what is disagreeable, one should preserve as gold those who ought not to be forsaken. Daily do men seek for fire and keep it in their house, though it has consumed both their good house and gold.”
— Nālaḍiyār ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', st. 5 (= st. 225), English translation in The Indian Antiquary (wisdomlib.org) [7]
In its chapter on bearing with the faults of others, the Nālaḍiyār teaches that those bound to us by past good are kept, like the household fire, even after they have done us grave harm.
Valluvar says even a near-mortal injury from a former benefactor fades when one recalls their one good deed, and the Nālaḍiyār likewise lets past worth outweigh present harm.
Sikhism
“He obtains ten things, and puts them behind him; for the sake of one thing withheld, he forfeits his faith. But what if that one thing were not given, and the ten were taken away? Then, what could the fool say or do?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 268, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes the ungrateful person who forgets ten gifts because of one thing he did not receive.
This is the Kural's moral calculus turned inside out: where the grateful let one remembered kindness erase a deadly injury, the ingrate lets one grievance erase ten kindnesses.
Greek Mythology
During the slaughter of the suitors, Telemachus begs Odysseus to spare the herald Medon, who had served in the suitors' company but had cared for Telemachus when he was a child; Odysseus spares him, along with the bard Phemius.
— Homer, Odyssey 22.354-380 [9]
In the middle of his revenge, Odysseus lets one of the suitors' servants live because that man had once looked after his son.
An old kindness saves a man even when he stands on the side of those who did deadly harm, echoing the Kural.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that a person who lives by the guidance of reason tries, as far as possible, to repay another's hatred, anger, or contempt with love or nobleness, because hatred answered with hatred only increases.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part 4, Proposition 46 [10]
The philosopher says a reasonable person answers hurt with kindness instead of revenge, since revenge just makes hatred grow.
Both texts aim to neutralise injury rather than retaliate, though the Kural's method is remembering a past kindness.

Real Life Example

Akbar pardons his former regent Bairam Khan
1560 - 1561 · Mughal Empire, northern India
Bairam Khan, a general and friend of Emperor Humayun, guided the young Akbar as regent after he inherited the throne at the age of 13 in 1556. In 1560 Akbar took the government into his own hands, and Bairam rebelled for a time before submitting; Akbar forgave him and offered him the choice of a high place in the army or an escort for a pilgrimage to Mecca, and Bairam chose the pilgrimage. Bairam was killed by Afghans on the way in 1561, and Akbar took his four-year-old son Abd ur-Rahim into his household; the boy grew up to reach the highest rank in the Mughal hierarchy.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Akbar treated a rebel who had once secured his throne with mercy and later raised the rebel's orphaned son, rather than letting the rebellion cancel the earlier service.
The kural says even a grievous injury from a former benefactor fades when one remembers the good he once did; Akbar's pardon of Bairam Khan shows past service outweighing present offence.
