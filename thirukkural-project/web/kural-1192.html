Kural 1192

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

வாழ்வார்க்கு வானம் பயந்தற்றால் வீழ்வார்க்கு வீழ்வார் அளிக்கும் அளி.
Vaazhvaarkku vaanam payandhatraal veezhvaarkku veezhvaar alikkum ali.

Meaning

The kindness a lover shows to the one who loves him is like rain from the sky to those whose lives depend on it.

Elementary

Think of a thirsty garden in summer. When the rain finally comes, every leaf perks up. When someone you love is kind to you, your heart perks up the same way.

University

The couplet sets the beloved’s aḷi (grace, tender kindness) beside the rain that the sky (vāṉam) yields (payanta) to living beings (vāḻvār), echoing the Kural’s own chapter on rain (vāṉ ciṟappu, ch. 2). Parimelazhagar and the modern commentators take vāḻvār as ‘those who live by it’ and insist on timeliness: Pappaiah stresses rain at the proper season, and Kalaignar reads it as lovers meeting at the right time. The point in this chapter is implicit contrast: the heroine is the parched field to whom no such rain is coming. Pope: ‘As heaven on living men showers blessings from above, / Is tender grace by lovers shown to those they love.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna teaches that living beings are sustained by food, food is produced from rain, and rain comes from sacrifice, so that the whole order of life hangs on the rain that falls in due season.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.14 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita presents rain as the link on which all living beings depend.
The Kural borrows exactly this dependence of life on rain to describe how a loving spouse’s kindness keeps the beloved alive.
Christianity
“he shall come unto us as the rain, as the latter and former rain unto the earth.”
— Hosea 6:3 (KJV) [4]
Hosea pictures the coming of the beloved Lord to his people as seasonal rain on dry ground.
Like the Kural, Hosea likens the arrival of love to rain that revives those who wait for it.
Islam
The Qur’an names as one of God’s signs that He created spouses for people so that they may find tranquillity in them, placing affection and mercy between them; a few verses later it names as another sign the rain He sends down to give life to the earth after its death.
— Qur’an 30:21 and 30:24 (paraphrase; cf. Saheeh International) [5]
Surah ar-Rum sets marital affection and life-giving rain side by side among the signs of divine care.
The Kural joins the same two signs into one simile: a spouse’s kindness is rain to the one who lives by it.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that any man who has no wife lives without joy, without blessing and without goodness.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 62b (paraphrase) [6]
The rabbis treat the love of a spouse as the source from which joy and blessing reach a person’s life.
As the Kural says love is rain to the one who lives by it, the Talmud says a life without that love is dry of joy and blessing.
Buddhism
In the Dhaniya Sutta a contented cowherd, his roof thatched and his fire lit, tells the sky it may rain if it likes, praising his wife as faithful, long his companion and pleasing to him; the Buddha answers each verse with his own freedom from all such supports.
— Sutta Nipata 1.2, Dhaniya Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The herdsman’s security rests on a loving wife and on rain that can do him no harm, while the Buddha points beyond both.
The Kural’s rain-and-love pairing appears here too, though the Buddhist sutta finally sets both aside in favour of non-attachment.
Jainism
In the Tamil Jain epic Jivaka Chintamani, the hero Jivaka wins and marries a series of brides, and the poet dwells on the mutual delight of each newly united couple before Jivaka finally renounces the world.
— Tiruttakkatevar, Jivaka Chintamani (paraphrase of the epic’s marriage episodes) [8]
The Jain epic describes reciprocal married love with real warmth, while framing it as a stage to be outgrown.
Its scenes of shared delight answer the Kural’s picture of a lover’s kindness falling like rain, though the Jain poem ends in renunciation.
Sikhism
“Just as the earth looks beautiful when the rain falls, so does the Sikh blossom forth meeting the Guru.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 758, Raag Soohee, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das compares the Sikh's joy on meeting the Guru to the dry earth turning green and beautiful under rain.
The Kural says a lover's kindness to the one who loves him is like rain to those whose life depends on it; Gurbani uses the same rain image for the Guru's grace to the longing Sikh.
Greek Mythology
In a fragment of Aeschylus’ lost Danaids, Aphrodite says the holy sky longs to wed the earth and the earth longs for union; rain falls from the bridegroom sky and the earth conceives pasture for flocks and grain for mortals.
— Aeschylus, Danaids, fragment 44 (Radt), preserved in Athenaeus, Deipnosophists 13.600b (paraphrase) [10]
Aphrodite herself presents rain as the act of love by which heaven gives life to the earth.
This is the Kural’s simile run in reverse: rain is love, as for the Kural love is rain.
Philosophy
Boethius’ Lady Philosophy sings that it is love which holds earth and sea together and rules the heavens, keeping the seasons in their order; if love slackened its reins, everything now bound in concord would fall into war.
— Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2, metre 8 (paraphrase) [11]
Boethius makes love the force that keeps the whole cosmos, seasons included, alive and in order.
The Kural’s comparison of love to life-sustaining rain fits Boethius’ claim that love is what keeps the world going.

Real Life Example

Robert Browning's letters reach the invalid Elizabeth Barrett
January 1845 – September 1846 · London, England
For years the poet Elizabeth Barrett lived as a chronic invalid, mostly confined to her room at 50 Wimpole Street. In January 1845 Robert Browning wrote to tell her he loved her verses with all his heart, and over the next twenty months the two exchanged 574 letters. They married secretly at St Marylebone Parish Church on 12 September 1846 and left for Italy, where her health improved and their son was born in 1849.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Browning's love came into a shut-in, sickly life and helped open it up into marriage, travel, motherhood and her best-known love poetry.
The kural likens a lover's tenderness to rain for those whose life depends on it, and Browning's devotion arrived like that rain in Elizabeth Barrett's confined life.
