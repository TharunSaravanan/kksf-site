Kural 495

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

நெடும்புனலுள் வெல்லும் முதலை அடும்புனலின் நீங்கின் அதனைப் பிற.
Nedumpunalul vellum muthalai adumpunalin Neengin athanaip pira.

Meaning

In deep water a crocodile can beat other animals. But once it leaves the water, those same animals can beat it.

Elementary

A fish is the best swimmer in the pond, but put it on the grass and it can hardly move. The crocodile is the same: a champion in the river, in trouble on dry land.

University

This is the chapter’s best-known image. ‘Nedumpunal’ is deep or wide water; ‘adum’ (kills, overcomes) is placed in the middle so that it can be read with either half. Parimelazhagar’s construction is: in deep water the crocodile defeats others; if it leaves the water, others (pira) kill it. The verse leaves out the verb of the second clause and borrows it from ‘adum’, typical Kural compression. The Sanskrit nīti tradition has the same image in a Pañcatantra verse (nakraḥ svasthānam āsādya gajendram api karṣati), where the crocodile in its own place drags even a lordly elephant but out of it is beaten by a dog. That suggests a shared South Asian stock of proverbs rather than direct borrowing.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“nakraḥ svasthānam āsādya gajendram api karṣati | sa eva pracyutaḥ sthānāc chunāpi paribhūyate”
— Pañcatantra III (Kākolūkīya), v. 43/46 depending on edition; cited in Apte and Böhtlingk–Roth s.v. nakra (wisdomlib.org) [3]
In its own place, the crocodile drags even a king of elephants; the same crocodile, fallen from its place, is overpowered even by a dog.
This Sanskrit maxim is almost a twin of Kural 495, crocodile included.
Christianity
“And he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither.”
— Psalm 1:3 (KJV) [4]
The psalm says the righteous person does well like a tree planted in its proper place beside water.
It gives the positive side of the crocodile image: in its right element, a living thing is at full strength.
Islam
“At Uhud the Prophet told his fifty archers, “Stick to your place, and don’t leave it even if you see birds snatching us, till I send for you.” When most of them left the hill to collect booty, the Quraysh cavalry circled round, and the Muslims were routed.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3039 [5]
The archers were decisive on their hill and became helpless once they left it.
Like the crocodile leaving the water, a force that abandons its own place gives its strength away.
Judaism
“If we are afraid in the water, our natural habitat which gives us life, then in a habitat that causes our death, all the more so.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 61b (William Davidson/Koren translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Akiva tells of a fox inviting frightened fish onto dry land, and the fish refuse because leaving their element would kill them for certain. He applies the parable to Israel and Torah.
The same creature-out-of-its-element logic as the crocodile verse.
Buddhism
“As a fish when pulled out of water and cast on land throbs and quivers, even so is this mind agitated.”
— Dhammapada 34 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada compares the untrained mind to a fish thrown onto land, helpless and thrashing.
The water creature that loses its power on land is the Kural’s central image.
Jainism
Jain metaphysics holds that souls and matter can move only where dharmastikaya, the medium of motion, is present, and come to rest by means of adharmastikaya, the medium of rest. Commentators illustrate this with the fish, which moves freely only in water, the water being the medium of its movement.
— Tattvartha Sutra 5.17 of Umasvati (paraphrase), with the traditional fish-in-water illustration from the commentaries [8]
In Jain cosmology the power to move depends on being in the right medium; outside that medium, motion is impossible however strong the being is.
Valluvar's crocodile is mighty in deep water and helpless out of it; the Jain doctrine of the medium of motion, with its fish-in-water illustration, likewise makes a being's power depend on the element it is in.
Sikhism
“O mind, love the Lord, as the fish loves the water.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 60, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak uses the fish, which thrives in water and cannot live outside it even for a moment, as an image of the soul's dependence on God.
The Kural's crocodile is mighty in deep water and helpless on land; Gurbani's fish likewise has its whole life and strength in its own element.
Greek Mythology
The giant Antaeus, son of Earth, could not be beaten while he touched the ground, which kept renewing his strength. Heracles defeated him by lifting him off the earth and crushing him in the air.
— Apollodorus, Library 2.5.11 (tr. J. G. Frazer) [10]
Antaeus’s strength belonged to his element; taken out of it, he was easily beaten.
He is the Greek crocodile, invincible in his own place and powerless once removed from it.
Philosophy
“I well deserve my fate, for what business had I on the land, when by my nature and habits I am only adapted for the sea?”
— Aesop, The Crab and the Fox, Perry 116 (tr. G. F. Townsend) [11]
A crab that leaves the shore to feed in a meadow is eaten by a fox and blames itself as it dies.
Almost the crocodile kural in fable form: a sea creature is lost on land.

Real Life Example

Tran Hung Dao destroys the Mongol fleet at the Bach Dang River
1288 · Bach Dang River, near Haiphong, Vietnam
During the third Mongol invasion of Dai Viet, the Vietnamese commander Tran Hung Dao avoided pitched battles on land and then chose to fight the withdrawing Yuan fleet on the Bach Dang River. His men drove stakes into the riverbed below the waterline, a tactic used at the same river in 938, and lured the Mongol ships onto them. The fleet was wrecked and destroyed, and the invasion ended in defeat.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Mongols, nearly unbeatable on the open plains, were defeated on a tidal river whose conditions their enemy understood far better.
The kural's crocodile is master only in its own element, and the Mongols, masters of land warfare, were overcome once they fought on Vietnamese waters.
