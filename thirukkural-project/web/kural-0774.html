Kural 774

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

கைவேல் களிற்றொடு போக்கி வருபவன் மெய்வேல் பறியா நகும்.
Kaivel kalitrodu pokki varubavan Meyvel pariyaa nagum.

Meaning

A hero throws his only spear at an elephant and looks for another weapon. He finds one stuck in his own body, pulls it out, and smiles.

Elementary

A firefighter who has used up all the water in one hose doesn’t give up; he grabs whatever is at hand and keeps going, even laughing at how tough the day is. The warrior in this verse is like that: nothing stops him.

University

Kaivel (the spear in hand) is sent off (pokki) with the kalitru (bull elephant), and the warrior, returning weaponless (varubavan), finds a meyvel, an enemy spear stuck in his own body (mey), which he plucks out (pariyaa) and laughs (nagum). Parimelazhagar and later commentators read the laughter as joy at having found a new weapon, not as indifference to pain, so the image is about undiminished fighting spirit. Solomon Pappaiah adds that he takes the spear to hurl at the next elephant. The couplet belongs to the Sangam puram tradition of the hero who welcomes wounds, and its hyperbole should be read as a literary ideal of morale, not a literal description. The two base texts agree verbatim.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“O son of Kuntī, the nonpermanent appearance of happiness and distress, and their disappearance in due course, are like the appearance and disappearance of winter and summer seasons. They arise from sense perception, O scion of Bharata, and one must learn to tolerate them without being disturbed.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.14 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna teaches Arjuna titiksha, the endurance of pain and pleasure as passing sensations.
The warrior who laughs as he pulls out the spear embodies this calm toleration of pain.
Christianity
“Thou therefore endure hardness, as a good soldier of Jesus Christ.”
— 2 Timothy 2:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul uses the soldier’s endurance as a model for Timothy’s perseverance in ministry.
The Kural’s warrior shows the literal endurance that Paul turns into a spiritual metaphor.
Islam
“And how many a prophet [fought and] with him fought many religious scholars. But they never lost assurance due to what afflicted them in the cause of Allah, nor did they weaken or submit. And Allah loves the steadfast.”
— Qur’an 3:146 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises fighters who, though wounded and afflicted, did not lose heart.
This is the Kural’s warrior: hurt, but undaunted.
Judaism
“Be strong and resolute; be not in fear or in dread of them, for it is indeed the ETERNAL your God who marches with you—who will not fail you or forsake you.”
— Deuteronomy 31:6 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Moses charges Israel before entering the land to remain strong and unafraid.
The resolve not to weaken matches the Kural’s warrior who keeps fighting with the weapon at hand.
Buddhism
“As an elephant in the battlefield withstands arrows shot from bows all around, even so shall I endure abuse. There are many, indeed, who lack virtue.”
— Dhammapada 320 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha borrows the war elephant that withstands arrows as an image of patient endurance of insults.
Both texts admire the one who takes the blows and stays composed, but Buddhism turns the battlefield into a lesson in patience.
Jainism
“If any misfortune happens and he suffers pain, he should cheerfully steady his mind, and bear the ills that attack him.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Among the hardships a monk must bear is bodily suffering, which he is to meet calmly and with good cheer instead of complaint.
The Kural's hero pulls an enemy spear from his own body and smiles, and the Jain monk is likewise told to meet pain in his body with a cheerful, steady mind.
Sikhism
“Kabir! The world is afraid of death - that death fills my mind with bliss. It is only by death that perfect, supreme bliss is obtained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1365, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 22) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir turns the common fear of death on its head; for one who has died to ego, death holds only joy.
The Kural's hero smiles as he pulls a spear out of his own body; Kabir likewise meets what the world fears most with joy rather than dread.
Greek Mythology
Wounded in the shoulder by an arrow from Pandarus, Diomedes has the arrow drawn out, prays to Athena, and returns to the fight with even greater fury.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 5 (paraphrase) [10]
Book 5 of the Iliad shows the wound spurring the hero on instead of stopping him.
Diomedes, like the Kural’s warrior, treats the weapon in his own body as no reason to stop.
Philosophy
Epictetus teaches that people are disturbed not by things themselves but by their judgments about things.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (paraphrase) [11]
For the Stoic, the same event can crush or not, depending on how the mind receives it.
The warrior’s laughter at his wound is a dramatic example of a mind that refuses to judge the pain as defeat.

Real Life Example

Rifleman Lachhiman Gurung fights on one-handed at Taungdaw
12-13 May 1945 · Taungdaw, west bank of the Irrawaddy, Burma (Myanmar)
Rifleman Lachhiman Gurung of the 4th Battalion, 8th Gurkha Rifles, was manning the most forward post of his platoon when it was attacked by a large enemy force at night. He threw back two grenades that landed in his trench, but a third exploded in his right hand, severely wounding him. Alone and disregarding his wounds, he loaded and fired his rifle with his left hand for about four hours, and his example helped his comrades hold their position; he was awarded the Victoria Cross, gazetted in July 1945.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Badly wounded and with his weapon hand gone, he simply carried on fighting with what he had left.
The kural's hero, having thrown his only spear, finds another in his own wound and smiles, and Lachhiman Gurung, his hand shattered, turned at once to fighting on with the other.
