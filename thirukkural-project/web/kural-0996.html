Kural 996

Courtesy · Panpudaimai · பண்புடைமை

பண்புடையார்ப் பட்டுண்டு உலகம் அதுஇன்றேல் மண்புக்கு மாய்வது மன்.
Panputaiyaarp pattuntu ulakam adhuindrel manpukku maaivadhu man.

Meaning

The world keeps going because good-mannered people live in it. Without them, it would sink into the earth and die away.

Elementary

A tent stands up because of its poles. Take the poles away and the whole tent falls flat on the ground. Valluvar says kind, well-mannered people are the poles that hold up the whole world.

University

Ulagam (உலகம்) here means the ongoing life of human society; it 'exists' (undu, உண்டு) because it is pattu (பட்டு, 'resting upon, attached to') the panbudaiyaar (பண்புடையார், 'people of character'). Without that support it would man-pukku maaivadhu (மண்புக்கு மாய்வது), 'enter the earth and perish', the final man (மன்) being an emphatic particle. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses pattu as 'by being joined to' the courteous; Kalaignar reads ulagam as 'the conduct of the world'; Pope's prose 'The (way of the) world subsists by contact with the good' follows the traditional line. Sivayogi Sivakumar takes the second line very differently, as 'even supplication would be meaningless', which departs from the classical gloss of man-pukku as 'entering the soil'. The kural echoes kural 571 on kannottam, where the world exists through the gracious. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If I did not perform prescribed duties, all these worlds would be put to ruination. I would be the cause of creating unwanted population, and I would thereby destroy the peace of all living beings.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.24 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna says that if he ceased to act rightly as an example, the worlds would fall into ruin.
Like the Kural, it ties the survival of the world to right conduct, though here the support is divine rather than courteous people.
Christianity
“And the LORD said, If I find in Sodom fifty righteous within the city, then I will spare all the place for their sakes.”
— Genesis 18:26 (KJV) [4]
God tells Abraham that a city may be preserved from destruction for the sake of the righteous within it.
The idea that the good keep a whole community from perishing is close to the Kural's image of the world resting on the courteous.
Islam
“And if it were not for Allah checking [some] people by means of others, the earth would have been corrupted, but Allah is full of bounty to the worlds.”
— Qur'an 2:251 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an teaches that God uses some people to restrain others, and that without this the earth would be ruined.
Like the Kural, it holds that the world is kept from ruin through the action of good people.
Judaism
“Shimon the Righteous was one of the last of the men of the great assembly. He used to say: the world stands upon three things: the Torah, the Temple service, and the practice of acts of piety.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:2 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
One of the founding sayings of rabbinic ethics names acts of loving-kindness (gemilut hasadim) as one of the pillars on which the world stands.
The image of the world 'standing upon' kindly conduct is the same as the Kural's world resting on people of good character.
Buddhism
“Generosity, sweet speech, / Helpfulness to others, / Impartiality to all, / As the case demands. / These four winning ways make the world go round, / As the linchpin in a moving car.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (tr. Narada Thera, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha's four bases of social harmony (sangaha-vatthu), generosity, kind speech, helpfulness and impartiality, are said to keep the world turning like a linchpin.
This is almost exactly the Kural's claim: courtesy and kindness are what hold the world together.
Jainism
“The most excellent twice-born men who possess these good qualities, are able to save themselves and others.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.35 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Gayaghosha concludes that those who truly possess the virtues he has described can rescue not only themselves but others too.
The Jain text, like the Kural, sees people of real virtue as the ones on whom others' welfare depends.
Sikhism
“That mortal who renounces possessiveness, greed, emotional attachment and egotism - says Nanak, he himself is saved, and he saves many others as well.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1427, Salok Mahala 9, salok 22, Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Tegh Bahadur teaches that the person who conquers ego and greed does not stop at their own freedom but becomes a means of others' deliverance too. In Sikh thought the Gurmukh's goodness steadies and uplifts those around them.
The Kural says the world keeps going because it rests on people of good character, and Guru Tegh Bahadur likewise describes the selfless person as one who carries many others with them.
Greek Mythology
“And then Aidos and Nemesis, with their sweet forms wrapped in white robes, will go from the wide-pathed earth and forsake mankind to join the company of the deathless gods: and bitter sorrows will be left for mortal men, and there will be no help against evil.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 197–201 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White; sacred-texts.com) [10]
Hesiod foresees that when the goddesses of shame and righteous indignation abandon humanity, nothing will remain to protect people from ruin.
Like the Kural, Hesiod imagines a world that collapses once decency and right feeling depart.
Philosophy
“Zeus feared that the entire race would be exterminated, and so he sent Hermes to them, bearing reverence and justice to be the ordering principles of cities and the bonds of friendship and conciliation.”
— Plato, Protagoras 322c (tr. Benjamin Jowett, Internet Classics Archive) [11]
In Protagoras' myth, humans gathered in cities begin to destroy each other until Zeus gives them reverence and justice as the bonds of social life.
This is the Kural's thesis in mythic form: without the qualities of refined character, human society would perish.

Real Life Example

Gander shelters the passengers of 38 diverted airliners
11-15 September 2001 · Gander, Newfoundland, Canada
When United States airspace closed after the attacks of 11 September 2001, 38 airliners carrying roughly 6,600 passengers and crew were diverted to Gander, Newfoundland, a town of about 10,000 people. Schools, churches and halls were turned into shelters, residents took strangers into their own homes, and local businesses and volunteers supplied food, clothing and medicines for several days until flights resumed. The town was later formally thanked by the United States for its help.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Ordinary people's kindness to strangers held things together at a moment when the world seemed to be coming apart.
The kural says the world keeps going because it rests on people of courteous character, and the people of Gander showed how much a frightened world depends on such people.
