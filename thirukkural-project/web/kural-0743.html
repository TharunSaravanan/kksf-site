Kural 743

The Fortification · Aran · அரண்

உயர்வகலம் திண்மை அருமைஇந் நான்கின் அமைவரண் என்றுரைக்கும் நூல்.
Uyarvakalam thinmai arumaiin naankin amaivaran endruraikkum nool.

Meaning

Books on this subject say a fort's wall needs four things: it must be high, thick, strong, and hard to reach.

Elementary

A good sandcastle wall has to be tall so the waves can’t jump over it, thick so they can’t push through, packed hard so it doesn’t crumble, and hard to reach, maybe behind a ditch. If any one of these is missing, the sea gets in. Valluvar says the old books on forts ask for the same four things.

University

After the natural defenses (742), Valluvar turns to the man-made wall (seyarkai aran, as Pappaiah calls it) and gives its four standard qualities: uyarvu (height, so that it cannot be scaled), akalam (breadth, which Pappaiah explains as room for defenders to stand and move on the rampart), thinmai (solidity, so that it cannot be battered down) and arumai (difficulty or inaccessibility). Parimelazhagar’s tradition explains arumai as the difficulty of approach created by engines and traps (pori) set on the walls, a reading that Pappaiah follows. The phrase endru uraikkum nool, ‘so says the treatise’, is one of the places where Valluvar openly cites earlier military and political science. Commentators link it to the arthashastra tradition, whose fort specifications (for example, a rampart ‘six dandas high and twice as much broad’) are very similar. The sandhi-joined text reads ‘உயர்வகலந் திண்மை யருமையிந் நான்கி / னமைவர ணென்றுரைக்கு நூல்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“a rampart six dandas high and twice as much broad shall be erected by heaping mud upwards and by making it square at the bottom, oval at the centre pressed by the trampling of elephants and bulls, and planted with thorny and poisonous plants in bushes.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 2.3 ‘Construction of Forts’ (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
The Arthashastra gives exact measurements for a fort’s rampart, packed hard by elephants and bulls and planted with thorny bushes.
It sets out the Kural’s four qualities: height, breadth, strength (packed earth) and inaccessibility (thorns).
Christianity
“And had a wall great and high, and had twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels.”
— Revelation 21:12 (KJV) [4]
John’s vision of the New Jerusalem describes a great, high wall with gates guarded by angels.
The ideal heavenly city has the Kural’s height and greatness, with guarded and hard-to-reach entrances.
Islam
“Bring me sheets of iron” - until, when he had leveled [them] between the two mountain walls, he said, “Blow [with bellows],” until when he had made it [like] fire, he said, “Bring me, that I may pour over it molten copper.”
— Qur’an 18:96 (Saheeh International) [5]
Dhul-Qarnayn builds his barrier from iron fused with molten copper and fills the gap between two mountains with it.
The barrier is as high as the mountains and as solid as metal, showing the Kural’s height and strength.
Judaism
“Walk around Zion, circle it; count its towers, take note of its ramparts; go through its citadels, that you may recount it to a future age.”
— Psalms 48:13-14 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition 2023, via Sefaria; KJV 48:12-13) [6]
The psalmist invites pilgrims to walk around Jerusalem and admire its towers and ramparts as a sign of God’s protection.
The psalm praises the same features the Kural lists: tall towers and strong ramparts.
Buddhism
“Furthermore, the royal frontier fortress has ramparts: high & thick & completely covered with plaster.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.63, Nagara Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The seventh requisite of the Buddha’s fortress is a high, thick, fully plastered rampart.
High, thick and sealed is almost the Kural’s height, breadth and strength.
Jainism
“It may be noted that three gunavratas and four siksavratas are grouped together and are known as silavratas, i.e., supplementary vows because these vows perform the work of supplementing or protecting the five main anuvratas just as towns are protected or guarded by the encircling walls built around them.”
— Vilas A. Sangave, ‘Ethics of Jainism: Prescription of Ethical Code’, section on the twelve vratas (jainworld.com) [8]
Jain householder ethics treats the seven supplementary vows as a protective wall thrown around the five small vows of non-violence, truth, non-stealing, chastity and non-possession.
Where the Kural specifies the height, breadth, strength and inaccessibility of a town's wall, Jainism borrows the same image of the encircling wall to explain how the supplementary vows guard the core vows.
Sikhism
“How can the beautiful fortress be conquered, O Siblings of Destiny? It has double walls and triple moats.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1161, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir pictures the ego-bound self as a fortress with double ramparts and triple moats, defended by the passions, so strong that no ordinary person can take it.
The Kural defines a fort by height, breadth, strength and inaccessibility; Kabir's fortress with its double walls and triple moats has exactly those qualities, though he means the fortress of the mind.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Odysseus sees in the underworld Antiope’s twin sons Amphion and Zethus, who founded seven-gated Thebes and surrounded it with towers, because even with all their strength they could not live in wide Thebes without walls. Later tradition says Amphion’s lyre made the stones move into place by themselves.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.260-265 [10]
The founders of Thebes knew that even great strength needs a high, towered wall around it.
The Kural’s tall, strong wall is the same thing the mythic founders of Thebes thought a city could not do without.
Philosophy
“not only should cities have walls, but care should be taken to make them ornamental, as well as useful for warlike purposes, and adapted to resist modern inventions.”
— Aristotle, Politics VII.11 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle wants city walls that look fine but are above all built to resist the latest siege engines.
Aristotle’s wall ‘adapted to resist’ attack is the Kural’s idea of arumai: a wall made hard to overcome.

Real Life Example

Theodosius II builds the land walls of Constantinople
413 · Constantinople (now Istanbul), Turkey
Under Emperor Theodosius II and his prefect Anthemius, a new line of land walls was built across the peninsula of Constantinople, with work dated to about 413 and repeatedly strengthened afterwards. The system combined a massive main wall with 96 towers, a lower outer wall in front of it and a moat. World History Encyclopedia gives the inner wall as almost 5 metres thick and about 12 metres high, with a moat about 20 metres wide that could be flooded, an arrangement that kept enemy siege engines far from the main wall.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The layered walls protected Constantinople through many sieges over the following centuries and became the classic example of a city defence that height, thickness and obstacles made almost impossible to approach.
The kural names height, breadth, strength and inaccessibility as the four qualities of a fortress wall, and the Theodosian walls combined tall towers, thick masonry and a moat that kept attackers at a distance.
