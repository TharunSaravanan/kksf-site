Kural 56

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

தற்காத்துத் தற்கொண்டாற் பேணித் தகைசான்ற சொற்காத்துச் சோர்விலாள் பெண்.
ThaRkaaththuth thaRkoNdaaR peaNith thagaisaandra, soRkaaththuch soarvilaaL peN.

Meaning

A true wife guards herself, looks after her husband, protects the family’s good name, and never grows careless in any of these.

Elementary

Think of someone holding three things at once — their own promise, their partner, and the family's good name — and never letting any of the three drop, not even when they're tired. This kural says that's not just a nice thing to do; that's what the word 'wife' actually means.

University

Structurally this is a definition-by-enumeration, listing four duties and then delivering the predicate in a single final word: peN. Valluvar's three objects of guarding move outward in concentric circles — thaR-kaaththu (guarding the self), thaR-koNdaan peaNi (tending the one who took her), soR-kaaththu (guarding the word, i.e. reputation) — and the fourth term, soarvu-ilaaL ('she in whom there is no slackening'), converts them from acts into a sustained disposition; Parimelazhagar glosses soarvu as maRavi, forgetfulness or lapse, making constancy of attention the operative virtue. The word soL here is usually read by commentators as puhaz, the spoken good name that a village confers, which locates a significant part of wifely virtue in the social rather than the private sphere. Tamil tradition has long linked this couplet to Vasuki Ammaiyar, Valluvar's legendary wife, and the anthologists of this chapter pair it with a line from the deuterocanonical Ben Sira on the doubled days of a good wife's husband. Note also the quiet reciprocity implied: the kural says 'the man who took her' (thaR-koNdaan), keeping the marriage contractual and two-sided even while the duties enumerated are hers.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Shakuntala tells Dushyanta that the wife is a man's own half and his first friend — a formulation the tradition developed into the terms ardhangini ('half-body') and sahadharmini ('co-bearer of dharma'), making her the joint holder of the household's obligations and its honour.
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva §74; the ardhangini / sahadharmini formulations in classical dharma literature [4]
The sahadharmini concept makes protection of the husband and of the family's dharmic standing the wife's own duty, not a service rendered.
Both traditions make guarding the husband and the household's name part of the definition of wifehood itself.
Christianity
“The heart of her husband doth safely trust in her, so that he shall have no need of spoil. She will do him good and not evil all the days of her life… She looketh well to the ways of her household, and eateth not the bread of idleness.”
— Proverbs 31:11–12, 27 (King James Version) [5]
The Proverbs 31 wife is defined by unwearying diligence sustained 'all the days of her life' — the exact opposite of soarvu, slackening.
'Never slackens' and 'eateth not the bread of idleness' are the same virtue named in two languages.
Islam
“They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them.”
— Qur'an 2:187 (Saheeh International translation) [6]
The Qur'anic libās metaphor makes each spouse the other's covering — protection, warmth, and the preservation of the other's honour from exposure.
Guarding oneself, one's spouse, and the couple's good name is exactly what 'being each other's clothing' means.
Judaism
“One who loves his wife as he loves himself, and who honors her more than himself, and who instructs his sons and daughters in an upright path… about him the verse states: And you shall know that your tent is in peace.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 62b (Koren Noé / William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Talmudic baraita defines the well-ordered household by a standard of unremitting mutual honour rather than by wealth.
The same picture of a marriage held together by sustained, deliberate care for the other's standing.
Buddhism
In the Samajīvina Sutta the Buddha tells Nakulapitā and Nakulamātā that a couple who wish to be together in this life and the next should be matched in faith, ethics, generosity and wisdom — a marriage sustained by both partners' unslackening practice rather than by one partner's effort.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 4.55, Samajīvina Sutta (Paṭhama) [8]
Early Buddhism frames marital constancy as a jointly maintained discipline with a definite ethical content.
The same insistence that wifely (and husbandly) virtue is a continuous practice, not an occasional act.
Jainism
Añjanāsundarī, rejected by her husband Pavanañjaya on their wedding night, stayed faithful in her in-laws' house and loved him from a distance; later she was banished by her in-laws on a false charge of unchastity and gave birth to Hanumān in a forest cave, until her fidelity was vindicated.
— Jain Rāma narratives (Paumacariya; Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭi-śalākā-puruṣa-caritra, Book VII); summarised in Jainpedia, 'Añjanāsundarī' [9]
In the Jain Rāma tradition Añjanā is the satī who protects her own virtue and her husband's name through long neglect and false accusation without ever giving way.
Valluvar defines the wife as one who guards herself, cherishes her husband, keeps the household's name unstained and never slackens, and Añjanā's story is a lifetime of exactly that steadiness under the worst conditions.
Sikhism
“What is that word, what is that virtue, and what is that magic mantra? What are those clothes, which I can wear to captivate my Husband Lord?” … “Humility is the word, forgiveness is the virtue, and sweet speech is the magic mantra. Wear these three robes, O sister, and you will captivate your Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, Saloks 126-127 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are in the Sikh scripture, has the bride ask what will win her husband, and answers that it is not clothes or charms but humility, forgiveness and sweet speech.
Like Valluvar, who defines a true wife by a set of steady inner virtues rather than by appearance, Farid lists the qualities that make the bride truly a bride.
Greek Mythology
“Therefore the fame of her virtue shall never perish, but the immortals shall make among men on earth a pleasant song in honor of constant Penelope.”
— Homer, Odyssey 24.196–198 (trans. A. T. Murray, Loeb Classical Library, 1919) [11]
Agamemnon's ghost praises Penelope for having guarded both herself and her absent husband's name for twenty years — and says the gods will reward that guardianship with permanent renown.
Guarding the good name (soR-kaappu) is precisely what Homer says wins Penelope her immortal song.
Philosophy
“But in marriage there must be above all perfect companionship and mutual love of husband and wife, both in health and in sickness and under all conditions, since it was with desire for this as well as for having children that both entered upon marriage.”
— Musonius Rufus, Fragment XIII A, 'On the Chief End of Marriage' (trans. Cora E. Lutz, Yale Classical Studies X, 1947) [12]
Musonius Rufus, the Roman Stoic, defines marriage by a companionship that must hold 'under all conditions' — a formal refusal of slackening.
'Never slackens' is Valluvar's phrase for what Musonius calls constancy in health and in sickness and under all conditions.

Real Life Example

Ruth Williams Khama stands by Seretse Khama through exile
1948-1956 · England and Bechuanaland (now Botswana)
In September 1948 Ruth Williams, the daughter of a retired British army officer, married Seretse Khama, heir to the chieftaincy of the Bangwato people of Bechuanaland. The marriage caused controversy in Britain and Bechuanaland, and under pressure from South Africa's government the British exiled the couple to England in 1951. After five years they were allowed to return home as private citizens in 1956, and Seretse went on to become the first president of independent Botswana in 1966.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The Khamas' marriage withstood official disapproval and years of exile, and their partnership became part of the story of a new nation.
The kural describes the true wife as one who guards herself, cherishes her husband, keeps the household's good name and never slackens, and Ruth Khama remained steadfast at her husband's side through exile and public hostility.
