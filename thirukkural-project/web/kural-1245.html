Kural 1245

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

செற்றார் எனக்கை விடல்உண்டோ நெஞ்சேயாம் உற்றால் உறாஅ தவர்.
Setraar enakkai vidtalundo nenjaeyaam utraal uraaa thavar.

Meaning

My heart, he does not come to us even when we long for him. But can we really give him up, saying, "He hates us"?

Elementary

Imagine a friend who hasn’t called you back in a long time. You could decide, “They don’t like me anymore, I’ll forget them,” but deep down you know you can’t stop caring about them.

University

The heroine tests the idea of abandoning her husband and rejects it: ceṟṟār eṉa (‘saying he is one who hates us’, from ceṟu ‘to hate, be hostile’) kaiviṭal uṇṭō (‘is there such a thing as letting go?’). The second line contrasts yām uṟṟāl (‘though we draw close, long for him’) with uṟāatavar (‘he who does not draw close’), the lengthened uṟāa being an alapeṭai (prosodic lengthening) for metre. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read the question as rhetorical (‘can we give him up?’), whereas Pappaiah turns it toward her own will (‘do I have the firmness of heart to give him up?’). The couplet thus marks the chapter’s turn from reproaching the heart to confessing its constancy. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“So let us give up all friendship with this dark-complexioned boy, even if we can’t give up talking about Him.”
— Srimad Bhagavatam 10.47.17 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
In the ‘Song of the Bee’, the gopis, feeling abandoned by Krishna, list his apparent cruelties and resolve to give him up, yet admit they cannot stop speaking of him.
The Kural’s heroine reaches the same impasse: she can call him unloving, but she cannot let him go.
Christianity
“Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes true love as something that does not give up, however much it must endure.
The Kural’s heroine finds her love for her absent husband cannot be abandoned, even when he seems not to return it.
Islam
“…he has impassioned her with love. Indeed, we see her [to be] in clear error.”
— Qur’an 12:30 (Saheeh International) [5]
The women of the city say that the governor’s wife is so consumed with love for Joseph, who does not return it, that she has lost all judgment.
Like the Kural’s heroine, she loves one who does not draw near to her and cannot give that love up.
Judaism
“Many waters cannot quench love, Neither can the floods drown it; If a man would give all the substance of his house for love, He would utterly be contemned.”
— Song of Songs 8:7 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The Song of Songs declares that love, once kindled, cannot be put out by any force.
The Kural’s heroine finds that even the thought ‘he hates us’ cannot quench her love.
Buddhism
“Therefore hold nothing dear, for separation from the dear is painful. There are no bonds for those who have nothing beloved or unloved.”
— Dhammapada 211 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that holding someone dear creates a bond that makes separation painful.
The Kural’s heroine discovers just such a bond: she cannot let go of the one she holds dear, even though it hurts.
Jainism
The courtesan Kośā loved Sthūlabhadra, who left her to become a monk; when he returned years later to spend a rainy season in her house, he did not return her love, yet her devotion did not die, and in the end she was won over to the Jain path herself.
— Hemacandra, Pariśiṣṭaparvan, story of Sthūlabhadra and Kośā (paraphrase) [8]
This famous Jain story shows a woman whose love outlasts the beloved’s withdrawal, until it is transformed rather than abandoned.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Kośā cannot simply give up the one who no longer draws near to her.
Sikhism
“What sort of love is this, which clings to duality? O Nanak, he alone is called a lover, who remains forever immersed in absorption.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad teaches that true love does not drift elsewhere when the Beloved seems distant; a real lover stays absorbed in the one Beloved.
The Kural's wife decides she cannot give up a husband who does not come, calling him hostile; Guru Angad likewise holds that a true lover never turns away from the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
The nymph Echo, rejected by Narcissus, hides in the woods in shame, yet her love clings to her and even grows with the pain of being refused.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.393-397 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid portrays a love that survives open rejection and cannot be torn out.
The Kural’s heroine finds the same: even if he does not come to her, she cannot cast her love aside.
Philosophy
Aristotle asks whether a friendship should be broken off when the other person changes, and answers that one should not break it off hastily, but keep some regard for the friend for the sake of the past.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.3 (1165b13-36) (paraphrase) [11]
Aristotle argues that a bond once formed should not be lightly abandoned even when the other has changed.
The Kural’s heroine reaches a similar conclusion about her husband: she cannot write him off as one who hates her.

Real Life Example

Juliette Drouet's fifty years of devotion to Victor Hugo
1851-1883 · Brussels, Jersey and Guernsey
Juliette Drouet was Victor Hugo's companion for about fifty years and wrote him some 22,000 letters, at least one a day. After the coup of 1851 she helped Hugo escape France with false papers and joined him in Brussels within days of his arrival. Through his long exile she never lived with him, staying with friends, in hotels or in rented rooms, and from 1864 in a house he bought for her, yet she did not leave his side for a single day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her letters are one of the largest records of a single love in literary history.
The kural's heroine asks whether she can ever give up a man who does not come to her when she longs for him, and Drouet, never sharing Hugo's home, still could not give him up in fifty years.
