Kural 59

The Worth of a Wife · Vaazhkkaithunai Nalam · வாழ்க்கைத்துணை நலம்

புகழ்புரிந்த இல்லிலோர்க்கு இல்லை இகழ்வார்முன் ஏறுபோல் பீடு நடை.
Pugazhpurindha illiloarkku illai igazhvaarmun, ERupoal peedu nadai.

Meaning

A man whose wife does not care about the family’s good name cannot walk proudly, like a lion, in front of people who mock him.

Elementary

Imagine walking into a room where people are whispering about you. If you know you've done nothing wrong at home, you can walk in with your head up. If you know you have, your feet get heavy. This kural says a family's good name is what lets someone stand tall in front of people who don't like them.

University

Valluvar shifts register here from the household's interior to its public face, and the image he chooses — ERu, the bull or male lion, with peedu nadai, a gait of dignified swagger — is drawn from the heroic vocabulary of Sangam puRam poetry, where such a walk signals a warrior's untouchable standing. Parimelazhagar reads the couplet as stating the specific defect that follows when the soR-kaappu of Kural 56 is neglected, which makes 55–59 a tightly argued sequence rather than a list. The sociology behind it is worth naming: in the honour economy of ancient Tamil society, a household's puhaz was a collective asset that any member could deplete, so Valluvar's claim is descriptive as much as normative — the man's public bearing is materially constituted by his wife's reputation. Modern readers rightly note the asymmetry (the wife's conduct determines the husband's honour, but the couplet says nothing of the reverse) and the fact that this logic is precisely what later hardened into honour-based control of women; it is worth setting beside Kural 57, where Valluvar explicitly rejects control as a method. Comparative wisdom literature converges here almost uncannily: Proverbs 31:23, Bava Metzia 59a, and Odyssey 11 all tie a man's standing in the public assembly directly to his wife.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Where women are honoured, there the gods are pleased; but where they are not honoured, no sacred rite yields rewards.”
— Manusmriti 3.56 (trans. G. Bühler, Sacred Books of the East XXV) [4]
Manu ties the standing and efficacy of an entire household — even its ritual power — to how its women are treated and how they fare.
Both texts make a family's public and sacred standing contingent on the honour surrounding its women.
Christianity
“Her husband is known in the gates, when he sitteth among the elders of the land.”
— Proverbs 31:23 (King James Version) [5]
Proverbs places the excellent wife's effect precisely where the Kural does — at the city gate, the public forum where a man's standing is measured.
The same proposition: a wife's worth is what lets a man hold his place among his peers.
Islam
“The best of you are those who are best to their wives, and I am the best of you to my wives.”
— Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3895 (also Sunan Ibn Majah 1977), narrated by 'A'isha [6]
The Prophet makes the treatment of one's wife the public measure of a man's rank among men, and offers himself as the standard.
Both sayings make a man's standing before others a function of his marriage.
Judaism
“And Rabbi Ḥelbo says: A person must always be careful about sustaining the honor of his wife, as blessing is found in a person's house only because of his wife… And that is what Rava said to the residents of Meḥoza: Honor your wives, so that you will become rich.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 59a (Koren Noé / William Davidson Edition) [7]
The Gemara makes a household's prosperity and honour flow through the wife, and turns that into a practical instruction to the men of the town.
Both texts say a man's visible fortunes stand or fall with his wife's honour.
Buddhism
In the Bhariya Sutta the Buddha describes seven kinds of wife — three of them 'like a murderer', 'like a thief' and 'like a tyrant', who bring their households to ruin and themselves to a bad destination, and four (like a mother, a sister, a friend, a servant) who bring the household to flourishing.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 7.63, Bhariya Sutta (the Buddha to Sujātā, in Anāthapiṇḍika's house) [8]
The Buddha tells Sujata that these types differ not in wealth or status but in the reputation and outcome they produce for the whole house.
The kural's negative case — the household whose name the wife does not guard — is the Bhariya Sutta's first three types exactly.
Jainism
In Vimalasūri's Paumacariya, Rāma sends the pregnant Sītā into exile because of public scandal about her, though she is innocent; she gives birth to Lavaṇa and Aṅkuśa in the forest and is later vindicated before the people.
— Vimalasūri, Paumacariya, the exile-of-Sītā episode; summarised in 'Paumacariya (critical study)', wisdomlib.org [9]
The Jain Rāmāyaṇa shows a king who believed his public standing depended on his wife's reputation, while making clear that the slander, not Sītā, was at fault.
Valluvar says a man whose wife does not guard the household's name cannot walk proudly before his detractors; the Jain epic shows the same tie between a wife's name and a husband's public standing, though from the side of an unjustly slandered wife.
Sikhism
“According to the actions the mortal commits, so is he proclaimed.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 730, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak teaches that each person's standing, what they are called and known as, comes from their own deeds.
Kural 59 ties a man's public standing to his wife's care for the family name; Sikh teaching instead roots each person's reputation in their own actions.
Greek Mythology
Agamemnon returns from Troy as the greatest conqueror of his age and is murdered in his bath by Clytemnestra; his shade tells Odysseus in the underworld that she poured shame on herself and on all women to come, so that even a good wife now inherits the ill report she created.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.405–434 (the shade of Agamemnon to Odysseus) [11]
Homer's most powerful illustration that no quantity of public glory survives a household's disgrace.
Agamemnon is the man who cannot walk with the lion's stride, however many cities he sacked.
Philosophy
Aristotle's portrait of the megalopsychos — the great-souled man — includes his outward bearing: a deep voice, a level speech, an unhurried gait, because he holds few things in high enough esteem to be hurried by them; yet Aristotle also insists that greatness of soul cannot be sustained without the external goods that give a claim to honour.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.3, 1123a34–1125a16 [12]
The Nicomachean Ethics both describes the proud walk the Kural names and explains why it cannot be bluffed when the grounds for honour are missing.
Valluvar's lion-stride is Aristotle's megalopsychos gait — and both make it depend on having real standing to walk on.

Real Life Example

Julius Caesar divorces Pompeia after the Bona Dea scandal
62 BCE · Rome, Italy
In December 62 BCE the women-only rites of the Bona Dea were held in the house of Julius Caesar, then pontifex maximus, presided over by his wife Pompeia, and the politician Publius Clodius Pulcher was discovered to have entered disguised as a woman. Caesar divorced Pompeia at once, yet when called as a witness at Clodius's trial he said he knew nothing about the charges. According to Plutarch, when the prosecutor asked why, then, he had divorced his wife, Caesar answered that his wife ought not even to be under suspicion.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Caesar's reply became proverbial for the idea that a public figure's standing depends on his household being beyond reproach.
The kural says a man whose wife does not guard the household's good name cannot hold his head high before his critics, and Caesar, an ambitious public man, ended his marriage rather than let any suspicion touch his household.
