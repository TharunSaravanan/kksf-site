Kural 1321

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

இல்லை தவறவர்க்கு ஆயினும் ஊடுதல் வல்லது அவர்அளிக்கு மாறு.
Illai thavaravarkku aayinum ootudhal Valladhu avaralikku maaru.

Meaning

Even when he has done nothing wrong, a little sulking can make him show me even more love.

Elementary

Sometimes two best friends pretend to be cross for a minute, just so the other one will come and make a fuss over them. Nobody did anything wrong; the little pout simply makes the kindness show. The wife in this poem says a small, pretend quarrel does the same for her husband’s love.

University

The final chapter of the Kural, ūṭal uvakai (‘the joy of lovers’ pique’), turns ūṭal, the feigned or playful sulking of a beloved, from a source of pain in the preceding chapters (pulavi, pulavi nuṇukkam) into a source of delight. Here the wife concedes illai tavaṟu avarkku (‘there is no fault in him’), yet ūṭutal (sulking) is vallatu (‘has the power’) to bring about avar aḷi (his tender grace, aḷi meaning lavish affection). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss the line as ūṭal making him shower greater love on her, and Kalaignar generalises it to lovers on both sides; Pope’s ‘fretful jealousy’ reads the pique more darkly than the Tamil commentators do. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; commentators traditionally assign the opening couplets of the chapter to the wife.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna playfully teases his devoted queen Rukmini by suggesting she would have done better to marry a worthier king; she is so stricken that she faints, and he at once lifts and consoles her, after which their affection is described as all the sweeter.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.60 (Krishna teases Rukmini; paraphrase) [3]
The episode is the tradition’s classic lovers’ quarrel (praṇaya-kalaha) in which no real wrong has been done, yet the brief hurt brings out an outpouring of tenderness.
Like the Kural’s wife, the quarrel has no fault behind it, and its only result is a greater show of love.
Christianity
“Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame.”
— Song of Solomon 8:6 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs, read in Christian tradition as a love poem and an allegory of divine love, joins love’s strength to the burning intensity of jealousy.
The Kural, too, treats a flare of pique as part of love’s strength rather than a sign that love has failed.
Islam
The Prophet told Aisha that he could tell when she was pleased with him and when she was cross: when pleased she swore by the Lord of Muhammad, and when cross by the Lord of Abraham. Aisha answered that even then she left out only his name, not her love for him.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5228 (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith shows a loving marriage in which small displeasures are noticed, gently teased, and understood as love in disguise.
As in the Kural, the wife’s little pique is affectionate rather than hostile, and her husband meets it with fond attention.
Judaism
The Mishnah teaches that any love which depends on some particular thing ends when that thing is gone, but love which depends on nothing never ends, giving David and Jonathan as its example.
— Pirkei Avot 5:16 (paraphrase) [6]
Pirkei Avot distinguishes conditional love from love that needs no cause and therefore cannot be undone.
The Kural’s couple has such a love: because nothing is wrong, the sulk cannot threaten it and only makes it show.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells a householder that a husband should honour his wife by courtesy, by not despising her, by faithfulness, by handing over authority to her and by giving her adornments, and that she in turn shows him love in her own ways.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (tr. Narada Thera; paraphrase) [7]
The Sigalovada Sutta describes marriage as mutual, attentive regard on both sides.
The tenderness the Kural’s wife draws out by sulking is the kind of attentive regard the Buddha asks husbands to show.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra teaches that anger destroys affection, pride destroys humility, deceit destroys friendship, and greed destroys everything.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.38 (paraphrase) [8]
Jain ethics treats anger (krodha) as one of the four passions (kaṣāya) and warns that it ruins love between people.
The Kural claims the opposite for a playful, faultless sulk, which the Jain text would still count as a small stirring of passion.
Sikhism
“Everyone makes mistakes; only the Guru and the Creator are infallible.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 61, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that every person errs, but the Guru and the Creator are without fault.
The Kural's wife admits there is no fault in her beloved and sulks anyway; Gurbani's soul-bride likewise knows the faults are all on her side and that the Beloved is faultless.
Greek Mythology
Pausanias tells how Hera, angry with Zeus, withdrew from him to Euboea; Zeus, unable to win her back, dressed a wooden image as a bride and pretended to be marrying it. Hera rushed to the procession, tore off the veil, found the statue, laughed, and was reconciled to him.
— Pausanias, Description of Greece 9.3.1-2 (paraphrase) [10]
The Boeotian Daedala festival remembered a divine lovers’ quarrel that ended in laughter and reunion.
Hera’s withdrawal, like the Kural’s sulk, serves to draw out a wooing that ends in renewed affection.
Philosophy
In Terence’s comedy Andria, an old man remarks that the quarrels of lovers are the renewal of love (Latin: amantium irae amoris integratio est).
— Terence, Andria 555 (Act III, scene 3) [11]
The line became proverbial in Europe for the idea that a lovers’ quarrel freshens rather than ends their love.
It is almost a Latin restatement of this couplet’s claim that a faultless sulk draws out greater love.

Real Life Example

Nizami tells how Khosrow pleads at the gate of Shirin's castle
late 12th century · Ganja (in present-day Azerbaijan)
The Persian poet Nizami of Ganja wrote the romance Khosrow and Shirin, later counted among the five poems of his Khamsa, about the Sasanian king Khosrow Parviz and the Armenian princess Shirin. In one of its most famous episodes Shirin, displeased with Khosrow's behaviour and his marriage to another woman, withdraws to her castle. After his queen's death Khosrow comes again and again to see her, standing at her gate at dawn in the depth of winter, until Shirin finally yields and the lovers are reconciled.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The scene became one of the most painted subjects in Persian art, a classic image of a beloved's withdrawal drawing out a lover's humble and devoted pleading.
This kural says a little sulking has the power to draw out more of a lover's tenderness, and Shirin's retreat to her castle draws from King Khosrow the most devoted pleading of the whole romance.
