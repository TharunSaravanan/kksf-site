Kural 1064

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

இடமெல்லாம் கொள்ளாத் தகைத்தே இடமில்லாக் காலும் இரவொல்லாச் சால்பு.
Idamellaam kollaath thakaiththe idamillaak kaalum iravollaach chaalbu.

Meaning

A person who refuses to beg, even when they have nothing to live on, has a greatness so big that the whole world cannot hold it.

Elementary

Think of someone who has almost nothing but still won't ask for handouts, because they want to stand on their own feet. The Kural says that kind of courage is so big that even the whole world isn't big enough to hold it.

University

The couplet turns on a pun on idam (இடம்): idamillaak kaalum means 'even when there is no means (of life)', while idamellaam kollaa means 'which all the room (of the world) cannot hold'. The quality praised is saalbu (சால்பு, 'nobility, fullness of character', the same root as saanraanmai, ch. 99), here specified as iravu ollaa, 'not consenting to beg'. In the line of commentary that follows Parimelazhagar, Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah take the second half as 'even when one is destitute' and the first as 'a greatness not equalled by all the worlds together'. Pope renders it 'Who ne'er consent to beg in utmost need, their worth / Has excellence of greatness that transcends the earth.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, the poor Brahmin Sudama visits his childhood friend Krishna, now a king, but out of self-respect does not ask him for anything; Krishna honours him and grants him prosperity unasked.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.80-81 (story of Sudama) (paraphrase) [3]
Sudama's refusal to beg even from a willing, divine friend is celebrated as true greatness.
Like the Kural, the story praises a destitute man who will not ask even when he has nothing.
Christianity
“That ye may walk honestly toward them that are without, and that ye may have lack of nothing.”
— 1 Thessalonians 4:12 (KJV) [4]
Paul urges believers to work with their own hands so that they live honourably and depend on no one.
The Kural's dignity of not depending on others is the same aim Paul sets for the Thessalonians.
Islam
“An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign. They do not ask people persistently [or at all].”
— Qur'an 2:273 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an praises poor people whose restraint is so great that others mistake them for well off.
These are exactly the Kural's people: destitute, yet unwilling to beg.
Judaism
“And anyone who needs to take and does not take, will not die of old age until he supports others with his own money. Concerning him the verse says: ‘Blessed is the man who trusts in the Lord and whose hope is the Lord’ (Jeremiah 17:7).”
— Mishnah Peah 8:9 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah blesses the needy person who refrains from taking charity and promises that he will one day support others.
The Mishnah, like the Kural, honours the person who will not ask even in real need.
Buddhism
“A monk who does not despise what he has received, even though it be little, who is pure in livelihood and unremitting in effort — him even the gods praise.”
— Dhammapada 366 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada praises the monk who is content with little and pure in how he lives.
Like the Kural, it gives cosmic praise to someone who keeps his dignity with very little.
Jainism
“Is it, therefore, a reproach to a man if he starve and keep his integrity inviolate, rather than fill his stomach by the disgraceful practice of mendicity?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 2 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet asks whether there is any shame in starving with one's integrity whole rather than eating by begging.
This is the Kural's idamillaak kaalum iravollaa saalbu: refusing to beg even when there is nothing to eat.
Sikhism
“Fareed begs, O Lord, do not make me sit at another's door. If this is the way you are going to keep me, then go ahead and take the life out of my body.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1380, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 42) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sufi Sheikh Farid prays never to be made dependent on another person's door, and says he would rather die.
The Kural praises the nobility that will not beg even when it has nothing to live on; Farid's salok shows the same spirit, preferring death to sitting at another's door.
Greek Mythology
“Work is no disgrace: it is idleness which is a disgrace.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days l. 311 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod tells Perses that no work is shameful; only idleness, which leads to want and begging, is.
The Kural's nobility likewise counts poverty without begging as honourable.
Philosophy
Kant teaches that a person has a duty of self-respect and should not become anyone's lackey or let others trample on his rights with impunity, because servility betrays one's own dignity as a rational being.
— Immanuel Kant, The Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Virtue, on servility (paraphrase) [11]
For Kant, abasing oneself before others violates a duty owed to the humanity in oneself.
The Kural's saalbu, refusing to beg even in want, is a form of the self-respect Kant calls a duty.

Real Life Example

Garibaldi refuses all rewards and returns to his farm on Caprera
November 1860 · Naples and Caprera, Italy
After his Expedition of the Thousand conquered Sicily and Naples in 1860, Giuseppe Garibaldi handed his dictatorship over southern Italy to King Victor Emmanuel II. He turned down all the rewards offered to him and went back at once to his small farm on the rocky island of Caprera, where he had settled to farm some years before.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Having won half a kingdom, Garibaldi accepted nothing for himself, and his refusal became a celebrated example of selflessness in the Italian Risorgimento.
The kural praises the nobility that will not consent to take from others even when one has little, a greatness the whole world cannot contain, and Garibaldi chose a modest farm over every reward a grateful king could give.
