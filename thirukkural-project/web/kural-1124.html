Kural 1124

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

வாழ்தல் உயிர்க்கன்னள் ஆயிழை சாதல் அதற்கன்னள் நீங்கும் இடத்து.
Vaazhdhal uyirkkannal aayizhai saadhal adharkannal neengum idaththu.

Meaning

When my jeweled love is with me, she is like life to my soul. When she leaves, she is like death to it.

Elementary

When your best friend is with you the whole day feels bright, and when they go home it can feel empty and dark. The poet says the person he loves feels like life itself when near, and leaving feels like losing life.

University

The couplet balances vaazhdhal (வாழ்தல், living) against saadhal (சாதல், dying), with annal (அன்னள், 'she is like') repeated in each half and neengum idaththu ('at the time she departs') as the hinge. Aayizhai (ஆயிழை) is an epithet, 'she of chosen jewels'; Kalaignar extends it to 'she who wears chosen virtues as her ornaments'. Mu. Varadarasanar: 'when she joins me she is like life to my soul, when she parts she is like death to it'; Solomon Pappaiah reads the first half as 'like the body to the soul', a slightly different construal of the same syntax. Pope: 'Life is she to my very soul when she draws nigh; / Dissevered from the maid with jewels rare, I die!' Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Rama tries to leave her behind for his forest exile, Sita answers that being with him is heaven and being without him is hell, and insists on going with him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda, Sita's plea to accompany Rama (paraphrase) [3]
In Valmiki's Ramayana Sita treats separation from her husband as worse than any hardship of the forest.
Like the Kural, she makes the beloved's presence life and his absence a kind of death.
Christianity
“For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain.”
— Philippians 1:21 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that his whole life is bound up with Christ, so that even death is welcome because it brings him closer.
As the Kural's beloved is life to the lover, Christ is life itself to Paul, though Paul reverses the Kural's fear of death.
Islam
“He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur'an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an presents spouses as a source of rest and of a God-given affection between two people.
The Kural's beloved is the life of the lover's soul; the Qur'an speaks of a spouse as the one in whom the soul finds rest.
Judaism
“Either friendship or death”
— Babylonian Talmud, Taanit 23a (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
After Honi awakes from seventy years of sleep and finds no one who recognises him, he prays for death, and Rava cites this folk saying.
The Kural's lover feels that losing his companion is like dying; the Talmud's saying says the same of losing all companionship.
Buddhism
“Therefore hold nothing dear, for separation from the dear is painful. There are no bonds for those who have nothing beloved or unloved.”
— Dhammapada 211 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says the pain of parting comes from holding someone dear in the first place.
The Kural's lover feels parting as death; the Buddha recognises the same pain and points to non-attachment as the way out.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra names, as one form of 'sorrowful' (arta) meditation, the anguished brooding over separation from what one holds dear and the longing to be reunited with it.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.30-34, on arta-dhyana (paraphrase; cf. tr. Nathmal Tatia) [8]
Jainism classes this grief among the unwholesome states of mind that bind fresh karma to the soul.
The Kural's 'like death when she leaves' is exactly the state of mind the Jain text diagnoses as a karmic danger.
Sikhism
“Chanting it, I live; forgetting it, I die.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 9, So Dar (Rehras), Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the evening prayer So Dar (Rehras), Guru Nanak says that remembering the Name is life itself and that forgetting it is a kind of death.
The Kural's beloved is life when present and death when she leaves, exactly the two poles Guru Nanak sets between remembering and forgetting the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
After Eurydice dies, Orpheus descends to the underworld to bring her back; when he loses her a second time he grieves until his own death and refuses all other love.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1-85 and 11.1-66; Virgil, Georgics 4.453-527 (paraphrase) [10]
The myth makes the loss of the beloved the end of the lover's life in all but name.
The Kural's lover says the beloved's departure is death to him; Orpheus lives that out.
Philosophy
“Love will make men dare to die for their beloved-love alone; andwomen as well as men. Of this, Alcestis, the daughter of Pelias, is a monument to all Hellas; for she was willing to lay down her life on behalf of her husband”
— Plato, Symposium 179b, speech of Phaedrus (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
In Plato's Symposium, Phaedrus praises love as the force that makes lovers value each other above their own lives.
The Kural's beloved is the lover's life; Phaedrus's example shows a lover giving up her life for the beloved.

Real Life Example

The wife of Yi Eung-tae writes a farewell letter
1586 · Andong, Joseon Korea (now South Korea)
In 1586 Yi Eung-tae, a member of the Goseong Yi clan in Andong, Korea, died young, and his pregnant wife wrote him a letter in the Korean script, addressing him as the father of their son Won. In it she wrote that she could not live without him and wanted to go to him, asked how their unborn child should know its father, and begged him to come and speak to her in her dreams. The letter was laid in his grave, where archaeologists found it centuries later, and it helped them identify him.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letter is one of the most moving personal documents to survive from Joseon Korea, and it shows a deep marriage of love at a time when such feelings were rarely written down.
The kural says the beloved is like life to the soul when near and like death when parted, and this young widow's letter says almost exactly that: without her husband she felt she could not go on living.
