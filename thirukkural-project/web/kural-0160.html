Kural 160

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

உண்ணாது நோற்பார் பெரியர் பிறர்சொல்லும் இன்னாச்சொல் நோற்பாரின் பின்.
Unnaadhu norpaar periyar pirarsollum, Innaachchol norpaarin pin.

Meaning

People who go without food to train themselves are great. But they come second to people who put up with the cruel words others say to them.

Elementary

Going without food for a whole day to be good is really hard, and people admire it. This kural says something is even harder and better: when someone says mean things to you and you swallow your anger and stay kind, you are the real champion.

University

Valluvar closes the chapter by ranking two kinds of நோன்பு (austerity): உண்ணாது நோற்றல், enduring hunger by fasting, and இன்னாச்சொல் நோற்றல், enduring cruel speech. By placing the faster 'after' (பின்) the one who bears insult, he puts the most visible ascetic practice of his time, prominent in both Jain and Brahmanical asceticism, below an inner, social virtue. Repeating நோற்பார் in both lines insists that patience is itself tapas, completing the argument begun in Kural 159. The ranking resonates with the Dhammapada's 'Enduring patience is the highest austerity,' with the hadith that tells a fasting person to answer abuse by saying 'I am fasting,' and with Isaiah's prophetic critique that a true fast is measured by ethical conduct.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“forgiveness is stored ascetic merit; forgiveness protecteth the ascetic merit of the future; forgiveness is asceticism; forgiveness is holiness”
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Section XXIX (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
In the verses Yudhishthira attributes to the sage Kashyapa, forgiveness is declared to be asceticism (tapas) itself and the guardian of all ascetic merit.
Like the kural, it counts forbearance as the true austerity, above physical penances.
Christianity
“Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake.”
— Matthew 5:11 (KJV) [5]
Jesus pronounces those who are reviled and slandered 'blessed.' In the same sermon (Matthew 6:16-18) he warns that fasting done for show has no value.
Enduring others' evil words is blessed, while external fasting is played down, which comes close to the kural's ranking.
Islam
“Fasting is a shield (or a screen or a shelter). So, the person observing fasting should avoid sexual relation with his wife and should not behave foolishly and impudently, and if somebody fights with him or abuses him, he should tell him twice, 'I am fasting.'”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1894 [6]
The Prophet teaches that a fast is only complete if the faster also refuses to return abuse. Bearing insult is part of the fast's real substance.
Both texts link fasting and enduring abusive speech, and make patience under insult the deeper discipline.
Judaism
“Is not this the fast that I have chosen? To loose the fetters of wickedness, To undo the bands of the yoke, And to let the oppressed go free, And that ye break every yoke?”
— Isaiah 58:6 (JPS 1917) [7]
Isaiah rebukes people who fast while behaving badly. God values ethical conduct above abstaining from food.
Like the kural, it ranks moral virtue above fasting as the true austerity.
Buddhism
“Enduring patience is the highest austerity. ‘Nibbana is supreme,’ say the Buddhas. He is not a true monk who harms another, nor a true renunciate who oppresses others.”
— Dhammapada, verse 184 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
In the verses known as the Ovāda Pātimokkha, the Buddhas declare patient endurance (khanti) the supreme form of tapas.
Almost a direct statement of the kural: patient endurance ranks above every other austerity, fasting included.
Jainism
“A monk should not be angry if beaten, nor should he therefore entertain sinful thoughts; knowing patience to be the highest good, a monk should meditate on the Law.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.26 (cf. 2.2–3 on hunger) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The same Uttarādhyayana lecture that begins its list of hardships with hunger calls patience under abuse and violence 'the highest good'.
Valluvar ranks those who fast below those who endure cruel words, and the Uttarādhyayana, which counts hunger among the hardships a monk must bear, likewise names patience the highest good.
Sikhism
“To practice forgiveness is the true fast, good conduct and contentment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 223, Raag Gauri, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak redefines fasting: the real vow is forgiveness, together with good conduct and contentment.
The Kural ranks those who fast below those who endure cruel words; Guru Nanak likewise puts forbearance in the place of the outward fast.
Greek Mythology
“He endured both being struck and insulted without a word, though he was in his own house”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book XXIV (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
The dead suitor Amphimedon recalls how the disguised Odysseus, a starving beggar in his own hall, silently bore their blows and insults.
Odysseus endures hunger and insult together, and Homer presents his silent endurance of abuse as his greatest test.
Philosophy
“for the most part he was despised and laughed at, yet bore all this ill-usage patiently.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers II.21 (trans. R. D. Hicks) [12]
Diogenes Laertius reports that Socrates was famous for plain living and endurance of hunger and cold, but even more for calmly bearing mockery and abuse, including Xanthippe's scolding.
Socrates combines bodily austerity with patience under insult, and it is the patience his biographer dwells on, much as the kural ranks it higher.

Real Life Example

Michael Faraday bears Humphry Davy's opposition to his election to the Royal Society
1824 · London, United Kingdom
Michael Faraday had begun his scientific career in 1813 as an assistant to Humphry Davy, the leading chemist of his day, at the Royal Institution. When Faraday was put forward for Fellowship of the Royal Society, Davy opposed his election, and Faraday's own papers record that Davy was angry with him at the time. Faraday did not respond with hostility; he consulted the chemist William Wollaston and called on Davy, and he was elected a Fellow in 1824.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Faraday's restraint toward the patron who tried to block him allowed him to keep working within the scientific establishment, where he went on to become one of the greatest experimental scientists in history.
The kural ranks those who endure cruel words above even those who endure fasting; Faraday endured the hostility of his own mentor without retaliating, an austerity of the inner life rather than the body.
