Kural 431

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

செருக்குஞ் சினமும் சிறுமையும் இல்லார் பெருக்கம் பெருமித நீர்த்து.
Serukkunj chinamum sirumaiyum illaar Perukkam perumitha neerththu.

Meaning

People who are free from pride, anger and mean, petty ways will grow in success, and that success will be truly honored.

Elementary

Some people get rich and then turn bossy, angry, and greedy for low pleasures. This kural says that when someone does well in life but stays humble, calm, and clean-minded, their success is the kind people truly respect.

University

The chapter opens by naming three faults (குற்றம்) a ruler must purge: செருக்கு (arrogance, the swelling of self that success breeds), சினம் (anger), and சிறுமை (literally ‘smallness’; Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasan gloss it as காமம், base lust or petty desire). The claim is conditional and political: பெருக்கம் (increase, prosperity) only becomes பெருமிதம் (dignity, honourable greatness) when these three are absent. Wealth and power are morally neutral amplifiers; what they amplify is character. The triadic diagnosis of pride, anger and desire as the roots of ruin is one of the most widely shared moral analyses across ancient traditions, from the Gita’s ‘demoniac’ qualities to the Mishnah’s list of things that ‘put a man out of the world’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Bewildered by false ego, strength, pride, lust and anger, the demons become envious of the Supreme Personality of Godhead, who is situated in their own bodies and in the bodies of others, and blaspheme against the real religion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.18 [3]
Krishna describes how people with power and strength are knocked off balance by ego, pride, lust and anger, and turn against what is good.
The Gita and the Kural name nearly the same cluster of faults (pride, lust, anger) as what corrupts the strong; the Kural states the positive side: strength without them is honourable.
Christianity
“For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world.”
— 1 John 2:16 (KJV) [4]
John names desire and pride as the marks of a worldly life that pulls a person away from God.
Both texts single out desire and pride as the faults that spoil a life; the Kural adds anger and applies it to prosperous rulers.
Islam
“Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good;”
— Quran 3:134 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran praises those who stay generous in good and bad times and who hold back their anger and forgive.
Restraining anger while prospering is exactly what the Kural says makes success dignified.
Judaism
“Rabbi Elazar Ha-kappar said: envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 [6]
A rabbi of the Mishnah lists three inner drives that drive a person out of a good life.
Like the Kural, the Mishnah diagnoses character through a triad of faults; lust and craving for honour (a form of arrogance) overlap directly.
Buddhism
“One should give up anger, renounce pride, and overcome all fetters. Suffering never befalls him who clings not to mind and body and is detached.”
— Dhammapada 221 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches letting go of anger and pride, and cutting the fetters of craving, so that suffering no longer takes hold.
The same three faults, anger, pride and craving, are named as what must be dropped; the Kural says the reward is dignified prosperity, the Dhammapada says freedom from suffering.
Jainism
“He will sink through anger; he will go down through pride; delusion will block up his path; through greed he will incur dangers in both worlds.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.54 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Nami, renouncing his throne, names the passions (kashāyas) that ruin a person: anger, pride, delusion and greed. Indra then praises him for having conquered each of them.
Valluvar says only those free of arrogance, anger and base desire turn prosperity into true dignity; Nami, a king himself, names the same cluster of faults as the cause of a person's fall.
Sikhism
“Unfulfilled sexual desire and unresolved anger waste the body away, as gold is dissolved by borax.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 932, Raag Raamkalee (Dakhanee Oankaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Dakhanee Oankaar Guru Nanak names lust (kaam) and anger (krodh), two of the five thieves, as forces that quietly wear a person down from within, as borax melts gold.
The Kural says prosperity has dignity only when it is free of lust, anger and arrogance; Guru Nanak shows the same faults eating away at the person who harbours them.
Greek Mythology
Niobe, queen of Thebes, rich in wealth and fourteen children, boasts that she is greater than the goddess Leto and forbids her worship; Apollo and Artemis kill her children and she is turned to weeping stone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6 (Niobe) [10]
A queen with everything turns arrogant about her good fortune, and her pride destroys all she has.
The negative image of the Kural: prosperity joined to arrogance loses its dignity and ends in ruin.
Philosophy
Plato divides the soul into reason, spirit (the seat of anger) and appetite (desire), and argues that a person, like a city, is just and well-ordered only when reason governs spirit and appetite.
— Plato, Republic, Book IV [11]
Plato thinks a good life needs your thinking mind in charge of your temper and your cravings.
The Kural’s absence of anger and base desire is what Plato calls the rule of reason over spirit and appetite; both link inner order to worthy public standing.

Real Life Example

Louis IX hears his subjects' cases himself at Vincennes
1226-1270 · Vincennes and Paris, France
King Louis IX of France often administered justice in person, and his companion and biographer Jean de Joinville recorded that at Vincennes, after Mass, the king would sit in the open and let anyone with a dispute approach him directly, without ushers in the way. From 1247 he sent out commissions of inquiry to hear complaints against his own royal officials. His reputation for fairness was so great that foreign rulers asked him to arbitrate their disputes, and he was canonised in 1297.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Louis IX's power and wealth were remembered not as a cause for fear but as a source of honour, because he used them with restraint and approachability.
The kural says prosperity has real dignity only when free of arrogance, anger and base desire, and Louis IX's open, patient justice gave his greatness that dignity.
