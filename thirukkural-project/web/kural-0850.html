Kural 850

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

உலகத்தார் உண்டென்பது இல்லென்பான் வையத்து அலகையா வைக்கப் படும்.
Ulagaththaar undenbadhu illenbaan vaiyaththu alagaiyaa vaikkap padum.

Meaning

A person who says no to what the wise people of the world say is true will be thought of as a demon on earth.

Elementary

If everyone has seen the sun rise every morning and one boy keeps insisting “There is no sun!”, people stop taking him seriously and leave him out of games. The poet says that someone who denies what everyone knows is true gets treated like a spooky outsider.

University

Ulagaththaar (உலகத்தார்) here, as often in the Kural, means not the crowd but the learned of the world (Pappaiah: ‘the great ones’, uyarndhor), and their ‘undu’ (it exists) is denied by the fool’s ‘il’ (it does not). The traditional commentators gloss what is denied as established truths such as the other world, merit and the divine, attested by the learned; Kalaignar gives a modern secular reading: denying what has been clearly shown with evidence. Such a person is placed (vaikkappadum) as an alagai (அலகை, a demon, ghost or pey) on earth, that is, shunned as something not quite human. Sivayogi Sivakumar and Mu. Varadarasanar both stress exclusion (oduhukkappaduvaan, vilakkappaduvaan). The chapter thus ends with perverse denial as the extreme of pullarivu. Text identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“They say that this world is unreal, with no foundation, no God in control. They say it is produced of sex desire and has no cause other than lust.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.8 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
In the chapter on divine and demoniac natures, Krishna lists the denial of the world’s divine order as a mark of the asuric (demonic) person.
Valluvar likewise brands the denier of what the wise affirm as an alagai, a demon on earth.
Christianity
“Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools,”
— Romans 1:22 (KJV) [4]
Paul says those who refuse to acknowledge what is plainly evident in creation became fools while claiming wisdom.
Denial of what is evident marks the fool, as in the kural.
Islam
“And they rejected them, while their [inner] selves were convinced thereof, out of injustice and haughtiness. So see how was the end of the corrupters.”
— Qur’an 27:14 (Sahih International) [5]
Pharaoh’s people deny Moses’ clear signs out of arrogance, though inwardly convinced.
This is the perverse denial of evident truth Kalaignar reads into the kural.
Judaism
“The scoundrel thinks, “God does not care.”
— Psalms 14:1 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition) [6]
The Psalm’s naval (often rendered ‘fool’) says in his heart what the JPS note gives literally as ‘There is no God’.
The denier of what the world affirms is the Psalmist’s naval, a morally corrupt fool.
Buddhism
In the Payasi Sutta, Prince Payasi insists there is no other world, no spontaneously reborn beings and no fruit of good or bad deeds, and the monk Kumara Kassapa answers him with a long series of parables until he relents.
— Digha Nikaya 23, Payasi Sutta [7]
The sutta treats denial of the other world and of karmic results as a wrong view to be patiently corrected.
Payasi denies exactly what the traditional commentators say the kural’s fool denies.
Jainism
“A monk should not think: 'There is, indeed, no life to come, nor an exalted state to be acquired by penances; in short, I have been deceived.'”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.44 (cf. 2.45) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Among the twenty-two troubles a monk must overcome, the last is the temptation to deny what the Jinas taught: the next life, the fruit of austerity, and the Jinas themselves.
The Kural's fool flatly denies what the wise affirm, such as the other world and the fruit of deeds, and the Uttarādhyayana lists exactly this denial as a failing of right faith to be overcome.
Sikhism
“Kabir! Do not associate with the faithless cynics; run far away from them. If you touch a vessel stained with soot, some of the soot will stick to you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir (salok 131) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir tells the seeker to keep well away from the saakat, the faithless cynic who turns from God, since such company stains like soot.
The Kural says one who denies what the wise affirm is set apart like a demon; Kabir likewise sets the faithless cynic apart and tells the seeker to keep away.
Greek Mythology
King Pentheus of Thebes refuses to accept the divinity of Dionysus that the women and elders of the city acknowledge, imprisons the god in disguise, and is torn apart by the maenads.
— Euripides, Bacchae [10]
Euripides presents Pentheus’ obstinate denial as a fatal madness.
The man who denies what all around him affirm ends as an outcast and horror.
Philosophy
Aristotle holds that what everyone believes is so, and that someone who attacks this common belief will hardly have anything more credible to put in its place.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.2 [11]
For Aristotle, shared reputable opinion (endoxa) carries real evidential weight.
The kural likewise treats flat denial of what the world affirms as a mark of folly.

Real Life Example

Alfred Russel Wallace wins the flat-earth wager on the Bedford Level
March 1870 · Old Bedford River, the Fens, England
In January 1870 John Hampden, who maintained that the Earth was flat, offered a wager of up to 500 pounds to anyone who could prove its curvature. The naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace accepted, and in March 1870, using markers along a six-mile straight stretch of the Old Bedford River, showed the curvature to the satisfaction of the referee, John Henry Walsh, editor of The Field. Hampden refused to accept the verdict and pursued Wallace with abusive letters and attacks for years afterwards, and was widely regarded as a crank.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The episode is remembered as a case of a man who flatly denied an established fact even after it was demonstrated before an agreed referee.
The kural says whoever flatly denies what the wise of the world affirm will be regarded as a demon and kept apart, and Hampden's denial of the Earth's curvature after it had been shown to him left him a byword for obstinate folly.
