Kural 153

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

இன்மையுள் இன்மை விருந்தொரால் வன்மையுள் வன்மை மடவார்ப் பொறை.
Inmaiyul inmai virundhoraal vanmaiyul, Vanmai madavaarp porai.

Meaning

The worst kind of being poor is turning away a guest. The greatest kind of strength is staying patient with foolish people.

Elementary

Being poor isn't only about having little money. This kural says the saddest kind of poor is not being able to share even a snack with a friend who visits. Being strong isn't only about big muscles either: the strongest kind of strong is staying calm when someone acts silly or rude to you.

University

Valluvar builds the couplet on a double superlative, இன்மையுள் இன்மை ('the poverty within poverty') and வன்மையுள் வன்மை ('the strength within strength'), and uses it to pair two virtues that seem unrelated. The first half looks back to the chapter on hospitality (விருந்தோம்பல், ch. 9), where turning away a guest is the gravest failure of a household. The second half redefines might as the ability to bear the provocations of மடவார், 'the ignorant' or 'foolish.' Setting them side by side suggests a shared logic: hospitality and forbearance both measure inner abundance, not outward resources. Redefining strength as self-mastery is one of the most widely shared moral reversals across traditions, from the Mishnah's 'Who is mighty?' to the hadith about the strong man who controls his anger.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“These two, O king, live (as it were) in a region higher than heaven itself, viz., a man of power endued with forgiveness, and poor man that is charitable.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXIII, Vidura's counsel (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura names two people who stand above heaven itself: a powerful man who forgives, and a poor man who still gives.
It pairs the same two ideas as the kural, generosity in poverty and forbearance in power, in one saying.
Christianity
“And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient,”
— 2 Timothy 2:24 (KJV) [5]
Paul tells Timothy that a servant of God must not quarrel but stay gentle and patient. The next verse applies this to people who oppose him and need instruction.
Being patient and gentle with the contentious and unteaching is the kural's 'strength of strengths,' bearing with the foolish.
Islam
“The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength, but the strong is the one who controls himself while in anger.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 [6]
The Prophet redefines strength: the truly strong person is not the wrestler who throws others down but the one who masters himself when provoked.
This is the kural's 'strength within strength' exactly: real power is holding back, not fighting.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city’ (Proverbs 16:32).”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 4:1 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Ben Zoma's famous teaching redefines might as control over one's own impulses, and backs it with the proverb that the slow-to-anger outrank warriors.
The same reversal as the kural: the greatest strength is restraint in the face of provocation.
Buddhism
“He who without resentment endures abuse, beating and punishment; whose power, real might, is patience — him do I call a holy man.”
— Dhammapada, verse 399 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha describes the true holy man as someone whose real power is patience under abuse.
'Real might is patience' is almost a translation of the kural's வன்மையுள் வன்மை ... பொறை.
Jainism
“If a layman abuses a monk, he should not grow angry against him; because he would be like a child, a monk should not grow angry.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Jacobi notes that the word translated 'child' (bāla) also means an ignorant man. Mahāvīra's teaching is that one who answers a fool's abuse with anger becomes a fool too, so patience is the stronger course.
Valluvar calls bearing with fools the greatest strength of all, and the Uttarādhyayana gives the same rule of not raging at the ignorant, though it has no counterpart to the Kural's first half on failing a guest.
Sikhism
“if you are powerful, be weak; … How rare is one who is known as such a devotee.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, Salok 128 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
A salok in the Guru Granth Sahib describes the rare devotee who, though strong, lives as if powerless; the same salok also praises sharing with others even when one has little.
The Kural calls bearing with fools the strength within strength and failing a guest the poverty within poverty; this salok joins strength held back with generosity in the same way.
Greek Mythology
Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as travelers, are turned away from a thousand wealthy homes. The poor old couple Baucis and Philemon welcome them and share the little food they have.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book VIII (Baucis and Philemon) [11]
In Ovid's tale the gods reward the poor couple's hospitality and punish the stingy neighbors. However poor they were, Baucis and Philemon never fell into the 'poverty' of refusing a guest.
It illustrates the kural's first half: the real poverty is being unable to welcome a guest, not a lack of means.
Philosophy
“Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius starts each day expecting to meet difficult people, and reminds himself that their faults come from ignorance, so he shouldn't be angry with them.
Bearing with the foolish (மடவார்) because they are ignorant is the kural's picture of real strength.

Real Life Example

Lincoln appoints Edwin Stanton despite Stanton's earlier snub
1855-1862 · Cincinnati, Ohio, and Washington, D.C., United States
In 1855 Abraham Lincoln, then an Illinois lawyer, was retained for the defence in the McCormick v. Manny reaper patent case and prepared a brief, but when the trial moved to Cincinnati the eastern lawyers leading the defence, including the eminent attorney Edwin M. Stanton, kept him from taking part. Lincoln nonetheless supplied the brief he had written. After he became president, Lincoln chose Stanton as his Secretary of War, and Stanton became one of his most important advisers.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Lincoln's willingness to set aside a personal humiliation and appoint the most capable man available is often cited as an example of his magnanimity.
The kural calls patiently bearing with the slights of others the strength within strength; Lincoln bore Stanton's slight and later gave him one of the highest offices in the land.
