Kural 1069

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

இரவுள்ள உள்ளம் உருகும் கரவுள்ள உள்ளதூஉம் இன்றிக் கெடும்.
Iravulla ullam urugum karavulla ulladhooum indrik kedum.

Meaning

Just thinking about begging makes the heart melt with sadness. Thinking of people who hide what they have and refuse breaks it completely.

Elementary

When you see someone who has to ask for help, your heart feels soft and sad. But when you think of someone who has plenty and still says 'I have nothing', your heart feels crushed. The Kural describes both feelings.

University

The couplet sets two thoughts side by side: iravu ulla (இரவுள்ள, 'when one thinks of begging') the ullam urugum ('heart melts'); karavu ulla ('when one thinks of concealment/refusal') ulladhooum ('even what there is', i.e. the melting heart itself) indrik kedum ('perishes, ceasing to exist'). The pun on ulla ('to think') and ullam ('heart') and ulladhu ('what exists') is characteristic. In the Parimelazhagar line, as given by Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah, the speaker's compassion for beggars is overwhelmed by horror at those who refuse while having plenty. Pope renders it 'The heart will melt away at thought of beggary, / With thought of stern repulse 'twill perish utterly.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita, Krishna says that those who cook food only for their own enjoyment, without first offering and sharing it, eat nothing but sin.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.13 (cf. Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) (paraphrase) [3]
The Gita condemns keeping one's food for oneself as sinful.
The Kural's horror at those who hide what they have reflects the same judgment on selfish keeping.
Christianity
“But whoso hath this world's good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him?”
— 1 John 3:17 (KJV) [4]
John asks how anyone who closes his heart to a needy brother can have the love of God in him.
The Kural's melting heart and the heart 'shut up' against the needy are the two sides John sets against each other.
Islam
“Here you are - those invited to spend in the cause of Allāh - but among you are those who withhold [out of greed]. And whoever withholds only withholds [benefit] from himself.”
— Qur'an 47:38 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says that those who withhold out of greed only harm themselves.
The Kural's karavu, greedy withholding, is condemned here as self-destructive.
Judaism
“If, however, there is a needy person among you, one of your kindred in any of your settlements in the land that the ETERNAL your God is giving you, do not harden your heart and shut your hand against your needy kindred.”
— Deuteronomy 15:7 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The Torah forbids hardening one's heart and closing one's hand against a needy person.
The Kural's melting heart is what the Torah demands, and the hard, concealing heart is what it forbids.
Buddhism
“Overcome the angry by non-anger; overcome the wicked by goodness; overcome the miser by generosity; overcome the liar by truth.”
— Dhammapada 223 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says the miser is to be overcome by generosity.
Where the Kural's heart is crushed by the concealer, the Dhammapada offers generosity as the answer to him.
Jainism
“Since one's mind melts with angnish when one reflects on a life of mendicity, what must their feelings be who receive alms!”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 5 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet says even the thought of begging melts the mind with anguish, so the actual experience must be far worse.
This is the Kural's first line almost word for word: the heart melts at the very thought of begging.
Sikhism
“Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 155) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in the Guru Granth Sahib, Bhagat Kabir links greed with death and forgiveness with God's own presence.
The Kural says the heart that melts at the thought of begging is destroyed at the thought of the misers who refuse; Kabir likewise ties the grasping, refusing spirit to death.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, Erysichthon cuts down Ceres' sacred oak and refuses all pity; he is punished with a hunger that consumes his wealth, makes him sell his own daughter, and finally drives him to devour himself.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Erysichthon) (paraphrase) [10]
The pitiless, grasping man is destroyed from within by his own insatiable greed.
Like the Kural's concealer, Erysichthon's hardness ends in his own utter ruin.
Philosophy
Adam Smith opens The Theory of Moral Sentiments by arguing that however selfish people may be, there are principles in human nature that make them care about the fortune of others, so that we feel sorrow at the sight of misery.
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments I.i.1 (paraphrase) [11]
Smith grounds morality in the natural sympathy we feel for those in distress.
The Kural's heart that melts at begging is Smith's sympathy, and the concealer is the one in whom it has died.

Real Life Example

Dickens publishes A Christmas Carol
19 December 1843 · London, England, United Kingdom
Troubled by a parliamentary report on child labour, Charles Dickens first planned a factual pamphlet but instead wrote A Christmas Carol in about six weeks, publishing it on 19 December 1843; the first printing of 6,000 copies sold out by Christmas Eve. In the story the miser Ebenezer Scrooge coldly turns away gentlemen collecting for the poor, before being shown the suffering his hardness causes and the joy of generosity.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The book became one of the best-loved stories in English and fixed Scrooge's name as the type of the person who refuses help to those in need.
The kural says the heart melts at the thought of those who must beg but is crushed at the thought of those who refuse them, and Dickens wrote his story out of pity for the poor and made the refuser its central figure.
