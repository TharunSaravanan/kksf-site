Kural 1234

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

பணைநீங்கிப் பைந்தொடி சோரும் துணைநீங்கித் தொல்கவின் வாடிய தோள்.
Panai-neengip paindhoti soarum thunaineengith tholkavin vaadiya thoal.

Meaning

With your partner gone, your gold bangles slip off, and your shoulders have lost their old beauty and fullness.

Elementary

Have you ever worn a ring or bracelet that fit perfectly, and then it got loose after you were sick for a while? In this verse, a girl is so sad that her loved one has gone away that she grows thin, and her bangles keep sliding off her arms.

University

The couplet builds a double loss with two parallel absolutives: paṇai nīṅki (‘the plumpness having gone’) and tuṇai nīṅki (‘the companion having gone’), so the departure of the tuṇai, the spouse, causes the departure of the paṇai, the fullness of the arm. Tolkaviṉ vāṭiya tōḷ means ‘shoulders whose former (tol) beauty (kaviṉ) has withered’, and pain̮toṭi cōrum, ‘the fresh-gold bangles slip down’, introduces the image of the loose bangle (toṭi, vaḷai) that dominates 1234-1236. The slipping bangle is a stock sign of separation in Sangam akam poetry and in Sanskrit kāvya alike. Pope’s gloss (‘In the absence of your consort, your shoulders… your bracelets… have become loose’) makes the confidante the speaker. Mu. Varadarasanar reads the shoulders as causing the bangles to slip; Kalaignar glosses paintoṭi as பசும்பொன் வளையல்கள். Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the very opening of the Meghaduta, the exiled Yaksha, separated from his wife, has grown so thin that the golden bracelet has slipped from his forearm, leaving the wrist bare.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Purvamegha 2 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [3]
Kalidasa uses the slipped bracelet as the first, defining image of a lover wasted by separation.
The Kural’s loose bangles are exactly this sign, here worn by the abandoned wife rather than the exiled husband.
Christianity
“Can a maid forget her ornaments, or a bride her attire? yet my people have forgotten me days without number.”
— Jeremiah 2:32 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah uses a bride’s ornaments as the image of what a loving partner would never forget.
The Kural’s heroine has lost the bloom her ornaments once graced because the partner seems to have forgotten her.
Islam
In Nizami’s romance, Layla, married against her will and separated from Majnun, pines in secret; her beauty wanes, she grows thin and pale, and she finally sickens and dies of her longing.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (paraphrase; cf. tr. R. Gelpke) [5]
The Persian poem makes the beloved’s slow physical wasting the measure of her hidden love.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Layla loses her former beauty because the one she loves is out of reach.
Judaism
“And gone is from the daughter of Zion all her splendour.”
— Lamentations 1:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Lamentations personifies Jerusalem as a woman whose former glory has departed with her losses.
The Kural’s heroine, too, has seen her ‘old beauty’ (tolkaviṉ) depart along with her companion.
Buddhism
“Like rounded door-bars — both of them — my arms were once splendid. With age, they’re like dried up patali trees.” Ambapali adds that her hands, once adorned with gold and delicate rings, are now like onions and tubers.”
— Therigatha 13.1, Ambapali (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The former courtesan, now a nun, describes how her once-lovely arms and jewelled hands have withered.
The Kural’s heroine sees the same withering of arms that once filled their ornaments, though grief, not age, has done it.
Jainism
The Jain didactic anthology Naladiyar repeatedly warns that youth and bodily beauty wither like a flower, so that the body once admired soon becomes something no one wishes to look at.
— Naladiyar, chapters on the instability of youth and the body (paraphrase; cf. tr. G.U. Pope, 1893) [8]
Tamil Jain ethics uses the fading of beauty to turn the mind from the body to dharma.
The Kural shows that fading happening in a single season of grief; the Jain poets would draw from it a lesson about the body’s impermanence.
Sikhism
“O Lord! Without my Husband, all these adornments are useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1361, Phunhay, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan's bride-soul has put on every ornament, but says none of it means anything while her Husband is away.
The Kural's wife, her partner gone, has lost the fullness of her shoulders, and her gold bangles slip from her arms; the Guru's bride likewise finds her ornaments worthless without the Husband.
Greek Mythology
Writing to her husband Protesilaus at Troy, Laodamia says that while he is at war she will not dress her hair or wear gold and fine robes; she lives like a widow in mourning until he comes home.
— Ovid, Heroides 13 (Laodamia to Protesilaus) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s heroine sets aside her ornaments as a sign of her husband’s absence.
The Kural’s heroine does not remove her bangles; grief makes them fall away on their own.
Philosophy
Robert Burton lists the symptoms of love-melancholy: lovers grow lean, pale and hollow-eyed, lose their appetite and sleep, and pine away, so that the disease can be read in their bodies.
— Robert Burton, The Anatomy of Melancholy, Part 3, Section 2, Member 3 (Symptoms of Love-Melancholy) (paraphrase) [11]
Burton’s encyclopedia of melancholy treats love-sickness as a real wasting illness with visible signs.
The Kural’s thin shoulders and loose bangles are just such symptoms, recorded from the patient’s side.

Real Life Example

Li Qingzhao grows thinner than the yellow flowers
Early 12th century · Song dynasty China
Li Qingzhao, the Song dynasty poet, married the official and antiquarian Zhao Mingcheng in 1101, and during his frequent absences she wrote poems of longing for his return. In her song lyric to the tune Drunk under the Shadow of Flowers, set on the Double Ninth festival, the speaker drinks alone by the eastern hedge after dusk and says she has grown thinner than the yellow flowers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
She is China's most celebrated woman poet, and her lyrics are the classic Chinese picture of a wife wasting away while her husband is gone.
The kural says that with her partner gone the wife's shoulders have lost their fullness and her bangles slip, and Li Qingzhao's speaker, alone at dusk, has grown thinner than the flowers.
