Kural 1208

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

எனைத்து நினைப்பினும் காயார் அனைத்தன்றோ காதலர் செய்யும் சிறப்பு.
Enaiththu ninaippinum kaayaar anaiththandroa kaadhalar seyyum sirappu.

Meaning

However much I think of him, he never gets angry with me for it. Isn't that the great kindness my loved one shows me?

Elementary

Some people get grumpy if you call them too many times. But the lady in this poem says that no matter how often she thinks about her husband, he never gets cross about it. She feels that is his special gift to her.

University

The heroine finds consolation in a small, wry point: however much (eṉaittu) she thinks of him, he does not grow angry (kāyār, from kāy, ‘to be angry, to burn’). Aṉaittu aṉṟō, ‘is it not that much?’, makes this the measure of the ciṟappu, the special honour or favour her beloved bestows. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar render ciṟappu as ‘great help’ (uṭavi), Solomon Pappaiah as ‘the joy he gives’, and Pope as ‘This honour doth my love on me bestow.’ The humour is gentle: he cannot object to what he cannot see, so her freedom to remember is the one gift his absence still allows. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna says that for one who sees him everywhere and sees everything in him, he is never lost, nor is that person ever lost to him.
— Bhagavad Gita 6.30 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita promises that the Lord never withdraws from one who constantly holds him in mind.
The Kural’s beloved never resents being remembered; Krishna goes further and is never lost to the one who remembers him.
Christianity
“Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:4-5 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as patient and slow to anger.
The Kural’s husband who is never angry, however often he is thought of, embodies love that ‘is not easily provoked’.
Islam
In a hadith qudsi God says: I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me; if he remembers Me within himself, I remember him within Myself.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7405 (paraphrase) [5]
The divine Beloved welcomes and returns every remembrance.
The Kural’s beloved never resents being remembered; in this hadith the Beloved responds to remembrance with remembrance.
Judaism
“Hatred stirreth up strifes; but love covereth all transgressions.”
— Proverbs 10:12 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs contrasts hatred’s quarrels with love’s readiness to pass over offences.
The beloved who never grows angry at being endlessly thought of shows love that covers all things.
Buddhism
Among a husband’s duties to his wife, the Buddha lists honouring her, not disparaging her, being faithful to her, giving her authority in the home and providing her with adornments.
— Digha Nikaya 31, Sigalovada Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Narada Thera) [7]
The Sigalovada Sutta sets out a husband’s respectful kindness toward his wife.
The Kural’s husband who never grows angry with his wife is a small instance of the non-disparaging regard the Buddha asks of husbands.
Jainism
Samantabhadra tells the Jina that, being free from attachment and having shed all enmity, he has no need of worship and no displeasure at blame, yet remembrance of his virtues purifies the mind of the one who remembers.
— Samantabhadra, Svayambhu Stotra, verse 57 (paraphrase) [8]
In Jain devotion the Jina is never angered, however he is thought of, and the benefit of remembering him falls to the devotee.
The Kural’s beloved is never angry at being remembered; the Jina, beyond all passion, cannot be, though he also feels no affection in return.
Sikhism
“He does not know any bitter words; the Perfect Lord God does not even consider my faults and demerits.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 784, Raag Soohee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes the Beloved Lord as never harsh, never speaking a bitter word, and never holding the devotee's faults against them.
The Kural's wife takes comfort that however much she thinks of her beloved he is never angry with her for it; Gurbani likewise praises a Beloved who never answers with anger or bitter words.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, married from youth and grown old together in a poor cottage, live in such harmony that when the gods grant them a wish they ask only that neither should outlive the other.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611-724 (Baucis and Philemon) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s old couple embody a love without quarrels that lasts a lifetime.
Their unruffled mutual devotion is the gentle, never-angry love the Kural’s wife credits to her husband.
Philosophy
Aristotle says that complaints and reproaches arise mainly in friendships of utility, whereas friends who love one another for their goodness are eager to do each other good and do not quarrel.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.13, 1162b (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.D. Ross) [11]
Friendship grounded in the other’s goodness is, for Aristotle, free of recrimination.
The beloved who never grows angry, however much he is thought of, shows the untroubled goodwill of Aristotle’s best friendship.

Real Life Example

Johannes Brahms and his lifelong devotion to Clara Schumann
1853–1896 · Düsseldorf and elsewhere, Germany
The young Johannes Brahms came to the Düsseldorf home of Robert and Clara Schumann in 1853 and was welcomed as a genius. When Robert became gravely ill in 1854 and spent his last two years in an asylum, Brahms helped Clara run the household, care for the children and return to her concert career, and he came to love her. After Robert's death in 1856 they went separate ways but remained lifelong friends through their letters; Brahms never married, and he died within a year of Clara's death in 1896.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Brahms's long love for Clara was never rejected or resented; it was received as a deep and lasting friendship.
The kural's heroine takes comfort that however much she thinks of her beloved, he is never angry with her for it, and Clara accepted Brahms's devotion for forty years without turning it away.
