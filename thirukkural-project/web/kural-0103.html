Kural 103

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

பயன்தூக்கார் செய்த உதவி நயன்தூக்கின் நன்மை கடலின் பெரிது.
Payandhookkaar seydha udhavi nayandhookkin Nanmai kadalin peridhu.

Meaning

When someone helps you without thinking about what they will get back, the goodness of that help, if you weigh it, is bigger than the sea.

Elementary

Some people help you because they want something later, like a sticker trade. Others help just because they care. This kural says the second kind of help is bigger than the whole sea.

University

The couplet hinges on a pun on தூக்கு, 'to weigh': the benefactor is பயன்தூக்கார், one who does not weigh the return, while the recipient is invited to weigh (நயன்தூக்கின்) the help's goodness or love (நயன், from நயம்). Gratitude is thus framed as an act of moral appraisal that the giver refused to perform on his own behalf; the asymmetry is the point. Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah gloss நயன் as the love (அன்பு) behind the act, so the 'sea' measures not the material help but the disinterested affection it expresses. This parallels the Bhagavad Gita's category of sattvic giving (17.20), made 'without expectation of return', and Aristotle's remark (Nicomachean Ethics 8.13) that it is noble to benefit another without a view to repayment even though most people prefer to receive. The kural quietly criticises transactional benevolence while insisting that recipients never take pure generosity lightly.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Charity given out of duty, without expectation of return, at the proper time and place, and to a worthy person is considered to be in the mode of goodness.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.20 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna says the highest, purest kind of giving is giving that expects nothing back.
The Gita praises exactly the kind of help the Kural says is 'vaster than the sea': help given without weighing the return.
Christianity
“But love ye your enemies, and do good, and lend, hoping for nothing again; and your reward shall be great, and ye shall be the children of the Highest: for he is kind unto the unthankful and to the evil.”
— Luke 6:35 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his followers to do good and lend without hoping to get anything back, because that is how God himself behaves.
'Hoping for nothing again' is the Kural's பயன்தூக்கார், the one who does not weigh the return.
Islam
“[Saying], “We feed you only for the countenance of Allah. We wish not from you reward or gratitude.”
— Qur'an 76:9 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the righteous feeding the poor, the orphan and the captive while telling them they want neither payment nor even thanks, only God's pleasure.
This is the purest form of help 'that does not weigh the return', whose worth the Kural calls greater than the sea.
Judaism
Commenting on Jacob's request that Joseph bury him in Canaan, Rashi explains that kindness done for the dead is called chesed shel emet, 'true kindness', because the one who does it expects no repayment.
— Rashi on Genesis 47:29 [6]
Jewish tradition says the truest kindness is the kind you do for someone who can never pay you back, like caring for the dead.
Judaism, like the Kural, ranks help that cannot be repaid or calculated as the highest and most precious form.
Buddhism
The Diamond Sutra teaches that a bodhisattva should practise giving without being supported by any notion (of self, recipient, gift, or reward), and that the merit of such giving is as immeasurable as space in every direction.
— Vajracchedika Prajnaparamita (Diamond Sutra), section 4 [7]
Buddhist teaching says the best giving is done without thinking 'I am giving' or 'what will I get', and that such giving is too vast to measure.
Its 'immeasurable as space' matches the Kural's 'greater than the sea' for help given without calculation.
Jainism
“They say that in the time of drought the well of spring-water will preserve the inhabitants, though by drawing its water they subsist. So the duty of liberality is found with the great, even when in declining circumstances; with others, even when they are rich, it is rare.”
— Nālaḍiyār ch. 19 'Greatness', st. 4 (= st. 184), English translation in The Indian Antiquary (wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain-authored Nālaḍiyār pictures true generosity as a spring-fed well that keeps giving in drought without asking anything back.
Valluvar's benefactor gives without weighing the return, and the Nālaḍiyār's well that sustains the village in drought is the same unreckoning goodness that the recipient should judge greater than the sea.
Sikhism
“One who performs selfless service, without thought of reward, shall attain his Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 286, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Seva done nihkam, without desire for any return, is one of the central Sikh ideals; Sukhmani Sahib makes such service the path to God.
The Kural's benefactor who helps without weighing the return is exactly the Sikh nihkam sevadar, though the Sikh verse praises the giver where the Kural asks the receiver to weigh the gift.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid's tale, the poor old couple Baucis and Philemon welcome two unknown travellers (Jupiter and Mercury in disguise) whom richer households had turned away, and share their meagre food with no hope of return; the gods reward them by turning their hut into a temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 (Roman retelling of a Phrygian legend) [10]
A very poor couple shared everything they had with strangers without expecting anything back, and the gods counted that kindness as enormous.
Hospitality given without weighing any return is judged immensely great, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“now it is noble to do well by another without a view to repayment, but it is the receiving of benefits that is advantageous.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.13, 1162b (W. D. Ross trans.) [11]
Aristotle observes that helping without expecting repayment is the noble thing, even though most people would rather be on the receiving end.
Aristotle names the same 'not weighing the return' that the Kural says makes help vaster than the sea.

Real Life Example

CERN puts the World Wide Web in the public domain
30 April 1993 · CERN, Geneva, Switzerland
British scientist Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989 while working at CERN, the European particle physics laboratory, to help scientists share information. On 30 April 1993 CERN published a statement putting the World Wide Web software in the public domain, making it available royalty-free to anyone; a later release was issued under an open licence. CERN states that these actions allowed the web to flourish.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By declining to charge for the technology, CERN let anyone add a server, create pages or build a browser, and the web spread around the world within a few years.
The kural praises help given by those who do not weigh what they will get back; releasing the web free of royalties is a gift whose value to the world is hard to measure.
