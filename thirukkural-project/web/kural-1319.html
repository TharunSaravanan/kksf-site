Kural 1319

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

தன்னை உணர்த்தினும் காயும் பிறர்க்கும்நீர் இந்நீரர் ஆகுதிர் என்று.
Thannai unarththinum kaayum pirarkkum-neer inneerar aakudhir endru

Meaning

Even when I comfort her out of her sulk, she gets angry again and says, "So this is how you sweet-talk other women too!"

Elementary

Imagine you finally cheer up a grumpy friend with a funny joke, and she says, ‘Hmph, I bet you tell that joke to everyone!’ Even making up becomes a new reason to tease.

University

Taṉṉai uṇarttiṉum means ‘even if I appease her (end her sulk)’; uṇarttal is the technical term for coaxing a lover out of ūṭal. Yet she grows angry (kāyum), saying piṟarkkum nīr innīrar ākutir (‘you must be like this with others too’). Puliyur Kesikan, Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah agree on this reading, and Kalaignar adds that the husband had humbled himself to win her back. The couplet shows pulavi feeding on its own remedy, the reconciliation itself becoming fresh evidence; Pope: ‘I then began to soothe and coax, to calm her jealous mind; “I see,” quoth she, “to other folks how you are wondrous kind.”’ The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the tenth canto of the Gita Govinda, Krishna pleads with the angry Radha with tender flattery, begging her to set her foot upon his head as a sign of forgiveness.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 10 (paraphrase) [3]
Indian love poetry made the lover’s coaxing of a sulking beloved into an art of its own.
The husband here is doing the same coaxing, but his wife turns even his skill at it into a new charge.
Christianity
“Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes love as willing to believe and bear with the other.
The wife plays at refusing to believe even her husband’s reconciliation, the opposite of love that ‘believeth all things’.
Islam
“And if a woman fears from her husband contempt or evasion, there is no sin upon them if they make terms of settlement between them - and settlement is best.”
— Qur’an 4:128 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an counsels husband and wife to settle their differences, declaring reconciliation the better way.
The husband offers just such a settlement, and the wife’s refusal to accept it is the comic heart of the couplet.
Judaism
“A soft answer turneth away wrath; but a grievous word stirreth up anger.”
— Proverbs 15:1 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs teaches that gentle words usually calm anger.
The joke of the Kural is that the husband’s soft answer does not turn away his wife’s wrath, but stirs up a new sulk.
Buddhism
“Let none find fault with others; let none see the omissions and commissions of others. But let one see one’s own acts, done and undone.”
— Dhammapada 50 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha urges people to stop examining others for faults and look at themselves instead.
The wife keeps finding fault even in her husband’s kindness, the habit the verse asks us to drop.
Jainism
“As the crane is produced from an egg, and the egg is produced from a crane, so they call desire the origin of delusion, and delusion the origin of desire.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching pictures desire and delusion as an endless loop, each producing the other.
The wife’s sulking works like that loop: each reconciliation gives birth to a new sulk.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, the Husband Lord enjoys His bride when she is pleasing to His Mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 243, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that union comes to the soul-bride who pleases the Lord, not to one who frets about others.
The Kural's wife grows angry that her husband must soothe other women the same way; Gurbani turns the bride's attention from rivals to being pleasing herself.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, when Jupiter hides his love Io by turning her into a heifer, Juno is not soothed by his explanations but asks for the heifer as a gift and sets hundred-eyed Argus to guard her.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.601-627 (paraphrase) [10]
Juno’s suspicion survives every attempt by her husband to smooth things over.
The Kural’s wife, too, will not let her husband’s soothing words put her suspicion to rest.
Philosophy
In Terence’s comedy The Girl from Andros, an old man says that the quarrels of lovers are the renewal of love (amantium irae amoris integratio est).
— Terence, Andria 555 (paraphrase of a proverbial line) [11]
The line, from Roman comic drama rather than philosophy proper, became a proverb for the idea that lovers’ quarrels refresh love.
This is the spirit of the whole chapter: the wife’s endless mock quarrels keep love lively.

Real Life Example

Franz Liszt's years of adulation end his life with Marie d'Agoult
1839-1844 · Europe (Paris and the Rhine island of Nonnenwerth)
Franz Liszt and the Countess Marie d'Agoult lived together in Switzerland and Italy and had three children, but late in 1839 their relationship became strained and she returned to Paris. Liszt then spent about eight years touring Europe, where his concerts caused a sensation and he was received everywhere with great adulation. The couple still spent summers together on the Rhine island of Nonnenwerth until 1844, when their stormy relationship ended in a bitter parting.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Liszt was the first great celebrity performer, and his story shows how a lover's charm for the whole world can unsettle the person closest to him.
The wife in this kural cannot enjoy her husband's coaxing because she thinks he must charm other women the same way, and Marie d'Agoult watched Liszt's charm win over audiences across Europe before their relationship broke apart.
