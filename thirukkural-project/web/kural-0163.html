Kural 163

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அறன்ஆக்கம் வேண்டாதான் என்பான் பிறனாக்கம் பேணாது அழுக்கறுப் பான்.
Aran aakkam vendaadhaan enbaan piranaakkam Penaadhu azhukkaruppaan.

Meaning

Someone who is jealous when others do well, instead of being happy for them, is really saying they do not want goodness or success for themselves.

Elementary

If your friend wins a prize and you sulk instead of cheering, you're not just being unkind to them. You're blocking your own chance to be good and to do well. Being jealous is like saying “I don't want good things for myself either.”

University

The couplet makes a psychological claim: envy is self-defeating, not just unkind. Parimelazhagar reads அறனாக்கம் as both அறம் (virtue) and ஆக்கம் (prosperity). The envious person's attitude implicitly rejects both, since virtue grows through goodwill and prosperity through good conduct. பேணாது (“without cherishing”) implies the right response to another's good fortune is active appreciation, close to the Buddhist mudita (sympathetic joy). Aristotle's definition fits closely: the envious man “is pained at all good fortune,” even when it is deserved.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At Yudhishthira's Rajasuya sacrifice, Duryodhana sees the Pandavas' wealth and splendour and is consumed with jealousy. He tells his father he cannot bear their prosperity, which leads to the rigged dice game.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Dyuta sub-parva), Ganguli translation [3]
Duryodhana cannot welcome his cousins' success and turns to deceit, abandoning dharma for envy.
He becomes the Kural's envious man exactly: by resenting another's prosperity he gives up both righteousness and, in the end, his own fortune.
Christianity
“Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep.”
— Romans 12:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers to share in others' joy rather than resent it.
The Kural's ideal of cherishing (பேணுதல்) another's prosperity is what Paul commands here.
Islam
“Or do they envy people for what Allāh has given them of His bounty?”
— Qur'an 4:54 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an rebukes those who resent the favor God has given to others.
Both texts condemn resentment of another's blessing, which in the Qur'anic frame means resenting God's own gift.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's.”
— Exodus 20:17 (KJV; Exodus 20:14 in Jewish numbering) [4]
The tenth commandment forbids even the inner wanting of what belongs to one's neighbour.
Both texts target the inner reaction to another's prosperity, not just outward acts.
Buddhism
“One should not despise what one has received, nor envy the gains of others. The monk who envies the gains of others does not attain to meditative absorption.”
— Dhammapada 365 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha warns that envying others' gains blocks one's own spiritual progress.
Close to the Kural: envying another's prosperity costs the envier his own highest good.
Jainism
“Spite against knowledge–pradoṣa, concealment of knowledge–nihnava, non-imparting of knowledge out of envy–mātsarya, causing impediment to acquisition of knowledge–antarāya, disregard of knowledge–āsādana, and disparagement of true knowledge–upaghāta, lead to the influx (āsrava) of knowledge-obscuring (jñānāvaraṇa) and faith-obscuring (darśanāvaraṇa) karmas.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.10, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [7]
In Jain karma theory envy (mātsarya) and spite towards another's learning do not harm the other person; they draw in karmic matter that clouds the envier's own knowledge and faith.
Valluvar says the man who envies another's prosperity in effect wants neither virtue nor prosperity for himself, and the Tattvārtha shows the mechanism: envy blocks the envier's own spiritual growth.
Sikhism
“Give up your envy of others, you fool!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 889, Raag Raamkalee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan calls envy of other people plain foolishness and tells the mind to drop it, since it profits nothing in a short human life.
The Kural says the man who envies another's prosperity in effect rejects his own virtue and wealth; Gurbani likewise treats envy as self-defeating folly.
Greek Mythology
Aglauros, daughter of Cecrops, is filled with envy by the goddess Envy (Invidia) at her sister Herse's good fortune in being loved by Mercury. She wastes away and blocks the god at her sister's door.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.708-832 (trans. A. S. Kline) [9]
Instead of rejoicing in her sister's blessing, Aglauros is eaten up by resentment and tries to stop it.
She shows the Kural's point: resenting another's prosperity ruins the resenter's own life.
Philosophy
“The man who is characterized by righteous indignation is pained at undeserved good fortune, the envious man, going beyond him, is pained at all good fortune.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2.7 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle separates indignation at undeserved success from envy, which resents any success at all, including deserved success.
The Kural's envier resents the prosperity of others whatever its source; Aristotle defines envy the same way.

Real Life Example

Haydn praises Mozart to his father
February 1785 · Vienna, Austria
In February 1785 Joseph Haydn, then the most celebrated composer in Europe, heard the last three of Mozart's new string quartets played at a gathering in Vienna where Mozart's father Leopold was present. Haydn told Leopold that his son was the greatest composer he knew either in person or by reputation, and that he had taste and the deepest knowledge of composition; Leopold reported the remark in a letter to his daughter. Mozart dedicated the six quartets to Haydn when they were published later that year, calling him his dear friend.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Haydn, the established master, openly rejoiced in the rise of a younger composer who could have been seen as his rival, and their mutual admiration enriched both men's music.
The kural condemns envying another's prosperity instead of welcoming it, and Haydn did the opposite, welcoming Mozart's genius with generous praise.
