Kural 1302

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

உப்பமைந் தற்றால் புலவி அதுசிறிது மிக்கற்றால் நீள விடல்.
Uppamain thatraal pulavi adhusiridhu mikkatraal neela vidal.

Meaning

A little sulking is like the right amount of salt in food. Letting it go on too long is like putting in too much salt.

Elementary

A pinch of salt makes soup taste wonderful, but too much salt spoils it. A small, short sulk between people who love each other is like that pinch; a long one ruins everything.

University

The famous image here is culinary: pulavi is uppu amaintaṟṟu (‘like salt properly set’ in food), but to let it lengthen (nīḷa viṭal) is like salt that has become a little excessive (atu ciṟitu mikkaṟṟu). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read ciṟitu with mikku (‘even slightly too much’), stressing how quickly the right measure is lost, while Kalaignar reads the measure as the interval between quarrel (ūṭal) and reunion (kūṭal), and Solomon Pappaiah relates it to the joy of union it is meant to season. The couplet is generally taken as the confidante’s counsel to the wife, balancing the previous verse’s encouragement. Pope renders ‘A cool reserve is like the salt that seasons well the mess, / Too long maintained, ’tis like the salt’s excess.’ ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gita Govinda, Radha’s friend urges her not to keep up her proud anger (mana) against Krishna, who has come to her humbly, warning her that by being harsh to one so gentle she only hurts herself.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Sarga 9 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Barbara Stoler Miller) [3]
The sakhi plays exactly the Kural confidante’s role, telling the heroine that her pique has gone on long enough.
This is the salt warning in Sanskrit lyric form: a little pique adds savour, but prolonged pride spoils the love it was meant to season.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath:”
— Ephesians 4:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul allows that anger may arise but insists it must not be carried past the end of the day.
The Kural accepts a little sulking but, like Paul, sets a firm limit on how long it may last.
Islam
“It is not lawful for a man to desert his brother Muslim for more than three nights. (It is unlawful for them that) when they meet, one of them turns his face away from the other, and the other turns his face from the former, and the better of the two will be the one who greets the other first.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6077 [5]
The Prophet sets a strict time limit on estrangement and praises whoever ends it first.
The Kural’s salt image makes the same point: a short coolness is tolerable, a prolonged one corrupts the relationship.
Judaism
“For His anger is but for a moment, His favour is for a life-time; weeping may tarry for the night, but joy cometh in the morning.”
— Psalms 30:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist describes divine anger as brief and divine favour as lasting.
The Kural wants love’s anger in the same proportion: a moment of sulk inside a lifetime of affection.
Buddhism
When the monk Sona strains too hard in his practice, the Buddha reminds him that a lute is neither in tune nor playable when its strings are too tight or too slack, but only when they are evenly tuned.
— Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, Sona Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha teaches balance: too much or too little effort both spoil the practice.
The lute string and the Kural’s salt teach the same lesson of right measure, applied here to a lovers’ sulk.
Jainism
The Dasavaikalika Sutra teaches that anger destroys affection, pride destroys humility, deceit destroys friendship and greed destroys everything.
— Dasavaikalika Sutra 8.38 (some editions 8.37; paraphrase) [8]
Jain teaching names anger (krodha) and pride (mana) as passions that eat away at relationships.
The Kural warns that a sulk kept up too long, like over-salting, destroys the affection it was meant to spice.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Asa di Vaar, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak names gentle sweetness and humility as the heart of good conduct.
The Kural says a lovers' sulk is good only in small measure, like salt; Gurbani points to the same balance, keeping sweetness rather than letting pride or sourness take over.
Greek Mythology
Angered over the loss of Briseis, Achilles withdraws to his tent and refuses even the generous amends Agamemnon’s envoys bring, prolonging his wrath until it costs the life of his dearest friend Patroclus.
— Homer, Iliad 9 and 16 (paraphrase) [10]
Achilles’ wrath, held too long, turns from a just grievance into a disaster.
This is the Kural’s too-much-salt in epic form: a grievance nursed beyond its measure ruins what it was meant to defend.
Philosophy
Aristotle places good temper at the mean in anger and describes ‘sulky’ people as hard to appease, holding their anger a long time.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.D. Ross) [11]
For Aristotle the virtuous person feels anger at the right things, toward the right people, and for the right length of time.
The Kural’s salt is Aristotle’s mean in the kitchen: the right amount of sulk at the right moment, and not a moment longer.

Real Life Example

Chopin and George Sand’s rift is never mended
1847–1848 · Nohant and Paris, France
The composer Frédéric Chopin and the novelist George Sand were together for nearly a decade, spending their summers at her country house at Nohant. A family dispute arising from the marriage of Sand’s daughter, Solange, strained their relationship, and in 1847 it ended bitterly. By 1848 the rift was complete, and, as Britannica puts it, pride prevented either of them from making the reconciliation both actually wanted; Chopin, already in poor health, died in 1849.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A quarrel that might have passed was allowed to harden until it could no longer be undone, ending one of the best-known partnerships of the Romantic era.
The kural says a lovers’ sulk is like salt, good in the right measure but ruinous when it runs on too long, and Chopin and Sand let their quarrel run on until it spoiled everything.
