Kural 644

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

திறனறிந்து சொல்லுக சொல்லை அறனும் பொருளும் அதனினூஉங்கு இல்.
Thiranarindhu solluka sollai aranum Porulum adhaninoongu il.

Meaning

Speak only after you understand the people listening. Nothing does more good or brings more gain than speech that fits its listeners.

Elementary

You explain a game differently to your little cousin than to your teacher. This kural says the smart thing is to know who is listening and choose your words for them, and that doing this is one of the best things you can do.

University

Tiṟan aṟintu, “knowing the tiṟan”, is the interpretive crux. Parimelazhagar takes tiṟan as the qualities of the audience, and Solomon Pappaiah lists them: birth, learning, conduct, wealth, appearance and age. Mu. Varadarasanar reads tiṟan as the right manner or force of the words themselves, and Kalaignar as the underlying reason for speaking. The second line makes a striking claim with the comparative atanin ūungu, “beyond that”, lengthened for metre: neither aṟam (virtue, the first puruṣārtha) nor poruḷ (wealth, the second) exceeds well-fitted speech. The claim makes sense within Porutpal, where the minister’s speech is the means by which both dharma and wealth are secured for the kingdom.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking truthfully and beneficially and in avoiding speech that offends.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 [3]
Krishna defines disciplined speech as words that are true, pleasant and beneficial and do not upset the listener.
Speech shaped to benefit and not agitate the hearer requires the attention to the listener that the Kural demands.
Christianity
“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man.”
— Colossians 4:6 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the Colossians to speak graciously and fittingly so that they can answer each person appropriately.
Knowing how to answer every person is the Kural’s tiṟan aṟintu, knowing the listener before speaking.
Islam
“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best.”
— Qur’an 16:125 (Sahih International) [5]
The verse sets out graded forms of speech (wisdom, good exhortation, gracious argument), which exegetes relate to different kinds of listeners.
Choosing among wisdom, exhortation and argument according to the audience is the discernment the Kural asks for.
Judaism
The Talmudic principle that the Torah speaks in the language of human beings (dibbra Torah ki-lshon benei adam) holds that even divine teaching is expressed in forms its hearers can grasp.
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 31b [6]
The rabbis explain the Torah’s ordinary phrasing as God fitting speech to the human audience.
If even revelation adapts to its listeners, the Kural’s advice to know the hearer before speaking has deep support.
Buddhism
“It is spoken at the right time. It is spoken in truth. It is spoken affectionately. It is spoken beneficially. It is spoken with a mind of good-will.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.198 (Vaca Sutta), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
The Buddha lists five factors that make a statement well-spoken and blameless before the wise.
Right time and benefit to the listener depend on knowing one’s audience, which is the Kural’s point.
Jainism
“He who follows (the instruction) may believe something untrue; (one should) kindly (tell him) 'It is thus or thus.' One should never hurt him by outrageous language, nor give long-winded explanations of difficult passages. (If the pupil does not understand his short explanation), he should explain at greater length. When the pupil has heard it, he will correctly understand the Truth.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.23–24 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The monk who teaches is told to fit his explanation to the hearer: kindly and briefly at first, then at greater length if the pupil has not understood.
Valluvar says to speak only after knowing the nature of one's hearers; the Sutrakritanga gives a concrete method for shaping speech to what the listener can take in.
Sikhism
At Hardwar, according to the Janamsakhi tradition retold by Macauliffe, Guru Nanak saw pilgrims throwing water eastward to their ancestors and began throwing water westward himself. When the gathered crowd asked why, he said he was watering his fields in the Punjab; when they laughed that it could never reach so far, he asked how their water could then reach ancestors far beyond.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion, vol. 1 (1909), Life of Guru Nanak, ch. IV, pp. 50-51 [9]
Rather than lecture the pilgrims, the Guru staged a gesture suited to his audience that drew a crowd and let them reach the lesson through their own objection.
The Kural tells the speaker to know the nature of his hearers before speaking, since nothing is more effective than words fitted to them; the Hardwar episode shows Guru Nanak choosing a form of teaching exactly matched to his listeners.
Greek Mythology
When the Greek army rushes for the ships in Iliad 2, Odysseus turns them back by speaking gently and respectfully to each chieftain but sharply to common soldiers, fitting his words to each listener.
— Homer, Iliad 2.188-206, trans. Samuel Butler [10]
Odysseus rescues the expedition by changing how he speaks according to the rank and mood of each person.
This is a narrative example of the Kural’s rule: know the listener, then speak.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, Socrates says a true art of speech must know the kinds of souls and the kinds of speeches, and fit each speech to the soul that will be moved by it.
— Plato, Phaedrus 271c-272b [11]
Plato makes knowledge of the audience the foundation of any real rhetoric.
Plato’s matching of speech to soul is almost exactly the Kural’s tiṟan aṟintu.

Real Life Example

John F. Kennedy speaks to West Berliners in their own words
26 June 1963 · Rudolph Wilde Platz, West Berlin, West Germany
Two years after the Berlin Wall went up, President John F. Kennedy spoke to a vast crowd of West Berliners outside the Rathaus Schoeneberg. Early in the speech he declared Ich bin ein Berliner, a German phrase he had practised beforehand with his interpreter Robert Lochner in Mayor Willy Brandt's office, to show that the United States stood with the people of the divided city. The line bonded him to his audience and became one of the best-known moments of the Cold War.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kennedy's message was not new, but he shaped it to the exact fears, pride and language of the people in front of him.
The kural says to speak only after understanding the nature of one's hearers, since nothing is worth more than speech fitted to them, and Kennedy's Berlin speech is a celebrated example of exactly that.
