Kural 175

Anti-covet Action · Vehkaamai · வெஃகாமை

அஃகி அகன்ற அறிவென்னாம் யார்மாட்டும் வெஃகி வெறிய செயின்.
Ahki akandra arivennaam yaarmaattum Vehki veriya seyin.

Meaning

What good is a sharp and wide-ranging mind if its owner wants other people's things and acts foolishly toward anyone at all?

Elementary

Imagine the smartest kid in class who uses their brains to trick friends out of their toys. Being clever doesn’t make that okay; it just makes the cleverness useless. This kural says being smart means nothing if greed makes you act foolishly.

University

The first line is a small masterpiece of sound and sense: அஃகி அகன்ற அறிவு (ahki akandra arivu) describes knowledge that is at once narrowed to a fine point (ahku, ‘to become subtle/minute’) and broadened (akal, ‘to spread wide’), in other words both deep and wide. The rhetorical question ‘என்னாம்?’ (ennaam, ‘of what use?’) then cancels that learning entirely if its possessor, driven by covetousness, does வெறிய (veriya, senseless or improper deeds) toward யார்மாட்டும் (yaarmaattum, ‘anyone whatsoever’). The kural anticipates the chapter on learning (40-43) by insisting that intellect is not a moral safeguard; covetousness can hijack it. The Gita’s chain of desire, anger, delusion, and ‘loss of intelligence’ and Plato’s image of reason enslaved to money-making make the same diagnosis.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“From anger, delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost, one falls down again into the material pool.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.63 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Continuing the previous verse (2.62, where attachment breeds desire and desire breeds anger), Krishna traces how unchecked desire ends by destroying the intellect itself.
The Kural’s point exactly: however great your intelligence, covetous desire can make it useless.
Christianity
“And though I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries, and all knowledge; and though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing.”
— 1 Corinthians 13:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul says that even total knowledge is worth nothing without love.
Same rhetorical structure as the Kural (‘what use is all knowledge if…?’), although Paul’s condition is lack of love rather than presence of greed.
Islam
“The example of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not take it on is like that of a donkey who carries volumes [of books].”
— Qur’an 62:5 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an compares people who carry knowledge but do not live by it to a donkey loaded with books it cannot use.
Like the Kural, it declares learning useless when conduct contradicts it.
Judaism
“And thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous.”
— Exodus 23:8 (KJV) [4]
The Torah forbids judges to take bribes because the desire for gain blinds even wise people.
Both texts warn that desire for another’s wealth disables even the sharpest mind.
Buddhism
“To his own ruin the fool gains knowledge, for it cleaves his head and destroys his innate goodness.”
— Dhammapada 72 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says that knowledge in the hands of someone without virtue works against him and destroys his goodness.
Both texts insist that intelligence alone cannot save someone whose conduct is bad.
Jainism
“Knowledge without right conduct, acceptance of the asceticism without right faith and observance of austerities without self-control are all futile.”
— Saman Suttam, verse 210 (Ratnatrayasutra; English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [7]
Jainism's three jewels must go together: learning that is not joined to right conduct does not lead to liberation and is counted worthless.
Valluvar asks what use a deep and wide intellect is if its owner covets others' goods and acts senselessly, and the Jain verse likewise calls knowledge without right conduct futile.
Sikhism
“If an educated person is a sinner, then the illiterate holy man is not to be punished.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak teaches that learning does not save a person who does wrong, while a simple person who lives rightly does not suffer for lacking it.
The Kural asks what use a sharp and wide mind is if its owner covets and acts senselessly; Guru Nanak likewise holds that learning is worth nothing without right conduct.
Greek Mythology
King Erysichthon, wanting timber for his banquet hall, cut down Demeter’s sacred grove despite every warning. Demeter cursed him with insatiable hunger; he devoured his fortune, sold his own daughter, and at last consumed his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Erysichthon); also Callimachus, Hymn 6 [9]
A king with every advantage let greed for what was sacred and not his drive him into total madness and self-destruction.
Erysichthon shows the Kural’s veriya (senseless) conduct: greed turns a powerful man’s judgment into madness.
Philosophy
In Plato’s account of the oligarchic man, the money-loving part of the soul is set on the throne, and reason is made to sit at its feet, permitted only to calculate how more money can be made.
— Plato, Republic VIII, 553b-d [10]
Plato describes a person whose intelligence is still working but is now just a servant of his desire for wealth.
The Kural’s diagnosis precisely: a sharp, wide intellect made worthless by covetousness.

Real Life Example

The arrest of Nicolas Fouquet after the fete at Vaux-le-Vicomte
17 August - 5 September 1661 · Vaux-le-Vicomte and Nantes, France
Nicolas Fouquet, Louis XIV's superintendent of finances and a cultivated patron of writers and artists, built the magnificent chateau of Vaux-le-Vicomte and entertained the king there with a lavish fete on 17 August 1661. On 5 September 1661 he was arrested at Nantes by d'Artagnan and charged with embezzlement of public funds. After a trial lasting about three years the judges voted for banishment, but the king changed the sentence to life imprisonment, and Fouquet died in the fortress of Pignerol in 1680.
— Sources: [11] [12]
One of the most brilliant and refined men in France ended his life in prison because of the way he had enriched himself from state funds.
The kural asks what use a subtle and broad mind is if its owner covets others' goods, and Fouquet's taste and talent could not save him once he was charged with taking public money.
