Kural 604

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

குடிமடிந்து குற்றம் பெருகும் மடிமடிந்து மாண்ட உஞற்றி லவர்க்கு.
Kudimadindhu kutram perugum madimadindhu Maanda unjatri lavarkku.

Meaning

When people fall into laziness and make no real effort, their family's good name falls apart and their faults keep growing.

Elementary

When you stop doing your homework, it’s not just one problem. Soon you’re behind, you start making excuses, and you get into more trouble. Laziness is like a door that lets many other problems in, and the whole family feels it.

University

The couplet names two consequences: குடி மடிந்து, ‘the family perishing’, and குற்றம் பெருகும், ‘faults multiplying’. It gives one cause: மடி மடிந்து, ‘having fallen into sloth’. Here மடிந்து is used in the sense of ‘sinking’ or ‘being caught’, which Varadarasanar and Pappaiah both gloss as சோம்பலில் அகப்பட்டு / வீழ்வதால். The rare word உஞற்று (effort, exertion) together with மாண்ட (excellent, worthy) specifies what is missing: not activity of any kind but dignified, purposeful effort (Pope’s ‘noble objects’). Parimelazhagar notes that idleness is productive, of vice, which fits a line of Indian moral thought in which inactivity invites other faults. The same second-line phrase recurs in 607 (மாண்ட உஞற்றிலவர்), which ties the two couplets together. Both sources agree; the older text has the sandhi forms பெருகு மடிமடிந்து and மாண்ட வுஞற்றி.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“You should thus perform your prescribed Vedic duties, since action is superior to inaction. By ceasing activity, even your bodily maintenance will not be possible.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.8 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna argues that action is better than inaction: even keeping the body alive requires work, so giving up activity leads to decline.
If idleness cannot sustain even one body, it certainly cannot sustain a family, which is the Kural’s குடி மடிந்து.
Christianity
“And withal they learn to be idle, wandering about from house to house; and not only idle, but tattlers also and busybodies, speaking things which they ought not.”
— 1 Timothy 5:13 (KJV) [4]
Paul observes that idleness does not stay alone: it soon turns into gossip, meddling and improper speech.
‘Not only idle, but tattlers also and busybodies’ is a precise illustration of குற்றம் பெருகும், faults multiplying from sloth.
Islam
“Indeed, the hypocrites [think to] deceive Allah, but He is deceiving them. And when they stand for prayer, they stand lazily, showing [themselves to] the people and not remembering Allah except a little,”
— Quran 4:142 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran links laziness in worship with a cluster of further faults: hypocrisy, showing off, and neglect of the remembrance of God.
Laziness appearing alongside several other vices mirrors the Kural’s claim that faults multiply around sloth.
Judaism
“Rabbi Eliezer says: Even if she brought him a hundred maidservants, he can compel her to make thread from wool, since idleness leads to licentiousness. … Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says: … since idleness leads to idiocy.”
— Mishnah Ketubot 5:5 (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
In a ruling about household duties, two sages state a general principle: idleness breeds immorality and dulls the mind, so no one in a household should be entirely without work.
‘Idleness leads to’ further faults is the rabbinic form of the Kural’s குற்றம் பெருகும், and it is also set in the context of the household.
Buddhism
“The man who is fond of sleep and company, inactive and lazy, and manifesting anger —this is the cause of his downfall.”
— Sutta Nipāta 1.6, Parābhava Sutta (trans. Piyadassi Thera) [7]
In the Discourse on Downfall, the Buddha lists sleepiness, idleness and laziness, together with anger, as the third cause of a person’s ruin.
Laziness appearing next to other faults and leading to downfall parallels both halves of the Kural: faults multiply, and the house declines.
Jainism
“There are five causes which render wholesome discipline impossible: egoism, delusion, carelessness, illness, and idleness.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana counts carelessness and idleness among the causes that make right discipline impossible, so faults multiply where they take hold.
Valluvar says that for those sunk in sloth faults keep multiplying; the Jain text names idleness as one of the roots that block all wholesome discipline.
Sikhism
“The lustful, the angry, the clever, the deceitful and the lazy waste their lives in slander, and never remember their Lord in meditation.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1105, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir places the lazy beside the lustful, the angry and the deceitful, as people whose lives drain away into faults.
The Kural says that for those sunk in laziness who make no worthy effort, faults keep multiplying; Kabir likewise counts laziness among the vices that gather together and waste a life.
Greek Mythology
“Both gods and men are angry with a man who lives idle, for in nature he is like the stingless drones who waste the labour of the bees, eating without working”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 302-306 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod calls the idle man a drone who eats what others have worked for, and says he earns the anger of gods and men alike.
The drone’s idleness brings both waste and disgrace, which is the Kural’s double outcome of a ruined family and growing fault.
Philosophy
In his essay ‘Of Idleness’, Montaigne compares an idle mind to fallow land that, left alone, grows a mass of wild and useless weeds, and says it produces fantastic ‘chimeras’ unless kept busy with a fixed purpose.
— Michel de Montaigne, Essays I.8, ‘Of Idleness’ (trans. C. Cotton) [11]
Montaigne observes from his own retirement that an unoccupied mind does not rest but runs wild and breeds disorder.
Montaigne’s weed-choked field of the mind is a vivid picture of the Kural’s faults multiplying in the absence of worthy effort.

Real Life Example

Shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa withdraws into the arts as the Onin War begins
1449-1490 · Kyoto, Japan
Ashikaga Yoshimasa became shogun in 1449 but proved an ineffective ruler, and the great lords increasingly ignored his orders. A succession dispute between his brother Yoshimi and his son Yoshihisa helped set off the Onin War of 1467-1477, which devastated Kyoto and left shogunal authority badly weakened. Yoshimasa devoted himself to the tea ceremony, painting and patronage of the arts, and in retirement built his villa at Higashiyama, later the temple Ginkakuji.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Yoshimasa's patronage shaped Japanese culture, but his neglect of government coincided with the collapse of his house's real power and the start of a century of civil war.
The kural says that for those who sink into sloth and make no worthy effort the family collapses and faults multiply, and under Yoshimasa the Ashikaga shogunate declined while disorder spread across Japan.
