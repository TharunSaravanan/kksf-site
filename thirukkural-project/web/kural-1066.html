Kural 1066

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

ஆவிற்கு நீரென்று இரப்பினும் நாவிற்கு இரவின் இளிவந்த தில்.
Aavirku neerendru irappinum naavirku iravin ilivandha thil.

Meaning

Even if you beg only for water for a thirsty cow, nothing is more shameful for your tongue than begging.

Elementary

Imagine you need to ask a stranger for water, not even for yourself but for a thirsty animal. The Kural says that even then, asking feels hard and lowering, which shows how heavy begging is.

University

The couplet chooses the most innocent possible request: aavirku neer (ஆவிற்கு நீர், 'water for a cow'), a deed of dharma, since caring for cattle was held highly meritorious. Puliyur Kesikan, following the Parimelazhagar line, adds that the cow is dying of thirst and the request is made out of compassion; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses 'asking out of regard for dharma'. Even so, naavirku iravin ilivandhadhu il, 'for the tongue there is nothing more debasing than begging'. The figure is an a fortiori argument: if even this is lowering, begging for oneself is far worse. Pope renders it 'E'en if a draught of water for a cow you ask, / Nought's so distasteful to the tongue as beggar's task.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Indian tradition often recalls that when Vishnu went to King Bali to beg for land, he had to take the form of Vamana, a dwarf, and the story is commonly read as showing that even God is made small by asking.
— Vamana and Bali (Bhagavata Purana 8.18-22) with the traditional reading of it (paraphrase) [3]
The Vamana avatara is popularly cited to show how asking diminishes even the highest being.
The Kural's point is the same: asking lowers the asker even when the cause is good.
Christianity
“Then the steward said within himself, What shall I do? for my lord taketh away from me the stewardship: I cannot dig; to beg I am ashamed.”
— Luke 16:3 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus' parable, the dismissed steward says he is too weak to dig and too ashamed to beg.
The steward's shame at begging shows the same sense of degradation the Kural describes.
Islam
“A man keeps on asking others for something till he comes on the Day of Resurrection without any piece of flesh on his face.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1474 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet warns that habitual begging strips away a person's face, that is, his honour.
Like the Kural, the hadith says asking disgraces the one who asks.
Judaism
The Talmud records the counsel to make one's Sabbath like a weekday rather than become dependent on other people.
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 112a (paraphrase) [6]
Rabbinic teaching says it is better to give up even the honour due to the Sabbath than to ask others for help.
As in the Kural, avoiding the need to ask is placed above even a religious good.
Buddhism
In the Kasibharadvaja Sutta, the Buddha refuses food offered to him after he has recited verses, saying that what is gained by reciting verses is not fit for him to eat.
— Kasibharadvaja Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.4 (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha will not accept food that has been earned by what looks like asking or performing for it.
Like the Kural, it shows sensitivity to how a request, even a dignified one, can taint what is received.
Jainism
“Is the gently walking in the way of asceticism more grievous than the saying to others, Give me at least something, thus debasing one's dignity of the custom of doing homage to others, to whom he has attached himself by making their acquaintance?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 9 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet says even the hard ascetic path is easier than debasing oneself by saying 'give me something'.
This matches the Kural's claim that nothing degrades the tongue more than the words of asking.
Sikhism
“Cursed is the life, and cursed are the clothes, of one who wanders around, begging from door to door.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 550, Raag Bihagra, Bihagre ki Vaar (salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Bihagre ki Vaar, Guru Amar Das condemns the life of someone who wears the clothes of a holy man yet lives by begging from house to house.
The Kural says nothing degrades the tongue more than asking, even for water for a cow; Guru Amar Das likewise calls a life of begging from door to door a cursed life.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Odysseus, disguised as a beggar in his own hall, has to go round the suitors asking each for a scrap and endures their mockery and blows to carry out his plan.
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (paraphrase) [10]
Even the hero, begging for a just purpose, is shown suffering the humiliation that begging brings.
As in the Kural, a good cause does not remove the indignity of asking.
Philosophy
Seneca writes that nothing costs so dear as what is bought with prayers, and that the words 'I ask' are heavy and painful to say, so a good benefactor should spare people the need to ask.
— Seneca, On Benefits 2.1-2 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) (paraphrase) [11]
Seneca treats the act of asking as itself painful and humiliating to the one who asks.
The Kural says the same: nothing is more degrading to the tongue than asking.

Real Life Example

Booker T. Washington describes the burden of raising money for Tuskegee
1901 · Tuskegee, Alabama, and the northern United States
In the chapter Raising Money in his autobiography Up From Slavery (1901), Booker T. Washington described years of travelling to ask northern donors for the funds that kept Tuskegee Institute running beyond its small state grant. He recalled walking through a cold storm in Stamford, Connecticut, to call on a possible donor who heard him out and gave nothing, and he explained that he kept to the rule of doing his duty whether or not money came.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Washington's account shows that even asking for a worthy cause on behalf of others was hard, wearing and often fruitless work.
The kural says that even asking water for a thirsty cow is lowering to the tongue, and Washington's honest account of soliciting money for his students shows how heavy asking remains even for the noblest purpose.
