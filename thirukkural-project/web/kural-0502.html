Kural 502

Selection and Confidence · Therindhu Thelithal · தெரிந்து தெளிதல்

குடிப்பிறந்து குற்றத்தின் நீங்கி வடுப்பரியும் நாணுடையான் சுட்டே தெளிவு.
Kudippirandhu kutraththin neengi vaduppariyum Naanudaiyaan sutte thelivu.

Meaning

Trust a person who comes from a good family, has no faults, and is so afraid of shame that they guard their good name.

Elementary

Think about who you would let hold the class pet over the weekend. You would pick the kid from a caring home, who does not break rules, and who would feel really bad if people thought they had been careless. Feeling embarrassed about doing wrong is actually a superpower that keeps people honest.

University

Three qualifications are stacked: birth in a good lineage (குடிப்பிறப்பு), freedom from faults (குற்றம்), and நாண், the moral shame that fears a வடு, literally a scar, meaning a stain on one’s reputation. சுட்டே தெளிவு means ‘confidence rests on the one so pointed out’. Varadarasan and Pappaiah read குடி as literal family background, while Kalaignar reverses the logic: one who is faultless and ashamed of wrongdoing is thereby shown to be truly ‘high-born’, a reading that plays down birth in favour of conduct. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads the first phrase as removing the faults of one’s birth-group. Both sources agree on the text; the idea of நாண் as a guard against wrongdoing returns at length in chapter 102 (நாணுடைமை).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“…gentleness; modesty; steady determination; vigor; forgiveness; fortitude; cleanliness; and freedom from envy and from the passion for honor – these transcendental qualities, O son of Bharata, belong to godly men endowed with divine nature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna lists the qualities of a divine nature, including hrī (modesty, shame at doing wrong), and calls such a person abhijāta, ‘well-born’.
Being well-born and having a sense of shame are paired as marks of a trustworthy nature, as in the Kural.
Christianity
“Moreover he must have a good report of them which are without; lest he fall into reproach and the snare of the devil.”
— 1 Timothy 3:7 (KJV) [4]
Paul requires that a church overseer be well regarded even by outsiders, so he does not bring disgrace.
Concern for an unstained reputation matches the Kural’s ‘shame that dreads a stain’.
Islam
“If you don’t feel ashamed (from Haya’: pious shyness from committing religeous indiscretions) do whatever you like.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6120 (Sunnah.com translation) [5]
The Prophet quotes an old prophetic saying: without ḥayāʾ (moral shame), nothing holds a person back.
The Kural trusts the man of நாண் for the same reason: shame is what keeps him from wrong.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that shame is a good sign in a person, since one who feels shame will not quickly come to sin, and it links a shamefaced disposition with descent from those who stood at Sinai.
— Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 20a [6]
Rabbinic tradition treats bushah (shame) as a moral safeguard and even as a mark of good lineage.
Lineage and shame as a guard against sin are the two anchors of this Kural.
Buddhism
The Buddha calls hiri (conscience, shame at wrongdoing) and ottappa (concern for blame) the two bright qualities that guard the world; without them society would fall into confusion.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 2.9 (Lokapāla Sutta) [7]
These two qualities are what stop a person from doing wrong even when unobserved.
நாண் that fears a stain is almost exactly hiri joined with ottappa.
Jainism
“hence none is either high or low. After knowing this, who will feel proud of taking birth in respectable or high family?”
— Saman Suttam v. 90 (Precepts on Religion, uttama-mardava; English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [8]
Because every soul has been born many times into high and low families, Jainism holds that family rank confers no real distinction; worth lies in conduct.
The kural looks first to good family as a ground for confidence; the Jain teaching sets family lineage aside and would rest confidence on the faultlessness and moral shame the kural also names.
Sikhism
“Recognize the Lord's Light within all, and do not consider social class or status; there are no classes or castes in the world hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 349, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak tells his hearers to see God's light in everyone and not to ask about anyone's caste or social rank.
The Kural asks that trust go to someone of good family who fears any stain on his name; Guru Nanak rejects birth and status as a measure of a person, so Sikhism would keep the Kural's sense of shame but not its test of lineage.
Greek Mythology
In Protagoras’ myth, Zeus fears the human race will destroy itself and sends Hermes to give every person aidōs (shame, respect) and dikē (justice), ordering that anyone lacking them be put to death as a plague to the city.
— Plato, Protagoras 320c-322d [10]
The myth makes shame a precondition for anyone to take part in civic life.
The Kural likewise makes shame a precondition for public trust.
Philosophy
“In any office of trust or stewardship, on the other hand, the opposite rule should be observed; for more virtue than ordinary is required in the holder of such an office, but the necessary knowledge is of a sort which all men possess.”
— Aristotle, Politics, Book V, ch. 9 (1309a–b) (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle names loyalty to the constitution, administrative capacity and virtue as the qualifications for high office, and says that posts of trust call above all for exceptional virtue.
Like the Kural, Aristotle makes character, not skill alone, the test of whom a ruler should trust.

Real Life Example

Lord Carrington resigns as Foreign Secretary
5 April 1982 · London, United Kingdom
Three days after Argentina invaded the Falkland Islands, Peter Carington, 6th Baron Carrington, resigned as British Foreign Secretary together with two junior ministers. In his resignation letter he said that much of the criticism was unfounded, but that he had been responsible for the conduct of the policy and thought it right to resign. A later official inquiry did not find him at fault, and his acceptance of responsibility was widely praised.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A minister of old family and long service chose to give up high office rather than let a failure of his department rest on anyone else, an unusually clear example of personal honour in politics.
The kural asks for someone of good family who dreads any stain on his name, and Carrington's resignation shows that sense of shame acting as a safeguard of public trust.
