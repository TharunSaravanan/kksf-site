Kural 425

Knowledge Possession · Arivudaimai · அறிவுடைமை

உலகம் தழீஇய தொட்பம் மலர்தலும் கூம்பலும் இல்ல தறிவு.
Ulagam thazheeiya thotpam malarthalum Koombalum illa tharivu.

Meaning

Wisdom is making friends with the great people of the world and keeping that friendship steady, not opening up like a flower at first and closing up later.

Elementary

Some people are super friendly one day and cold the next, like a flower that opens in the morning and shuts at night. Valluvar says it is smart to make friends with good people. It is wise to stay the same kind friend every day, not hot one day and cold the next.

University

This is the most debated couplet in the chapter. ஒட்பம் (otpam) means excellence or sagacity, and உலகம் (ulagam, 'the world') is read by Parimelazhagan and most commentators as 'the great ones of the world' (uyarndhor). The first half therefore says that true excellence is to befriend the eminent. The second half uses a flower image: மலர்தல் (blooming, opening) and கூம்பல் (closing, as a flower does at dusk). Wisdom is keeping that friendship constant, neither over-effusive at the start nor withdrawing later. Some modern readers (e.g. Karunanidhi, Mu. Varadarasan) take the flower image more broadly as emotional equanimity in joy and sorrow. Both readings stress stability: arivu produces steadiness of disposition, especially in relationships. Aristotle's view that friendships of the good endure because goodness endures is a close parallel.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not disturbed in mind even amidst the threefold miseries or elated when there is happiness, and who is free from attachment, fear and anger, is called a sage of steady mind.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.56 [4]
Krishna describes the sthita-prajna, the person of steady wisdom, who neither swells in good times nor collapses in bad.
This matches the Kural's 'no blooming and no closing', which the equanimity reading takes as the mark of wisdom.
Christianity
“A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity.”
— Proverbs 17:17 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs defines true friendship by its constancy, the same in good times and in hardship.
The Kural's steady friendship, neither opening wide nor shutting, is the friend who 'loveth at all times'.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you.”
— Qur'an 57:23 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an teaches believers to hold an even keel: not crushed by loss, not puffed up by gain.
This parallels the flower image of neither blooming (exulting) nor closing (despairing).
Judaism
The Mishnah teaches that any love dependent on some particular thing ceases when that thing ceases, while love not dependent on anything never ceases. The love of David and Jonathan is its example.
— Pirkei Avot 5:16 [7]
Avot distinguishes fickle, conditional attachment from steady love that does not wax and wane with circumstances.
This is the Kural's steady friendship, which does not open and close with changing fortunes.
Buddhism
In the Sigalovada Sutta the Buddha describes the true-hearted friend as one who is the same in happiness and in sorrow. Such a friend does not desert you in trouble and would even give up his life for you.
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 [8]
The Buddha lists the marks of genuine friendship to help a layman tell steady friends from fair-weather ones.
'The same in happiness and sorrow' is the Kural's friend who neither blooms nor closes.
Jainism
“This is the road to it: to serve the Gurus and the old (teachers), to avoid throughout foolish people, to apply oneself earnestly to study, and to ponder zealously on the meaning of the Sûtras.” … “If he does not meet with a clever companion who surpasses or equals him in virtue, he should live by himself, abstaining from sins and not devoted to pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.3, 32.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [9]
The lecture on the causes of carelessness makes the company one keeps part of the road to liberation: serve worthy elders, avoid fools, and choose only a companion who equals or surpasses oneself in virtue, or else live alone.
The Kural's first half says excellence lies in befriending the worthy of the world; the Jain text likewise makes association with the wise, and avoidance of fools, a deliberate discipline.
Sikhism
“They alone are my friends, who travel along with me; and in that place, where the accounts are called for, they appear standing with me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729, Raag Soohee, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Raag Soohee Guru Nanak says true friends are those who stay with a person to the very end, even at the final reckoning.
The Kural praises befriending the worthy and keeping that friendship steady, without blooming and then closing; Guru Nanak names steadfastness to the end as the test of true friendship.
Greek Mythology
Pylades stays with Orestes through exile, madness, and mortal danger. In Iphigenia in Tauris, when only one of them may leave alive, each insists on dying for the other.
— Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris 674-715 [11]
Orestes and Pylades became the Greek byword for friendship that holds steady whatever the circumstances.
They embody the Kural's friendship that never closes up when fortune turns.
Philosophy
“Aristotle argues that friendships of utility and pleasure dissolve easily, but the friendship of the good lasts: “their friendship lasts as long as they are good — and goodness is an enduring thing.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.3, 1156b (tr. W. D. Ross) [12]
For Aristotle the best friendship is between good people and is stable because it rests on character, not on changing advantage or pleasure.
Both texts link friendship with the excellent to a constancy that neither blooms nor fades.

Real Life Example

The friendship of Goethe and Schiller
1794-1805 · Jena and Weimar, Germany
After a conversation following a meeting of a natural science society in Jena in July 1794, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and Friedrich Schiller began a close friendship and literary partnership. They worked together on Schiller's journal Die Horen, jointly wrote the satirical epigrams known as the Xenien, commented closely on each other's works, and in 1797 wrote a celebrated series of ballads. The friendship lasted until Schiller's death in Weimar in May 1805, after which Goethe wrote that he had lost half of his own existence.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Their partnership is one of the most celebrated friendships in literary history, remaining steady and productive for more than a decade.
The kural praises befriending the worthy and keeping that friendship steady rather than blossoming and then closing, and the bond between Goethe and Schiller stayed constant from its beginning until Schiller's death.
