Kural 696

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

குறிப்பறிந்து காலங் கருதி வெறுப்பில வேண்டுப வேட்பச் சொலல்.
Kurippu arindhu kaalam karudhi veruppila Venduba vetpach cholal.

Meaning

Understand the king's mood, wait for the right time, and then say what is needed in a pleasant way he will want to hear.

Elementary

If you want to ask for a later bedtime, you would not ask when Mum is rushing or cross. You would wait until she is relaxed, ask kindly, and explain why it is a good idea. Choosing the right moment and the right words makes people ready to listen.

University

The couplet lays out three steps. Kurippu arindhu means reading the ruler’s inner disposition from outward signs, the subject of the very next chapter (71, Kurippu Arithal). Kaalam karudhi means weighing the right time, echoing chapter 49 (Kaalam Arithal). Then comes the manner of speech: veruppila (things not repugnant) and venduba (things needed or useful), spoken vetpa (so that he desires to hear them). Parimelazhagar notes that what is needful must still be said, but framed so that it is welcome. That makes this not flattery but an art of timing and packaging, close to what Greek rhetoric called kairos. The traditional text is the same apart from sandhi.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him say what is true, let him say what is pleasing, let him utter no disagreeable truth, and let him utter no agreeable falsehood; that is the eternal law.”
— Manusmriti 4.138 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu sets the rule for virtuous speech: it should be both true and pleasant, avoiding harsh truths and sweet lies alike.
Valluvar’s ‘needed and not displeasing, said so he wants to hear it’ is a court-specific form of Manu’s true-and-pleasing ideal.
Christianity
“Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man.”
— Colossians 4:6 (KJV) [4]
Paul asks that speech always be gracious and well-seasoned, fitted to each listener.
Both texts value truth delivered gracefully and fitted to the listener.
Islam
“And speak to him with gentle speech that perhaps he may be reminded or fear [Allah].”
— Quran 20:44 (Saheeh International) [5]
God sends Moses and Aaron to Pharaoh, the most powerful and hostile king of their time, and tells them to speak gently so that their message might be received.
Even a hard truth to a king should be spoken so that he can hear it, which is the Kural’s ‘so he desires to hear’.
Judaism
“And I said unto the king, If it please the king, and if thy servant have found favour in thy sight, that thou wouldest send me unto Judah, unto the city of my fathers’ sepulchres, that I may build it.”
— Nehemiah 2:2-5 (KJV) [4]
Nehemiah, cupbearer to the Persian king, waits until the king notices his sad face and asks what he wants. He silently prays, then makes his request in carefully courteous terms, and it is granted.
Nehemiah reads the moment, waits for the opening, and frames a needed request pleasingly, which is the Kural’s method exactly.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, and endearing & agreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them.”
— Abhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 58 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha tells Prince Abhaya that even true, beneficial and pleasing words should be spoken only at the right time.
The Buddha’s three tests of true, beneficial and timely match the Kural’s needful, not displeasing, and at the right time.
Jainism
“Guessing the teacher's thoughts and the purport of his words, one should express one's assent, and execute (what he desires to be done).”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.43 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The disciple is to read the teacher's unspoken intention and respond to it fittingly.
The Kural asks the courtier to read the king's mood and choose the moment before speaking; the Jain rule likewise asks the disciple to read a superior's mind and respond in a way that fits it.
Sikhism
“So what offering can we place before Him, by which we might see the Darbaar of His Court? What words can we speak to evoke His Love? In the Amrit Vaylaa, the ambrosial hours before dawn, chant the True Name, and contemplate His Glorious Greatness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Jap (Japji Sahib, Pauri 4), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Japji Guru Nanak asks how one should approach the divine Court and with what words, and answers with the right time (the hours before dawn) and the right speech (the True Name and His praise).
The Kural says to read the king's mood, wait for the right moment and then say what he will want to hear; Guru Nanak's question and answer about approaching the heavenly Court turn on the same two things, timing and welcome words.
Greek Mythology
Croesus, captured by Cyrus, first asks whether he may speak or must keep silent. Given leave, he points out that Cyrus’s soldiers are plundering what is now Cyrus’s own property and suggests a tactful remedy. Cyrus is pleased and adopts it.
— Herodotus, Histories 1.88-89 (trans. A. D. Godley) [9]
Herodotus shows a captive speaking to a conqueror: he asks permission, picks his moment, and offers advice framed around the king’s own interest.
Croesus uses the Kural’s full method of reading the moment and framing useful counsel so that the king wants to hear it.
Philosophy
“He, therefore, added one more to the number of those who have shown to their bitter cost how little kings care for their friends' good advice.”
— Seneca, De Ira (Of Anger), III.14 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [10]
Seneca tells how Praexaspes advised King Cambyses to drink less, and the drunken king shot the adviser's son through the heart to prove his hand was steady. Seneca judges that Praexaspes was wiser in holding his tongue afterwards than in offering correction to such a king in the first place.
The tale shows the danger the Kural guards against, which is giving a king sound but unwelcome advice without reading his mood or waiting for the right moment.

Real Life Example

Galileo names Jupiter's moons the Medicean Stars
1610 · Padua and Florence, Italy
In January 1610 Galileo discovered four moons orbiting Jupiter and published the findings in Sidereus Nuncius in March 1610. He had written to his former pupil, Grand Duke Cosimo II de' Medici, about the discoveries and named the moons the Medicean Stars, announcing the name on the book's title page and in a dedication, and he sent the Grand Duke a copy together with the telescope he had used. The Grand Duke was pleased and invited Galileo to become Philosopher and Mathematician to the court in Florence, which Galileo accepted.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Galileo presented a discovery that challenged the prevailing view of the heavens in a form his prince was glad to receive, and so won the patronage to keep working.
The kural asks the counsellor to read the ruler, choose the moment and say what is needful in a way he will want to hear, as Galileo did by offering an important truth wrapped in an honour to the Medici.
