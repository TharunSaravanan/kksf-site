Kural 33

Assertion of the Strength of Virtue · Aran Valiyuruthal · அறன் வலியுறுத்தல்

ஒல்லும் வகையான் அறவினை ஓவாதே செல்லும்வாய் எல்லாஞ் செயல்.
Ollum vakaiyaan aravinai ovaadhe, Sellumvaai ellaanj cheyal.

Meaning

In every way you can, and without ever stopping, do good wherever good can be done.

Elementary

You do not need to be strong to help. If you can carry one book, carry one book. This kural says: do whatever good you can manage, wherever you happen to be, and do not stop.

University

Three qualifiers do the work here. ஒல்லும் வகையான் ('by the means available to you') scales the obligation to individual capacity — Parimelazhagar specifies that the householder gives according to his means and the ascetic according to his bodily strength. ஓவாதே ('without ceasing') removes any notion of virtue as an occasional act. செல்லும்வாய் எல்லாம् ('in every place it can reach') is glossed by the commentators as the three instruments of mind, speech and body (மனம், வாக்கு, காயம்), yielding good thought, good word and good deed. The verse thus converts virtue from a discrete achievement into a continuous disposition, and does so without ever specifying a quantity — a characteristically Tamil ethical move that keeps the demand universal and the standard personal.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Therefore, without being attached to the fruits of activities, one should act as a matter of duty, for by working without attachment one attains the Supreme.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.19 (Bhaktivedanta, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna's central instruction in the karma-yoga chapter is that right action is to be performed constantly (satatam) as duty, without calculating its returns.
Both verses make virtuous action a continuous obligation rather than an occasional or result-driven one.
Christianity
“And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. As we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith.”
— Galatians 6:9-10 (KJV) [4]
Paul urges the Galatians to sustained rather than sporadic good-doing, and to take every opportunity as it arises.
It reproduces all three of the Kural's qualifiers — as you are able, without slackening, wherever the chance arises.
Islam
“So be mindful of Allah to the best of your ability, hear and obey, and spend in charity—that will be best for you.”
— Qur'an 64:16, At-Taghabun (Dr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The phrase mā istaṭa'tum — 'to the extent you are able' — is the Qur'an's standard scaling of religious obligation to individual capacity.
It is the Qur'anic equivalent of ஒல்லும் வகையான்: do the good that lies within your power, whatever that power happens to be.
Judaism
“It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you at liberty to neglect it.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:16 (trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon's maxim releases a person from the impossible demand of completing the moral task while forbidding them to opt out of it.
It captures the Kural's balance exactly: do what you are capable of, but never stop doing it.
Buddhism
“Hasten to do good; restrain your mind from evil. He who is slow in doing good, his mind delights in evil.”
— Dhammapada 116, Papavagga, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
The Dhammapada treats slowness in doing good not as neutral but as actively dangerous, since the unoccupied mind drifts toward harm.
It sharpens the Kural's ஓவாதே: there is no safe pause in the practice of virtue.
Jainism
Umasvati's Tattvartha Sutra defines yoga as the activity of body, speech and mind, says this activity is the channel through which karma flows into the soul (asrava), and teaches that wholesome activity is the cause of merit (punya) while unwholesome activity is the cause of demerit (papa).
— Tattvartha Sutra 6.1-3 (Umasvati; cf. tr. Nathmal Tatia, That Which Is, 1994) [8]
In Jain doctrine, every movement of mind, speech and body draws karmic matter to the soul. The layperson is therefore asked to make each of these three channels wholesome, as far as their capacity allows, while the monk aims to restrain them altogether.
Parimelazhagar glosses the Kural's 'every place good can reach' as the three instruments of mind, speech and body. These are exactly Jainism's three yogas, which must be kept wholesome without letting up.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak! He knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Sarang (Sarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok that rejects holy men who live by begging, Guru Nanak defines the true path as honest labour and sharing from one's own earnings, the Sikh ideals of kirat karni and vand chhakna.
Like Valluvar's 'by whatever means you are able', it measures the duty of doing good by what each person actually has and earns.
Greek Mythology
Heracles, to expiate his crime, is set twelve labours by Eurystheus and must complete every one of them; when two are disallowed on technicalities he is made to perform substitutes, and only after the whole sequence is finished without exception does he earn release and, ultimately, immortality.
— Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.5 (the Twelve Labours of Heracles) [10]
The myth makes the point that the labours cannot be partially discharged — the reward attaches to the unbroken completion of the whole series, in whatever terrain and by whatever means each demands.
It offers a mythic image of the Kural's 'ceaselessly, by every means, wherever the work can be done'.
Philosophy
“For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1 (1103a-b), trans. W. D. Ross [11]
Aristotle's doctrine of habituation holds that moral character is not taught but built up through the repeated performance of the corresponding actions.
It supplies the reason behind the Kural's insistence on unceasing practice: virtue exists only as sustained activity.

Real Life Example

Chiune Sugihara issues transit visas to refugees
July-August 1940 · Kaunas (Kovno), Lithuania
In the summer of 1940 Chiune Sugihara, Japan's consul in Kaunas, was approached by Jewish refugees from Poland who needed Japanese transit visas to escape Europe. Although many applicants did not meet the conditions his Foreign Ministry required, he kept issuing visas until his consulate closed, stamping some passports himself and enlisting helpers when time ran short, and by the time he left Lithuania he had issued visas to more than 2,000 people. Yad Vashem recognised him as Righteous Among the Nations in 1984.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Sugihara used the one tool his position gave him, his consular stamp, as fully and as long as he could, and thousands of people escaped because of it.
The kural urges doing good by whatever means one has, without letting up, wherever good can reach, and Sugihara kept writing visas with the means of his office until the very last day it was open.
