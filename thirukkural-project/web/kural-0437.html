Kural 437

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

செயற்பால செய்யா திவறியான் செல்வம் உயற்பால தன்றிக் கெடும்.
Seyarpaala seyyaa thivariyaan selvam Uyarpaala thandrik kedum.

Meaning

The riches of a stingy person who does not use them for what they should be used for will not last. They will be lost without doing any good.

Elementary

Money is meant to be used for good things: helping people, caring for family, doing what is right. This kural says that if someone just sits on their money out of stinginess, it ends up wasted and lost anyway.

University

செயற்பால (‘things that ought to be done’) are the proper uses of wealth, which Valluvar elsewhere lists as giving, enjoyment and public works; இவறியான் is the one who clings (from இவறு, to hoard or be miserly). உயற்பாலது அன்றி means ‘without the quality of surviving / being saved’: hoarded wealth has no future. The argument is not only moral but practical: wealth that is not put to use is exposed to theft, confiscation and decay, and so the miser loses both the good he could have done and the wealth itself. The couplet returns to இவறல் from Kural 432 and prepares for 438’s verdict that miserliness is the worst of all faults.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartṛhari teaches that wealth has only three possible fates: giving (dāna), enjoyment (bhoga) and destruction (nāśa); the one who neither gives nor enjoys is left with the third.
— Bhartṛhari, Nītiśataka (verse on the three fates of wealth, c. 43 in common editions) [3]
A classical Sanskrit verse says money that is not given away or used will simply be destroyed.
Nearly the Kural’s argument: wealth not used for its proper ends ‘perishes without being saved’.
Christianity
“Your riches are corrupted, and your garments are motheaten. Your gold and silver is cankered; and the rust of them shall be a witness against you, and shall eat your flesh as it were fire. Ye have heaped treasure together for the last days.”
— James 5:2-3 (KJV) [4]
James warns the hoarding rich that their piled-up wealth is rotting, and that its decay will testify against them.
Both say hoarded wealth decays uselessly and turns against its owner.
Islam
“And those who hoard gold and silver and spend it not in the way of Allāh - give them tidings of a painful punishment.”
— Quran 9:34 (Sahih International, latter part of verse) [5]
The Quran condemns those who pile up gold and silver without spending it for good.
Both condemn hoarding in place of using wealth for its proper purposes.
Judaism
“There is a sore evil which I have seen under the sun, namely, riches kept for the owners thereof to their hurt. But those riches perish by evil travail: and he begetteth a son, and there is nothing in his hand.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Ecclesiastes (Kohelet) describes wealth hoarded to its owner’s harm and then lost in a bad venture, leaving nothing even for his child.
Almost the Kural’s exact claim: wealth kept back instead of used ‘perishes’ without benefit.
Buddhism
“When his wealth isn't properly put to use, kings make off with it, or thieves make off with it, or fire burns it, or water sweeps it away, or hateful heirs make off with it. Thus his wealth, not properly put to use, goes to waste and not to any good use.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 3.19, Aputtaka Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
After a miserly millionaire dies childless and his fortune goes to the king’s treasury, the Buddha explains that unused wealth is like a pure pond no one drinks from.
The Buddha’s ‘not properly put to use, goes to waste’ is the Kural’s ‘perishes without being saved’.
Jainism
“Or he heaps up treasures for the benefit of some spendthrifts, by pinching himself.” … “Then at one time, his manifold savings are a large treasure. Then at another time, his heirs divide it, or those who are without a living steal it, or the king takes it away, or it is ruined in some way or other, or it is consumed by the conflagration of the house.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.2.1.4 and I.2.3.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [7]
The Acaranga describes the man who starves himself to pile up wealth, only to have it divided by heirs, stolen, seized by the king, or burnt with his house, so that he gets no good from it.
Valluvar says the miser's wealth, never put to its proper use, will perish without benefit to anyone; the Acaranga lists the very ways in which hoarded wealth slips away.
Sikhism
“The bee stores up loads of honey; then someone comes and takes the honey, and throws dust in its mouth.” … “He takes the wealth of Maya, and buries it in the ground.” … “His wealth remains buried in the ground, while his body turns to dust.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1252, Raag Saarang, Bhagat Namdev (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Bhagat Namdev describes the hoarder who toils for wealth and buries it, only to leave it in the ground at death, like the bee whose stored honey is taken by another.
The Kural says the miser's wealth, never put to its proper use, perishes without benefit; Namdev's buried treasure and robbed honey make the same point.
Greek Mythology
King Midas asks Dionysus that all he touches turn to gold; his food and drink become gold, and he nearly starves amid riches until he begs to be freed from the gift.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11 (Midas) [9]
Midas gets endless gold but cannot use it for anything, not even to eat.
Midas pictures the Kural’s point that wealth not usable for life’s proper ends is worthless and ruinous.
Philosophy
“The life of money-making is one undertaken under compulsion, and wealth is evidently not the good we are seeking; for it is merely useful and for the sake of something else.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.5 (tr. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle argues that wealth has no value in itself; it is only worth what it is used for.
If wealth is only ‘useful for something else’, then the miser who never uses it, as the Kural says, has wealth that comes to nothing.

Real Life Example

Nadir Shah carries off the Mughal treasury and the Peacock Throne
1739 · Delhi, India
In 1739 the Persian ruler Nadir Shah defeated the larger army of the Mughal emperor Muhammad Shah at Karnal and then entered Delhi. He took away the Mughal treasury and the jewelled Peacock Throne built for Shah Jahan, and the Koh-i-Noor diamond most likely formed part of the plunder. The loss left the Mughal treasury depleted and the empire greatly weakened.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Wealth that had been gathered and stored over generations did nothing to protect the empire and was lost to another in a matter of weeks.
The kural says the wealth of one who will not put it to its proper use does not endure but perishes without benefit, and the Mughal hoard passed to a foreign conqueror.
