Kural 65

The Wealth of Children · Pudhalvaraip Perudhal · புதல்வரைப் பெறுதல்

மக்கள்மெய் தீண்டல் உடற்கின்பம் மற்றுஅவர் சொற்கேட்டல் இன்பம் செவிக்கு.
Makkalmey Theendal Udarkinbam Matravar, Sorkettal Inbam Sevikku.

Meaning

Touching your children is a joy to your body, and hearing them talk is a joy to your ears.

Elementary

Your body has different ways of being happy. Hugging someone you love makes your whole self feel warm — that's happiness for your skin. Listening to someone you love chatter away makes your ears happy. This kural says children give you both at once.

University

Valluvar constructs the couplet as a strict parallel built on two of the five senses: உடற்கு இன்பம் ("pleasure for the body") paired with செவிக்கு இன்பம் ("pleasure for the ear"), with தீண்டல் (tīṇṭal, touching) answering கேட்டல் (kēṭṭal, hearing). Read against kural 64, which handled taste, the three verses form a deliberate sensory triptych — the chapter argues for the value of children not by doctrine but by cataloguing the specific bodily goods they confer. Parimēlaḻakar makes an interesting interpretive choice here: he notes that Valluvar writes simply சொல் ("speech") rather than the மழலைச்சொல் ("lisping speech") of the following couplet, and concludes that the verse covers not only infant babble but the learned speech of grown children as well, so that the ear's pleasure lasts a lifetime. The grammatical abstract nouns தீண்டல் and கேட்டல் are, in his reading, standing by metonymy for the acts themselves — a small point of Tamil grammatical commentary that shows how closely this tradition read the couplets.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Kṛṣṇa childhood narratives Yaśodā's happiness is rendered almost entirely through the senses — the weight of the child on her hip, the smell of his hair, the sound of his prattle and of the anklet bells as he crawls — and these become the canonical images of vātsalya-bhāva, the devotional mood of parental love.
— Bhāgavata Purāṇa 10.8 and the vātsalya tradition [5]
Hindu devotional poetry treats the physical, sensory experience of handling and hearing a small child as a legitimate and even the highest form of religious feeling.
Like the Kural, it locates the value of children in the concrete pleasures of touch and hearing rather than in abstract benefit.
Christianity
“And he took them up in his arms, put his hands upon them, and blessed them.”
— Mark 10:16 (King James Version) [6]
When the disciples try to keep children away from Jesus, he is indignant, and the Gospel records the physical detail — he picks them up and lays hands on them rather than merely speaking a blessing.
Mark records the same instinct the Kural describes: with children, the body wants contact.
Islam
“Allah's Messenger kissed Al-Hasan bin 'Ali while Al-Aqra' bin Habis at-Tamim was sitting beside him. Al-Aqra said, “I have ten children and I have never kissed anyone of them.” Allah's Messenger cast a look at him and said, “Whoever is not merciful to others will not be treated mercifully.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5997, Book of Good Manners (narrated by Abū Hurayrah) [7]
A chief boasts of never having embraced any of his ten children, and is rebuked — physical tenderness toward children is treated here not as sentimentality but as a moral obligation.
The hadith defends precisely the bodily pleasure the Kural celebrates: the touch of one's children is a good, and refusing it is a fault.
Judaism
“then shall ye suck, ye shall be borne upon her sides, and be dandled upon her knees. As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you.”
— Isaiah 66:12–13 (King James Version) [6]
Isaiah reaches for the most immediate physical images available — nursing, being carried on the hip, being bounced on the knees — to describe the consolation God intends for Jerusalem.
The prophet assumes exactly the Kural's premise: handling a small child is one of the most intensely good bodily experiences there is.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212, Piyavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada warns that every bond of affection — and the commentarial story attached to these verses concerns a man devastated by the death of his son — carries an exactly proportionate load of future sorrow.
This is the honest counter-voice to the Kural: the very sensory delight Valluvar praises is, for early Buddhism, the precise mechanism by which suffering gets in.
Jainism
“Feeling her child quivering, trembling, moving, and stirring, the Kshatriyânî Trisalâ--glad, pleased, and joyful, &c.--spoke thus: ‘No, forsooth, the fruit of my womb has not been taken from me, it has not died, it is not fallen, it is not lost. Formerly it did not move, but now it does move.’”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahâvîra §§92–94 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
After the unborn Mahâvîra lay still out of compassion for his mother and she feared him dead, he stirred slightly, and her grief turned to joy at the feel of her child moving.
Trisalâ's delight at simply feeling her child is a scriptural instance of what this kural says: the touch of one's child is the body's greatest pleasure.
Sikhism
“The mind of the poor child is so fickle that he even touches snakes and fire. His mother and father hug him close in their embrace, and so he plays in joy and bliss.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1266, Raag Malaar, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan draws on the parent's embrace of a small child as a picture of how God holds and protects the devotee.
Like the Kural's delight in touching one's children, the verse makes the parents' embrace the centre of the image, a moment of shared joy.
Greek Mythology
“Glorious Hector pulled the glittering helmet off and set it on the ground. Then he kissed his dear son and held him in his arms.”
— Homer, Iliad 6.472–474 (trans. Ian Johnston, Vancouver Island University) [11]
Hector reaches for his baby son on the walls of Troy; the child screams at the bronze and the nodding horsehair crest, so the greatest warrior of Troy takes off his helmet, laughing, in order to hold him.
It is the Kural's line dramatised — a man who sets down his armour because the touch of his child is worth more.
Philosophy
“Thee now no more / The joyful house and best of wives shall welcome, / Nor little sons run up to snatch their kisses / And touch with silent happiness thy heart.”
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 3.894–896 (trans. William Ellery Leonard, 1916) [12]
Lucretius is reporting — in order to refute — the standard human lament about death, and the thing he takes as the most obviously precious loss is small children running up to be kissed.
The Epicurean poet picks out the identical two goods the Kural does: the child's rush into your arms and the wordless delight it produces.

Real Life Example

Kangaroo mother care is developed in Bogota
1978 · Bogota, Colombia
Facing a shortage of incubators and high infection rates among low birth weight babies, the Colombian paediatricians Edgar Rey Sanabria and Hector Martinez-Gomez began in 1978 to have mothers carry their premature infants in continuous skin-to-skin contact on their chests. The method, combined with breastfeeding and early discharge, became known as the Kangaroo Mother Program. It was later studied internationally, and the World Health Organization published guidelines on kangaroo mother care in 2003.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Doctors found that a parent's touch, not only machines, could keep the most fragile newborns warm and help them thrive, and the practice spread around the world.
The kural calls the touch of one's child the body's delight, and kangaroo care made that very touch, skin to skin, into a recognised form of medicine for both mother and baby.
