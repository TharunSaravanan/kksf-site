Kural 1306

Pouting · Pulavi · புலவி

துனியும் புலவியும் இல்லாயின் காமம் கனியும் கருக்காயும் அற்று.
Thuniyum pulaviyum illaayin kaamam kaniyum karukkaayum atru.

Meaning

Love without its deep sulks and light sulks is like fruit that is either too ripe or still hard and green.

Elementary

A mango that is too soft and mushy is no good, and one that is still hard and green is no good either. Love that never has a single little disagreement is a bit like that: it has lost its just-right sweetness.

University

Two grades of lovers’ anger are named: tuṉi, the grown or deep sulk, and pulavi, the young or light sulk, as Solomon Pappaiah glosses them (vaḷarnta ūṭal and iḷam ūṭal); without them kāmam is like kaṉi (fruit gone too ripe) and karukkāy (fruit not yet ripe). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read both fruits as useless, the over-ripe one past its best and the unripe one inedible, while Kalaignar adds ‘rotten’ to the over-ripe fruit; some readers pair the two terms with the two fruits, but the commentators quoted take the comparison as a whole. Pope’s couplet, ‘Love without hatred is ripened fruit; / Without some lesser strife, fruit immature,’ makes that pairing explicit. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A much-quoted verse of Sanskrit poetics holds that love in union does not reach its fullness without love in separation, just as a cloth first treated with astringent takes a deeper dye.
— Verse on śṛṅgāra cited in alaṅkāra works such as Viśvanātha’s Sāhityadarpaṇa (paraphrase; attribution not verified) [3]
Indian aesthetic theory treats separation and pique (vipralambha, including mana) as what gives union its depth of flavour.
The Kural’s ripe-fruit image makes the same claim: without its quarrels, love lacks its full savour.
Christianity
“Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby.”
— Hebrews 12:11 (KJV) [4]
Hebrews teaches that what is painful at the moment can ripen into good fruit.
The Kural likewise sees love’s small pains as part of how it ripens.
Islam
The Qur’an twice declares that with hardship comes ease.
— Qur’an 94:5-6 (paraphrase) [5]
Difficulty and relief are presented as bound together in the divine order of things.
The Kural similarly treats the friction of sulking as inseparable from love’s sweetness.
Judaism
“A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones together; A time to embrace, and a time to refrain from embracing;”
— Ecclesiastes 3:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Ecclesiastes sees both embracing and holding back as part of life’s proper seasons.
The Kural too gives holding back a place within love, without which love would lose its right ripeness.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that attachment to the beloved brings pain, and advises freedom from both liking and disliking.
Where the Kural welcomes love’s little pains as part of its sweetness, the Dhammapada sees them as a reason to loosen attachment altogether.
Jainism
Jain karma theory grades each passion, such as anger (krodha) and pride (mana), by intensity and duration, from the most binding kind that can last a lifetime down to the mildest, flickering kind that passes in about a fortnight.
— Jain doctrine of the four grades of kashaya (e.g. Karmagrantha; summarized in P.S. Jaini, The Jaina Path of Purification) (paraphrase) [8]
Jainism carefully distinguishes deep from light passions, but treats all of them as binding karma to be overcome.
The Kural also distinguishes a deep sulk (tuni) from a light one (pulavi), though it values both where Jainism would seek to remove them.
Sikhism
“The color of my Lord's Love, however, is permanent, like the dye of the madder plant. So says Ravi Daas, the tanner.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 346, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas contrasts passing worldly attachments with the deep, fast colour of love for God.
The Kural says love without its quarrels is like fruit too ripe or too green; Ravidas also speaks of love in its mature, lasting state, like a dye that does not fade.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls Eros glukupikron, ‘sweet-bitter,’ a creature that cannot be fought off.
— Sappho, fragment 130 (paraphrase; cf. tr. D.A. Campbell, Greek Lyric I) [10]
For Sappho love is by nature a mixture of sweetness and pain.
The Kural agrees that love without its bitter edge is not fully ripe.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that hatred which is fully overcome by love passes into love, and that the love is then greater than if hatred had not come before it.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Proposition 44 (paraphrase) [11]
On Spinoza’s account, a conflict overcome can leave affection stronger than before.
This is close to the Kural’s claim that love’s quarrels, once resolved, make it richer, like fruit brought to the right ripeness.

Real Life Example

Pablo Neruda publishes The Captain’s Verses anonymously
1952 · Capri and Naples, Italy
While in exile in Italy, the Chilean poet Pablo Neruda finished writing The Captain’s Verses on the island of Capri in 1952, addressing the poems to Matilde Urrutia. The collection contains poems of love, ecstasy and devotion, but also of fury, including lovers’ anger and quarrels. Later that year he published it anonymously in an edition of fifty copies, fourteen years before he and Matilde legally married.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Neruda’s book treated anger and quarrelling as part of the full experience of love rather than its opposite.
The kural says love without its deeper quarrels and small sulks is like fruit that is over-ripe or still green, and Neruda’s love poems deliberately include fury alongside tenderness.
