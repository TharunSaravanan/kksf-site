Kural 555

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

அல்லற்பட்டு ஆற்றாது அழுதகண் ணீரன்றே செல்வத்தைத் தேய்க்கும் படை.
Allarpattu aatraathu azhuthakan neerandre Selvaththaith theykkum padai.

Meaning

The tears of people who are suffering more than they can bear are a weapon that wears away a cruel king’s riches.

Elementary

Water dripping on a stone for a long, long time can wear a hole right through it. Valluvar says the tears of people treated unfairly are like that water: drop by drop, they wear away the riches of the ruler who made them cry.

University

அல்லல் is distress, ஆற்றாது ‘unable to bear it’, and படை ‘army’ or ‘weapon’. The verb தேய்க்கும் (wears away, grinds down) has led translators to render படை as a file or saw (Pope has ‘sharp instruments’, and the prose translation ‘a saw’). Parimelazhagar calls the figure ஏகதேச உருவகம் (partial metaphor): Valluvar does not say ‘the tree of wealth’, but the verb implies it. He explains that the destructive work really done by the sin of causing suffering is credited to the tears that are its sign, ‘because nothing is crueller than those tears’, and that the sense is that the wealth wastes away quickly. He groups kurals 553-556 as stating the evils that befall the tyrant himself. The text is the same across the sources consulted.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Take care that the eyes of the weak do not burn thee with thy kinsmen. In a race scorched by the eyes of the weak, no children take birth.”
— Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva, Section XCI (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
The sage Utathya warns King Mandhatri that the grief of the oppressed has a power that can destroy even the mightiest ruler.
It is the Kural’s idea exactly: the suffering of the weak becomes a weapon against the tyrant.
Christianity
“Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth.”
— James 5:4 (KJV) [5]
James warns oppressive rich men that the cries of those they cheated have reached God, and that their riches are already rotting.
As in the Kural, the cry of the wronged is what destroys the oppressor’s wealth.
Islam
When sending Mu‘ādh to Yemen, the Prophet told him to beware of the supplication of the oppressed, for there is no barrier between it and God.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2448 [6]
The cry of the wronged goes straight to God and brings about justice.
Like the tears in the Kural, the prayer of the oppressed works as a weapon against the unjust.
Judaism
“Ye shall not afflict any widow, or fatherless child. If thou afflict them in any wise—for if they cry at all unto Me, I will surely hear their cry— My wrath shall wax hot, and I will kill you with the sword…”
— Exodus 22:21-23 (JPS 1917 verse numbering) [7]
The Torah promises that God hears the cry of the defenceless and turns it against their oppressor.
The victim’s cry becomes the ‘sword’, just as in the Kural the tears become the army.
Buddhism
“Like fine dust thrown against the wind, evil falls back upon that fool who offends an inoffensive, pure and guiltless man.”
— Dhammapada 125 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
Harm done to the innocent comes back on the one who does it.
In the Kural, the suffering a tyrant inflicts comes back to wear away his own wealth.
Jainism
“Suffering, sorrow, agony, moaning, injury and lamentation, in oneself, in others or in both, lead to the influx of karmas which cause unpleasant feeling.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.11, English rendering on jainworld.com (Tattvarth Sutra, Chapter 6) [9]
Jain karma theory holds that causing others to grieve, weep and lament draws in karmic matter that later ripens as suffering for the one who caused it.
The Kural says the tears of oppressed subjects are the weapon that wears away a tyrant's wealth. Jain doctrine also turns the victims' weeping back on the oppressor, through karma.
Sikhism
“But if a powerful tiger attacks a flock of sheep and kills them, then its master must answer for it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 360, Raag Aasaa (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In his Babar hymns Guru Nanak cries out over the slaughter of the defenceless and insists that the suffering of the weak calls the one responsible for them to account.
The Kural says the tears of those who can no longer bear their suffering wear away the tyrant's wealth; Guru Nanak likewise makes the cry of the oppressed a demand that power must answer.
Greek Mythology
Phoenix tells Achilles of the Prayers (Litai), lame and wrinkled daughters of Zeus, who follow after Ruin. If a man rejects them, they go to Zeus and ask that Ruin should dog that man until he pays in full.
— Homer, Iliad IX.502-512 [11]
In Homer, the rejected pleas of suppliants become a force that brings ruin on the hard-hearted.
It is close to the Kural’s idea that unanswered distress turns into a destructive power.
Philosophy
Cicero argues that fear is a poor guardian of lasting power and goodwill a faithful one, and that no amount of power can withstand the hatred of the many, as the fall of tyrants shows.
— Cicero, De Officiis II.23-26 [12]
For Cicero, the resentment of the oppressed eventually overthrows even the strongest ruler.
It gives the Kural’s ‘tears as an army’ a political explanation.

Real Life Example

The Duke of Alba's Tenth Penny and the Dutch Revolt
1569-1573 · The Low Countries (now the Netherlands and Belgium)
Sent by Philip II of Spain to govern the Netherlands, the Duke of Alba ruled through a special tribunal, the Council of Troubles, and in 1569 announced a permanent ten per cent sales tax, the Tenth Penny. The tax united Catholics and Protestants against Spanish rule. When rebels seized the port of Brielle on 1 April 1572 other towns joined the revolt, and Alba left the Netherlands in 1573 without having restored control.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The suffering caused by harsh rule and heavy taxation turned the population against the crown and began the long process by which Spain lost its richest provinces.
The kural says the tears of people who can no longer bear their suffering are the weapon that wears away an oppressive ruler's wealth, and the revolt against Alba wore away Spain's hold on the Netherlands.
