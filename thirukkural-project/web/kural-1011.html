Kural 1011

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

கருமத்தால் நாணுதல் நாணுந் திருநுதல் நல்லவர் நாணுப் பிற.
Karumaththaal naanudhal naanum thirunudhal nallavar naanup pira.

Meaning

True shame is pulling back from a bad deed. Other kinds of shyness are just the natural modesty of lovely young women with bright foreheads.

Elementary

Some kids feel shy when they have to sing in front of the class; that is one kind of shy. But Valluvar says the shame that really matters is the feeling that stops your hand just before you do something mean or wrong. That feeling is like a good friend inside you.

University

The chapter opens by defining its subject: naanudhal (நாணுதல், 'shrinking, being abashed') that arises karumaththaal (கருமத்தால், 'on account of a deed'), i.e. recoiling from a base act, is the naan (நாண்) of the good. Puliyur Kesikan, following the commentarial line of Parimelazhagar, glosses the second line as saying that the other shrinkings of mind, speech and body are the modesty proper to women of good family (thirunudhal nallavar, 'the good ones of beautiful brow'), so Valluvar separates a moral emotion open to everyone from a customary, gendered bashfulness. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah read it the same way, and Pope renders the moral sense as ‘generous shame’. Placed in the Kudiyiyal after Nanriyil Selvam (ch. 101) and before Kudiseyalvagai (ch. 103), the chapter treats naan as the inner guard of the well-born person. The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (ytamizh writes the same words; only spacing differs).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Hrī means that one should be very modest and must not perform some act which is abominable.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3, purport (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Commenting on the list of divine qualities in Gita 16.1-3, Prabhupada defines hrī, modesty, as the refusal to do anything abominable.
This is the Kural's own definition: real shame is recoiling from a base deed.
Christianity
“how then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?”
— Genesis 39:9 (KJV) [4]
Joseph refuses the advances of Potiphar's wife, recoiling from the act itself rather than from the risk of being caught.
Joseph's recoil is the ‘shame at a deed’ that the Kural calls true naan.
Islam
“The Prophet (ﷺ) was more shy than a virgin in her separate room. And if he saw a thing which he disliked, we would recognize that (feeling) in his face.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6102 (sunnah.com) [5]
Abu Sa'id describes the Prophet's haya' as deeper than a secluded maiden's, and as showing on his face at anything objectionable.
The hadith brings together the two kinds of shame the Kural separates: a maiden's bashfulness and a good person's recoil from what is wrong.
Judaism
“It is a good sign in a person that he is one who experiences shame. Others say: Any person who experiences shame will not quickly sin”
— Babylonian Talmud, Nedarim 20a (William Davidson Edition, Koren-Steinsaltz English, via Sefaria) [6]
Reading Exodus 20:17 (‘that His fear may be upon your faces’) as shame, the baraita teaches that shame leads to fear of sin.
Like the Kural, it values shame because it holds a person back from wrong deeds.
Buddhism
“And what is the treasure of conscience? There is the case where a disciple of the noble ones feels shame at [the thought of engaging in] bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, mental misconduct.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.6, Dhana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha counts hiri, shame at misconduct of body, speech and mind, among the seven noble treasures.
It defines shame exactly as the Kural does: shrinking from wrong action.
Jainism
“By repenting of one's sins before the Guru the soul obtains humiliation; feeling humiliated, he will leave off all blameable occupations, and apply himself to praiseworthy occupations”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 29.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Among the exertions in righteousness, garhā, owning one's faults before the teacher, produces a humbling shame that turns a person away from blameworthy acts.
For the Jain monk as for the Kural, the valuable shame is the one that makes a person drop blameworthy deeds.
Sikhism
“Make modesty your circumcision, and good conduct your fast. In this way, you shall be a true Muslim.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 140, Raag Majh, Majh ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Speaking to Muslims in Majh ki Vaar, Guru Nanak turns outward religious marks into inner virtues, making saram (modesty, recoil from wrong) the real sign of faith.
The Kural separates true shame, recoil from a wrong deed, from customary bashfulness; Guru Nanak likewise sets inward modesty above the outward form.
Greek Mythology
Hector tells Andromache he cannot hang back from the fighting, because he would feel deep shame (aidos) before the Trojan men and long-robed women if he shrank from battle like a coward.
— Homer, Iliad 6.440-446 [10]
In the Iliad, aidos is the feeling that keeps a hero from a base act even when safety tempts him.
Hector's aidos, like the Kural's naan, is shame at a deed and not mere shyness.
Philosophy
Aristotle says shame (aidos) is a kind of fear of disrepute, more a feeling than a virtue. It suits the young, who still go wrong. A decent person should not need it, because he should not do disgraceful things in the first place.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.9 (1128b10-35) [11]
Aristotle places shame in actions: it is felt for voluntary bad acts, and its value lies in steering us away from them.
He agrees with the Kural that real shame is about deeds, but he ranks it below virtue, where Valluvar treats it as an ornament of the noble.

Real Life Example

General Ludwig Beck resigns rather than prepare an attack on Czechoslovakia
August 1938 · Berlin, Germany
As chief of the German army general staff, Ludwig Beck sent Hitler memoranda in 1938 opposing the planned attack on Czechoslovakia. In the summer of 1938 he urged the senior generals to resign together to prevent a European war; when they would not, he stepped down from his post alone that August.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Beck recoiled from a deed he judged wrong even though going along with it was the safe and expected course, and he became a leading figure of the German resistance.
Valluvar's first kural defines true shame as shrinking back from a wrongful deed, and Beck's resignation was exactly such a refusal to put his hand to a deed he found base.
