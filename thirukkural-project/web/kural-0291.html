Kural 291

Veracity · Vaaymai · வாய்மை

வாய்மை எனப்படுவது யாதெனின் யாதொன்றும் தீமை இலாத சொலல்.
Vaaymai enappaduvadhu yaadhenin yaadhondrum Theemai ilaadha solal.

Meaning

If you ask what truthfulness is, it is speaking words that cause no harm of any kind to anyone.

Elementary

Being truthful isn’t only about not telling lies. Imagine your words are like gifts you hand to people — a truthful gift is one that never hurts anybody. So truth means saying things that are real AND that don’t cause harm.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a deliberately surprising definition: vaaymai is not defined by correspondence to fact (the usual Western criterion) but by its effect — speech ‘without even the least evil’ (yaadhondrum theemai ilaadha). The standard commentators (Parimelazhagar, Mu. Varadarasan, Solomon Pappaiah) all read theemai as harm to others, so truth becomes an ethical rather than merely epistemic category. This places the Kural within the broader Indian tradition that fuses satya with ahimsa, and it sets up the controversial next couplet (292) on beneficial untruth. The verse’s rhetorical form — a question answered in a definition (enappaduvadhu yaadhenin, ‘if one asks what is called…’) — marks it as the chapter’s thesis statement.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Words that do not cause distress, are truthful, inoffensive, and beneficial, as well as regular recitation of the Vedic scriptures—these are declared as austerity of speech.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna defines the discipline of speech as words that are true AND do not upset or harm the listener. The commentary on this verse also cites Manu 4.138: speak the truth, speak what is pleasing, do not speak an unpleasant truth.
Like the Kural, the Gita builds harmlessness into the very definition of right, truthful speech.
Christianity
“Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.”
— Ephesians 4:29 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers that every word should build people up and do them good, never tear them down.
Both texts judge speech by its effect on the hearer: good speech ‘ministers grace,’ never harm.
Islam
“O you who have believed, fear Allāh and speak words of appropriate justice.”
— Qur’an 33:70 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an asks believers to speak ‘qawlan sadīdan’ — speech that is straight, true, and right, the next verse promising that God will set their deeds right in return.
‘Appropriate, just’ speech unites truth with rightness toward others, echoing the Kural’s harmless truth.
Judaism
“Speak ye every man the truth to his neighbour; execute the judgment of truth and peace in your gates.”
— Zechariah 8:16 (KJV) [4]
The prophet Zechariah joins truth and peace as a pair: speaking truth is meant to build a peaceful community.
Truth in Zechariah is always paired with peace, just as the Kural pairs it with harmlessness.
Buddhism
“Speak only the speech that neither torments self nor does harm to others. That speech is truly well spoken.”
— Subhasita Sutta, Sutta Nipata 3.3 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
In the Subhasita Sutta, Vangisa praises the Buddha’s teaching that good speech must hurt neither the speaker nor anyone else; the same verses call truth ‘deathless speech.’
Almost the Kural’s definition: right speech is speech free of harm, grounded in truth.
Jainism
“Speaking what is not commendable–asat–is falsehood (anṛta).” … “What is not laudable? That which causes pain and suffering to the living beings is not commendable. It does not matter whether it refers to the actual fact or not.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.14, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Umāsvāti's definition of falsehood, as explained by Pūjyapāda, rests on harm rather than on factual accuracy. Speech that causes pain to living beings counts as untruth because every Jain vow serves ahiṃsā.
Valluvar defines truthfulness as speech that harms no one in any way. Pūjyapāda defines its opposite as speech that causes pain to living beings, 'whether it refers to the actual fact or not.' The two definitions mirror each other almost exactly.
Sikhism
“Do not utter even a single harsh word; your True Lord and Master abides in all.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In his saloks Sheikh Farid tells the seeker never to wound anyone with speech, because God dwells in every heart.
The Kural defines truthful speech as speech that harms no one in any way. Farid grounds the same ideal of harmless speech in God's presence in all beings.
Greek Mythology
“And Sea begat Nereus, the eldest of his children, who is true and lies not: and men call him the Old Man because he is trusty and gentle and does not forget the laws of righteousness, but thinks just and kindly thoughts.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 233-236 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [9]
Hesiod’s Nereus, the ‘Old Man of the Sea,’ is the Greek god famed for never lying — and Hesiod pairs his truthfulness with gentleness and kindly thoughts.
Nereus embodies truth that is also gentle and kind — truth without harm.
Philosophy
Immanuel Kant, in his 1797 essay ‘On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy,’ argued that truthfulness in all statements is an unconditional duty of reason — one may not lie even to a murderer asking where a friend is hiding.
— Kant, On a Supposed Right to Lie (1797) [10]
Kant defines truthfulness purely as not asserting falsehood, whatever the consequences to others.
An instructive contrast: Kant defines truth by the statement, Valluvar by its effect on others — and would likely judge Kant’s truthful answer to the murderer as not vaaymai at all.

Real Life Example

Elisabeth Kubler-Ross talks honestly with dying patients
1965-1969 · University of Chicago, Chicago, Illinois, United States
In 1965 the psychiatrist Elisabeth Kubler-Ross joined the University of Chicago and began a series of seminars in which terminally ill patients were interviewed and allowed to speak openly about their illness and their care. At the time many doctors avoided telling patients that their condition was terminal, and some colleagues thought her seminars cruel, but she found that patients wanted to be treated as people and spoken to frankly. Her book On Death and Dying (1969), drawn from these interviews, helped change the medical approach to the care of the dying.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Kubler-Ross showed that honest speech, offered with compassion, relieved the isolation of the dying rather than harming them, and her work helped make truthful, caring conversation part of end-of-life medicine.
The kural defines truthfulness as speech that brings no harm to anyone, and Kubler-Ross demonstrated that even the hardest truths could be spoken in a way that comforted rather than wounded.
