Kural 796

Investigation in Forming Friendships · Natpaaraaythal · நட்பாராய்தல்

கேட்டினும் உண்டோர் உறுதி கிளைஞரை நீட்டி அளப்பதோர் கோல்.
Kettinum undor urudhi kilaignarai neetti alappadhor kol.

Meaning

Even bad times have one good side. They are a measuring stick that shows how far your friends and relatives will really go for you.

Elementary

When it rains, you find out which umbrellas have holes. When something bad happens to you, you find out which friends stay. That is the one good thing about a bad day: it shows you who your real friends are.

University

Kedu (கேடு) is ruin or misfortune and urudhi (உறுதி) is ‘benefit’ or ‘firm good’; the -um in kettinum gives ‘even in ruin’. Kilaignar (கிளைஞர்) literally means kin, ‘those of the same branch’, but Parimelazhagar and the modern commentators extend it to friends, since the chapter is about friendship. The image of neetti alappadhu oor kol (நீட்டி அளப்பதோர் கோல், ‘a rod that measures by stretching out’) is a surveyor’s measuring rod laid end to end, which measures exactly how far each person’s affection reaches. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both stress the paradox that harm itself becomes the instrument of knowledge. The sandhi-joined text reads கேட்டினு முண்டோ ருறுதி, the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Test a servant while in the discharge of his duty, a relative in difficulty, a friend in adversity, and a wife in misfortune.”
— Chanakya Niti 1.11 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya says each kind of relationship has its own testing ground, and for a friend that test is adversity.
Chanakya makes the same claim as the Kural: hardship is the test that measures a friend.
Christianity
“A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity.”
— Proverbs 17:17 (KJV) [5]
The proverb says real friendship is constant and that hard times are what a brother is for.
Both texts say adversity is what reveals and proves a true friend or kinsman.
Islam
When the Prophet left Mecca under threat of death, Abu Bakr alone went with him and hid with him in the cave of Thawr while their pursuers searched nearby; the Qur’an remembers him as ‘the second of two’ to whom the Prophet said, ‘Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.’
— Qur’an 9:40 and the Hijra narrative (Sira) [6]
Qur’an 9:40 recalls the Hijra, the moment of greatest danger, when Abu Bakr’s loyalty was proven.
The hour of danger showed who the true companion was, just as the Kural says misfortune measures friends.
Judaism
“But Ruth replied, ‘Do not urge me to leave you, to turn back and not follow you. For wherever you go, I will go; wherever you lodge, I will lodge; your people shall be my people, and your God my God.’”
— Ruth 1:16 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, Sefaria) [7]
Widowed and destitute, Naomi urges her daughters-in-law to go home; Orpah leaves, but Ruth refuses to abandon her.
Naomi’s ruin works as the Kural’s measuring rod: it shows who stays and who goes.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who is the same in happiness and sorrow be understood as a warm-hearted friend: (i) he reveals his secrets, (ii) he conceals one’s own secrets, (iii) in misfortune he does not forsake one, (iv) his life even he sacrifices for one’s sake.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [8]
Among the marks of a true friend, the Buddha lists that he does not leave you when misfortune comes.
The Buddha and Valluvar both name misfortune as the moment a true friend is recognised.
Jainism
“men will remain until they have done their work in an old house the thatch of which is untied, keeping out the water br a dam, and being drenched with the rain falling down upon them. Thus will friends! remain with one until their business is finished.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 24 'Improper Friendship', stanza 1 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar compares self-interested friends to workers who put up with a leaking house only until their job is done, and then leave.
The Kural says misfortune is a measuring rod that shows how far friends really go for you, and the Naladiyar's leaking-house image likewise shows how far a fair-weather friend's loyalty reaches before it runs out.
Sikhism
“In good times, there are many companions around, but in bad times, there is no one at all. Says Nanak, vibrate, and meditate on the Lord; He shall be your only Help and Support in the end.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1428, Salok Mahalla 9 (salok 32), Guru Tegh Bahadur (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Tegh Bahadur observes that prosperity gathers companions and adversity scatters them, and he turns from fair-weather friends to God as the one support who remains.
The Kural says even misfortune has one benefit, since it measures exactly how far friends will go for you; Guru Tegh Bahadur's salok records the result of that measurement: many in good times, none in bad.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus comes home to Ithaca disguised as a ragged beggar and uses his apparent ruin to test his household: the swineherd Eumaeus shares his meagre food with him, while the goatherd Melanthius kicks and insults him, and the loyalty of each is revealed.
— Homer, Odyssey 14-17 [11]
In the second half of the Odyssey, Odysseus deliberately takes on the look of misfortune in order to measure who is faithful.
His disguise turns misfortune into exactly the Kural’s measuring rod for friends and dependants.
Philosophy
“And though what Ennius says is quite true,—‘the hour of need shews the friend indeed,’—yet it is in these two ways that most people betray their untrustworthiness and inconstancy”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia §17, quoting Ennius (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [12]
Laelius quotes the poet Ennius’ line, the source of the English proverb ‘a friend in need is a friend indeed’, and adds that prosperity and adversity both expose false friends.
Ennius’ ‘hour of need’ is the Kural’s measuring rod of misfortune.

Real Life Example

Mark Twain publishes the memoirs of the ruined Ulysses S. Grant
1884-1885 · New York, United States
In May 1884 the collapse of the Wall Street firm of Grant & Ward, run by his partner Ferdinand Ward, wiped out former President Ulysses S. Grant's savings, and later that year he learned that he had incurable throat cancer. His friend Mark Twain, who had long urged him to write his memoirs, arranged to publish them through his own publishing firm, and Grant raced to finish the manuscript, completing it only days before his death in July 1885. The memoirs sold about 300,000 sets and earned his widow, Julia, more than 400,000 dollars.
— Sources: [13] [14]
When Grant lost his fortune and faced death, Twain's friendship provided the means by which he saved his family from poverty.
The kural says that even ruin has one benefit, since it is a measuring rod that shows how far one's friends truly reach, and Grant's ruin showed the full length of Twain's friendship.
