Kural 150

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

அறன்வரையான் அல்ல செயினும் பிறன்வரையாள் பெண்மை நயவாமை நன்று.
Aranvaraiyaan alla seyinum piranvaraiyaal, Penmai nayavaamai nandru.

Meaning

Even if a man does not live by what is right and does many wrong things, it is still good if he does not desire another man’s wife.

Elementary

Imagine someone who breaks lots of rules. This kural says: even if they have not been good at many things, they should at least keep this one rule and never go after someone else's husband or wife. It is the one line everyone must never cross.

University

The closing couplet makes a realistic concession: even a man who does not stay within virtue's limits (அறன் வரையான்) and does what should not be done (அல்ல செயினும்) should at least not desire one who lives within another's limits (பிறன் வரையாள்). The repetition of வரை ('boundary') creates a pointed wordplay: he may cross the bounds of virtue, but must not cross the bounds of another's marriage. By holding this line even for the imperfect, Valluvar marks adultery as a floor below which no one should fall, not merely an ideal for saints. The move is pastoral as well as moral, offering even the flawed a place to begin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, Vibhishana, the brother of the demon-king Ravana, belongs to a race known for wrongdoing, yet he repeatedly urges Ravana to return Sita to Rama. When Ravana refuses, Vibhishana leaves him and joins Rama.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (narrative summary) [3]
Even within a household known for evil deeds, one voice insists that taking another's wife is the line that must not be crossed.
The Kural says even those who do wrong should hold this one line; Vibhishana holds it amid a family of wrongdoers.
Christianity
“Flee fornication. Every sin that a man doeth is without the body; but he that committeth fornication sinneth against his own body.”
— 1 Corinthians 6:18 (KJV) [4]
Paul singles out sexual sin as different from all other sins, because it is a sin against one's own body.
Both texts treat this sin as special: whatever else a person does, this one must be fled.
Islam
“Allah will give shade, to seven, on the Day when there will be no shade but His. (These seven persons are) ... a man who refuses the call of a charming woman of noble birth for illicit intercourse with her and says: I am afraid of Allah.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 660 [5]
The Prophet listed, among seven people God will shelter on Judgement Day, a man who refuses a tempting invitation to sin out of fear of God.
Both texts show that holding this one line brings great good, whatever else a person's record may be.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not commit adultery.”
— Exodus 20:14 (KJV) [4]
The seventh commandment states the ban on adultery as an absolute, with no conditions or exceptions.
The Kural sets this as the one line even the imperfect must keep; the Torah states it as a basic, unconditional commandment.
Buddhism
“Such a man acquires demerit and an unhappy birth in the future. Brief is the pleasure of the frightened man and woman, and the king imposes heavy punishment. Hence, let no man consort with another's wife.”
— Dhammapada 310 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada ends its warning with a plain rule for everyone: no one should go to another man's wife.
Like the Kural's closing verse, this sets a universal minimum rule that applies to every person.
Jainism
In Vimalasūri's Jain Rāmāyaṇa (Paumacariya), Rāvaṇa once paid homage to the omniscient monk Anantavīrya and took a vow never to force himself on any unwilling woman. Even after abducting Sītā, he refuses his queen Mandodarī's urging to use force, because of that vow.
— Vimalasūri, Paumacariya 14.1–153 (Rāvaṇa's vow) and 46 (Sītā in Laṅkā); summarised in K. R. Chandra, 'A Critical Study of Paumacariyam' (1970), wisdomlib.org [7]
The Jain tradition portrays Rāvaṇa as a great but flawed man who commits grave wrongs, including the abduction itself, yet keeps this one vow about another man's wife. His remaining desire for Sītā still leads to his downfall.
Valluvar concedes that even a man who does many wrongs should at least not desire another's wife, and the Jain Rāvaṇa is that kind of man: a wrongdoer who still holds a line he has vowed not to cross, though by desiring Sītā at all he falls short of the Kural's ideal.
Sikhism
“Abandoning his own wife, he is engrossed in sexual desire; his thoughts are on the wives of others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1013, Raag Maru, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak describes a man who has left his wife for a renunciant's life only to find his mind fixed on other men's wives.
The Kural holds that even a man who fails at much else must keep this one boundary; Guru Nanak shows a man who has given up much and still fails at this very point.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad (Book 7), during the war, the Trojan elder Antenor urges his own people to give Helen and her treasure back to the Greeks. Paris agrees to return the treasure but refuses to give up the woman, and the war goes on.
— Homer, Iliad, Book 7 (narrative summary) [9]
Even in the middle of a war, a wise Trojan saw that keeping another man's wife was the wrong at the root of all their suffering.
The Kural marks another's wife as the line not to cross whatever else one does; Antenor saw the same, even though his city did not listen.
Philosophy
“Nor does goodness or badness with regard to such things depend on committing adultery with the right woman, at the right time, and in the right way, but simply to do any of them is to go wrong.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle says adultery can never be done 'the right way': it is always wrong, whatever the situation.
Both texts treat adultery as an absolute line: the Kural says even the imperfect must keep it, and Aristotle says no circumstance makes it right.

Real Life Example

Ulysses S. Grant's lifelong devotion to Julia Dent Grant
1848-1885 · St. Louis, Missouri, and elsewhere, United States
Ulysses S. Grant married Julia Dent in 1848 after an engagement of four years delayed by the Mexican-American War, during which he wrote her many letters. In 1854 he resigned suddenly from the army amid reports that he had begun drinking heavily while separated from his family, and he then failed as a farmer and in several other jobs before the Civil War. Through these setbacks and his later fame as general and president, the couple remained devoted; the National Park Service describes their long marriage as marked by love, trust and respect.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Grant's career included real failures and a reputation for drinking, yet his loyalty to his wife was never in question, and his letters to her are preserved at the Library of Congress.
The kural says that even a man who falls short in many ways should at least not desire another's wife; Grant, whatever his failings, held firmly to that line.
