Kural 558

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

இன்மையின் இன்னாது உடைமை முறைசெய்யா மன்னவன் கோற்கீழ்ப் படின்.
Inmaiyin innaathu udaimai muraiseyyaa Mannavan korkeezhp padin.

Meaning

Under an unfair king, having money brings more misery than having nothing at all.

Elementary

Imagine a class where a mean monitor takes the best toy from anyone who brings one. Soon, the kids who bring nice toys are the most worried ones. Valluvar says that under a greedy king, being rich becomes more dangerous than being poor.

University

The paradox turns on இன்மை (having nothing) and உடைமை (possessing). Parimelazhagar explains it by describing what the covetous king does: those who own property are the ones who suffer seizures, torture and binding ‘till the hands ache’ (கைந்நோவ யாப்புண்டல்), so under such a ruler wealth is worse than poverty. He applies it to everyone (யாவர்க்கும்), not just the rich, and groups kurals 557-558 as stating the evils that fall on those who live in the tyrant’s land. Manakkudavar agrees (‘even the wealthy suffer’) and counts 557-559 as the evils of failing to do justice. கோற்கீழ்ப் படின் means ‘if one falls under the sceptre’. The text is the same across the sources consulted.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhishma teaches that a king should draw revenue gently, as a bee gathers honey without harming the flower, or as one milks a cow without cutting the udder, so that the people can go on producing wealth.
— Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva, Section LXXXVIII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Greedy taxation that crushes the producers destroys the source of all wealth.
Under a king who ignores this, wealth becomes a burden, which is exactly the Kural’s point.
Christianity
Naboth refused to sell King Ahab the vineyard he had inherited, so Queen Jezebel had false witnesses accuse him. He was stoned to death and the king took the vineyard.
— 1 Kings 21:1-16 (KJV) [5]
Naboth’s small property was exactly what brought the unjust king’s power down on him.
It illustrates the Kural’s paradox: under an unjust king, owning something is more dangerous than owning nothing.
Islam
“As for the ship, it belonged to poor people working at sea. So I intended to cause defect in it as there was after them a king who seized every [good] ship by force.”
— Qur’an 18:79 (Saheeh International) [6]
Al-Khidr damages the poor men’s boat to save it, because a sound boat would be taken by the tyrant king.
This is the Kural’s paradox exactly: under an unjust king, having something good is worse than having less.
Judaism
“And they covet fields, and seize them; And houses, and take them away; Thus they oppress a man and his house, Even a man and his heritage.”
— Micah 2:2 (JPS 1917) [7]
Micah condemns the powerful who take land and houses from ordinary people.
Under such rulers, a man’s property is exactly what draws oppression, as the Kural says.
Buddhism
The Buddha describes a man who works hard to gain wealth and then suffers anxiety guarding it, thinking ‘How can I keep kings and thieves from taking it?’, and grieves bitterly when kings seize it.
— Majjhima Nikāya 13, Mahādukkhakkhandha Sutta [8]
Among the dangers of wealth, the sutta names seizure by kings as a standard source of suffering.
It spells out why property can hurt more than poverty under a grasping ruler.
Jainism
Kumārapāla, the Caulukya king of Gujarat who became a disciple of the Jain teacher Hemacandra, is remembered for abolishing the royal right to seize the property of people who died without heirs, which had left widows and families destitute.
— Kumarapala (Chaulukya dynasty), Wikipedia (summarising Jain prabandha sources) [9]
Jain tradition presents the end of this confiscation as an act of compassion inspired by Jain teaching: the king stopped preying on his subjects' property.
The Kural says that under an unjust king owning wealth is worse than poverty, because the owners are the ones who get stripped. Kumārapāla's reform removed one such predatory practice.
Sikhism
“Their wealth and youthful beauty, which gave them so much pleasure, have now become their enemies.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa (Babarvani), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Describing the households of Saidpur when Babar's soldiers fell on them, Guru Nanak says the very wealth and beauty they had enjoyed now turned against them.
The Kural says that under an unjust sceptre having wealth brings more misery than having nothing; Guru Nanak shows exactly this, wealth becoming the owner's enemy under lawless power.
Greek Mythology
Asked for advice on ruling, the tyrant Thrasybulus led Periander’s messenger through a cornfield, silently cutting off every ear of grain that stood above the rest. Periander understood that he should do away with the most prominent citizens.
— Herodotus, Histories V.92 [11]
Under tyranny, standing out through wealth or distinction makes a person a target.
It is a vivid picture of the Kural’s paradox: under the cruel sceptre, the well-off suffer most.
Philosophy
Aristotle notes that tyrants deliberately impoverish their subjects, through heavy taxes and great public works like the pyramids of Egypt, so that the people are too busy and poor to plot against them.
— Aristotle, Politics V.11, 1313b (trans. B. Jowett) [12]
He sees stripping people of property as a deliberate tool of tyranny.
It explains why, as the Kural says, wealth becomes a misery under such a king.

Real Life Example

Sulla's proscriptions
82-81 BCE · Rome, Roman Republic
After winning the civil war, the dictator Lucius Cornelius Sulla posted public lists of people declared outlaws, and the property of those named was confiscated and sold, often cheaply to his friends and supporters. Britannica describes the victims as some 520 wealthy opponents whose property went to Sulla's veterans, and their sons and grandsons were barred from public office. Ancient writers report that some people were added to the lists simply because others wanted their estates.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Under Sulla's rule owning property became a danger in itself, since wealth could put a person's name on the lists.
The kural says that under an unjust ruler having wealth brings more misery than having nothing, and the proscriptions made riches a reason to be targeted.
