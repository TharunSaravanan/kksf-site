Kural 858

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இகலிற்கு எதிர்சாய்தல் ஆக்கம் அதனை மிக்லூக்கின் ஊக்குமாம் கேடு.
Ikalirku edhirsaaidhal aakkam adhanai mikalookkin ookkumaam kedu.

Meaning

Turning away from hostility brings good things. If you push it further, harm comes rushing toward you.

Elementary

A small fire can be stamped out, but if you keep feeding it sticks it grows until it burns the house. The poet says anger is like that: put it out and good things grow, feed it and trouble comes running.

University

The first line repeats the key verb of 855, edhir saaidhal (எதிர் சாய்தல், to lean away from), now naming its reward: aakkam (ஆக்கம், prosperity, growth). The second line plays on ūkkam (ஊக்கம், urging, zeal): if one urges hostility on (mikal ūkkin), ruin ‘urges itself’ (ūkkumaam kēdu) toward that person, an elegant mirroring. Puliyur Kesigan reads the first line as checking hostility before it rises in one’s own heart; Pappaiah as ‘not welcoming’ it; Kalaignar as confronting and removing it. Textual note: as in 855, both base sources print ‘மிக்லூக்கின்’ while ytamizh romanizes ‘miklookkin’; the morphology is mikal + ūkkin, and the printed cluster looks like a shared digital slip. Otherwise identical.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna names anger as one of three doors to ruin and urges renouncing it.
The Kural similarly says giving up hostility brings gain, while feeding it brings ruin.
Christianity
“And the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace of them that make peace.”
— James 3:18 (KJV) [4]
James teaches that peace is the soil from which good fruit grows.
The Kural similarly says turning away from hostility yields prosperity.
Islam
Muslims are urged not to cut off a brother for more than three days and to restrain anger, since the Prophet taught that ‘the strong is the one who controls himself while in anger’; the tradition treats unchecked quarrel as self-harming.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6065 and 6114 (summarized) [5]
Islamic ethics sets limits on how long estrangement may be fed.
Like the Kural, it treats nursing hostility as harmful and checking it as beneficial.
Judaism
“If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat; and if he be thirsty, give him water to drink: For thou shalt heap coals of fire upon his head, and the LORD shall reward thee.”
— Proverbs 25:21-22 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Proverbs promises reward to the one who meets an enemy with kindness instead of hostility.
The Kural likewise says turning from hostility brings gain.
Buddhism
“Whatever harm an enemy may do to an enemy, or a hater to a hater, an ill-directed mind inflicts on oneself a greater harm.”
— Dhammapada 42 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says a mind turned toward hatred hurts its owner more than any enemy could.
This is the Kural’s second line: feed hostility and ruin comes to you.
Jainism
“A cruel man does cruel acts and is thereby involved in other cruelties; but sinful undertakings will in the end bring about misery.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.8.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Jacobi's note glosses the 'cruel man' (Prakrit vêri, Sanskrit vairin) as one who is hostile and hurts living beings. The verse teaches that such enmity drags a person into further enmity and ends in misery.
Valluvar says that whoever pushes hostility further invites ruin to hurry toward him, and the Sūtrakṛtāṅga describes the same spiral: the hostile man's cruelties breed more cruelties and end in misery.
Sikhism
“Do not be angry with anyone else; look within your own self instead. Be humble in this world, O Nanak! And by His Grace you shall be carried across.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 259, Raag Gauree, Bavan Akhri (salok before pauri 44), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Bavan Akhri Guru Arjan turns the seeker from anger at others to self-examination and humility, promising that grace will carry him across.
The Kural says that turning away from hostility brings prosperity while pressing it on brings ruin; Guru Arjan likewise tells the seeker to turn from anger at others and promises a good end.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Seven Against Thebes, the brothers Eteocles and Polynices, cursed by their father and unwilling to yield, nurse their quarrel over the throne until they kill each other at the seventh gate.
— Aeschylus, Seven Against Thebes [9]
Neither brother would turn away from hostility, and both were destroyed.
They embody the Kural’s warning that whoever urges hostility on is met by ruin.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that hatred is increased by being returned, and can on the other hand be destroyed by love.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Prop. 43 [10]
Feeding hatred makes it grow; meeting it with love dissolves it.
This is the same two-way law the Kural states.

Real Life Example

The Rush-Bagot Agreement disarms the Great Lakes
28-29 April 1817 · Washington, D.C., United States
Less than three years after the War of 1812, acting U.S. Secretary of State Richard Rush and the British minister Sir Charles Bagot concluded an agreement by exchange of notes limiting naval forces on the Great Lakes and Lake Champlain. Each side was allowed only a few small vessels of no more than 100 tons, each armed with a single 18-pound cannon. The U.S. Senate approved it in April 1818.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The agreement is regarded as one of the most successful disarmament agreements in history and helped lower tensions between the United States and British North America after the war.
The kural says turning away from hostility brings prosperity while pushing it further invites ruin, and the two former enemies chose to stand down their warships on the lakes rather than continue a naval race.
