Kural 1116

The Praise of Her Beauty · Nalam Punaindhuraithal · நலம் புனைந்துரைத்தல்

மதியும் மடந்தை முகனும் அறியா பதியின் கலங்கிய மீன்.
Madhiyum madandhai mukanum ariyaa padhiyin kalangiya meen.

Meaning

The stars cannot tell the moon apart from this young woman's face, so they are confused and wander from their places.

Elementary

Imagine the stars as a class of children who always stand near their teacher, the moon. One night a girl with a face as bright as the moon walks by, and the stars get mixed up: 'Which one is our moon?' They start running about in a muddle.

University

A cosmic hyperbole: the meen (மீன், stars; literally 'fish', the common Tamil word for stars) cannot tell madhi (மதி, moon) from the madandhai’s (மடந்தை, young woman’s) mukan (முகன், face) and are kalangiya (கலங்கிய, 'agitated, confused') in their pathi (பதி, 'fixed station'). In the Parimelazhagar tradition the stars, which in Indian astronomy move in fixed relation to the moon through the lunar mansions (naksatra), are thrown out of their courses by seeing two moons. All the modern commentators (Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar, Puliyur Kesikan) agree on 'the stars are troubled and leave their places'. This kural opens a run of four (1116-1119) built on the moon-and-face comparison. Pope: 'The stars perplexed are rushing wildly from their spheres; / For like another moon this maiden’s face appears.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Indeed, the moon and thou inspire confidence, only to deceive the host of lovers.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
The love-sick king complains to the god of Love that both Love and the moon promise comfort but only torment lovers.
In both texts the moon’s light unsettles those who look on it, though Kalidasa blames the moon and the Kural blames the beloved’s rival brightness.
Christianity
“My dove, my undefiled is but one; she is the only one of her mother, she is the choice one of her that bare her. The daughters saw her, and blessed her;”
— Song of Solomon 6:9 (KJV) [4]
The lover says his beloved is unique, and all who see her are moved to praise her.
As the Kural’s stars are thrown into confusion by her face, everyone who sees the Song’s beloved is overwhelmed.
Islam
“I saw the Prophet (ﷺ) on a clear night, so I looked at the Messenger of Allah (ﷺ) and at the moon, and he was wearing a red Hullah, and he looked better than the moon to me.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2811 [5]
Jabir ibn Samurah recalls looking back and forth between the Prophet and the moon on a bright night and finding the Prophet the more beautiful.
Like the Kural’s stars, Jabir compares a face with the moon on the same night, and the face wins.
Judaism
“And Esther obtained favour in the sight of all them that looked upon her.”
— Esther 2:15 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
When Esther’s turn came to go before the king, everyone who saw her was won over.
As the stars are unsettled by the Kural’s heroine, all who look on Esther are moved by her.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says that one who takes up the good path after heedlessness lights up the world like the moon freed from clouds.
— Dhammapada 172 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The moon emerging from cloud is a Buddhist image for the radiance of a transformed life.
The Kural’s face shines like the moon; Buddhism keeps the image of moonlight but gives it to the light of a purified mind.
Jainism
“The kingfisher, mistaking my wife's eyes for a gyal-fish, will fly after her, but when it sees her beautiful eyebrow it will forbear to strike, afraid and supposing it a bow.”
— Naladiyar 40.5 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In the Naladiyar’s love chapter a kingfisher is fooled by the wife’s fish-like eyes and then frightened off by her bow-like brow.
Like the Kural’s stars, a creature of nature mistakes the beloved’s features for the real thing and is thrown into confusion.
Sikhism
“In the bowl of the sky, the sun and moon are the lamps; the stars in the constellations are the pearls.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 663, Raag Dhanasari, Aarti, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Aarti, Guru Nanak pictures the whole sky as a worship tray, with the sun, moon and stars arranged in praise of the Lord.
In the Kural the stars are thrown into confusion because the beloved's face looks like a second moon; Guru Nanak too puts the moon and stars in the service of the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
“The stars about the full moon lose their bright beauty when she, almost full, illumines all earth with silver.”
— Sappho, fragment 34 (Lobel-Page), as rendered in Poems of Sappho, no. 4 (sacred-texts.com) [10]
Sappho observes that the stars seem to hide their brightness when the full moon rises, a figure she uses for one woman outshining others.
The Kural’s stars are confused by a face like the moon; Sappho’s stars fade beside the moon itself, the same celestial picture of overwhelming radiance.
Philosophy
Plato’s Socrates says that of all the Forms, beauty alone shines through the clearest of our senses, sight, so that it is the most visible and most loved.
— Plato, Phaedrus 250b-d (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato, beauty is the one ideal whose radiance can be seen directly in the world.
The Kural’s face is so radiant that even the stars mistake it for the moon, a playful picture of beauty that shines out beyond everything else.

Real Life Example

Chinese astronomers record the guest star of 1054
4 July 1054 · Song dynasty China
On 4 July 1054 astronomers of the Song dynasty in China recorded the sudden appearance of a new star, which they called a guest star, near the star now known as Zeta Tauri. It was bright enough to be seen in daylight for about 23 days and remained visible at night for nearly two years. Modern astronomers identify it as the supernova whose remains form the Crab Nebula.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Chinese records are among the most important observations in the history of astronomy, linking an ancient sighting to an object still studied today.
The kural imagines the stars thrown into confusion because a second moon, the beloved's face, has appeared among them; the guest star of 1054 is a real occasion when a strange new brilliance appeared and disturbed the familiar order of the sky.
