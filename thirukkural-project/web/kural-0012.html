Kural 12

The Excellence of Rain · Vaan Sirappu · வான் சிறப்பு

துப்பார்க்குத் துப்பாய துப்பாக்கித் துப்பார்க்குத் துப்பாய தூஉம் மழை.
Thuppaarkkuth thuppaaya thuppaakkith thuppaarkkuth, Thuppaaya thooum mazhai.

Meaning

Rain grows the food that people eat. Then rain itself also becomes food for them, as the water they drink.

Elementary

Rain is like a cook who grows the vegetables, cooks the meal, and then also pours you a glass of water to go with it. It does both jobs at once — it makes your food, and it is your drink.

University

This is one of the most technically dazzling couplets in the Kural: the syllable துப்பு (thuppu) is repeated five times, shifting sense each time between "food/sustenance," "strength," and the verbal sense "to eat or enjoy," a figure Tamil poetics calls யமகம் (yamakam). The elongated தூஉம் (thooum) is an அளபெடை, a metrical lengthening that also lets the word carry the sense of drinking. Semantically the verse makes a double claim that anticipates modern hydrology and nutrition alike — rain is the upstream cause of food and is simultaneously the substance consumed — a point later elaborated in chapter 104 on உழவு (agriculture). Commentators from Parimelazhagar to Salomon Pappaiah note that this two-sidedness is precisely why rain, and not the farmer, is given primacy in the book's opening argument.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“All living bodies subsist on food grains, which are produced from rain. Rains are produced by performance of yajña [sacrifice], and yajña is born of prescribed duties.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.14 (Bhaktivedanta, Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, 1972) [4]
Krishna sets out a causal chain — action produces sacrifice, sacrifice produces rain, rain produces grain, grain produces living beings.
The first two links of that chain are exactly this Kural: rain makes the food that eaters eat.
Christianity
“For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud, that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater:”
— Isaiah 55:10 (KJV) [5]
Isaiah uses rainfall as a figure for the reliability of God's word, but the image itself is a precise account of rain producing seed and bread.
"Bread to the eater" is almost a translation of துப்பார்க்குத் துப்பாய — food for those who eat.
Islam
“It is He who sends down rain from the sky; from it is drink and from it is foliage in which you pasture [animals].”
— Qur'an 16:10, An-Nahl (Sahih International) [6]
The verse divides rain's benefit in two: it is the water people drink, and it is the growth that feeds their livestock.
This is the Kural's exact double structure — rain as the drink and rain as the maker of food.
Judaism
“He watereth the hills from his chambers: the earth is satisfied with the fruit of thy works. He causeth the grass to grow for the cattle, and herb for the service of man: that he may bring forth food out of the earth;”
— Psalms 104:13–14 (KJV, rendering the Hebrew Tanakh) [5]
Psalm 104's creation hymn traces a direct line from rain falling on the hills to grass for cattle and bread for human beings.
Like the Kural it treats rain as the single upstream cause of everything eaters eat.
Buddhism
“What is one? All beings subsist on food.”
— Khuddakapāṭha 4, Sāmaṇera Pañha (Thanissaro Bhikkhu translation) [7]
The very first question put to a Buddhist novice, and its answer — the most basic fact about existence is that every living thing depends on nutriment (āhāra).
It shares the Kural's premise that eating is the universal condition of life, though it stops short of naming rain as its source.
Jainism
“There are beings living in water, many lives; of a truth, to the monks water has been declared to be living matter.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.1.3.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
Jainism counts water itself as a body of one-sensed souls (apkaya), so monks strain or boil their drinking water and limit its use to avoid harming those lives.
Where the Kural celebrates rain as both the maker of food and food itself, Jainism adds a distinctive ethical note: the very drink rain provides is alive and must be used with restraint.
Sikhism
“He commands the god of rain, and the rain pours down in torrents. Corn and wealth are produced in great abundance and prosperity... He reaches out and gives sustenance to all beings. Eating this, peace is produced.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1281, Raag Malar, Vaar Malar, Salok Mahala 3, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das describes a single chain: rain falls, grain grows, and people eat and are at peace. The rain itself comes from God's command.
The Kural's point is that rain makes the food and nourishes those who eat it. This salok traces the same path from rain to food to the eater's contentment.
Greek Mythology
Demeter, goddess of grain, gives the young prince Triptolemus of Eleusis the first seed corn and sends him out in a winged chariot to teach agriculture to all mankind, so that human beings can eat cultivated food rather than forage.
— Homeric Hymn 2, To Demeter (and the Eleusinian Triptolemus tradition) [10]
The Eleusinian myth makes grain a divine gift rather than a human invention, and makes the goddess of that gift the centre of Greece's most important mystery cult.
Like the Kural, it locates the origin of the food that eaters eat outside human labour, in a power that must give before people can consume.
Philosophy
“Now the first and greatest of necessities is food, which is the condition of life and existence.”
— Plato, Republic 369d (Jowett translation) [11]
Socrates, building an imaginary city from scratch, ranks food ahead of every other need as the precondition of a society existing at all.
It supplies the philosophical premise the Kural is arguing from — that everything else in human life is downstream of what people eat.

Real Life Example

The Nabataeans harvest rainwater at Petra
Nabataean period, flourishing in Hellenistic and Roman times · Petra, Jordan
The Nabataeans made Petra, in an arid region between the Red Sea and the Dead Sea, the rock-cut capital of their kingdom and a major caravan centre. They built channels, tunnels, diversion dams and a vast network of cisterns and reservoirs that controlled and conserved the seasonal rains. This water system allowed a large population to live in an essentially arid area.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Petra shows a desert city whose food, drink and very existence depended on catching rain that fell only in one season.
The kural says rain both produces the food people eat and is itself their drink, and Petra's cisterns and channels kept the stored rain serving the city in both ways.
