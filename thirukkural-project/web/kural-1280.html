Kural 1280

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

பெண்ணினால் பெண்மை உடைத்தென்ப கண்ணினால் காமநோய் சொல்லி இரவு.
Penninaal penmai udaiththenpa kanninaal kaamanoi solli iravu.

Meaning

People say that to show the pain of love with your eyes, and so to plead with the one you love, is the most womanly thing a woman can do.

Elementary

Sometimes the bravest thing a shy person can do is to show with their eyes how much they need someone, without saying it out loud. The poet says that this gentle, silent asking is the finest kind of grace.

University

The couplet generalises the chapter: kaṇṇiṉāl kāma nōy colli iravu, ‘telling the sickness of love with the eyes and entreating’, is said (eṉpa, ‘they say’) to be peṇmai, womanliness, added to womanliness (peṇṇiṉāl peṇmai uṭaittu). Varadarasanar and Kesikan specify the entreaty as asking the husband not to leave; Kalaignar makes it a plea to be taken along, linking it to 1279’s udanpokku; Solomon Pappaiah frames it as asking that the love-sickness be cured. Pope renders the sense as ‘womanhood’s most womanly device’. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; its framing assumes the classical ideal that a woman should not declare love in words, which modern readers may note as a convention of its time.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the fourth canto, Radha’s friend goes to Krishna and describes how Radha, wasting with longing, can only show her love-sickness in her sighs and looks, pleading for him to come.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 4 (paraphrase) [3]
Jayadeva’s Radha conveys her love-sickness through signs that her friend interprets to Krishna.
The Kural praises exactly this: love-sickness told without words, as a silent plea.
Christianity
“I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love.”
— Song of Solomon 5:8 (KJV) [4]
The bride wants her beloved to know of her love-sickness and sends word through others.
The Kural’s heroine makes the same plea, telling her love-sickness, but with her eyes alone.
Islam
“An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign. They do not ask people persistently [or at all].”
— Qur’an 2:273 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an praises those in need who, out of dignity, do not ask aloud but whose need can be read in their bearing.
The Kural likewise honours an entreaty made through signs rather than open asking.
Judaism
“And it was so, when the king saw Esther the queen standing in the court, that she obtained favour in his sight; and the king held out to Esther the golden sceptre that was in his hand.”
— Esther 5:2 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Esther’s first plea to the king is made simply by standing before him, and he grants her favour before she speaks.
The Kural, too, honours a plea made by presence and look rather than by words.
Buddhism
“As a bee gathers honey from the flower without injuring its color or fragrance, even so the sage goes on his alms-round in the village.”
— Dhammapada 49 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddhist monk asks for alms only by his quiet presence, taking without burdening the giver.
The Kural similarly honours an asking done gently and wordlessly.
Jainism
“It will always cause difficulties to a houseless monk to get everything by begging, and nothing without begging.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana counts the need to ask (yacana) among the troubles a monk must bear with dignity.
The Kural also concerns the dignity of asking, honouring a woman who pleads through her eyes rather than in words.
Sikhism
“Listen, O my Friend, to my message of love; my eyes are fixed upon You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 302, Raag Gauree, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das pleads with the Beloved, with his eyes fixed on Him, to hear his message of love.
The Kural praises the woman who tells her love-sickness with her eyes and so pleads with her beloved; Guru Ram Das likewise pleads with eyes fixed on the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
Nausicaa asks her father for a wagon to wash clothes, too shy to mention her hopes of marriage, but he understands everything she leaves unsaid.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.66-67 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows a young woman’s unspoken wish read perfectly by someone who loves her.
The Kural praises the same modest, unspoken asking that is nonetheless understood.
Philosophy
Montaigne lists the many things we do with our hands, head, eyes and brows, asking, promising, entreating, refusing, and says they form a language anyone can understand.
— Michel de Montaigne, Essays II.12, ‘Apology for Raymond Sebond’ (paraphrase) [11]
Montaigne argues that gestures and looks make up a full, universal language.
The Kural’s entreaty made with the eyes is a case of this silent language at its most delicate.

Real Life Example

Dorothea Lange photographs Migrant Mother
March 1936 · Nipomo, California, United States
In March 1936, while photographing migrant farm workers for the federal Resettlement Administration, Dorothea Lange came upon Florence Owens Thompson and her children in a camp of pea pickers whose crop had failed. She made five exposures, moving closer each time. The photograph known as Migrant Mother was widely circulated in magazines and newspapers and became a symbol of the hardship of the Great Depression, though Thompson later disputed parts of Lange's account of their meeting.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Without a word of appeal, the mother's face and gaze moved the public to feel the plight of migrant families.
The kural praises pleading with the eyes rather than with words; the silent, worried look in Migrant Mother made a plea that words could not.
