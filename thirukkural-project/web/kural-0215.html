Kural 215

Knowledge of What Is Befitting · Oppuravarithal · ஒப்புரவறிதல்

ஊருணி நீர்நிறைந் தற்றே உலகவாம் பேரறி வாளன் திரு.
Ooruni neernirain thatre ulagavaam Perari vaalan thiru.

Meaning

The riches of a wise person who loves to help the world are like a village pond filled to the top, there for everyone to use.

Elementary

Think of a big water tank in the middle of a village that everyone fills their pots from. When a wise, kind person becomes rich, their money is like that full tank: the whole village benefits.

University

Ooruni is specifically the common tank from which a village draws its drinking water, a piece of shared infrastructure in Tamil rural life. Parimelazhagar notes the figure of speech — ‘filling’ really belongs to the water but is attributed to the tank — and explains the point: such wealth does not go to waste but lasts long and unfailingly supplies everyone's needs. Ulagu avaam (‘desiring the world’) is read by Parimelazhagar as loving and practising ulaga nadai, while Manakkudavar reads it as ‘desired by the whole world.’ The kural begins a triad (215-217) of public-good images — tank, fruit tree, medicinal tree — that together treat private wealth in virtuous hands as a quasi-public resource.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The riches of the liberal never waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.1 (trans. R. T. H. Griffith) [4]
The Rig Veda's hymn on liberality says generous wealth never runs dry, while the miser ends up alone.
Echoes Parimelazhagar's point that the generous person's wealth, like the village tank, does not go to waste.
Christianity
“But by an equality, that now at this time your abundance may be a supply for their want, that their abundance also may be a supply for your want: that there may be equality”
— 2 Corinthians 8:14 (KJV) [5]
Paul asks one community to share its surplus with another in need, so that resources flow where they are needed.
Like the full tank, one person's abundance becomes the supply for others' needs.
Islam
“And from their properties was [given] the right of the [needy] petitioner and the deprived.”
— Qur'an 51:19 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an describes the righteous as those whose wealth contained a rightful share (haqq) for the poor.
Treats private wealth as carrying a public claim, as the Kural's tank belongs to the whole village.
Judaism
“The liberal soul shall be made fat: and he that watereth shall be watered also himself.”
— Proverbs 11:25 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs promises that the generous person, who ‘waters’ others, will be refreshed in turn.
Shares the water imagery and the idea that generous wealth keeps on supplying.
Buddhism
“Planters of parks or groves, those who build a bridge, a drinking place and well, and those who give monastic quarters. Their merit always grows by day and by night.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 1.47, Vanaropa Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha praises those who build public goods — wells, drinking places, bridges, groves — for the whole community.
Names the very kind of public water source the Kural uses as its image of virtuous wealth.
Jainism
“He quitted and rejected his real, valuable property, such as riches, gold, precious stones, jewels, pearls, conches, stones, corals, rubies, &c.; he distributed presents through proper persons, he distributed presents among indigent persons.”
— Kalpa Sūtra, Life of Mahāvīra §112 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
Before his renunciation Mahāvīra gave away his riches, distributing them through proper persons and to the poor.
Valluvar compares a wise benefactor's wealth to a full village tank open to all; Mahāvīra's great giving before renunciation shows wealth turned into a common supply for the needy.
Sikhism
During a siege of Anandpur, Bhai Kanhaiya went among the wounded on the battlefield giving water from his water-skin to men of both armies alike. When some Sikhs complained to Guru Gobind Singh, Bhai Kanhaiya said he saw the Guru in every face, and the Guru praised him and gave him salve to treat all the wounded.
— SikhNet, The Story of Bhai Kanhaiya Ji (paraphrase of the Sikh tradition) [9]
Sikhs remember Bhai Kanhaiya as the model of seva offered to all without distinction, even to enemies.
The Kural likens the wise benefactor's wealth to a brimming village drinking-pond from which everyone may draw; Bhai Kanhaiya's water was given to all who were thirsty, friend or foe.
Greek Mythology
“A bad neighbour is as great a plague as a good one is a great blessing; he who enjoys a good neighbour has a precious possession.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 342–351 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod advises that a good neighbour is one of the most valuable things a person can have.
Recognises, like the Kural, that a good person's resources are a blessing for the whole neighbourhood.
Philosophy
“It is clearly better that property should be private, but the use of it common; and the special business of the legislator is to create in men this benevolent disposition.”
— Aristotle, Politics II.5 (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristotle argues that property should be owned privately, but good people should let it be used for the common benefit.
Exactly the Kural's model: privately held wealth that functions like the village's common tank.

Real Life Example

Queen Udayamati's stepwell, the Rani-ki-Vav
11th century CE · Patan, Gujarat, India
The Rani-ki-Vav, or Queen's Stepwell, on the banks of the Saraswati River at Patan, was built in the 11th century and is traditionally attributed to Queen Udayamati as a memorial to the Chaulukya king Bhima I. UNESCO describes it as a memorial that also functions as a single water management system, designed as an inverted temple with seven levels of stairs leading down to an underground reservoir and decorated with hundreds of sculptures. Silted over for centuries, it was excavated and restored by the Archaeological Survey of India and inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2014.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Royal wealth was turned into a monumental public reservoir, combining remembrance of a king with a store of water for the community.
The kural compares the wealth of a wise person who loves the world to the village tank filled to the brim, and the stepwell is literally royal wealth turned into a great public store of water.
