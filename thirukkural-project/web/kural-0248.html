Kural 248

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

பொருளற்றார் பூப்பர் ஒருகால் அருளற்றார் அற்றார்மற் றாதல் அரிது.
Porulatraar pooppar orukaal arulatraar, Atraarmat raadhal aridhu.

Meaning

People who lose their money may do well again one day. But people who lose their kindness are truly poor, and they almost never get it back.

Elementary

A tree can lose all its leaves in winter and still bloom again in spring. But if the tree’s roots die, it cannot grow back. Losing money is like losing leaves. Losing kindness is like losing the roots.

University

The verb pooppar, ‘they will blossom’, turns poverty into a season: the poor are like a plant between flowerings, and fortune may return orukaal, at some time. The loss of arul is described with a deliberate repetition, arul atraar atraar, ‘those bereft of grace are bereft’: they are destitute in the absolute sense, and a change in their state (maTru aadhal) is aridhu, rare or difficult. Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar stress this asymmetry: wealth can be recovered, but compassion, once lost, rarely returns. Philosophically the kural makes a claim about character, that compassion is a root disposition and not a possession, so its loss changes who a person is rather than what they have. The word ‘rare’ rather than ‘impossible’ leaves room for moral recovery.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Attaining repeated birth amongst the species of demoniac life, such persons can never approach Me. Gradually they sink down to the most abominable type of existence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.20 [3]
Krishna describes those of demonic, pitiless nature as falling ever further, birth after birth.
Both texts describe a pitiless state that tends to reinforce itself rather than recover.
Christianity
“But whoso hath this world’s good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him?”
— 1 John 3:17 (KJV) [4]
John asks how anyone with possessions who closes his heart to the needy can have the love of God in him.
Possessing wealth but lacking compassion is true poverty, the kural’s arul atraar atraar.
Islam
“Mercy is not removed (from anyone) except from a wicked one.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1923 (graded hasan) [5]
The Arabic shaqī means one who is wretched or damned, so losing mercy is a mark of ultimate misfortune.
Losing compassion is the sign of true wretchedness, the kural’s ‘truly destitute’.
Judaism
“Due to [biglal] this thing means that it is a wheel [galgal] that turns in the world, upon which people continuously rise and fall.”
— Talmud, Shabbat 151b [6]
The same Talmudic passage adds that ‘anyone who has compassion for God’s creatures will receive compassion from Heaven’, and that anyone who lacks it will not.
Poverty is a turning wheel, so the poor may rise again, which is the kural’s pooppar orukaal; lack of compassion is what Heaven does not overlook.
Buddhism
Devadatta, who repeatedly tried to kill the Buddha and split the Sangha, is described by the Buddha as bound for hell for an aeon and ‘incurable’ (atekiccho).
— Anguttara Nikaya 6.62; Vinaya, Cullavagga 7 [7]
His persistent cruelty is presented as having carried him beyond easy remedy.
An example of the ‘rarely recovers’ state of one who has lost compassion; commentarial tradition does, however, foresee his eventual awakening in the distant future.
Jainism
“The capital is human life, the gain is heaven; through the loss of that capital man must be born as a denizen of hell or a brute animal. … Having once forfeited them, he will have to endure these two states of misery; it will be difficult for him to attain an upward course for a long time to come.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 7.16-18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the parable of three merchants, the one who loses his capital stands for the sinner who wastes human life, and the Sutra warns that such a loss is very hard to recover.
Valluvar says those who lose wealth may flourish again, but those who lose compassion rarely recover; the Uttaradhyayana likewise sets ordinary loss against a moral loss from which the way back is long and hard.
Sikhism
“How can anyone know the pain of another, if there is no compassion and sympathy within? The discarded bride is miserable, and loses both worlds”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 793, Raag Soohee, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Ravidas says that one with no feeling within cannot know another's pain, and that such a soul loses both this world and the next.
The Kural says those who have lost compassion are truly destitute and seldom recover; Ravidas likewise describes the heart without sympathy as wretched and bereft in both worlds.
Greek Mythology
Erysichthon, a wealthy Thessalian, ignores the pleas of a dryad and cuts down Ceres’ sacred oak; the goddess sends Famine into him, and his insatiable hunger devours his whole fortune until, having even sold his daughter, he consumes his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738-878 [10]
His pitiless act leads to a ruin that no amount of wealth can halt.
A rich man who lost compassion loses everything, beyond recovery, while the kural suggests mere poverty would have been survivable.
Philosophy
“if it often happens, this kind of separation, it makes it difficult for that which detaches itself to be brought to unity and to be restored to its former condition.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.8 (trans. George Long) [11]
Marcus compares a person who cuts himself off from others through hatred to a branch cut from a tree: it can be grafted back, but repeated separation makes restoration hard.
The same asymmetry as the kural: estrangement from fellow-feeling, once habitual, is rarely reversed.

Real Life Example

Henri Dunant, ruined and forgotten, receives the first Nobel Peace Prize
1867-1901 · Geneva and Heiden, Switzerland
Henri Dunant, whose efforts after the Battle of Solferino led to the founding of the Red Cross, went bankrupt in 1867, left Geneva and spent years in poverty and obscurity. A journalist found him living in a hospice in the village of Heiden in 1895, and in 1901 he shared the first Nobel Peace Prize with Frederic Passy, honoured for his humanitarian efforts to help wounded soldiers. The prize money was kept from his creditors and remained untouched in a Norwegian bank when he died in 1910.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dunant lost his fortune but never his compassion, and it was the compassion that the world finally honoured.
The kural says those who lose wealth may flourish again, but those who lose compassion are truly destitute, and Dunant, poor but rich in compassion, flourished again in the world's esteem.
