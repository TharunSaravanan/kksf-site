Kural 1290

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

கண்ணின் துனித்தே கலங்கினாள் புல்லுதல் என்னினும் தான்விதுப் புற்று.
Kannin thuniththae kalanginaal pulludhal enninum thaanvidhup putru.

Meaning

She showed she was upset only with her eyes. Then, wanting the hug even more than I did, she melted into my arms.

Elementary

Have you ever tried to look cross at someone you missed, only to break into a smile and give them a hug first? The husband in this poem says his wife did just that: her eyes looked upset, but she was quicker than him to hug.

University

Here the husband speaks, recalling a meeting: kaṇṇiṉ tuṉittē, ‘sulking (tuṉi, displeasure) only by/with the eyes’; kalaṅkiṉāḷ, ‘she was stirred, melted’; pullutal eṉṉiṉum tāṉ vituppuṟṟu, ‘she being more eager (vitumpu, the root of the chapter title) than I to embrace’. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan gloss that she sulked only as far as a glance, and then, wanting the embrace more eagerly than he did, forgot her displeasure and came into his arms; Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar read the same. The Sivayogi Sivakumar gloss on ytamizh takes kalaṅkiṉāḷ more literally as her eyes clouding with tears. By ending with the husband’s voice, the chapter confirms that the eagerness for reunion was mutual and that it overcame the convention of ūṭal on both sides. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Krishna reappears to the gopis after vanishing, one clasps his hand, another places his arm on her shoulder, while another, biting her lip with knitted brows, glares at him as if wounded by his absence, before all rejoice in his presence.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.32.4-8 (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Bhagavata depicts the gopis’ mingled reproach and eager welcome when Krishna returns.
The Kural’s wife likewise shows displeasure only with her eyes before rushing into her husband’s embrace.
Christianity
“And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him.”
— Luke 15:20 (KJV) [4]
In the parable, the father runs to embrace his returning son before any word of apology.
The Kural’s wife, too, outruns her partner in eagerness to embrace, any grievance forgotten.
Islam
In a hadith qudsi, God says that if His servant draws near Him by a hand’s span He draws near by an arm’s length, and if the servant comes walking He comes running.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7405 (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith pictures divine love outpacing human approach in eagerness.
The Kural’s wife is more eager than her husband to embrace; the hadith uses the same image of one party running ahead in love.
Judaism
“Scarce had I passed from them, when I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go.”
— Song of Songs 3:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The bride of the Song, finding her beloved after searching, clings to him.
The Kural’s wife, too, seizes the moment of reunion with an eagerness greater than her beloved’s.
Buddhism
When the Buddha first returned to Kapilavastu, Yasodhara stayed in her chamber, saying that if she had any worth he would come to her himself; when he came, she fell at his feet, clasped his ankles and laid her head upon them.
— Jataka Nidanakatha (paraphrase; cf. tr. T.W. Rhys Davids) [7]
The Jataka introduction tells of Yasodhara’s reserve giving way to an overwhelming welcome.
As in the Kural, a show of reserve dissolves into eager reunion, though Yasodhara’s greeting is reverential rather than marital.
Jainism
After Prince Aristanemi (Neminatha) turned back from his wedding to renounce the world, his bride Rajimati, instead of resenting him, chose to follow him by taking vows herself.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana tells how Rajimati’s love for Neminatha led her to follow him into the ascetic life.
The Kural’s wife overcomes her displeasure in eagerness to join her husband; Rajimati also follows her beloved, though into renunciation rather than embrace.
Sikhism
“The Gurmukhs are honored in the Court of the Lord. The Lord Himself takes them in His Loving Embrace.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 42, Sri Raag, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says the Lord Himself draws those who follow the Guru into His loving embrace.
The Kural's husband recalls how his wife, after a brief sulk with her eyes, melted into his arms more eagerly than he; Guru Ram Das likewise ends in the Beloved's loving embrace.
Greek Mythology
Penelope holds back from Odysseus, testing him coldly, until he describes their bed; then her knees and heart give way, and she runs to him, throws her arms around his neck and kisses him.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.205-208 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer shows Penelope’s reserve dissolving all at once into an eager embrace.
This is close to the Kural’s scene: displeasure shown first, then an embrace more eager than the husband’s.
Philosophy
Terence’s line that the quarrels of lovers are the renewal of love became a classic maxim of European moral writing.
— Terence, Andria 555 (paraphrase) [11]
The Roman playwright observes that lovers’ quarrels end by strengthening their love.
The Kural ends the chapter the same way: a brief glance of displeasure leads straight to a fuller reunion.

Real Life Example

Eleanor of Castile goes on crusade with Edward
1270–1273 · Acre, Kingdom of Jerusalem (now in Israel)
In August 1270 the future Edward I of England set out on crusade to the Holy Land, and his wife Eleanor of Castile went with him instead of staying behind. They reached Acre in 1271, and their daughter Joan, known as Joan of Acre, was born there about a year later. Eleanor travelled with Edward until 1273; the popular story that she saved his life by sucking poison from a dagger wound is regarded as legend.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rather than wait years at home, Eleanor chose to share a long and dangerous journey so as not to be parted from her husband.
The kural’s husband says his wife was even more eager than he for their embrace, and Eleanor’s choice to follow Edward across the world shows a wife whose eagerness for togetherness outran convention.
