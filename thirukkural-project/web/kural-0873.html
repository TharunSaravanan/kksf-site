Kural 873

Recognizing the Quality of Enmity · Pakaithirandherithal · பகைத்திறந்தெரிதல்

ஏமுற் றவரினும் ஏழை தமியனாய்ப் பல்லார் பகைகொள் பவன்.
Emur ravarinum ezhai thamiyanaaip pallaar pakaikol pavan.

Meaning

A person who is alone and still makes enemies of many people is more foolish than someone who has lost their mind.

Elementary

Picture one kid on the playground who has no friends on his side, yet goes around picking fights with every group. Even someone who is not thinking clearly would not do that. The poet says this is the silliest thing a person can do.

University

The comparison turns on ēmuṟṟavar (ஏமுற்றவர்), ‘those who are deluded or deranged’: Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah gloss them as the mad (பித்தர்), Kalaignar as the arrogant, while Sivayogi Sivakumar reads ‘the despised’. Ēḻai (ஏழை) here means ‘senseless, witless’, not ‘poor’, as all the commentators agree. Tamiyaṉāy (‘being alone’) and pallār (‘many’) set up the arithmetic: a man without allies who collects many enemies has made the one choice even a madman might avoid. Pappaiah applies it directly to a ruler (ஆட்சியாளன்), in keeping with the Porutpal setting. The text matches in both ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Ravana rejected the counsel of his brother Vibhishana to return Sita, insulted him, and drove him out; Vibhishana went over to Rama, leaving Ravana isolated against a growing alliance.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (cantos 9-16) [3]
Ravana’s pride turned his own kin into enemies while he already faced a powerful foe.
He is the Kural’s figure of folly: alone, and multiplying his enemies.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand?”
— Luke 14:31 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses the image of a prudent king who counts his forces before taking on a stronger enemy.
The Kural condemns exactly the one who skips this reckoning and takes on many while alone.
Islam
The Qur’an tells believers not to fall into disputes among themselves, lest they lose courage and their strength depart.
— Qur’an 8:46 [5]
Internal quarrels are presented as a direct cause of weakness and defeat.
Both texts link isolation and many quarrels with the loss of strength.
Judaism
“For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up.”
— Ecclesiastes 4:10 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Kohelet observes that the solitary person has no one to rely on when trouble comes.
The Kural’s lone man facing many enemies is exactly the one Kohelet pities.
Buddhism
In the Sammodamana Jataka, a flock of quail escape a fowler’s net by lifting it together, but once they begin quarrelling they no longer cooperate and the fowler catches them.
— Sammodamana Jataka (Jataka No. 33) [6]
Unity protects; quarrelling leaves each bird alone and easily caught.
The Kural’s lone man with many enemies is in the position of the quarrelling quail.
Jainism
“A cruel man does cruel acts and is thereby involved in other cruelties; but sinful undertakings will in the end bring about misery.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.8.7, with Jacobi's note (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
Jacobi's note glosses the 'cruel man' (veri) as vairin, literally 'one who bears enmity'. The verse says such a man becomes caught in ever-widening hostility that ends in his own misery.
The Kural calls the lone man who makes many enemies worse than a madman, and the Jain verse shows how the man of enmity piles up further hostilities until they ruin him.
Sikhism
“O egotism, you are the root of birth and death and the cycle of reincarnation; you are the very soul of sin. You forsake friends, and hold tight to enemies.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sahaskriti (salok 49), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan addresses ahankar, pride, as the root of sin that makes a person drop friends and cling to enemies.
The Kural calls the man who stands alone yet makes many enemies more foolish than a madman; Guru Arjan names the ego as the folly that drives friends away and gathers enemies.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the father and his sons, a bundle of sticks cannot be broken while bound together, but each stick snaps easily on its own.
— Aesop’s Fables, The Father and His Sons (tr. G. F. Townsend) [9]
Strength lies in having others with you; alone, anyone is easily broken.
The Kural’s solitary man who takes on many is the single stick.
Philosophy
Socrates argues that even a band of thieves cannot accomplish anything if its members wrong one another, and that injustice makes a person at odds with others and even with himself.
— Plato, Republic 351c-352a [10]
Plato shows that hostility on all sides makes effective action impossible.
The Kural similarly treats making enemies everywhere as self-defeating folly.

Real Life Example

Paraguay fights the Triple Alliance
1864-1870 · Paraguay and the Rio de la Plata region, South America
Under Francisco Solano Lopez, Paraguay became entangled in disputes with Brazil and Argentina and in Uruguay's civil war, seized Brazilian vessels, attacked Brazilian territory and declared war on Argentina. Argentina, Brazil and Uruguay then formed an alliance against Paraguay in 1865, and the small country fought all three for five years. By the war's end in 1870 Paraguay lay in ruins, having lost more than half of its population and large areas of territory.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A small state without allies that turned three neighbours against it at once suffered one of the most devastating defeats in modern history.
The kural calls the one who stands alone yet makes enemies of many more senseless than a madman, and Paraguay's war against three neighbours shows the cost of that choice.
