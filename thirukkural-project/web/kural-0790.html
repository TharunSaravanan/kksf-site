Kural 790

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

இனையர் இவரெமக்கு இன்னம்யாம் என்று புனையினும் புல்லென்னும் நட்பு.
Inaiyar ivaremakku innamyaam endru punaiyinum pullennum natpu.

Meaning

When friends go around saying, “He means this much to me, and I mean that much to him,” the friendship becomes cheap, however nicely they praise it.

Elementary

Imagine two kids who keep telling everyone, ‘I’m her best friend, and I gave her THREE presents, and she’s SO lucky to have me!’ It starts to sound like showing off, and the friendship feels less special. Real friends don’t keep score or brag about it; they just are friends.

University

Inaiyar (இனையர்) means ‘such-and-such persons’ and innam (இன்னம்) ‘such-and-such’; the friend who says ‘he is so much to us, we are so much to him’ is measuring and advertising the friendship. Punaiyinum (புனையினும், ‘even if one adorns or praises it’) and pullennum (புல்லென்னும், ‘it looks mean or paltry’, from pul, grass, a common figure for worthlessness) complete the thought. Parimelazhagar reads the couplet as a warning that true friends do not set their mutual services side by side or display their closeness, because keeping such accounts belongs to traders, not friends. The chapter thus ends where it began, by setting real friendship above any outward show. Both web sources give the same text; இவரெமக்கு is இவர் எமக்கு joined by sandhi.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Sudama, Krishna’s poor boyhood friend, visits him at Dwaraka, he is too shy to ask for anything and offers only a handful of flattened rice; Krishna receives him with great love, mentions no favour, and Sudama returns home to find his hut quietly turned into a palace.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.80-81 [3]
Neither friend counts or announces what the other means to him: Sudama asks for nothing, and Krishna gives without a word.
Their unspoken, unmeasured friendship is the opposite of the boasting the Kural warns against.
Christianity
“He that blesseth his friend with a loud voice, rising early in the morning, it shall be counted a curse to him.”
— Proverbs 27:14 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says loud, showy praise of a friend backfires and is taken as a curse.
This is the Kural’s point exactly: advertising a friendship cheapens it.
Islam
“A man praised another man in front of the Prophet. The Prophet said to him, ‘Woe to you, you have cut off your companion’s neck, you have cut off your companion’s neck,’ repeating it several times”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2662 [5]
The Prophet warns that lavish praise of a companion harms him, and that such praise should be cautious and modest.
Both texts teach that over-praising a friend does damage rather than honour.
Judaism
Shammai teaches that one should say little and do much, and receive everyone with a cheerful face.
— Pirkei Avot 1:15 [6]
The Mishnah prefers quiet deeds to many words.
The Kural’s true friendship is likewise shown in deeds rather than declared in words.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who renders lip-service be understood as a foe in the guise of a friend: (i) he makes friendly profession as regards the past, (ii) he makes friendly profession as regards the future, (iii) he tries to gain one’s favor by empty words, (iv) when opportunity for service has arisen, he expresses his inability.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha calls the person who keeps declaring friendship, past and future, but fails when needed, a foe dressed as a friend.
This is the Kural’s talking-up of friendship, shown to be hollow.
Jainism
“If after contracting a firm friendship with any one, I set myself to note his good and bad qualities, may I be cast into the hell where the traitor who discovers the secrets of his friend is punished, and may I be scoffed at by the whole world!”
— Naladiyar, ch. 23 'The bearing with the faults of others', stanza 10 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The speaker swears never to audit a friend once a firm friendship has been formed, treating such reckoning as a betrayal.
The Kural says friendship becomes cheap when friends go around measuring what each is to the other, and the Naladiyar likewise treats keeping score inside a friendship as unworthy of it.
Sikhism
“O Nanak! He alone is called a lover, who remains forever immersed in absorption. But one who feels good only when good is done for him, and feels bad when things go badly - do not call him a lover. He trades only for his own account.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad says that love which keeps track of what it receives is not love but trade.
The Kural says friendship cheapens when friends measure and advertise what each is to the other; Guru Angad likewise calls love that keeps accounts mere trading.
Greek Mythology
Homer never has Achilles and Patroclus boast of what they are to each other; their bond shows in deeds, as when Patroclus puts on Achilles’ armour to save the Greeks, and Achilles speaks of it only in grief, after Patroclus is dead.
— Homer, Iliad 16 and 18 [10]
The greatest friendship in Greek epic is told almost entirely through action, not through declarations.
This is the Kural’s ideal: a friendship that needs no announcing.
Philosophy
“there can be nothing more utterly subversive of friendship than flattery, adulation, and base compliance.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 25 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Laelius names flattery and empty praise as the greatest destroyers of friendship.
Cicero agrees with the Kural that the decorated words of friends cheapen what they praise.

Real Life Example

Hardy and Littlewood agree never to keep score
c. 1912 · Cambridge, England, United Kingdom
From about 1911 the Cambridge mathematicians G. H. Hardy and J. E. Littlewood began a collaboration that lasted some 35 years. According to their colleague Harald Bohr, they agreed on a set of informal rules, which Mary Cartwright believed date from 1912: neither was obliged to answer or even read the other's letters, it did not matter whether what they wrote to each other was right or wrong, and every joint paper would carry both names regardless of whether one of them had contributed anything to it.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By refusing to keep accounts of who did what, the two built one of the most productive partnerships in the history of mathematics.
The kural warns that friends who keep count of what each is to the other cheapen their friendship, and Hardy and Littlewood made not keeping count a formal rule.
