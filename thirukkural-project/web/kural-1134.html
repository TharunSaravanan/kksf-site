Kural 1134

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

காமக் கடும்புனல் உய்க்கும் நாணொடு நல்லாண்மை என்னும் புணை.
Kaamak kadumpunal uykkum naanodu nallaanmai ennum punai.

Meaning

The wild flood of love sweeps away the raft made of modesty and a man's honor.

Elementary

Imagine you are floating safely on a little raft. Then a huge flood rushes in and carries the raft away. The poet says love is like that flood, and being careful and well-behaved is the raft it sweeps off.

University

The image is a rūpakam (extended metaphor): kāmak kaṭum-puṉal (காமக் கடும்புனல்), 'the fierce torrent of love', against puṇai (புணை), a raft or float, which here is made of two things, nāṇ (modesty) and nal-āṇmai (honourable manliness), both named in the previous couplet. Uykkum (உய்க்கும்) means 'carries off, drives along'. Commentators (Parimelazhagar's line, Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah) all take it as the hero's explanation of why the qualities he once had could not save him; Puliyur Kesikan adds an exclamation, 'what shall I do!'. Pope: 'Love's rushing tide will sweep away the raft / Of seemly manliness and shame combined.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Just as a strong wind sweeps a boat off its chartered course on the water, even one of the senses on which the mind focuses can lead the intellect astray.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.67 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna warns that a single sense, once the mind follows it, can carry away even a wise person's judgment.
Both texts use the image of a small vessel carried off by a force on the water to describe desire overpowering self-control.
Christianity
“Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it.”
— Song of Solomon 8:7 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs says no flood can put out love.
The Kural turns the image around: love is itself the flood, and it is reserve that drowns.
Islam
The Prophet is reported to have said that the Fire is surrounded by desires and passions, while Paradise is surrounded by things the self dislikes.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6487 (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith teaches that unchecked desire draws a person easily toward harm, while restraint is hard.
The Kural's torrent of love sweeping off the raft of restraint shows how easily desire can overpower a person.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that the evil inclination is at first like a spider's thread and in the end like a cart rope.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 52a (paraphrase) [6]
Rabbinic teaching describes desire as something that starts slight and grows until it holds a person fast.
The Kural's 'fierce torrent' is love at the end of that process, when it has grown strong enough to sweep restraint away.
Buddhism
“There is no fire like lust; there is no grip like hatred; there is no net like delusion; there is no river like craving.”
— Dhammapada 251 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha names craving as the strongest of rivers, one that carries beings along.
The Kural's 'torrent of love' is just such a river, sweeping off the raft of restraint.
Jainism
“If you fall in love with every woman you see, you will be without hold like the Haṭha-plant, driven before the wind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.44 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati warns Rathanemi that a person carried by desire has no anchor and drifts like a floating water-plant.
The Kural's image of restraint swept away by a flood matches this picture of a self driven helplessly by passion.
Sikhism
“You were not able to make yourself a raft when you should have. When the ocean is churning and over-flowing, then it is very difficult to cross over it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 794, Raag Soohee, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid warns that once the waters rise and churn it is too late to build the raft that would carry one across.
The Kural pictures the fierce flood of love sweeping away the raft made of modesty and manliness; Farid uses the same raft-and-flood image for a torrent too strong to cross.
Greek Mythology
Leander swims the Hellespont every night to reach Hero, guided by her lamp, until one stormy night the waves overwhelm him and he drowns.
— Ovid, Heroides 18-19 (Leander and Hero) (paraphrase) [10]
In Ovid's Heroides and Musaeus' poem, Leander's love drives him into waters that finally carry him off.
The Kural's 'fierce flood' takes literal form in the strait that sweeps the lover away.
Philosophy
Aristotle compares the person overcome by passion to someone asleep, mad or drunk, who has knowledge but cannot use it at that moment.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.3 (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle, akrasia (weakness of will) is not ignorance but knowledge swept aside by feeling.
The Kural's lover still knows the worth of modesty, but the torrent of love has carried it away.

Real Life Example

Emperor Xuanzong's devotion to Yang Guifei and the An Lushan Rebellion
c. 741-756 · Chang'an (now Xi'an), Tang China
Emperor Xuanzong of Tang, whose early reign brought reforms and the start of a golden age, came under the influence of his consorts in later life, above all Yang Guifei, and withdrew more and more from government while her relatives gained high posts. In 755 the general An Lushan rebelled, and Xuanzong fled the capital Chang'an with Yang Guifei and her family. During the flight his own troops demanded her death, the emperor consented, and he soon gave up the throne to his son.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A ruler once known for wise government let attachment carry away his judgement and his sense of duty, with grave results for his empire.
The kural says the fierce flood of love sweeps away the raft of restraint and honour; Xuanzong's passion swept away the careful rule that had once made his reign famous.
