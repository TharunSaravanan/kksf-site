Kural 1126

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

கண்ணுள்ளின் போகார் இமைப்பின் பருகுவரா நுண்ணியர்எம் காத லவர்.
Kannullin poakaar imaippin paruguvaraa nunniyarem kaadha lavar.

Meaning

My lover never leaves my eyes. Even when I blink, he is not hurt, because he is so fine and delicate.

Elementary

Imagine keeping a picture of someone you love in your mind so clearly that it stays even when you blink. Blinking can't hurt that picture because it is not made of anything solid. The girl in this poem says her love lives in her eyes like that.

University

With this couplet the voice passes to the heroine (talaivi), who answers her companion's worry that the hero is far away: he is always in her eyes. Kannullin poagaar (கண்ணுள்ளின் போகார், 'he does not leave from within my eyes') is followed by imaippin paruguvaraa (இமைப்பின் பருகுவரா, 'he does not suffer if I blink'), and nunniyar (நுண்ணியர், 'subtle, fine') explains why: he is too fine to be crushed by the eyelids. Mu. Varadarasanar, Pulavar Kesikan and Kalaignar all read paruguvaraa as 'will not be pained'; Solomon Pappaiah adds that 'others cannot perceive his subtle nature'. Pope takes it differently, 'I wink them not, lest I should pain him where he lies', which anticipates the not-blinking motif of 1129. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In a well-known bhajan, Mirabai asks Krishna, the son of Nanda, to come and dwell in her eyes.
— Mirabai, bhajan 'Baso more nainan mein Nandalal' (paraphrase) [3]
The bhakti poet speaks of her divine beloved as living within her sight at every moment.
The Kural's heroine says her lover never leaves her eyes; Mira makes the same request of Krishna.
Christianity
“I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go”
— Song of Solomon 3:4 (KJV) [4]
The woman in the Song of Songs finds her beloved after searching for him through the night and refuses to let him leave.
Like the Kural's heroine, she keeps her beloved close and will not let him go.
Islam
“To worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you cannot achieve this state of devotion then you must consider that He is looking at you.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 50 [5]
The Prophet defines ihsan, the highest level of faith, as living as if the unseen Beloved were before one's eyes.
The Kural's heroine keeps her absent lover always before her eyes; ihsan asks the believer to keep God there.
Judaism
“I have set the LORD always before me; Surely He is at my right hand, I shall not be moved.”
— Psalm 16:8 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist keeps God continually before his eyes; the verse is often displayed in synagogues as a shiviti.
The Kural's heroine keeps her beloved always within her eyes; the psalmist does the same with God.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada traces anxiety for others to the affection we feel for them.
The Kural's heroine worries that even her blinking might hurt her beloved; the Buddha would see that tender fear as born of endearment.
Jainism
Jain philosophy classes the soul, like space and the principles of motion and rest, among the formless (arupa) substances; only matter has form, so the soul itself cannot be struck or crushed.
— Tattvartha Sutra 5.4-5 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Nathmal Tatia) [8]
Because the soul has no material form, physical contact cannot injure it, even though the body it inhabits can be hurt.
The heroine says her lover is too subtle to be harmed by her eyelids, which a Jain would say is literally true of the soul.
Sikhism
“Kabeer: with my eyes, I see You, Lord; with my ears, I hear Your Name.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1370, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir asks that his eyes behold only the Lord, so that the Beloved fills his sight wherever he looks.
The Kural's heroine says her lover never leaves her eyes; Kabir likewise keeps the Beloved always before his eyes.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that from the eyes of the three Graces, as they glanced, flowed love that loosens the limbs, and that their glance from beneath their brows was beautiful.
— Hesiod, Theogony 907-911 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hugh G. Evelyn-White) [10]
In Greek myth love itself is thought to pass through and dwell in the eyes.
The Kural's heroine keeps her lover in her eyes; Hesiod makes the eyes the very fountain of love.
Philosophy
Lucretius argues that the mind and soul are made of extremely small, smooth, round particles, so fine that when they leave the body at death its shape and weight seem unchanged.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura III.177-230 (paraphrase) [11]
The Epicurean poet explains the subtlety of the soul by the fineness of its matter.
The Kural's lover is nunniyar, so subtle that blinking cannot hurt him; Lucretius gives a physical account of such subtlety.

Real Life Example

Calpurnia keeps Pliny's writings in his place
early 2nd century CE · Roman Italy
The Roman senator and author Pliny the Younger wrote letters to his young wife Calpurnia while they were apart, and some were included in his published collection of letters. In one of them (Letters 6.7) he replies to her complaint that she misses him terribly and comforts herself by embracing his writings instead of him and putting them in the place where he usually sits. He answers that he rereads her letters again and again, and asks her to write as often as she can.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Pliny's letters to Calpurnia are among the rare surviving glimpses of affection inside a Roman marriage.
The kural's heroine says her lover never leaves her eyes, so fine that even blinking cannot harm him; Calpurnia, too, kept her absent husband constantly before her by placing his words where he used to be.
