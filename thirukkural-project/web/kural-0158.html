Kural 158

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

மிகுதியான் மிக்கவை செய்தாரைத் தாந்தம் தகுதியான் வென்று விடல்.
Migudhiyaan mikkavai seydhaaraith thaandham, Thagudhiyaan vendru vidal.

Meaning

When proud people do you great wrongs because they think they are above you, beat them with your own goodness, which means your patience.

Elementary

Sometimes a bully acts big and mean, and you might think the only way to win is to be even meaner. This kural says you can beat them another way: by staying calm and good. Your patience works like a superpower that makes their meanness look small.

University

The couplet plays on மிகுதி ('excess, arrogance') and தகுதி ('fitness, worth'). The aggressor acts out of surplus (மிகுதியான் மிக்கவை, 'excessive deeds out of excess'), and is to be overcome by the victim's own proper measure (தாந்தம் தகுதியான்). Commentators consistently identify this தகுதி with பொறை, patience itself, so forbearance becomes a strategy for victory rather than passive defeat. The verb, வென்று விடல் ('conquer'), is deliberately martial: Valluvar claims the vocabulary of winning for the person who doesn't retaliate. The same logic appears in Paul's 'overcome evil with good,' in the Qur'an's 'repel evil with what is better,' and in Vidura's maxim that anger is conquered by forgiveness, a maxim that reappears almost word for word in the Dhammapada (v. 223).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Anger must be conquered by forgiveness; and the wicked must be conquered by honesty; the miser must be conquered by liberality, and falsehood must be conquered by truth.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXIX, Vidura's counsel (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Vidura teaches that each vice is defeated by its opposite virtue, and anger above all by forgiveness.
As in the kural, the aggressor is 'conquered' not by force but by one's own virtue of forbearance.
Christianity
“Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.”
— Romans 12:21 (KJV) [5]
Paul closes his teaching on non-retaliation by telling believers to defeat evil by doing good, not by answering in kind.
The same reversal of victory: the wronged person wins through goodness, not counter-attack.
Islam
“And not equal are the good deed and the bad. Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon, the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend.”
— Qur'an 41:34 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an tells believers to push back evil with something better, and promises that this can turn an enemy into a close friend.
Repelling the aggressor with better conduct is the kural's victory through one's own worth.
Judaism
“Others say: [Who is mighty?] Who turns an enemy into his friend.”
— Avot de-Rabbi Natan 23:1 (trans. A. Cohen, Soncino) [7]
Expanding Ben Zoma's 'Who is mighty?', Avot de-Rabbi Natan records the view that the mightiest person is the one who wins over his enemy and makes him a friend.
Victory through character rather than force is the kural's 'conquer them by your own worth'.
Buddhism
“Overcome the angry by non-anger; overcome the wicked by goodness; overcome the miser by generosity; overcome the liar by truth.”
— Dhammapada, verse 223 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada teaches that every kind of wrongdoer is defeated by the opposite virtue, the angry by non-anger.
This is the kural's teaching exactly: conquer the aggressor with your own virtue. It also echoes Vidura's Mahabharata maxim almost word for word.
Jainism
“When persons not our equals say unfitting things, to bear and to be still is worthy conduct. The world girt by foaming waters regards not a contrary course as praise-worthy, but as discreditable.”
— Nālaṭiyār 72 (ch. 8, 'Patience'), tr. G. U. Pope (1893); e-text at Project Madurai [9]
The Nālaṭiyār's chapter on patience says that the fitting answer to insolence is to bear it calmly, and that striking back only brings discredit in the world's eyes.
Valluvar tells us to overcome the arrogant through our own worth (தகுதி), meaning patience, and the Nālaṭiyār uses the same word: bearing such insults quietly is 'worthy conduct' (தகுதி).
Sikhism
“Humility is my spiked club. … No evil-doer can withstand these weapons.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 628, Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan describes humility as a weapon before which no wrongdoer can stand.
The Kural tells us to conquer the arrogant by our own worth, that is, by patience, and uses the language of victory; Guru Arjan likewise turns humility into a weapon that defeats the evil-doer.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus' Eumenides, the raging Furies threaten to poison Athens after Orestes is acquitted. Athena doesn't fight them. She calms them with patient persuasion and offers them honor, until they become the 'Kindly Ones.'
— Aeschylus, Eumenides (final scene) [11]
Athena wins over the enraged goddesses through patience and persuasion (Peitho), not force, and turns them from enemies into protectors.
Overcoming aggressors through one's own character rather than retaliation is the kural's victory by தகுதி.
Philosophy
Spinoza argues that a person who lives by reason tries, as far as possible, to repay others' hatred, anger and contempt with love and generosity, because hatred grows when returned and can be destroyed by love.
— Spinoza, Ethics, Part IV, Proposition 46 and proof [12]
For Spinoza, answering hostility with love is not weakness but the rational way to defeat hatred.
Defeating the aggressor through one's own better disposition is the kural's தகுதியான் வென்று விடல்.

Real Life Example

The satyagrahis at the Dharasana salt works
21 May 1930 · Dharasana, Gujarat, India
After Gandhi was arrested for announcing a march on the Dharasana salt works, the poet and Congress leader Sarojini Naidu led about 2,500 volunteers there on 21 May 1930. Column after column of marchers walked toward the police guarding the works and were beaten down with lathis, but as instructed they did not strike back or even raise their arms to ward off the blows. The American journalist Webb Miller's eyewitness report was carried by newspapers around the world and prompted an international outcry against British policy in India.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The marchers lost the confrontation physically but won it morally, because their refusal to retaliate exposed the force used against them to the world.
The kural urges overcoming those who heap outrageous wrongs on us through our own worth, that is, through patience; the satyagrahis turned forbearance into a form of victory.
