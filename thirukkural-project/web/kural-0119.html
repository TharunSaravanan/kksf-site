Kural 119

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

சொற்கோட்டம் இல்லது செப்பம் ஒருதலையா உட்கோட்டம் இன்மை பெறின்.
Sorkottam illadhu seppam orudhalaiyaa Utkottam inmai perin.

Meaning

Fair words are truly fair only when the heart is also firmly fair and does not secretly take one side.

Elementary

A shiny apple can still be rotten inside. Saying fair-sounding words isn’t enough; your heart has to be fair on the inside too, and then your words will be truly straight.

University

The couplet pairs சொற்கோட்டம் (“crookedness of word”) with உட்கோட்டம் (“inner crookedness”) and makes the first depend on the second: straight speech is செப்பம் (rectitude) only if one ஒருதலையா (“definitely, without fail”) has an unbent inner state. The target is the sophisticated partiality of someone who says the right things while privately favouring one side. That concern fits a chapter aimed at those who arbitrate, give testimony or advise. Commentators read it both ways. Varadarasanar treats inner straightness as the condition, while Pappaiah and Karunanidhi treat straight speech as its natural result. The same principle, that honest speech flows from an honest heart, appears in Jesus’ “out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh,” Achilles’ contempt for the man “who says one thing while he hides another in his heart,” and Plato’s distinction between the lie in words and the “true lie” in the soul.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking words that are truthful, pleasing, beneficial, and not agitating to others, and also in regularly reciting Vedic literature.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 [4]
The Gita treats right speech as a discipline (tapas) grouped with discipline of mind and body in the neighbouring verses.
Both treat truthful, straight speech as a moral discipline linked to inner purity.
Christianity
“for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh.”
— Matthew 12:34 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that words reveal and flow from what fills the heart.
The same dependence the Kural states: straight words need a straight heart.
Islam
“So follow not [personal] inclination, lest you not be just. And if you distort [your testimony] or refuse [to give it], then indeed Allah is ever, with what you do, Acquainted.”
— Qur’an 4:135 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an links inner inclination (hawā) with distorted testimony, and warns that God sees both.
Inner bias and twisted words are named together, the Kural’s utkottam and sorkottam.
Judaism
“He that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart.”
— Psalm 15:2 (KJV) [5]
The psalm describes the person fit to dwell with God as one whose truthfulness goes all the way into the heart.
“Speaketh the truth in his heart” is close to the Kural’s requirement that straightness be internal as well as spoken.
Buddhism
“Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with a pure mind a person speaks or acts happiness follows him like his never-departing shadow.”
— Dhammapada 2 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada opens by teaching that the mind comes first and speech and action follow from it.
Both locate the source of straight speech in the state of the mind.
Jainism
“Crooked and deceptive actions of mind, speach and body, and criticising the right actions and prompting for wrong actions cause the influx of inauspicious physique-making karmas.” The next sutra adds: “The opposites of these (namely straightforward activity and honestry or candour) cause the influx of auspicious physique-making karmas.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.22-23 (English rendering on jainworld.com) [8]
Umāsvāti names crookedness (yoga-vakratā) as a single fault running through mind, speech and body together, and its cure as straightforwardness (ārjava) in all three.
Valluvar says straight speech is true rectitude only if the heart is also unbent, and the Tattvārtha Sūtra likewise treats crookedness of mind and of speech as one fault to be removed together.
Sikhism
“They alone are true, whose love for God is deep and heart-felt. Those who have one thing in their heart, and something else in their mouth, are judged to be false.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 488, Raag Aasaa, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, condemns the gap between heart and mouth.
The Kural says straight speech counts as fairness only when the heart itself is free of crookedness; Farid says the same split between inner and outer marks a person as false.
Greek Mythology
“Him do I hate even as the gates of hell who says one thing while he hides another in his heart; therefore I will say what I mean.”
— Homer, Iliad 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Achilles rejects Agamemnon’s envoys, declaring that he despises anyone whose words differ from their heart.
The gap between heart and speech that the Kural condemns, named in Homer.
Philosophy
“the true lie, if such an expression may be allowed, is hated of gods and men” … “the lie in words is only a kind of imitation and shadowy image of a previous affection of the soul”
— Plato, Republic II, 382a–c (trans. Jowett) [11]
Socrates distinguishes the lie in words from the “true lie” in the soul, and says spoken falsehood is only a shadow of a prior distortion inside.
The Kural has the same two layers: crookedness in word depends on crookedness within.

Real Life Example

The secret file at Alfred Dreyfus's court-martial
December 1894 · Paris, France
In autumn 1894 French intelligence found that a secret military document had been sent to the German military attache in Paris, and Captain Alfred Dreyfus, the only Jewish officer on the army General Staff, was arrested on the basis of handwriting analysis. At his court-martial the war ministry placed before the judges a file of secret, and in some cases forged, documents that his lawyer was not allowed to see, and he was convicted of treason. Evidence later pointed to another officer, Ferdinand Walsin-Esterhazy, and in July 1906 the verdict against Dreyfus was set aside and he was exonerated.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The trial had the outward form of military justice, but the outcome was driven by hidden evidence and, according to the US Holocaust Memorial Museum, by anti-Jewish prejudice.
The kural says fair words count as fairness only when the heart is free of bias; the Dreyfus court-martial shows a correct-looking procedure corrupted by crooked intent within.
