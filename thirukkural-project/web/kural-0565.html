Kural 565

Absence of Terrorism · Veruvandha Seyyaamai · வெருவந்த செய்யாமை

அருஞ்செவ்வி இன்னா முகத்தான் பெருஞ்செல்வம் பேஎய்கண் டன்னது உடைத்து.
Arunjevvi innaa mugaththaan perunjelvam Paeey kandannadhu udaiththu.

Meaning

The great riches of a ruler who is hard to meet and who greets people with an unfriendly face are like treasure guarded by a demon. They are scary and help no one.

Elementary

Imagine a huge toy box that belongs to someone who never opens the door and scowls at everyone who knocks. Nobody gets to play with the toys, so it’s as if a monster were sitting on them. The kural says a leader who can’t be reached and always frowns makes his riches useless.

University

Two faults are named: அருஞ்செவ்வி, rare or difficult audience (செவ்வி = an opportune moment of access), and இன்னா முகம், a disagreeable countenance. The simile பேஎய் கண்டு அன்னது is read in the commentarial tradition as wealth that a pēy (demon, ghoul) has seen or seized: it exists but inspires only fear and benefits no one, which is why Pope renders ‘blasted by demon’s glance’ and Pappaiah ‘held by a bhūta’. The couplet widens the chapter’s idea of dread from punishment to everyday demeanour: an unapproachable, scowling ruler terrifies without lifting a rod. The spelling பேஎய் preserves the classical alapedai (vowel prolongation) for metre, and both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“When in the court, he shall never cause his petitioners to wait at the door, for when a king makes himself inaccessible to his people and entrusts his work to his immediate officers, he may be sure to engender confusion in business, and to cause thereby public disaffection, and himself a prey to his enemies.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.19 (trans. R. Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya makes accessibility a core royal duty, warning that an unreachable king breeds disaffection and falls to his enemies.
‘அருஞ்செவ்வி’ (rare audience) is precisely the inaccessibility the Arthashastra condemns.
Christianity
“There was in a city a judge, which feared not God, neither regarded man: And there was a widow in that city; and she came unto him, saying, Avenge me of mine adversary. And he would not for a while.”
— Luke 18:2–4 (KJV) [4]
Jesus’ parable describes a judge who has the power to help but will not hear the needy, contrasting him with a God who listens.
The unjust judge who will not give audience is the Kural’s அருஞ்செவ்வி ruler.
Islam
“No Imam closes his door on one in need, dire straits and poverty, except that Allah closes the gates of the Heavens from his dire straits, his needs, and his poverty.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 1332 [5]
On hearing this, Mu‘awiya appointed a man to look after the people’s needs so that access would not be blocked.
A ruler who shuts his door is the Kural’s hard-to-reach king, and his wealth becomes spiritually useless to him.
Judaism
“And Absalom said unto him, See, thy matters are good and right; but there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee.”
— 2 Samuel 15:3 (KJV) [4]
Absalom stands at the city gate exploiting the fact that King David has become hard to reach, and so ‘steals the hearts’ of Israel.
An unreachable king loses his people’s loyalty, which is the danger the Kural points to.
Buddhism
“At any time, whether I am eating, in the women's quarters, the bed chamber, the chariot, the palanquin, in the park or wherever, reporters are to be posted with instructions to report to me the affairs of the people so that I might attend to these affairs wherever I am.”
— Ashoka, Rock Edict VI (trans. S. Dhammika) [6]
Ashoka deliberately made himself reachable at every hour so that the people’s business never waited on his convenience.
Ashoka is the historical opposite of the Kural’s அருஞ்செவ்வி king.
Jainism
Jain tradition holds that after attaining omniscience a Tirthankara preaches in the samavasarana, a hall open to all, where gods, humans and animals gather to hear him, each understanding the sermon in their own language.
— Samavasarana in Jain tradition (summary in Wikipedia, 'Samavasarana') [7]
The samavasarana ('refuge to all') presents the ideal teacher as someone everyone can approach, with no being turned away.
Valluvar condemns the ruler who is hard to see and scowls at those who come; the Jain image of the Jina's open assembly is its opposite, a presence anyone can reach.
Sikhism
Guru Amar Das made it a rule that anyone who wished to see him had first to eat in the casteless langar, sitting with everyone else, and it was said that even the Mughal emperor Akbar ate there and sought his advice.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, ‘Guru Amar Das’ (paraphrase) [8]
The Guru's presence was open to all comers on equal terms, through a shared meal rather than through rank or favour.
The Kural says the wealth of a ruler who is hard to see and harsh of face is like treasure guarded by a demon; the Sikh tradition made access to the Guru open and equal for every visitor, ruler and commoner alike.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod tells of the terrible serpent, offspring of Ceto and Phorcys, that guards the golden apples in a secret place at the ends of the earth, a treasure that no one may approach.
— Hesiod, Theogony 333–335 [9]
The dragon-guarded hoard (later named Ladon) is wealth that exists but benefits no one, because terror surrounds it.
The Kural’s ‘wealth seen by a demon’ is the same image: riches made useless by the fearsome guardian sitting on them.
Philosophy
Aristotle advises that a ruler who wishes to preserve his rule should appear to his subjects not harsh but dignified, inspiring respect rather than fear, and should seem a guardian of the public treasury rather than its owner.
— Aristotle, Politics 5.11 (1314a–1315a) [10]
Aristotle links the ruler’s demeanour and his handling of wealth directly to the stability of his rule.
The Kural condemns the harsh face and the hoarded, unusable wealth; Aristotle recommends the dignified face and wealth held in trust.

Real Life Example

Emperor Rudolf II withdraws into Prague Castle with his collections
c. 1600-1612 · Prague, Bohemia (now Czech Republic)
Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II moved his court to Prague in 1583, and in later years, subject to severe depression, he lived in seclusion there among his vast collections of art, curiosities and scientific instruments. The Habsburg archdukes, dissatisfied with his government, forced him in 1608 to give up Hungary, Austria and Moravia to his brother Matthias. In 1611 Matthias's army virtually held him prisoner in Prague until he yielded Bohemia as well, and he died there in January 1612.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Rudolf owned one of the greatest treasure collections in Europe, yet as he grew more withdrawn and hard to reach, his family and subjects turned to his brother and his power slipped away.
The kural likens the wealth of a ruler who is hard to see to treasure held by a demon, and Rudolf's famous collections, locked away with a secluded emperor, did nothing to keep his crowns.
