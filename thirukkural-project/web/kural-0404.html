Kural 404

Ignorance · Kallaamai · கல்லாமை

கல்லாதான் ஒட்பம் கழியநன் றாயினும் கொள்ளார் அறிவுடை யார்.
Kallaadhaan otpam kazhiyanan raayinum Kollaar arivudai yaar.

Meaning

Even if an uneducated person’s cleverness happens to be very good, wise people will not accept it as reliable.

Elementary

Sometimes a person who never studied says something that sounds really smart. Wise people still don’t rely on it, because it’s a lucky hit, not something the person truly understands. Next time the same person may be completely wrong, and they won’t know the difference.

University

Otpam denotes native sharpness or cleverness; kazhiya nandru (‘exceedingly good’) concedes that it may even be impressive. Yet the wise (arivudaiyaar) do not ‘take’ (kollaar) it. Parimelazhagar’s explanation is epistemic: insight not grounded in learning is accidental and unreliable, so it cannot be built upon. This anticipates Plato’s distinction in the Meno between true opinion and knowledge — right opinions are valuable but ‘run away’ unless tied down by reasoning, like the statues of Daedalus. The kural is harder-edged than some traditions would allow: Pirkei Avot’s ‘look not at the vessel but at what is in it’ pushes the other way, and Cassandra’s myth dramatizes the tragedy of truth rejected because of its source. The accuracy notes below mark these as contrasts.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhagavad Gita 4.34 instructs the seeker to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master with humility, inquiry and service, because those who have seen the truth can impart knowledge.
— Bhagavad Gita 4.34 [4]
Real knowledge is to be received from those who have studied and realized it, not picked up from anyone who sounds clever.
Both locate reliable insight in those grounded in learning, which is why the wise do not rely on the unlearned.
Christianity
“The legs of the lame are not equal: so is a parable in the mouth of fools.”
— Proverbs 26:7 (KJV) [5]
A wise saying in the mouth of a fool is as unsteady as a lame person’s walk; it doesn’t carry him anywhere.
Even a good saying from one without understanding is not something to lean on — the Kural’s very point.
Islam
“O you who have believed, if there comes to you a disobedient one with information, investigate, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become, over what you have done, regretful.”
— Qur’an 49:6 (Sahih International) [6]
Information from an unreliable source is not to be accepted at face value; it must be checked first.
Both teach that the reliability of the source matters, and the wise do not simply accept what comes from an unqualified one.
Judaism
Rabbi Meir says: look not at the vessel but at what is in it — a new jug may be full of old wine, and an old jug may not even contain new wine.
— Pirkei Avot 4:20 [7]
Judge a teaching by its content rather than by the status of the one who delivers it.
This is a contrast: the Mishnah urges weighing content on its merits, while the Kural says the wise will not trust even good-seeming insight from the unlearned.
Buddhism
“Though all his life a fool associates with a wise man, he no more comprehends the Truth than a spoon tastes the flavour of the soup.”
— Dhammapada 64 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [8]
A fool may be surrounded by wisdom and even handle it, as a spoon handles soup, without ever tasting its meaning.
The unlearned person’s clever-sounding insight is like soup on a spoon: present, but not understood — so the wise do not rely on it.
Jainism
“Clever talking will not work salvation; how should philosophical instruction do it? Fools, though sinking lower and lower through their sins, believe themselves to be wise men.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 6.10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The lecture on the false ascetic warns that clever words do not make a person wise. Fools mistake their own cleverness for wisdom.
The Kural says the wise will not accept an unlearned man's cleverness, however fine it looks. The Jain verse likewise refuses to count mere cleverness as wisdom.
Sikhism
“Hundreds of thousands of clever tricks, but not even one of them will go along with you in the end.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib (pauri 1), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In the first pauri of Japji Sahib Guru Nanak says that no amount of cleverness carries a person through to God.
The Kural says the wise will not accept the cleverness of the unlearned, however striking; Guru Nanak says cleverness without true understanding does not hold up at all.
Greek Mythology
Cassandra is given the gift of prophecy by Apollo but cursed so that no one believes her; her true warnings about Troy and Agamemnon’s murder are ignored.
— Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1202–1212 [11]
Cassandra speaks the truth, but her hearers dismiss it because of who she is and how she speaks.
A cautionary counterpoint: the Kural says the wise will not trust the unlearned even when they are right; Cassandra shows the cost when a true word is rejected for its source.
Philosophy
In the Meno, Socrates says true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus: valuable while they stay, but they run away unless tied down by reasoning, and once tied they become knowledge.
— Plato, Meno 97d–98a [12]
Someone can be right by luck, but without the understanding that fixes it in place, their correct opinion cannot be relied upon.
This is precisely why the wise do not ‘take’ the unlearned man’s cleverness: it is right opinion without the tether of learning.

Real Life Example

The Royal Society tests Leeuwenhoek's reports of tiny animals
1676-1680 · Delft, Dutch Republic, and London, England
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Delft draper with no university education who knew no language other than Dutch, wrote to the Royal Society in October 1676 describing vast numbers of tiny living creatures he had seen in water through his home-made lenses. The Society did not accept the claims on his word: it asked for his methods, received testimonials from respected Delft citizens, and had the botanist Nehemiah Grew and then Robert Hooke try to reproduce the observations, which Hooke eventually succeeded in doing. Only after this verification was Leeuwenhoek elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1680.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The episode shows learned scientists declining to accept even a brilliant outsider's findings until trained observers had confirmed them, a caution that in this case ended by vindicating him.
The kural says the wise will not accept the cleverness of an unlearned man as sound, however good it may be, and the Royal Society withheld acceptance of Leeuwenhoek's genuine discoveries until learned members had checked them for themselves.
