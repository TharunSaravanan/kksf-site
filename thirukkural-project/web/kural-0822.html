Kural 822

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

இனம்போன்று இனமல்லார் கேண்மை மகளிர் மனம்போல வேறு படும்.
Inampondru inamallaar kenmai magalir manampola veru padum.

Meaning

The friendship of people who act like your own family but are not will keep changing, like the heart of someone whose love depends on being paid.

Elementary

Some kids are your friend only when you have the best snack at lunch. The day you have nothing to share, they sit somewhere else. Their friendship changes shape depending on what they can get.

University

Inam (இனம்) means one’s own circle or kin; inam-pondru inam-allaar are those who resemble kin but are not. Kenmai (கேண்மை) is intimacy or friendship. The comparison with magalir manam (மகளிர் மனம்) is read by Parimelazhagar and the modern commentators (Varadarasanar, Pappaiah, Kalaignar, Puliyur Kesigan) specifically as the mind of courtesans (பொதுமகளிர் / விலைமகளிர்), whose affection follows payment, not as a judgment on women in general; Puliyur Kesigan glosses the point as changing ‘according to the gain received’. The comparison reflects the social assumptions of its age, but its logical point is clear: an attachment driven by gain changes when the gain changes. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Hitopadesha’s first book, a jackal makes friends with a deer by flattering it, while really hoping to feed on it; the deer ignores the warnings of its old friend the crow until it is caught in a snare, and the jackal waits for it to die.
— Hitopadesha, Book I (Mitralabha), the deer, the crow and the jackal [3]
The fable shows a newcomer who copies the language of friendship but is moved only by what he can gain.
The jackal is ‘like kin but not kin’, and his friendship turns as soon as profit points elsewhere.
Christianity
“A double minded man is unstable in all his ways.”
— James 1:8 (KJV) [4]
James warns that a divided heart produces a life with no fixed direction.
The Kural’s false friend is ‘double-minded’ in just this sense, so his friendship keeps shifting.
Islam
“And when they meet those who believe, they say, "We believe"; but when they are alone with their evil ones, they say, "Indeed, we are with you; we were only mockers.”
— Qur’an 2:14 (Saheeh International) [5]
The hypocrites present themselves as one with the believers but change their words as soon as the company changes.
They ‘seem like our own people but are not’, and their loyalty shifts with the room, as the Kural says.
Judaism
“Each of you, beware of your friend! / Trust not even your kinsfolk! / For every sibling takes advantage, / Every friend deals basely.”
— Jeremiah 9:3 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria; KJV 9:4) [6]
Jeremiah laments a time when even those who look like family and friends seek their own advantage.
Both texts warn that the look of kinship guarantees nothing when advantage rules the heart.
Buddhism
“One is a bottle friend; one says, 'friend, friend' only to one's face; one is a friend and an associate only when it is advantageous.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha lists kinds of companions whose friendship lasts only as long as it is convenient or profitable.
A friend ‘only when it is advantageous’ is the Kural’s gain-driven friendship that changes form.
Jainism
“Men will remain until they have done their work in an old house the thatch of which is untied, keeping out the water by a dam, and being drenched with the rain falling down upon them. Thus will friends remain with one until their business is finished.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 24 ('Improper Friendship'), st. 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The chapter on improper friendship in the Nālaṭiyār describes friends who stay only as long as they have something to gain, like workmen who put up with a leaking house until their job is done.
Valluvar says friendship with those who only seem to be one's own shifts with what they can get; the Nālaṭiyār's friends who stay only 'until their business is finished' are the same gain-driven bond.
Sikhism
“I thought that he was a great swan, so I associated with him. If I had known that he was only a wretched heron from birth, I would not have touched him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 585, Raag Vadahans, Vadahans Ki Vaar (salok 2 before pauri 1), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das uses the swan and heron (a bird that only looks like a swan) to describe one who passes for a true companion but is of another nature altogether.
The Kural speaks of people who seem like one's own kind but are not; Guru Amar Das's heron that looked like a swan is the same kind of false likeness.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of the bat and the weasels, a bat caught by one weasel claims to be a mouse, and caught by another that hates mice claims to be a bird, changing what it is to suit whoever holds it.
— Aesop’s Fables, ‘The Bat and the Weasels’ (Townsend translation) [10]
The bat survives by switching sides, belonging fully to no one.
The Kural’s false kin likewise change their colours according to what each moment offers.
Philosophy
“For the essence of friendship being that two minds become as one, how can that ever take place if the mind of each of the separate parties to it is not single and uniform, but variable, changeable, and complex?”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 25 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Cicero argues that friendship requires a steady, single-minded character, and that a changeable mind makes it impossible.
Valluvar’s point is the same: a heart that ‘changes’ cannot hold a real friendship.

Real Life Example

Warwick the Kingmaker turns against Edward IV
1469-1471 · England
Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, Edward IV's cousin, had been the chief supporter who helped make him king in 1461. After quarrels over Edward's marriage to Elizabeth Woodville and his growing independence, Warwick rebelled, fled to France in 1470, and was reconciled there with his former enemy Margaret of Anjou, wife of the Lancastrian king Henry VI. Returning to England in September 1470, he drove Edward into exile and put Henry VI back on the throne, but Edward returned and Warwick was killed at the Battle of Barnet in April 1471.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Warwick moved from kinsman and kingmaker to enemy as his own influence rose and fell, showing how little a kin-like bond meant when interests changed.
The kural says the friendship of those who seem like your own people but are not will shift with what they can gain, and Warwick, Edward's own cousin and chief ally, changed sides when his standing at court declined.
