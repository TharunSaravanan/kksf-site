Kural 609

Unsluggishness · Madiyinmai · மடியின்மை

குடியாண்மை யுள்வந்த குற்றம் ஒருவன் மடியாண்மை மாற்றக் கெடும்.
Kudiyaanmai yulvandha kutram oruvan Madiyaanmai maatrak kedum.

Meaning

When a person drives out the laziness that controls them, the faults that have crept into their family and their own life will go away.

Elementary

If your room has become a mess because you kept putting things off, you don’t need magic. Start tidying today and the mess goes away. Kick out laziness and good things come back.

University

After the warnings of 601-608, the ninth couplet offers a remedy, built on the parallel compounds குடியாண்மை and மடியாண்மை. The first is read either as குடி + ஆண்மை, ‘family and manly worth’ (Varadarasanar: குடியிலும் ஆண்மையிலும்; Kalaignar: குடிப்பெருமைக்கும் ஆண்மைக்கும்), or as ‘the governing of one’s family’. The second, மடி + ஆண்மை, is ironic: sloth exercising ‘lordship’ (ஆண்மை) over a man, so that he is its subject (Pappaiah: சோம்பலுக்கு அடிமையாவது). மாற்ற, ‘when one changes, reverses’, puts the cure in the person’s own hands. Echoing 608, where sloth makes a man a slave, here overthrowing sloth’s rule restores him. The older text reads குற்ற மொருவன் with no change of sense.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (trans. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna places the responsibility for rising or falling on the individual: the same self that drags one down can lift one up.
The Kural’s ‘when one changes the rule of sloth’ is the Gita’s ‘elevate yourself by yourself’ applied to laziness.
Christianity
“Wherefore he saith, Awake thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light.”
— Ephesians 5:14 (KJV) [4]
Paul calls on sleepers to wake and rise, promising that light will be restored to them.
Waking from sleep so that light returns reverses the image of 601, and fits the Kural’s promise that faults vanish when sloth is overthrown.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves.”
— Quran 13:11 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran states that a community’s fortune changes only when its members first change themselves inwardly.
The Kural’s மாற்ற, changing one’s slothful habit so that the family’s faults disappear, is the same principle of self-change coming before restored fortune.
Judaism
“Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise: Which having no guide, overseer, or ruler, Provideth her meat in the summer, and gathereth her food in the harvest.”
— Proverbs 6:6-8 (KJV) [4]
The sluggard is told to learn from the ant, which works diligently without anyone forcing it, and to change his ways.
‘Consider her ways, and be wise’ is an invitation to the change of habit the Kural says will remove the family’s faults.
Buddhism
“He who having been heedless is heedless no more, illuminates this world like the moon freed from clouds.”
— Dhammapada 172 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha says that someone who was once careless but has become diligent shines again, like the moon coming out from behind clouds.
Former heedlessness cleared away and the light returning is almost exactly the Kural’s faults vanishing when sloth is overthrown.
Jainism
“By conquering his will, (a monk) reaches liberation, as a well-broken horse which is clad in harness (goes to battle). Be watchful in your young years; for thereby a monk quickly obtains liberation.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 4.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
The text holds that once the will is mastered and vigilance takes the place of negligence, faults fall away and freedom follows.
As the Kural says faults vanish when a man throws off sloth's rule over him, the Jain sutra says conquering one's own will, like breaking in a horse, removes what holds a person back.
Sikhism
“My mind is lazy and sleepy. The Lord, Har, Har, has led me to meet the Holy Guru; meeting the Holy, the shutters have been opened wide.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 697, Raag Jaithsree, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Ram Das confesses a lazy, drowsy mind and says that meeting the Guru threw open its closed doors.
The Kural says the faults of family and manhood vanish once a person throws off the rule of laziness; Guru Ram Das describes the same turn from sloth to openness, though he credits the Guru's grace rather than one's own effort.
Greek Mythology
In Aesop’s fable of Hercules and the Waggoner, a carter whose wagon is stuck in the mud prays to Hercules without lifting a finger. The god tells him to put his shoulder to the wheel and whip his horses, since the gods help those who help themselves.
— Aesop, Hercules and the Waggoner (trans. G. F. Townsend) [9]
Divine help comes only after the idle man changes his behaviour and starts working himself.
The stuck wagon moves only when the carter stops being passive, just as the Kural says a family’s faults end when a person changes his sloth.
Philosophy
In Nicomachean Ethics Book II, Aristotle argues that moral virtues come about by habituation: we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave acts, so our characters are formed by the activities we repeat.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1, 1103a-b (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
For Aristotle, character is not fixed; changing one’s habitual activity changes who one is.
Because character follows habit, Valluvar’s cure for a family’s faults is to change sloth’s ‘rule’, the habit that governs a person.

Real Life Example

The idle young Charles Darwin becomes a dedicated naturalist
1825-1836 · Shrewsbury, Edinburgh and Cambridge, England and Scotland, and the voyage of HMS Beagle
Darwin's father, a physician, regarded his teenage son as a wastrel interested mainly in shooting, and Darwin later recorded in his autobiography that his father once told him he cared for nothing but shooting, dogs and rat-catching and would be a disgrace to himself and his family. He abandoned medical studies at Edinburgh and was sent to Cambridge to prepare for the church, where he became a keen collector of beetles and a student of the botanist John Henslow. Through Henslow he joined HMS Beagle in 1831, and the five-year voyage turned him into a tireless naturalist whose work culminated in On the Origin of Species in 1859.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The young man his father feared would disgrace the family became its most celebrated member once he committed himself to sustained work.
The kural says that when a person throws off the rule of sloth, the faults that had attached to his family and his own worth disappear, and Darwin's transformation turned a feared family disgrace into lasting honour.
