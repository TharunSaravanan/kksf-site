Kural 130

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

கதங்காத்துக் கற்றடங்கல் ஆற்றுவான் செவ்வி அறம்பார்க்கும் ஆற்றின் நுழைந்து.
Kadhangaaththuk katradangal aatruvaan sevvi Arampaarkkum aatrin nuzhaindhu.

Meaning

Goodness itself waits on the path, looking for the right time to meet the person who controls anger, has learned much, and stays self-controlled.

Elementary

Usually we have to go looking for good things. But if you learn a lot, stay calm when you’re angry, and stay humble, goodness comes looking for you, like a friend waiting at your door.

University

The closing couplet personifies Aram (Virtue; Parimelazhagar calls it ‘the deity of Dharma’) as a suitor who enters a person’s path and waits for his sevvi, the opportune moment when his mind, speech and face are gracious enough to be approached. This reverses the usual quest: instead of the seeker pursuing virtue, virtue pursues the restrained person. The condition has three parts: katham kaaththu (guarding against anger), katru (having learned) and adangal aatruvaan (being capable of restraint). Manakkudavar adds that the learned must also restrain the pride their learning can breed. Parimelazhagar classes this couplet as restraint of mind (manavadakkam), which completes the body–speech–mind triad around which the chapter’s second half is built. The pairing of anger control with learning parallels the Talmudic teaching that anger drives wisdom out of the sage.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Before giving up this present body, if one is able to tolerate the urges of the material senses and check the force of desire and anger, he is well situated and is happy in this world.”
— Bhagavad Gita 5.23 [4]
Krishna says the one who checks the force of desire and anger is truly established and happy.
Both texts promise that the one who masters anger will find the good (happiness, or virtue itself) coming to him.
Christianity
“Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.”
— James 1:19–20 (KJV) [5]
James teaches that restraint in speech and anger opens the way to God’s righteousness, which human wrath cannot produce.
Both texts link anger-restraint and readiness to learn (‘swift to hear’) with the arrival of righteousness.
Islam
“Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good;”
— Qur’an 3:134 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an praises those who restrain their anger and forgive, and says God loves them. A famous hadith (Bukhari 6114) adds that the strong man is the one who controls himself when angry.
As in the Kural, the one who restrains anger becomes the object of the good’s love and favour, rather than having to chase it.
Judaism
Reish Lakish teaches that whoever becomes angry, if he is a sage, his wisdom departs from him, and if he is a prophet, his prophecy departs from him, and he proves it from Moses, who forgot a law after becoming angry.
— Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 66b (paraphrase) [7]
The Talmud warns that anger drives learning and prophecy out of even the greatest.
The same pairing as the Kural: learning stays with the person who guards against anger and leaves the one who doesn’t.
Buddhism
“He who checks rising anger as a charioteer checks a rolling chariot, him I call a true charioteer. Others only hold the reins.”
— Dhammapada 222 [8]
The Buddha says true mastery is checking anger the moment it rises.
Both texts make guarding against anger the defining mark of real self-mastery.
Jainism
“By conquering anger he obtains patience; he acquires no Karman productive of anger, and destroys the Karman he had acquired before.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.67 (with 29.24) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
In the same lecture, acquiring sacred knowledge 'destroys ignorance' (29.24), and conquering anger, pride, deceit and greed (29.67–70) leads the soul step by step toward liberation.
The Kural's three conditions, guarding against anger, learning and being restrained, map onto these Jain steps, and the good that 'waits' for such a person corresponds to the karmic release they bring.
Sikhism
“To practice forgiveness is the true fast, good conduct and contentment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 223, Raag Gauree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Nanak lists forgiveness, good conduct and contentment as the true discipline of the person in whom God dwells.
The Kural's person who guards against anger and stays restrained is the one Virtue seeks out; Gurbani makes forgiveness (the opposite of anger) and good conduct the real religious practice.
Greek Mythology
As Achilles begins to draw his sword against Agamemnon, the goddess Athena comes down from heaven, seizes him by the hair and urges him to hold back. He obeys and sheathes the sword, and she promises him gifts three times as great later.
— Homer, Iliad 1.188–222 (paraphrase) [11]
A goddess personally comes to meet the hero at the very moment he restrains his anger.
A striking echo of the Kural: when a man checks his anger, the divine comes to meet him on his path.
Philosophy
“The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, 1125b (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
Aristotle praises the good-tempered person (praotes), whose anger is governed by reason.
Both see governed anger as a mark of virtue; Aristotle allows fitting anger, while Valluvar urges guarding against it altogether.

Real Life Example

Abdul Ghaffar Khan and the nonviolent Servants of God
1929 onwards; Bharat Ratna 1987 · North-West Frontier Province, British India (now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan)
Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a Pashtun reformer from near Peshawar who founded schools to spread education, established the Khudai Khidmatgar (Servants of God) movement, dated by The Tribune to 1929, an unarmed volunteer force of tens of thousands that resisted British rule through civil disobedience. On joining, members swore to forgive anyone who oppressed or mistreated them, to lead a simple life and to perform social service for at least two hours a day. Repeatedly imprisoned by both British and Pakistani governments, he was honoured by India in 1987 as the first non-Indian recipient of its highest civilian award, the Bharat Ratna.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Khan asked people from a region with strong traditions of armed revenge to master their anger, and his steadfast restraint brought him honour in several countries in his own lifetime.
The kural says virtue itself waits on the path of one who guards against anger, is learned and is capable of restraint; recognition sought out Abdul Ghaffar Khan rather than the other way round.
