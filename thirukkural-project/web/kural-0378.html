Kural 378

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

துறப்பார்மன் துப்புர வில்லார் உறற்பால ஊட்டா கழியு மெனின்.
Thurappaarman thuppura villaar urarpaala Oottaa kazhiyu menin.

Meaning

Poor people, who have nothing to enjoy, would give up the world, if only fate let the sufferings meant for them pass by without making them go through them.

Elementary

Imagine someone with an empty lunchbox. You might think it would be easy for them to say ‘I don’t need lunch.’ But they still feel hungry. This kural says poor people would give up wanting things and become monks, but fate still makes them feel the troubles they are meant to go through, so they can’t let go.

University

This is the chapter’s most compressed and most debated couplet. துப்புரவு (thuppuravu) means the means of enjoyment; those without it (the destitute) would seem to be natural renunciants, since they have nothing to give up. The particle மன் signals an unrealised possibility: they *would* renounce, *if* the sufferings that are theirs to experience (உறற்பால) passed away without being fed to them (ஊட்டா கழியும்). The implication is ironic: poverty does not automatically produce detachment, because fate still insists that its allotted pains be tasted. This fits the karmic principle, shared with Buddhism and Jainism, that ripened deeds must be experienced and cannot simply be skipped.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who has taken his birth is sure to die, and after death one is sure to take birth again. Therefore, in the unavoidable discharge of your duty, you should not lament.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.27 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna names certain things as aparihārya, unavoidable, and something that must simply be passed through.
Both texts speak of experiences that cannot be skipped and must be undergone.
Christianity
“Seeing his days are determined, the number of his months are with thee, thou hast appointed his bounds that he cannot pass.”
— Job 14:5 (KJV) [4]
Job says each human life runs within limits God has fixed.
Like the Kural’s ‘sufferings that are one’s portion’, Job’s troubles run within set bounds.
Islam
“Say, ‘Never will we be struck except by what Allāh has decreed for us; He is our protector.’ And upon Allāh let the believers rely.”
— Qur'an 9:51 (Saheeh International) [5]
Every affliction that reaches a person has already been decreed.
Both hold that the troubles meant for someone will reach them.
Judaism
“For against your will were you formed, against your will were you born, against your will you live, against your will you will die.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:22 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah reminds people that the basic course of life is imposed on them, not chosen.
Both texts say a person cannot opt out of the experiences allotted to them.
Buddhism
The Buddha states that intentional deeds that have been done and accumulated do not come to an end without their results being experienced, whether in this life or later.
— Anguttara Nikaya 10.217 (Sancetanika Sutta) [7]
Ripened kamma must be lived through; it cannot be cancelled simply by wishing.
This is the very principle behind the Kural: the sufferings due to a person must be ‘fed’ to them before they pass.
Jainism
“By pondering (on what he has learned) he loosens the firm hold which the seven kinds of Karman, except the Âyushka (have upon the soul); he shortens their duration when it was to be a long one; he mitigates their power when it was intense.” … “By austerities he cuts off the Karman.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 29.22 and 29.27 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jainism agrees that bound karma has to come to fruition, but holds that reflection and austerity (tapas) can shorten it, weaken it, and wear it away before it ripens in full. That process, nirjarā, is the purpose of renunciation.
Valluvar suggests the destitute would renounce if destined suffering did not still have to be lived through. Jainism accepts that karma must be dealt with but answers differently: renunciation and austerity are how destined suffering is cut short.
Sikhism
“So many endure distress, deprivation and constant abuse. Even these are Your Gifts, O Great Giver!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib, Pauri 25, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the twenty-fifth pauri of Japji Guru Nanak says that the hunger and suffering many people endure are also part of what the Giver hands out.
The Kural, in its most debated couplet, says the destitute would renounce the world if fate did not make them live through their allotted sufferings; Japji also sees such suffering as allotted, though as a gift to be accepted rather than a debt fate collects.
Greek Mythology
When Pandora lifts the lid of the jar, countless ills fly out to roam among mortals, and only Hope stays inside. Hesiod says there is no way to escape the will of Zeus.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 90-105 [10]
Troubles are released into human life by divine design and cannot be avoided.
Both portray sufferings as a destined portion that mortals cannot escape.
Philosophy
“Demand not that events should happen as you wish; but wish them to happen as they do happen, and you will go on well.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 8 (tr. T. W. Higginson) [11]
Epictetus teaches peace through accepting whatever comes rather than demanding escape from it.
The Kural observes that the poor cannot reach detachment while fated sufferings press on them; Epictetus offers acceptance as the way through.

Real Life Example

Carolina Maria de Jesus publishes her favela diary
1960 · Sao Paulo, Brazil
Carolina Maria de Jesus, a Black single mother of three, lived in a shack in a Sao Paulo favela and supported her children by collecting paper and scrap to sell, writing a diary in notebooks she found in the rubbish. Her diary of the years 1955 to 1960 was published in Sao Paulo in 1960 as Quarto de Despejo, later translated into English as Child of the Dark. It described hunger and daily struggle in plain terms and became one of the best-selling books in Brazilian history.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her diary showed readers that poverty is not a peaceful simplicity but a daily round of hunger, worry and hard labour.
The kural observes that the destitute, who have nothing, would seem ready to renounce the world, yet fate still makes them taste the hardships allotted to them, and de Jesus's diary records how having little brought not detachment but constant struggle.
