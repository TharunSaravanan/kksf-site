Kural 164

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

அழுக்காற்றின் அல்லவை செய்யார் இழுக்காற்றின் ஏதம் படுபாக்கு அறிந்து.
Azhukkaatrin allavai seyyaar izhukkaatrin Edham padupaakku arindhu.

Meaning

Wise people know the trouble that comes from doing wrong, so they will never do wrong because of jealousy.

Elementary

If you know a path has a big muddy pit, you don't walk that way. Wise people know jealousy leads to doing mean things, and mean things lead to trouble, so they don't let jealousy push them into it.

University

The subject, “the wise” (அறிவுடையோர்), is understood from context, as the commentators note. The couplet treats restraint from envy-driven wrongdoing as a matter of knowledge: seeing where the “fault-path” (இழுக்காறு) leads. Valluvar moves from the feeling of envy (azhukkaaru) to the acts it produces (அல்லவை, unrighteous deeds), and the wise stop the chain at the first link. The reasoning is prudential as well as moral, like the Buddha's image of the trader who avoids a dangerous road. It also matches Aristotle's claim that envy, like theft or murder, has no right amount or right occasion; it is always a mistake.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“But those who, out of envy, disregard these teachings and do not practice them regularly, are to be considered bereft of all knowledge, befooled, and doomed to ignorance and bondage.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.32 [3]
Krishna says those who act out of envy (abhyasūyantaḥ) lose their knowledge and are ruined.
Both texts link envy-driven conduct to a lack of wisdom and its ruinous result.
Christianity
“For where envying and strife is, there is confusion and every evil work.”
— James 3:16 (KJV) [4]
James warns that envy is the soil in which every kind of wrongdoing grows.
The Kural's chain from envy to wrongful acts appears here: where envy is, “every evil work” follows.
Islam
“And do not wish for that by which Allāh has made some of you exceed others. For men is a share of what they have earned, and for women is a share of what they have earned. And ask Allāh of His bounty.”
— Qur'an 4:32 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an forbids longing for others' advantages and redirects the heart to asking God directly.
Both texts stop envy early, before it grows into wrongful acts, by pointing to a better path.
Judaism
“If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. And unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him.”
— Genesis 4:7 (KJV) [4]
God warns Cain, angry that Abel's offering was accepted, that sin is waiting at the door and that he must master it before he acts.
The Kural's wise person does what Cain failed to do: sees where envy leads and refuses to act on it.
Buddhism
“Just as a trader with a small escort and great wealth would avoid a perilous route, or just as one desiring to live avoids poison, even so should one shun evil.”
— Dhammapada 123 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha compares a wise person avoiding evil to a trader who knows a road is dangerous and takes another.
The Kural's wise avoid the envy-path because they know the danger on it; the Dhammapada uses the same logic.
Jainism
“The two sins attachment and aversion lead one to commit sinful acts. That monk who always besieges them will not wander in this mundane existence.”
— Saman Suttam, verse 130 (Samyamasutra; English tr. T.K. Tukol and K.K. Dixit; jainworld.com) [7]
Jainism traces all wrongdoing to the twin passions of rāga (attachment) and dveṣa (aversion), and teaches that one who sees this and checks them escapes the round of suffering in saṃsāra.
Valluvar's wise man refuses to do wrong out of envy because he knows the suffering it leads to, and the Jain verse likewise sees aversion, of which envy is a form, as the root of sinful acts whose fruit is continued wandering in suffering.
Sikhism
“Jealousy and envy bring terrible pain, and one is cursed throughout the three worlds.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1091, Raag Maaroo (Maaroo ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok in Maaroo ki Vaar, Guru Nanak warns that envy brings deep suffering and spoils a person everywhere.
The Kural's wise person avoids envy-driven wrong because he knows the suffering it brings; Guru Nanak names that suffering plainly.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod warns that the bad Strife “fosters evil war and battle, being cruel: her no man loves,” unlike the good Strife of honest rivalry.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 14-16 (trans. Evelyn-White) [9]
The poet tells his brother Perses to recognize the destructive form of rivalry and turn away from it.
Like the Kural's wise, Hesiod's listener must see where destructive rivalry leads and refuse to follow it.
Philosophy
“Some have names that already imply badness, e.g. spite, shamelessness, envy, and in the case of actions adultery, theft, murder ... It is not possible, then, ever to be right with regard to them; one must always be wrong.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2.6 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle says envy, unlike anger or fear, has no right amount; it is always wrong, just as theft or murder is.
This is why the Kural's wise never act from envy: there is no case in which envy-driven action is right.

Real Life Example

Darwin refuses to act in a paltry spirit toward Wallace
June-July 1858 · Down House, Kent, and the Linnean Society, London, England
In June 1858 Charles Darwin, who had worked on natural selection for about twenty years without publishing, received an essay from Alfred Russel Wallace setting out the same theory. Writing to Charles Lyell on 25 June, Darwin asked whether he could honourably publish his own sketch now and said he would rather burn his whole book than have Wallace or anyone think he had behaved meanly. Lyell and Joseph Hooker arranged for Wallace's essay and extracts from Darwin's earlier writings to be read together at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Darwin faced the loss of credit for his life's work, yet he refused to suppress or outrun his rival, and the joint reading gave both men their place.
The kural says the wise, knowing where wrongdoing leads, will not do wrong out of envy, and Darwin refused to act unfairly toward a rival even when his priority was at stake.
