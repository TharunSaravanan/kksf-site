Kural 1266

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

வருகமன் கொண்கன் ஒருநாள் பருகுவன் பைதல்நோய் எல்லாம் கெட.
Varukaman kon-kan oru-naal parukuvan paidhalnhoi ellaam keda.

Meaning

Let my husband come back, even for one day. I will drink in his love until all this sickness of sorrow is gone.

Elementary

When you are very thirsty, a cool drink makes you feel better all over. The lady says being with her husband again would be like that: after missing him for so long, being with him would wash away all her sadness.

University

Again an optative: varuka maṉ, ‘may he come’, with oru nāḷ, ‘even one day’, suggesting that a single day would suffice. Parukuvaṉ, ‘I shall drink’, is the chapter’s boldest metaphor: she will take in his presence as one drinks to slake thirst (Pope: ‘I’ll drink that nectar’), so that paital nōy, the anguished sickness of separation, is wholly destroyed (ellām keṭa). Mu. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah gloss parukuvaṉ as ‘enjoy fully’ (நுகர்வேன்), and Puliyur Kesikan as ‘drink in delight’ (இன்பத்தை பருகுவேன்). The image belongs to the conventions of akam poetry and should be read as the delight of reunion. Text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When the gopis meet Krishna again at Kurukshetra after long separation, they drink him in with their eyes, cursing the creator of eyelids for interrupting their gaze, and embrace him within their hearts.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.82 (Krishna meets the gopis at Kurukshetra) (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata pictures reunion as a thirsty drinking-in of the beloved through the eyes.
The gopis and the Kural’s heroine use the same metaphor: the long-absent beloved is drunk in until the pain of separation is gone.
Christianity
“It was but a little that I passed from them, but I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go, until I had brought him into my mother’s house, and into the chamber of her that conceived me.”
— Song of Solomon 3:4 (KJV) [4]
After searching the city at night, the bride finds her beloved and holds him fast.
The heroine longs for exactly this: to have him back and not let go until all the ache of absence is gone.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy. Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an names a spouse as a source of tranquillity, bound by affection and mercy.
The heroine seeks just that: in her husband’s return she will find rest from her anguish.
Judaism
“Stay ye me with dainties, refresh me with apples; for I am love-sick.”
— Song of Songs 2:5 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The bride calls herself love-sick and asks to be revived with sweet things.
The Kural’s heroine also names her longing a sickness and expects to drink her cure in the beloved’s presence.
Buddhism
In the Canda-Kinnara Jataka, a king shoots the kinnara Canda; his wife Canda laments over him until Sakka restores him to life, and the two rejoice together again.
— Canda-Kinnara Jataka (Jataka No. 485, tr. E.B. Cowell et al.; paraphrase) [7]
The Jataka tells of a wife’s grief over her lost husband turned to joy when he is restored to her.
Like the Kural’s heroine, the kinnari’s sorrow ends only with her husband given back to her.
Jainism
Citta and Sambhuta, brothers through five successive births, are born apart in the sixth; when they meet again, Citta the monk urges King Brahmadatta to renounce, but Brahmadatta, bound by pleasures, cannot.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 13, Citta and Sambhuta (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; paraphrase) [8]
The Jain story of a long-separated pair reunited turns the reunion into a test of whether one can let go of worldly delight.
The Kural’s heroine longs to drink in the delight of reunion; the Jain reunion story warns that such delight is what binds the soul.
Sikhism
“The Dear Lord is the fruit-bearing tree; drinking in the Ambrosial Nectar, thirst is quenched.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 114, Raag Maajh, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das pictures the Lord as a tree that gives nourishing fruit; drinking in His nectar ends the soul's thirst.
The Kural's wife wants to drink in her husband's presence until her sickness of longing is gone; Guru Amar Das likewise speaks of drinking in the Beloved's nectar until thirst is satisfied.
Greek Mythology
When Penelope at last recognises Odysseus, Homer compares her joy to that of shipwrecked sailors who reach the shore, and she cannot take her arms from around his neck.
— Homer, Odyssey 23 (paraphrase) [10]
The Odyssey’s great reunion turns twenty years of grief into overwhelming relief.
This is the reunion the Kural’s heroine longs for, when all her anguish will drain away at once.
Philosophy
In the Symposium, Aristophanes says that love is the desire and pursuit of the whole, the healer who seeks to make one of two and so cure the wound of human nature.
— Plato, Symposium 189d-193d, speech of Aristophanes (paraphrase) [11]
Aristophanes’ myth treats love as the cure for the pain of having been divided from one’s other half.
The heroine likewise sees reunion with her husband as the cure for the sickness of separation.

Real Life Example

Operation Homecoming brings American prisoners of war home
12 February - April 1973 · Hanoi, Vietnam; Clark Air Base, Philippines; Travis Air Force Base, California
Under the Paris Peace Accords, the first flight of Operation Homecoming carried 40 American prisoners of war out of Hanoi on 12 February 1973 to Clark Air Base in the Philippines. Flights continued into late March, and 591 prisoners returned to the United States, some after more than seven years in captivity. The first group of 20 reached Travis Air Force Base in California on 14 February 1973.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
After years of uncertainty for their families, the return flights ended long separations in a matter of days.
The kural's heroine says that if her husband comes back even for one day, all her grief will be gone; for the families of these prisoners, the day of return ended years of anxious waiting.
