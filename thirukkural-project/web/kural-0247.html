Kural 247

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

அருளில்லார்க்கு அவ்வுலகம் இல்லை பொருளில்லார்க்கு இவ்வுலகம் இல்லாகி யாங்கு.
Arulillaarkku avvulagam illai porulillaarkku, Ivvulagam illaagi yaangu.

Meaning

The next world has nothing for people without kindness, just as this world has nothing for people without money.

Elementary

You need money to buy things in this world, like a ticket for the bus. Valluvar says kindness is the ticket for the next world. Without it, you can’t get on.

University

This is one of the Kural’s most quoted parallelisms, built on the matched pairs arul and porul (grace and wealth) and avvulagam and ivvulagam (that world and this world). It is also notably realistic: Valluvar does not pretend that poverty is irrelevant here; he concedes that this world is closed to the destitute (compare the chapter on poverty, 105). That concession makes the second claim sharper, because compassion is to be treated as the currency of the hereafter. Kalaignar gives a non-metaphysical reading: wealth is essential to the householder’s life (illaram) and compassion to the renunciate’s (thuravaram), fitting this chapter’s place at the start of the ascetic section. The couplet shows the Kural’s characteristic balance between worldly realism and spiritual priority.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the gate of heaven, Yudhishthira refuses Indra’s demand that he leave behind the dog that has followed him, saying he will not abandon a devoted and helpless creature even to enter heaven; the dog is revealed as Dharma, who praises his compassion for all creatures.
— Mahabharata, Mahaprasthanika Parva 3 [3]
Heaven is opened to Yudhishthira precisely because he would not give up compassion to get there.
A story version of ‘without compassion there is no other world’: compassion is literally what admits him.
Christianity
“For he shall have judgment without mercy, that hath shewed no mercy; and mercy rejoiceth against judgment.”
— James 2:13 (KJV) [4]
James warns that those who show no mercy will receive none at the judgment.
The merciless have no share in the world to come, which is the kural’s claim almost word for word.
Islam
“Allah will not be merciful to those who are not merciful to mankind.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7376 [5]
Divine mercy, on which salvation depends, is withheld from those who withhold mercy from others.
Same claim: without compassion there is no share in the other world.
Judaism
“The wealthy Jews of Babylonia will descend to Gehenna because they do not have compassion on others.” ... “anyone who does not have compassion for God’s creatures, it is known that he is not of the descendants of Abraham, our father.”
— Talmud, Beitzah 32b [6]
Rav condemns rich men who refused both a loan and food to a visitor in need.
Striking parallel: having porul (wealth) but lacking arul (compassion) still means losing the world to come.
Buddhism
“One who, while himself seeking happiness, oppresses with violence other beings who also desire happiness, will not attain happiness hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 131 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The next verse gives the converse: whoever does not oppress others will find happiness hereafter.
Lack of compassion closes off happiness in the hereafter, as in the kural’s avvulagam illai.
Jainism
“In vain he adopts nakedness, who errs about matters of paramount interest; neither this world nor the next will be his; he is a loser in both respects in the world.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.49 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The sage says a false ascetic who goes wrong on what matters most loses both this world and the next.
Valluvar matches this world, closed to the poor, with the other world, closed to the pitiless; the Uttaradhyayana uses the same this-world/next-world pairing for one who errs in what matters most, which in the passage includes being 'without pity' (20.48).
Sikhism
“Kabeer, to use force is tyranny, even if you call it legal. When your account is called for in the Court of the Lord, what will your condition be then?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1374, Salok Bhagat Kabir 187 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir warns that oppression remains tyranny whatever justification it is given, and that it will be judged in the divine court.
The Kural says the other world holds nothing for those without compassion; Kabir asks what will become of the pitiless when their account is called for hereafter.
Greek Mythology
Phoenix tells Achilles that the Prayers (Litai) are daughters of Zeus, lame and wrinkled, who follow behind Ruin (Ate); whoever respects them is helped, but whoever rejects them is followed by Ruin until he pays the penalty.
— Homer, Iliad 9.502-512 [10]
Refusing a suppliant’s plea for mercy brings ruin on the person who refuses.
Rejecting compassion closes off divine favour, a this-worldly Greek counterpart to the lost ‘other world’.
Philosophy
Aristotle concedes that happiness needs external goods, since it is impossible, or at least not easy, to act nobly without resources such as friends, wealth and political power.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.8 (1099a31-b8) [11]
Aristotle is candid that virtue alone does not secure a flourishing life in this world.
This parallels the first half of the kural (without wealth there is no ‘this world’), and shares Valluvar’s realism about material conditions.

Real Life Example

Enrico Scrovegni builds the Arena Chapel painted by Giotto
1303-1305 · Padua, Italy
Reginaldo Scrovegni of Padua was a notorious moneylender whom Dante placed among the usurers in the Inferno. His son Enrico, also a banker, founded a chapel on the site of the Roman arena in Padua around 1303, which was consecrated in 1305 and decorated by Giotto with a celebrated fresco cycle. In its Last Judgment Enrico is shown kneeling and presenting the chapel to the Virgin, while usurers appear among the damned.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Art historians often read the chapel as an attempt by a rich banking family to secure spiritual standing that wealth alone could not buy.
The kural says wealth rules this world but compassion is the currency of the next, and the Scrovegni chapel shows a fortune made in this world being spent in hope of the other.
