Kural 487

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

பொள்ளென ஆங்கே புறம்வேரார் காலம்பார்த்து உள்வேர்ப்பர் ஒள்ளி யவர்.
Pollena Aange Puramveraar Kaalampaarththu Ulverppar Olli Yavar.

Meaning

Wise people do not burst out in anger right away. They keep it inside and wait for the right time to act.

Elementary

If someone is rude to you and you shout back at once, you may make things worse. A wise person stays calm on the outside, remembers what happened, and deals with it later at a better moment.

University

Pollena means suddenly or at once, and aange means right there. Puram veraar means ‘they do not sweat or seethe outwardly’, and ul verppar means ‘they seethe within’. Olliyavar are the bright or clear-minded. Parimelazhagar explains that when the wise are provoked by enemies, they do not show anger immediately, because doing so would warn the enemy. They hold it until kaalam paarththu, ‘watching for the time’. The couplet is about strategy, not ethical renunciation of anger: the feeling is kept and redirected, not dissolved. Commentators contrast it with Chapter 31 (Vekulaamai, ‘Not Being Angry’), where anger is to be given up entirely. The two sources agree; ‘olli yavar’ is a metrical split of olliyavar.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the dice hall, as Draupadi was humiliated, Bhima raged. Yudhishthira’s word, and the Pandavas’ bond to the wager, held him back from attacking the Kauravas then and there. Instead Bhima swore vows he fulfilled thirteen years later at Kurukshetra, breaking Duryodhana’s thigh and drinking Duhshasana’s blood.
— Mahābhārata, Sabhā Parva (Dyūta sub-parva) and Śalya/Karṇa Parvas (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Bhima’s anger was not given up but held in until the war, the right time.
This is the Kural’s ‘seething within, not without’, kept until the moment for revenge came.
Christianity
James tells believers to be swift to hear, slow to speak and slow to wrath, because human anger does not produce the righteousness of God.
— James 1:19–20 (KJV) [4]
Early Christian ethics warns against quick, uncontrolled outbursts of anger.
‘Slow to wrath’ matches the Kural’s refusal to blaze up on the spot, though James does not advise saving it for later.
Islam
“˹They are˺ those who donate in prosperity and adversity, control their anger, and pardon others. And Allah loves the good-doers.”
— Qur’an 3:134 (tr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The Qur’an praises al-kāẓimīn al-ghayẓ, literally ‘those who hold in their rage’, as a camel swallows back its cud.
Kaẓm al-ghayẓ, holding in rage, is almost a literal counterpart of ul verppar, ‘seething within’.
Judaism
“And Joseph made haste; for his bowels did yearn upon his brother: and he sought where to weep; and he entered into his chamber, and wept there. And he washed his face, and went out, and refrained himself, and said, Set on bread.”
— Genesis 43:30–31 (KJV) [4]
Overcome by feeling at seeing Benjamin, Joseph hides it, weeps in private, then returns composed. He does not yet reveal himself to the brothers who sold him, and waits until the test is complete.
This is powerful feeling hidden within and held back until the right moment, as the Kural describes.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada calls the one who checks rising anger, as a charioteer checks a rolling chariot, a true charioteer. Others merely hold the reins.
— Dhammapada 222 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
Mastery means stopping anger at the very moment it starts to roll.
The check on the sudden outburst (pollena) is shared, though Buddhism aims to extinguish anger, not store it.
Jainism
“He should not speak unasked, and asked he should not tell a lie; he should not give way to his anger, and bear with indifference pleasant and unpleasant occurrences.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The monk is told not to give way to anger at all and to meet both pleasant and unpleasant events with equanimity.
The kural's wise man hides his anger and waits for the right time; Jainism agrees that anger should not burst out, but it asks for anger to be given up rather than stored for later.
Sikhism
“O anger, you are the root of conflict; compassion never rises up in you.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1358, Salok Sehskritee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the Salok Sehskritee, Guru Arjan addresses anger (krodh), one of the five thieves, as the root of strife and the enemy of compassion.
The Kural has the wise keep their anger hidden until the right time; Sikh teaching goes further and treats anger itself as a vice to be overcome, not stored up.
Greek Mythology
When Agamemnon takes Briseis, Achilles half-draws his sword to kill him. Athena, visible to him alone, grips his hair and tells him to hold his hand, to use words only, and promises that he will one day receive gifts three times as great for this insult. Achilles pushes the sword back into its sheath.
— Homer, Iliad 1.188–222 (tr. Samuel Butler) [9]
The goddess of wisdom stops the instant outburst and points Achilles toward a later redress.
This is the Kural exactly: the wise do not strike on the spot but hold their wrath for the right time.
Philosophy
Aristotle praises the man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and also as he ought, when he ought and for as long as he ought. He blames both irascibility and a slavish lack of spirit.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, 1125b31–1126a3 (tr. W. D. Ross) [10]
For Aristotle, anger is not bad in itself. Its virtue is a matter of right measure and right timing.
The Kural’s wise man also keeps his anger but lets it out only ‘when he ought’.

Real Life Example

The young Kangxi Emperor removes the regent Oboi
1669 · Beijing, China
The Kangxi Emperor came to the Qing throne in 1661 at not quite seven years old, and four senior Manchu advisers, Sonin, Suksaha, Ebilun and Oboi, governed in his name. Although he began formally attending to state affairs in 1667, real power remained with the regents, Oboi chief among them. In 1669, at the age of 15, the emperor had Oboi arrested and removed the regents, taking control of the government himself.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
The young emperor bore the regents' domination for years and acted only when he was able to break it, beginning one of the longest and most successful reigns in Chinese history.
The kural says the wise do not flare up at once but hold their resentment inside and wait for the right time, and Kangxi waited until he could act decisively against the man who dominated his court.
