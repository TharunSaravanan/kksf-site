Kural 114

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

தக்கார் தகவிலர் என்பது அவரவர் எச்சத்தாற் காணப்ப படும்.
Thakkaar thakavilar enbadhu avaravar Echchaththaar kaanappa padum.

Meaning

Whether a person was fair or unfair will be shown by what they leave behind after they die, such as their good or bad name.

Elementary

You can tell what kind of gardener someone was by what is still growing in the garden after they leave. People are like that too: what remains after them shows whether they were fair.

University

The couplet turns on எச்சம் (echcham, “that which remains”). Parimelazhagar reads it as offspring: the worth of the just is visible in good descendants, a reading reflected in G. U. Pope’s translation (“By each one’s offspring shall the truth be clear”). Mu. Varadarasanar and Karunanidhi instead read it as the reputation, praise or blame, that remains after death, while Solomon Pappaiah combines wealth, fame and the conduct of one’s children. Either way the claim is that a verdict on a life’s justice can only be given in retrospect, from its durable effects. This echoes Solon’s advice to Croesus (in Herodotus) to call no one happy until their end is seen, Jesus’ “by their fruits ye shall know them,” and the hadith that a righteous child is among the deeds that continue after death. Note on the text: all three consulted editions (thirukkural.net, ytamizh.com, Project Madurai) print காணப்ப படும்; many printed editions give காணப் படும். The meaning is unaffected.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for a respectable person, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 [4]
Krishna warns Arjuna that people’s lasting verdict on his conduct will outlive him, and that a dishonoured name is worse than death.
Both treat the reputation that survives a person as the real verdict on their character.
Christianity
“Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them.”
— Matthew 7:20 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that true character shows itself in results, the way a tree is known by its fruit.
The Kural likewise says the just and unjust are known by what they produce and leave behind.
Islam
“When a man dies, his acts come to an end, but three, recurring charity, or knowledge (by which people) benefit, or a pious child, who prays for him (for the deceased).”
— Sahih Muslim 1631 [6]
The Prophet teaches that after death only three things keep adding to a person’s account, one of which is a righteous child.
Fits both readings of echcham: the good that remains after a person, and especially good offspring.
Judaism
“The memory of the just is blessed: but the name of the wicked shall rot.”
— Proverbs 10:7 (KJV) [5]
The proverb contrasts the lasting, blessed memory of the just with the decaying name of the wicked.
Almost exactly the Varadarasanar reading: praise or blame after death shows who was just.
Buddhism
“Not the sweet smell of flowers, not even the fragrance of sandal, tagara, or jasmine blows against the wind. But the fragrance of the virtuous blows against the wind. Truly the virtuous man pervades all directions with the fragrance of his virtue.”
— Dhammapada 54 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says the reputation of virtue spreads everywhere and lasts in a way no perfume can.
Both see virtue as leaving a lasting, perceptible trace by which the person is known.
Jainism
“My own Self is the doer and undoer of misery and happiness; my own Self, friend and foe, according as I act well or badly.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 20.37 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The monk Anāthin tells King Śreṇika that each person's condition is the result of their own good or bad conduct, with no outside protector.
Valluvar says a person's justice or injustice shows in what they leave behind, and Jain karma doctrine likewise holds that what follows a person is simply the fruit of acting well or badly.
Sikhism
“Celebrated and approved is the coming into the world of such a person, who saves all his generations as well.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1094, Raag Maaroo, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das says a life is approved when its good extends to the whole family line.
Read with Parimelazhagar's gloss of echcham as offspring, the Kural says the worth of the just is shown in what they leave behind; Gurbani likewise judges a life by its benefit to the generations.
Greek Mythology
“The fame, therefore, of her virtue shall never die, and the immortals shall compose a song that shall be welcome to all mankind in honour of the constancy of Penelope.”
— Homer, Odyssey 24 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
In the underworld, Agamemnon’s ghost contrasts Penelope’s undying good name with the hateful song that will follow his murderous wife Clytemnestra.
Shows the Kural’s contrast in mythic form: the worthy and the unworthy are told apart by the fame or infamy that outlives them.
Philosophy
“Call him, however, until he die, not happy but fortunate.” … “But in every matter it behoves us to mark well the end”
— Herodotus, Histories 1.32 (trans. George Rawlinson) [11]
Solon tells King Croesus that no life can be judged until its end is seen.
Like the Kural, it holds that a person’s true worth is visible only from what the whole life finally amounts to.

Real Life Example

Justice Harlan's lone dissent in Plessy v. Ferguson
1896; vindicated 1954 · Supreme Court of the United States, Washington, D.C., United States
In Plessy v. Ferguson the US Supreme Court, by a vote of 7 to 1, upheld a state law providing for racial segregation and established the separate but equal doctrine. Justice John Marshall Harlan was the lone dissenter, arguing that the Constitution recognised no superior or ruling class among citizens and warning that the decision would plant the seeds of race hatred in state law. In 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education and related cases, the Court repudiated the separate but equal doctrine, and Britannica notes that in the twentieth century the Court vindicated many of the positions Harlan had taken in dissent.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Harlan was outvoted in his lifetime, but the judgment that survived him was his, while the majority's ruling is now remembered as a grave injustice.
The kural says whether a person was just or unjust is revealed by what remains after them; history's verdict on Plessy showed who had judged fairly.
