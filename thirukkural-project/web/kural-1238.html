Kural 1238

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

முயங்கிய கைகளை ஊக்கப் பசந்தது பைந்தொடிப் பேதை நுதல்.
Muyangiya kaikalai ookkap pasandhadhu paindhotip paedhai nudhal.

Meaning

Once, when I only loosened my arms from our hug, the forehead of my innocent wife with her gold bangles turned pale at once.

Elementary

Imagine a little child holding a parent’s hand at a busy fair. If the parent lets go for just a second, the child’s face might look worried right away. The husband in this verse remembers that his wife looked worried and pale even when he let go of a hug for just a moment, so he knows how much she must be missing him now that he is far away.

University

With 1238 the voice changes: Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar gloss in the husband’s first person (‘when I loosened my arms’), and the traditional reading has the talaivaṉ, away from home, recalling his wife’s fragility to explain why he must hurry back. Muyaṅkiya kaikaḷai ūkka is ‘when [I] loosened (ūkka, ‘to slacken, withdraw’) the arms that embraced’; pacantatu… nutal, ‘the forehead turned sallow’, applies pacappu, the pallor of love-longing, to her brow, the classic site of a woman’s brightness (oḷ nutal, 1240). Paintoṭip pētai, ‘the innocent one with fresh-gold bangles’, uses pētai, ‘young, guileless woman’, a tender term. The logic is a fortiori: if a moment’s slackening paled her, what must long absence do? Solomon Pappaiah adds that he loosened his grip fearing his tight hold would hurt her. Pope: ‘One day the fervent pressure of embracing arms I checked, / Grew wan the forehead of the maid with golden armlet decked.’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Bhavabhuti’s Uttararamacharita, Rama, looking at paintings of their forest exile, recalls how Sita used to fall asleep with her head on his arm, and his tenderness for her is mixed with the pain of knowing he must soon part from her.
— Bhavabhuti, Uttararamacharita, Act 1 (paraphrase) [3]
Bhavabhuti dwells on a husband’s memory of his wife’s closeness in his arms.
The Kural’s husband likewise remembers the embrace and how fragile she was within it.
Christianity
“His left hand is under my head, and his right hand doth embrace me.”
— Song of Solomon 2:6 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song describes the beloved’s embrace as her rest and security.
The Kural shows the reverse side of the same embrace: when it is loosened even slightly, the beloved’s brightness falters.
Islam
“They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them.”
— Qur’an 2:187 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes spouses as each other’s garment, close, protecting and covering.
The Kural shows how a wife feels when that ‘garment’ is drawn back even for a moment.
Judaism
“I held him, and would not let him go, until I had brought him into my mother’s house.”
— Song of Songs 3:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Having found her beloved after a night of searching, the woman of the Song will not loosen her hold.
The Kural’s wife, too, cannot bear even a slackened embrace; she pales the moment it loosens.
Buddhism
“From affection springs grief, from affection springs fear. For one who is wholly free from affection there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 213 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada traces grief and fear to affection itself.
The wife’s instant pallor at a loosened embrace is the fear that affection brings, exactly as the verse diagnoses.
Jainism
The Uttaradhyayana’s lecture on carelessness teaches that one passionately attached to pleasant touch suffers as soon as it is withdrawn, and never finds satisfaction in it.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 32, verses on touch (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching treats attachment to touch as one of the senses’ bonds that produce pain at every loss.
The wife’s pallor at a slackened embrace is the pain of withdrawn touch; Jain doctrine would read it as the fruit of attachment.
Sikhism
“Without water, she cannot live, even for an instant. God knows the suffering of her mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 60, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak compares the soul's love to a fish that cannot live a moment out of water, and says God knows her inner pain.
In the Kural the husband remembers that the moment he loosened his embrace, his wife's forehead turned pale; Guru Nanak too speaks of a love that cannot bear a moment's separation, and of a Beloved who knows that pain.
Greek Mythology
Sappho describes what happens to her in the beloved’s presence: her tongue breaks, a fine fire runs under her skin, her ears hum, a sweat comes over her, and she is ‘greener than grass’, seeming to herself little short of dying.
— Sappho, fragment 31 (paraphrase) [10]
The most famous Greek poem of love-sickness names a greenish pallor as love’s mark on the body.
The Kural’s pacappu, the sallow-green pallor that spreads over the wife’s forehead, is the same colour of love.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Symposium, Aristophanes tells how humans were split in two, so that each half longs for its other; when they find each other they embrace and do not want to be parted even for a moment.
— Plato, Symposium 191a-192e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Aristophanes’ myth explains lovers’ desire never to loosen their embrace as a longing to be whole again.
The Kural’s wife pales at a slackened embrace because, in Aristophanes’ terms, she feels herself made half again.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela's prison letters to Winnie
1962-1990 · Robben Island and other prisons, South Africa
Nelson Mandela was a sentenced political prisoner from 1962 to 1990, held in four prisons. At first he could write and receive only one 500-word letter every six months, and on Robben Island he was allowed one visitor a year for 30 minutes. In his letters to his wife Winnie he professed his love and gave her moral support in her own struggle.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters show how a separated husband kept his marriage alive through rare, censored words over decades.
In this kural the absent husband remembers his wife's tenderness and worries about her, and Mandela, separated from Winnie for decades, poured the same remembering love into his few permitted letters.
