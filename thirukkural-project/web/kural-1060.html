Kural 1060

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

இரப்பான் வெகுளாமை வேண்டும் நிரப்பிடும்பை தானேயும் சாலும் கரி.
Irappaan vegulaamai vendum nirappidumbai thaaneyum saalum kari.

Meaning

A person who asks should not get angry when refused. The pain of their own need is proof enough of how things stand.

Elementary

If you ask a friend to share and they say no, don't get mad. You know how it feels not to have enough, so maybe they don't have enough either, or maybe it just wasn't the right time.

University

Vegulaamai (வெகுளாமை, 'not becoming angry') recalls Chapter 31 (Vegulaamai), here applied to the one who is refused. The second line is read in more than one way: nirappidumbai thaaneyum saalum kari (நிரப்பிடும்பை தானேயும் சாலும் கரி) can mean that the asker's own poverty is sufficient witness (kari, கரி) that things are not always available when wanted (Puliyur Kesikan, Pappaiah), that the asker's poverty teaches him (Mu. Varadarasanar), or that the refuser may be in the same plight as himself (Kalaignar). The commentarial tradition following Parimelazhagar takes the beggar's own want as reason enough for patience toward a refusal. Pope: 'Askers refused from wrath must stand aloof; / The plague of poverty itself is ample proof.' The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Riches come now to one, now to another, and like the wheels of cars are ever rolling.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.5 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Vedic hymn reminds the rich that wealth turns like a chariot wheel, moving from one person to another.
If fortune turns for everyone, the refused asker has no grounds for anger, since the giver too may be in want, which is Kalaignar's reading of the Kural.
Christianity
“Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content.”
— Philippians 4:11 (KJV) [4]
Thanking the Philippians for their gift, Paul says he has learned to be content whether he has plenty or is in want.
Contentment in want is the inner freedom that lets an asker meet refusal without anger.
Islam
“Who spend [in the cause of Allāh] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allāh loves the doers of good;”
— Qur'an 3:134 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an praises those who restrain anger and pardon people, together with those who give in ease and hardship.
Holding back anger and forgiving is what the Kural asks of the refused asker.
Judaism
Ben Zoma teaches that the rich person is the one who rejoices in his lot, and the mighty one is the one who conquers his own inclination.
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) (paraphrase) [6]
Pirkei Avot locates wealth in contentment and strength in self-control.
An asker who is content with his lot and masters his impulses will not rage at a refusal.
Buddhism
“Happy indeed we live, we who possess nothing. Feeders on joy we shall be, like the Radiant Gods.”
— Dhammapada 200 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Tradition links this verse to the day the Buddha went for alms in a village and came back with an empty bowl, yet stayed joyful (cf. SN 4.18).
The Buddha's calm in front of an empty bowl is the mendicant's freedom from anger at refusal that the Kural asks for.
Jainism
“He should beg food from the householder when his dinner is ready; a wise man should not care whether he gets alms or not. ‘I get nothing to-day, perhaps I shall get something to-morrow;’ a monk who thinks thus, will not be grieved by his want of success.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.30-31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira lists alabha, being refused, among the twenty-two hardships a monk must bear calmly, without grief or resentment.
This is the Kural's rule that the asker must not be angered by refusal, made into a monastic discipline.
Sikhism
“O mind, don't cry out that you are hungry, always hungry; stop complaining.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 27, Sri Raag, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das tells the mind to stop its constant cry of hunger and its complaints, and to rest in trust instead of grievance.
The Kural tells the asker not to grow angry when refused, since his own want explains his situation; Guru Amar Das likewise counsels the needy mind to give up complaint and resentment.
Greek Mythology
“For a moment he doubted whether or no to fly at Melanthius and kill him with his staff, or fling him to the ground and beat his brains out; he resolved, however, to endure it and keep himself in check”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Kicked by the goatherd Melanthius while disguised as a beggar, Odysseus masters his anger and stays on the path.
The beggar who keeps himself in check is the Kural's asker who does not give way to anger.
Philosophy
“He once begged alms of a statue, and, when asked why he did so, replied, ‘To get practice in being refused.’”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.49 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
Diogenes trained himself to meet refusal calmly by begging from a statue that could never give.
Practising refusal until it no longer stings is the Kural's advice that the asker must not be angered.

Real Life Example

Marconi, rebuffed in Italy, takes his wireless to England
1895-1897 · Bologna, Italy, and London, England
After his first wireless experiments at his family's estate near Bologna, the young Guglielmo Marconi offered his system to the Italian government, which showed little interest. In 1896 he took his apparatus to England, where William Preece, Engineer-in-Chief of the Post Office, supported his work and he was granted the world's first patent for a system of wireless telegraphy. In 1897 he returned to Italy to give a demonstration to the Italian government at La Spezia.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Marconi answered a refusal at home by seeking support elsewhere, and he later served Italy in war and diplomacy.
The kural says one who asks must not grow angry when refused, and Marconi, turned down by his own government, simply went where help could be had and later came back to demonstrate his invention to it.
