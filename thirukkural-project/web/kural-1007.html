Kural 1007

Wealth without Benefaction · Nandriyil Selvam · நன்றியில் செல்வம்

அற்றார்க்கொன்று ஆற்றாதான் செல்வம் மிகநலம் பெற்றாள் தமியள்மூத் தற்று.
Atraarkkondru Aatraadhaan Selvam Mikanalam Petraal Thamiyalmooth Thatru

Meaning

The riches of a person who gives nothing to the poor are like a very beautiful woman who grows old all alone.

Elementary

Picture the most beautiful flower blooming inside a locked room where nobody ever sees it, until it slowly fades. Valluvar says money that never helps poor people is like that hidden flower: something lovely, wasted.

University

Atraar (அற்றார், 'those who have nothing, the destitute') are the intended recipients, and aatraadhaan (ஆற்றாதான்) is 'one who does not do (even one thing) for them'. The simile mika nalam petraaL thamiyaL mooththatru (மிக நலம் பெற்றாள் தமியள் மூத்தற்று) compares such wealth to 'a woman endowed with great beauty growing old alone'; Solomon Pappaiah and Pope's explanation specify 'without a husband', Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar keep 'alone'. The point of the comparison is waste: a good thing that exists to be shared lapses unused, the same logic as the chapter's poisonous-tree image in 1008 and its counterpart in Kural 216, where a generous man's wealth is a fruit tree in the middle of the village. The simile reflects the social world of classical Tamil literature, in which beauty fulfilled in married life is the norm; modern readers may note the period assumption while keeping the moral about unshared resources. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The man with food in store who, when the needy comes in miserable case begging for bread to eat, / Hardens his heart against him-even when of old he did him service-finds not one to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.2 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith, sacred-texts.com) [3]
The Vedic hymn on liberality condemns the man who refuses food to the needy and promises him a friendless end.
The Kural likewise condemns wealth that does nothing for the destitute.
Christianity
John asks how the love of God can dwell in anyone who has this world's goods, sees a brother in need, and shuts up his heart of compassion against him.
— 1 John 3:17 [4]
Possessions withheld from the needy are a sign that love is absent.
It condemns exactly the Kural's wealthy man who gives nothing to the destitute.
Islam
Surat al-Ma'un describes the one who denies the Judgement as he who drives away the orphan and does not urge the feeding of the poor, and condemns those who withhold even small kindnesses.
— Qur'an 107:1–7 (Al-Ma'un) [5]
Refusing basic help to the destitute is presented as a mark of spiritual bankruptcy.
The Kural's man who does not give even one thing to the destitute is the Ma'un's withholder.
Judaism
The Torah commands that if there is a needy person among your brethren, you shall not harden your heart nor shut your hand, but open your hand wide and lend him enough for his need.
— Deuteronomy 15:7–8 [6]
Withholding from the poor is forbidden; generosity to the needy is a commandment.
The Kural blames the rich man who 'does not do one thing' for the destitute.
Buddhism
“Like water in a haunted place that, without being imbibed, dries up: such is the wealth acquired by a worthless person who neither enjoys it himself nor gives.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 3.19, Aputtaka Sutta, closing verse (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight) [7]
The Buddha compares unshared wealth to pure water in a place no one dares to drink from, which simply evaporates.
Like the Kural's beauty growing old alone, it pictures a good thing that withers unused because it is never shared.
Jainism
“The great wealth which the miserly-minded have attained, who neither know how to give it away nor to use it themselves, shall be like the beauteous damsels of a family, who when they have arrived at puberty are enjoyed by others; i.e. others than its owner shall enjoy it.”
— Nāladiyār, ch. 28 ('Illiberality'), st. 4 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain poet uses the same image of a young woman's beauty to show that a miser's wealth benefits others, never its owner.
Both texts compare unshared wealth to feminine beauty that its keeper does not rightly bring to fruition.
Sikhism
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, the young Nanak was given money by his father Mehta Kalu to make a profitable bargain (sacha sauda), but on the way he met a group of hungry holy men and spent all of it on food for them, calling this the true bargain.
— Janamsakhi tradition, as retold in M. A. Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak [9]
The story is remembered as the first act of the Sikh duty to share with the needy, later embodied in the langar.
The Kural says wealth never given to the destitute is wasted like beauty that ages alone; the young Nanak's true bargain is the opposite, money spent at once on those who had nothing.
Greek Mythology
The beautiful youth Narcissus, sought by many, scorns every suitor, and in the end falls in love with his own reflection and wastes away beside the pool, his beauty consumed without ever being shared.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.339–510 (Narcissus) [10]
Beauty turned wholly inward withers without bringing joy to anyone.
It parallels the Kural's image of great beauty fading in solitude as a picture of wasted, unshared riches.
Philosophy
“Now the things that have a use may be used either well or badly; and riches is a useful thing; and everything is used best by the man who has the virtue concerned with it; riches, therefore, will be used best by the man who has the virtue concerned with wealth; and this is the liberal man.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross, MIT Internet Classics Archive) [11]
Aristotle holds that wealth exists to be used, and is used best by the generous person.
The Kural's simile makes the same claim negatively: wealth not used for the needy wastes its purpose.

Real Life Example

Bokassa crowns himself emperor amid national poverty
4 December 1977 · Bangui, Central African Empire (now Central African Republic)
On 4 December 1977 Jean-Bedel Bokassa, ruler of the Central African Republic, which he had renamed the Central African Empire, crowned himself Emperor Bokassa I in a ceremony modelled on Napoleon's coronation, seated on a great gilded eagle throne. Britannica puts the cost at more than 20 million dollars, an enormous sum for one of the poorest countries in the world, and much of it was met by France. The spectacle drew international criticism, and Bokassa was overthrown in 1979.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Bokassa spent a fortune on his own glory in a country where most people lacked basic services, and the money vanished in a single ceremony.
The kural says that the wealth of one who gives nothing to the destitute is wasted like great beauty growing old alone, and Bokassa poured national wealth into one day's display while his people remained poor.
