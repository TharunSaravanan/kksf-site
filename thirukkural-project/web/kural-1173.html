Kural 1173

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

கதுமெனத் தாநோக்கித் தாமே கலுழும் இதுநகத் தக்க துடைத்து.
Kadhumenath thaanoakkith thaamae kaluzhum ithunakath thakka thudaiththu.

Meaning

These eyes rushed on their own to look at him, and now they cry on their own. Surely that is something to laugh at.

Elementary

Picture a child who runs to jump in a puddle, then cries because their socks are wet. It’s sad, but also a little funny, because they did it to themselves! The lady laughs at her eyes the same way: they hurried to look, and now they are the ones crying.

University

Katumeṉa (கதுமென) is an adverb of sudden speed, ‘in a flash’, and the parallel tām (themselves) / tāmē (they themselves) puts the whole action, the eager looking and the weeping, on the eyes alone. Naka-t-takkatu uṭaittu, ‘it has the quality of something to be laughed at’, gives the chapter its bitter humour: the heroine mocks her own organs. Puliyur Kesikan glosses the laughter as at the eyes’ ignorance; Solomon Pappaiah renders it colloquially, ‘when I see these eyes crying alone today, I can only laugh’. Pope: ‘The eyes that threw such eager glances round erewhile / Are weeping now. Such folly surely claims a smile!’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That happiness which is derived from contact of the senses with their objects and which appears like nectar at first but poison at the end is said to be of the nature of passion.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.38 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna classifies pleasure that rushes from the senses to their objects as passionate: sweet at the start, bitter at the end.
The heroine’s eyes enjoyed the nectar of the first eager look and now taste the poison of weeping, which she finds almost laughable.
Christianity
“Even in laughter the heart is sorrowful; and the end of that mirth is heaviness.”
— Proverbs 14:13 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs observes that laughter and sorrow sit close together, and that merriment can end in grief.
The Kural joins the two in the same way: the heroine laughs at eyes whose eager joy has ended in tears.
Islam
“And that it is He who makes [one] laugh and weep.”
— Qur’an 53:43 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an names laughter and weeping together as conditions that come over human beings in turn.
The Kural’s heroine finds both in a single moment: she laughs at the eyes that are weeping.
Judaism
“All things toil to weariness; Man cannot utter it, The eye is not satisfied with seeing, Nor the ear filled with hearing.”
— Ecclesiastes 1:8 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Koheleth notes that the eye is never sated by looking; it keeps reaching out, and the reaching leads only to weariness.
The Kural’s eyes, which flew to look and now weep, show the same restless, never-satisfied seeing.
Buddhism
“So long as an evil deed has not ripened, the fool thinks it as sweet as honey. But when the evil deed ripens, the fool comes to grief.”
— Dhammapada 69 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says that what tastes sweet at first can ripen into grief, catching the unwary by surprise.
The eyes’ eager look was honey at first; now it has ripened into weeping, which the heroine finds fit for laughter.
Jainism
“As the fruit of the Kimpâka is beautiful in taste and colour, when eaten; but destroys the life when digested, (being) poison; similar in their effect are pleasures.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.20 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana compares pleasures to the kimpaka fruit, lovely to look at and to taste but deadly afterwards.
The heroine’s eager look was the lovely fruit; the weeping that followed is its bitter after-effect.
Sikhism
“I used to see my beloved Lord without a blink of the eye as a ruddy sheldrake looks at moon. There used to be no break. But now I don't even see Him in a dream.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Kabitt Savaiye 670, English translation as given in the BaniDB database [9]
Bhai Gurdas recalls how the lover's eyes once gazed at the Beloved without blinking, like the chakor bird at the moon, and now cannot glimpse Him even in dreams. His Kabitt Savaiye are early exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural's heroine laughs bitterly at eyes that rushed of their own accord to look at the beloved and now weep of their own accord; Bhai Gurdas sets the same contrast between the eyes' eager gazing then and their deprivation now.
Greek Mythology
Psyche, unable to resist, lights a lamp to look at her sleeping husband, Eros; a drop of oil wakes him, he flies away, and she is left weeping for the husband her own eager look has driven off.
— Apuleius, Metamorphoses (The Golden Ass) 5.22-24, Cupid and Psyche (paraphrase) [10]
In Apuleius’s tale, Psyche’s longing to see is the very thing that costs her the beloved and leaves her in tears.
As in the Kural, the eyes that rushed to look are the same eyes that must now weep.
Philosophy
Hobbes defines laughter as ‘sudden glory’, a passion that arises when we suddenly see some defect or absurdity and feel ourselves above it.
— Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, ch. 6 (paraphrase) [11]
On Hobbes’s account we laugh at folly when we catch it plainly in view.
The heroine’s laughter at her eyes fits Hobbes’s description: she stands back and sees their folly, even though it is her own.

Real Life Example

Berlioz's obsession with the actress Harriet Smithson
1827-1833 · Paris, France
In 1827 the composer Hector Berlioz saw the Irish actress Harriet Smithson play Ophelia in an English-language Hamlet in Paris and became fixated on her. He waited at the stage door, bombarded her with love letters that she ignored, and rented rooms near her theatre, though neither spoke the other's language. He poured the unrequited passion into his Symphonie fantastique, premiered in December 1830; the two married in October 1833, but the marriage was unhappy and they later agreed to live apart.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Berlioz's headlong pursuit, set off by a single look at an actress on stage, became one of music history's most famous examples of love at first sight turning into torment.
The kural laughs at eyes that rushed of their own accord to look and now of their own accord weep, and Berlioz's eager gaze at the stage and the years of anguish that followed fit that rueful humour.
