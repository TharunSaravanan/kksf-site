Kural 159

The Possession of Patience · Poraiyudaimai · பொறையுடைமை

துறந்தாரின் தூய்மை உடையர் இறந்தார்வாய் இன்னாச்சொல் நோற்கிற் பவர்.
Thurandhaarin thooimai udaiyar irandhaarvaai, Innaachchol norkir pavar.

Meaning

People who can calmly take the harsh words of those who go too far are as pure as those who have given up the world.

Elementary

Monks and nuns who give up everything are often thought of as very pure. This kural says you don't have to leave home to be that pure. If someone yells nasty words at you and you stay calm, your heart is just as clean as theirs.

University

இறந்தார் here doesn't mean 'the dead.' It means 'those who have transgressed' (from இற, to go beyond), the same root as இறப்பு in Kural 152. Mu. Varadarasan and Kalaignar confirm this sense, though some popular glosses take it literally. The comparison with துறந்தார் ('renunciants') matters because these chapters belong to இல்லறவியல், the section on the householder's life: Valluvar claims that a householder who endures insult reaches the purity normally reserved for ascetics. Solomon Pappaiah makes this explicit, saying that even living a household life, such a person is as pure as a renunciant. The verb நோற்றல் ('to endure as an austerity') anticipates the next couplet's comparison with fasting and recasts patience itself as a form of tapas.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him patiently bear hard words, let him not insult anybody, and let him not become anybody's enemy for the sake of this (perishable) body. Against an angry man let him not in return show anger, let him bless when he is cursed”
— Manusmṛti 6.47-48 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
Among the rules for the wandering renunciant (sannyāsin), Manu requires him to bear harsh words patiently and to bless those who curse him.
Bearing harsh words is a defining discipline of the renunciant. The kural says a householder who does the same is equally pure.
Christianity
“Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously:”
— 1 Peter 2:23 (KJV) [5]
Peter holds up Christ, who did not answer insult with insult, as the model of holy conduct under abuse.
Bearing abusive words without answering is the mark of the purest life, as in the kural.
Islam
“You will surely be tested in your possessions and in yourselves. And you will surely hear from those who were given the Scripture before you and from those who associate others with Allāh much abuse. But if you are patient and fear Allāh - indeed, that is of the matters [worthy] of resolve.”
— Qur'an 3:186 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an warns believers that they will hear much hurtful speech, and says that patience with it, joined with God-consciousness, is a mark of true resolve.
Enduring hurtful words (இன்னாச்சொல்) is treated as a high spiritual achievement, as in the kural.
Judaism
“About those who are insulted and do not insult, who hear their shame and do not respond, who act out of love and are joyful in suffering, the verse says: ‘And they that love Him are as the sun going forth in its might’ (Judges 5:31).”
— Talmud Bavli, Shabbat 88b (William Davidson/Koren translation) [7]
The Talmud praises those who hear themselves shamed without answering, comparing them to the radiant rising sun.
Shining like the sun, the silent bearer of insult has a purity like the kural's renunciant.
Buddhism
“Just as when people throw what is clean or unclean on the earth — feces, urine, saliva, pus, or blood — the earth is not horrified, humiliated, or disgusted by it”
— Mahārāhulovāda Sutta, Majjhima Nikāya 62 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [8]
The Buddha teaches the young monk Rahula to make his mind like the earth, which isn't defiled by whatever filth is thrown on it.
A mind that foul words can't stain is the renunciant's purity the kural describes.
Jainism
“Ceasing to use the stick (i.e. cruelty) against living beings, abandoning the care of the body, the houseless (Mahâvîra), the Venerable One, endures the thorns of the villages (i.e. the abusive language of the peasants), (being) perfectly enlightened.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.8.3.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Ācārāṅga's account of Mahāvīra's wanderings in the rough country of Lāḍha shows the great renunciant meeting abuse with endurance instead of retaliation.
Valluvar says that those who bear the harsh words of transgressors are as pure as renunciants, and the Ācārāṅga shows the model renunciant himself enduring the 'thorns' of abusive speech.
Sikhism
“Those who are patient abide in patience; in this way, they burn their bodies. They are close to the Lord, but they do not reveal their secret to anyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, Salok 116 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sheikh Farid says the patient make patience itself their austerity, and so come close to God.
The Kural says those who bear insults from transgressors are as pure as renunciants; Farid likewise treats patience as an inner austerity that brings one near the divine.
Greek Mythology
“he resolved, however, to endure it and keep himself in check”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book XVII (trans. Samuel Butler) [11]
On the road home, the goatherd Melanthius reviles the disguised Odysseus with outrageous language and kicks him. Odysseus considers striking back but chooses to endure.
Bearing the insolent words of a man who overstepped all bounds reflects the kural's இறந்தார்வாய் இன்னாச்சொல் நோற்றல்.
Philosophy
“Remember, that not he who gives ill language or a blow insults, but the principle which represents these things as insulting.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 20 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [12]
Epictetus teaches that harsh words have power over us only if we accept them as injuries. The disciplined mind stays untouched.
A mind that insulting words can't stain has the inner purity the kural credits to those who bear harsh speech.

Real Life Example

Haile Selassie addresses the League of Nations
30 June 1936 · Geneva, Switzerland
Emperor Haile Selassie of Ethiopia, driven into exile by Italy's invasion of his country, came to address the Assembly of the League of Nations. As he was introduced, Italian journalists in the press gallery jeered and shouted insults and had to be removed; the emperor waited for them to be ejected and then delivered his speech in Amharic. He described the invasion, including the use of chemical weapons against his people, and asked what the League intended to do; the League took no effective action.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The speech became one of the most remembered appeals of the interwar years, and the emperor's composure in the face of jeering contrasted with the collapse of collective security it exposed.
The kural says those who patiently bear the harsh words of people who overstep every limit are as pure as renunciants; Haile Selassie bore the insults of his invaders' supporters without answering in kind.
