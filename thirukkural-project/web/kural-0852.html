Kural 852

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

பகல்கருதிப் பற்றா செயினும் இகல்கருதி இன்னாசெய் யாமை தலை.
Pakalkarudhip patraa seyinum ikalkarudhi innaasey yaamai thalai.

Meaning

Even if someone who wants to break away from you does things that hurt you, the best thing is not to hurt them back out of hostility.

Elementary

Suppose a classmate stops being your friend and does mean little things to you. You could plan to get back at them, but the poet says the best thing is not to. Not paying back meanness with meanness is what a really strong person does.

University

The couplet is built on a symmetry of motives: the other person acts pakal karudhi (பகல் கருதி, intending separation) and does patraa (பற்றா, unloving or disagreeable things), while you are warned against acting ikal karudhi (இகல் கருதி, out of hostility) and doing innaa (இன்னா, harmful things). The final word thalai (தலை, ‘head’, foremost) marks this restraint as the supreme course. Puliyur Kesigan and Mu. Varadarasanar both read it as forbearance under provocation; Solomon Pappaiah calls it a ‘superior quality’ (சிறந்த குணம்). The thought echoes the Aram chapters on Innaa Seyyaamai (ch. 32), especially Kural 314, but here it is set in Porutpal as practical statecraft: retaliation turns disagreement into lasting enmity. Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who is not envious but is a kind friend to all living entities, who does not think himself a proprietor and is free from false ego, who is equal in both happiness and distress, who is tolerant ... such a devotee of Mine is very dear to Me.”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.13-14 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes the ideal devotee as without hatred (adveṣṭā), friendly and forgiving toward all.
The Kural asks the same refusal of hatred even toward those who wrong us.
Christianity
“Recompense to no man evil for evil. Provide things honest in the sight of all men.”
— Romans 12:17 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells believers never to repay harm with harm.
This is nearly a word-for-word restatement of the Kural’s counsel.
Islam
“And the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation - his reward is [due] from Allah.”
— Qur’an 42:40 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an permits equal retaliation but ranks pardon and reconciliation higher.
Like the Kural, it names restraint, not retaliation, as the better path.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart ... Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.”
— Leviticus 19:17-18 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
The Torah forbids both inner hatred and outward revenge.
The Kural similarly forbids acting on hostility even when provoked.
Buddhism
“‘He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.’ Those who do not harbor such thoughts still their hatred.”
— Dhammapada 4 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that letting go of the record of wrongs is how hatred ends.
The Kural likewise asks us not to let another’s offences become our motive to harm.
Jainism
“If a layman abuses a monk, he should not grow angry against him; because he would be like a child, a monk should not grow angry. … A monk should not be angry if beaten, nor should he therefore entertain sinful thoughts; knowing patience to be the highest good, a monk should meditate on the Law.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.24 and 2.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Among the troubles a monk must bear, being abused and being struck are to be met with patience and without any thought of retaliation, since patience is the highest good.
The Kural says that even when someone deliberately does you harm, the highest course is not to strike back in hostility, and Mahavira's rule for abuse and blows says the same.
Sikhism
“Fareed, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 7), Bhagat Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, Sheikh Farid tells the seeker not to hit back at those who hit him.
The Kural says that even when someone does you harm out of a wish to break with you, the best course is not to harm them in return; Farid gives the same rule of non-retaliation.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad 24, Achilles, whose friend Patroclus was killed by Hector, receives Hector’s grieving father Priam, weeps with him, and gives back Hector’s body instead of continuing to dishonour it.
— Homer, Iliad 24 [9]
The greatest warrior finally sets aside his rage against an enemy who wronged him.
Achilles shows, late and at great cost, the restraint the Kural asks for from the start.
Philosophy
In the Crito, Socrates argues that one must never do wrong, and therefore must never return wrong for wrong or do evil to anyone, whatever one has suffered from them.
— Plato, Crito 49b-d [10]
Socrates rejects retaliation as itself a form of wrongdoing.
The Kural reaches the same conclusion: provocation never licenses harm.

Real Life Example

The Greensboro sit-in at the Woolworth lunch counter
1 February 1960 · Greensboro, North Carolina, United States
On 1 February 1960 four students from North Carolina A&T, Ezell Blair Jr., Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond, sat down at the whites-only lunch counter of the Woolworth store in Greensboro and politely asked to be served. When they were refused and asked to leave, they stayed quietly in their seats. Over the following six months hundreds of students and community members joined the protest and a boycott, and the counter was desegregated on 25 July 1960.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The sit-in sparked a wave of nonviolent sit-ins across the South and showed the power of patient, peaceful refusal in the face of injustice.
The kural teaches that even when others do us wrong, the highest course is not to hurt them in return, and the students answered refusal and exclusion with calm, nonviolent persistence.
