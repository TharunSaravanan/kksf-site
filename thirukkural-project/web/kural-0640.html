Kural 640

Ministry of State · Amaichu · அமைச்சு

முறைப்படச் சூழ்ந்தும் முடிவிலவே செய்வர் திறப்பாடு இலாஅ தவர்.
Muraippadach soozhndhum mudivilavae seyvar Thirappaadu ilaaa thavar.

Meaning

People without the skill to get things done will leave their work unfinished, even when they have planned it the right way.

Elementary

You can draw a perfect plan for a sandcastle, but if you do not know how to pack the sand, it falls apart halfway. This kural says a good plan is not enough; an adviser also needs the skill to finish the job.

University

Muraippadach soozhndhum means ‘even having deliberated in due order’, where soozhchi is the technical term for counsel. Such people mudivilavae seyvar, ‘do only things without completion’, if they are thirappaadu ilaadhavar, lacking executive ability. Ilaaa carries a prosodic lengthening (alapedai) needed for the metre. The thirukkural-thamizh.org edition prints ‘thirappaa delaaa thavar’, the older joined spelling of thirappaadu + ilaaa. Varadarasanar and Pappaiah take mudivila to mean half-done or defective work, while Kesikan reads it as ‘endless’, never-concluding activity. Closing the chapter this way balances its stress on deliberation. Counsel without execution fails, which leads into the later chapters on action (vinaiththooimai, vinaiththitpam, vinaiseyalvagai, 66-68).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A man engaged in devotional service rids himself of both good and bad reactions even in this life. Therefore strive for yoga, which is the art of all work.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.50 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
Krishna’s phrase yogah karmasu kaushalam, often rendered ‘yoga is skill in action’, makes skilful execution itself a spiritual ideal.
The Kural likewise says that without skill in action (thirappaadu) even good planning fails.
Christianity
“For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it? Lest haply, after he hath laid the foundation, and is not able to finish it, all that behold it begin to mock him, Saying, This man began to build, and was not able to finish.”
— Luke 14:28-30 (KJV) [5]
Jesus pictures a builder who lays foundations but cannot complete the tower and becomes a laughing stock.
This is the Kural’s mudivila: work begun but never finished.
Islam
“O you who have believed, why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do.”
— Qur’an 61:2-3 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an condemns people whose words and plans are not carried into action.
This parallels the Kural’s rebuke of those who plan well but cannot complete the deed.
Judaism
“Study is not the most important thing, but actions; whoever indulges in too many words brings about sin.”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 1:17 (trans. Joshua Kulp) [7]
Shimon ben Gamliel teaches that what counts is doing, not merely learning or talking.
Like the Kural, it ranks carrying things out above deliberation alone.
Buddhism
“Like a beautiful flower full of colour but without fragrance, even so, fruitless are the fair words of one who does not practise them.”
— Dhammapada 51 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada compares good words that are not practised to a flower that looks fine but has no scent.
In the same way, well-made plans that are never carried out, as the Kural warns, bear no fruit.
Jainism
“Just as bad bullocks are when put before a car, so are bad pupils when yoked, as it were, to the car of the Law; they break down through want of zeal.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 27.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The teacher Garga compares bad pupils to unruly bullocks that break down on the road, failing to carry through what they were set to do.
The Kural says those who lack the ability to carry things through leave their work unfinished even after proper planning; Garga's bullocks and pupils likewise break down short of the goal for lack of zeal.
Sikhism
“We are good at talking, but our actions are bad.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 85, Siree Raag (Siree Raag ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a salok of Siree Raag ki Vaar, Guru Nanak confesses the gap between fine words and the deeds that should follow them.
The Kural says those without the skill to carry things through leave their work unfinished even after good planning; Guru Nanak likewise names the gap between fine words and actual deeds.
Greek Mythology
In Plato’s myth, Epimetheus asks to hand out the gods’ gifts to the animals himself. He shares out speed, strength, fur and claws with careful balance, but uses them all up before he reaches human beings, who are left naked and defenceless until Prometheus steals fire for them.
— Plato, Protagoras 320d-321c [11]
Epimetheus (‘afterthought’) carries out a well-intended plan without the skill to finish it properly.
He shows the Kural’s person who plans properly but leaves the work incomplete.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that virtue has to be active, not just possessed: just as at the Olympic Games it is not the finest and strongest who are crowned but those who compete, so in life it is those who act rightly who win the prize.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.8 (trans. W. D. Ross) [12]
For Aristotle, capacity or intention without effective action achieves nothing.
The Kural similarly says that right deliberation without skill in execution is worthless.

Real Life Example

Babbage's Difference Engine No. 1 is left unfinished
1821-1842 · London, United Kingdom
From 1821 Charles Babbage designed Difference Engine No. 1, the first complete design for an automatic calculating engine, and the British government paid for its construction. Only a demonstration section of about 2,000 parts, one-seventh of the whole, was completed in 1832; work halted after a dispute with his engineer Joseph Clement, and the government finally abandoned the project in 1842 after spending about 17,500 pounds. When the Science Museum later built his Difference Engine No. 2 from his drawings, completing the calculating section in 1991, the machine worked.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Babbage's designs were sound, as the modern reconstruction proved, but the project failed in the practical business of managing the engineer, the costs and the work through to completion.
The kural says that those who lack the skill to carry things through leave their work unfinished even when they have planned it properly, and the brilliantly designed but never-completed Difference Engine is a striking case.
