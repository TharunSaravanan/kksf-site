Kural 393

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

கண்ணுடையர் என்பவர் கற்றோர் முகத்திரண்டு புண்ணுடையர் கல்லா தவர்.
Kannudaiyar enbavar katror mugaththirandu Punnudaiyar kallaa thavar.

Meaning

Only educated people can truly be said to have eyes. People without learning just have two sores on their face.

Elementary

Two holes in your face are not the same as seeing. If you never learn anything, your eyes look at the world but understand nothing. Valluvar says a person who learns has real eyes.

University

Continuing the eye metaphor of Kural 392, Valluvar reaches one of the harshest images in the chapter. The unlearned person's eyes are புண் (pun), wounds or sores: openings in the face that do nothing and may even be painful. The contrast rests on the difference between physical sight and understanding. Kalaignar's commentary draws the corollary that a learned person without physical sight still 'has eyes', while an unlearned person with sight does not. The rhetoric is deliberately provocative. It belongs to a statecraft section meant to push kings and citizens toward education, and the following chapter, Kallaamai (Ignorance), develops the same harshness. Similar contrasts between seeing eyes and a seeing mind run through the Gospels, the Qur'an and Plato.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A famous verse of Bhartrhari's Nitishataka says that a person without literature, music and the arts is a beast in plain sight, only lacking a tail and horns.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka (verse beginning “sāhitya-saṅgīta-kalā-vihīnaḥ”) [3]
Bhartrhari, the Sanskrit poet-grammarian, uses shock imagery to insist that culture and learning are what make someone fully human.
Same rhetorical strategy as the Kural: the unlearned person keeps an outwardly human form but lacks what that form is for, just as a face may carry 'sores' instead of eyes.
Christianity
“Therefore speak I to them in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand.”
— Matthew 13:13 (KJV) [4]
Jesus describes people whose eyes work perfectly but who still fail to perceive the truth in front of them.
Like the Kural, it separates having eyes from truly seeing. Understanding is what turns sight into vision.
Islam
“For indeed, it is not eyes that are blinded, but blinded are the hearts which are within the breasts.”
— Qur'an 22:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an says that true blindness is in the heart and mind that fail to understand, not in the physical eyes.
This strongly parallels the Kural: physical eyes do not count as sight without inner understanding.
Judaism
“A brute is not sin-fearing, nor is an ignorant person pious.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:5 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Hillel teaches that a person with no learning at all (bur, 'empty field') cannot even reach basic moral seriousness, and an unlettered person cannot be truly pious.
Like the Kural, it sees ignorance as a real deficiency in the person, not a neutral state. Its focus is on piety rather than 'sight'.
Buddhism
“The man of little learning grows old like a bull. He grows only in bulk, but, his wisdom does not grow.”
— Dhammapada 152 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares an unlearned person to an ox that gets bigger with age but never gets wiser.
Like the Kural, it uses a blunt image to show that a body without learning is a diminished life.
Jainism
“The saints have scriptures as their eyes; all the living beings have sense-organs as their eyes; the gods have clairvoyance as their eyes; and the Siddhas have eyes in every way.”
— Pravacanasara III.234 (cf. III.233) (Kundakunda, tr. A.N. Upadhye; jainworld.com) [8]
Kundakunda ranks kinds of 'eyes'. Ordinary beings see only with the senses, while the ascetic sees through scripture (agama-cakshu). The previous verse adds that a monk who lacks scriptural study 'does not know his self'.
Valluvar says only the learned truly have eyes, and the unlearned carry two sores in their face. Kundakunda likewise treats learning as the real eye and sense-sight as the lowest kind of seeing.
Sikhism
“Do not call them blind, who have no eyes in their face. They alone are blind, O Nanak, who wander away from their Lord and Master.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 954, Raag Raamkalee (Ramkali ki Vaar, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in Ramkali ki Vaar Guru Angad redefines blindness: the truly blind are not those without physical sight but those who have strayed from the Lord.
The Kural says only the learned truly have eyes and the unlearned merely carry two sores on their face; Guru Angad makes the same move of denying that physical eyes are real sight.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles' Oedipus the King, the blind prophet Tiresias sees the truth, while Oedipus, with perfect eyesight, is blind to who he is. When Oedipus finally understands, he puts out his own eyes.
— Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus (esp. the Tiresias scene, lines 300–462, and the self-blinding) [10]
The tragedy is built on the reversal of sight and insight. The man with eyes cannot see, and the eyeless seer knows everything.
This closely parallels the Kural. Eyes without understanding are useless or even wounds, and understanding is the real sight. Oedipus literally turns his eyes into wounds.
Philosophy
In Plato's Allegory of the Cave, prisoners watch shadows on a wall and take them for reality. Education is the painful turning of the soul toward the light, so that it finally sees what is real.
— Plato, Republic VII, 514a–518d [11]
Plato defines education not as putting sight into blind eyes but as turning the soul's existing 'eye' toward the truth.
Like the Kural, Plato holds that the uneducated have eyes that do not truly see. Learning is what makes vision real.

Real Life Example

Louis Braille devises a reading system for the blind
1824 · Royal Institute for Blind Youth, Paris, France
Louis Braille, blinded as a small child, entered the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris in 1819, where the library held only a few cumbersome books in embossed letters. Adapting a raised-dot system shown to the school by Charles Barbier, he worked out by the age of 15 a code of six-dot cells that blind people could read quickly with their fingertips and write with a simple tool. He published the system in 1829, and it was later adopted around the world.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Braille's code gave blind people direct access to reading and writing, showing that the power to learn does not depend on physical sight.
The kural says that only the learned truly have eyes, and Braille, who could not see, gave countless blind people those eyes of learning.
