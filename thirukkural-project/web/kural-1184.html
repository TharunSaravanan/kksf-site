Kural 1184

The Pallid Hue · Pasapparu Paruvaral · பசப்பறு பருவரல்

உள்ளுவன் மன்யான் உரைப்பது அவர்திறமால் கள்ளம் பிறவோ பசப்பு.
ulluvan manyaan uraippadhu avardhiramaal kallam piravo pasappu

Meaning

I think only of him and speak only of his goodness, yet the paleness has come anyway. Isn't that a trick?

Elementary

Imagine you did all your homework and studied hard, and still got a bad mark. You’d say, ‘That’s not fair, I did everything right!’ The wife says the same: she thinks about her husband all day and only says good things about him, yet the sad pale colour still came. It feels like a trick.

University

The heroine protests that she has kept faith in thought and speech: uḷḷuvan maṉ yāṉ (உள்ளுவன் மன் யான், ‘I do indeed think of him’), uraippatu avar tiṟam (உரைப்பது அவர் திறம், ‘what I speak is his worth’), so the pallor must be kaḷḷam (கள்ளம், ‘deceit, theft’) or come by some other way (piṟavō). The implied logic, made explicit by the commentators, is that constant remembrance should keep the absent one ‘present’, so the pallor of separation has no right to appear. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah render kaḷḷam as vañcaṉai/vañcakam (treachery); Kalaignar instead asks how the pallor came ‘without my knowing or by some other path’. Pope: ‘I meditate his words, his worth is theme of all I say, / This sickly hue is false that would my trust betray.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Uddhava brings Krishna’s message to Vraja, the gopis can speak of nothing but Krishna’s deeds and qualities, remembering every pastime with him, and yet their constant remembrance only deepens the grief of his absence.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata’s gopis show a love that lives on unceasing remembrance and praise of the absent beloved.
The Kural’s heroine also thinks and speaks only of her husband’s virtues, and is still overtaken by the pallor of separation.
Christianity
“I remembered God, and was troubled: I complained, and my spirit was overwhelmed. Selah.”
— Psalm 77:3 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist finds that remembering the one he longs for brings not comfort but trouble.
The heroine likewise remembers her beloved constantly and is surprised that remembrance does not keep sorrow away.
Islam
“They said, ‘By Allāh, you will not cease remembering Joseph until you become fatally ill or become of those who perish.’”
— Qur’an 12:85 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s sons warn him that his unceasing remembrance of the absent Joseph is wasting his body.
The Kural’s heroine also never stops remembering her beloved, and that very remembrance has not spared her body from wasting pallor.
Judaism
“His mouth is most sweet; Yea, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem.”
— Song of Songs 5:16 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Separated from him and asked what makes him special, the bride answers with a long praise of her beloved’s every quality.
Like the bride, the Kural’s heroine says that all she speaks of is her absent husband’s excellence.
Buddhism
“Whatever a monk keeps pursuing with his thinking & pondering, that becomes the inclination of his awareness.”
— Dvedhavitakka Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 19 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha observes that whatever the mind dwells on repeatedly shapes the whole bent of a person.
The heroine dwells on her husband all day, and the Buddhist view would say that this very dwelling bends her toward the grief she complains of.
Jainism
Among the forms of arta-dhyana (sorrowful, afflicted meditation) the Tattvartha Sutra lists the mind’s repeated dwelling on how to regain something agreeable from which one has been separated.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.31 (on arta-dhyana) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain psychology names constant longing for a lost beloved object as a kind of painful concentration that binds karma.
The heroine’s ceaseless thinking about her absent husband is exactly this ‘afflicted meditation’, and the Jain view would see her pallor as its fruit rather than a trick.
Sikhism
“I sing His Glories, I describe His Glories, I speak of His Glories, O my mother.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 40, Sri Raag, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das gives the voice of a soul-bride who fills her speech with the virtues of her Beloved, talking of nothing else.
The Kural's wife protests that she thinks only of him and speaks only of his goodness, yet the pallor came anyway; the Sikh soul-bride likewise speaks only of the Beloved's virtues, though in Gurbani this praise is the way to union rather than something the pallor cheats.
Greek Mythology
While her husband Ceyx is away at sea, Alcyone counts the nights, prays daily to Juno for his safe return and thinks of nothing but him, unaware that he has already drowned.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.573-582 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Alcyone keeps faith through constant thought and prayer for an absent husband.
Like Alcyone, the Kural’s heroine thinks only of her absent husband, and still sorrow finds its way to her.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines longing (desiderium) as the desire to possess something that is kept alive by remembering it, while the remembrance of other things excludes its existence.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Affects 32 (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, remembering an absent beloved breeds not satisfaction but a sorrowful longing.
This explains the heroine’s puzzle: her constant remembrance of him is precisely what feeds the longing that turns her pale.

Real Life Example

Queen Artemisia builds the Mausoleum for King Mausolus
c. 353-350 BCE · Halicarnassus, Caria (modern Bodrum, Turkey)
Artemisia II was the sister and wife of King Mausolus of Caria, and after his death around 353 BCE she ruled alone for about three years. She built for him at Halicarnassus the great tomb called the Mausoleum, which was counted among the Seven Wonders of the World. Artemisia died before it was completed and was buried there with her husband; later the Romans called any grand tomb a mausoleum after it.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Artemisia devoted her short reign to a monument that kept her husband's name before the world, which it still does in the word mausoleum.
The kural's wife says she thinks only of her husband and speaks only of his worth, and Artemisia turned her whole reign into a monument to her husband's name.
