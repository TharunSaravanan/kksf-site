Kural 1141

The Announcement of the Rumour · Alararivuruthal · அலரறிவுறுத்தல்

அலரெழ ஆருயிர் நிற்கும் அதனைப் பலரறியார் பாக்கியத் தால்.
alarezha aaruyir niRkum adhanaip palaraRiyaar paakkiyath thaal

Meaning

Now that the town gossips about our love, my precious life, which was slipping away, holds on. Luckily, the gossips do not know their talk is what keeps me alive.

Elementary

Imagine you have a secret friend you are not allowed to play with, and you feel very sad. Then the other children start teasing, ‘You two are best friends!’ Strangely, the teasing makes you feel better, because now everyone treats you as a pair. The young man in this verse feels just like that, and he is glad the teasers have not worked out that they are helping him.

University

Chapter 115, alar aṟivuṟuttal (அலர் அறிவுறுத்தல்), concerns alar (அலர்) and its synonym kauvai (கௌவை), the village gossip that breaks out once a secret courtship (kaḷavu) becomes known; Parimelazhagar’s preface says the chapter has the hero telling the confidante (tōḻi) how the rumour serves him, and the heroine and tōḻi telling the hero of it to press him toward marriage (varaivu) or elopement (uṭanpōkku), and that it follows the chapter on abandoning reserve because it arises once nāṇ is given up. Parimelazhagar sets this couplet as the hero’s reply when the tōḻi, after a failed tryst, uses the rumour to urge marriage: the rumour makes the hard-to-win girl seem attainable, so his ‘precious life’ (ār uyir) has a support (paṟṟukkōṭu); pākkiyam (பாக்கியம்) is glossed teyvam, ‘fate, providence’, which keeps the gossips from realising this, for if they knew they would fall silent and his life would go. Manakkudavar instead reads ār uyir as the heroine’s life. The text is identical on ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net and thirukkural.com; Pope renders ‘By this same rumour’s rise, my precious life stands fast.’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Gita Govinda opens with Nanda telling Radha to take Krishna home through the darkening forest, and the poem then follows the two lovers’ meeting by the Yamuna, away from the eyes of the village.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, canto 1, opening verse (paraphrase) [4]
Jayadeva’s poem treats Radha and Krishna’s love as something lived on the edge of village knowledge, half hidden and half known.
The Kural’s lover lives in that same space, where a secret love becomes the talk of the village and the talk turns out to sustain it.
Christianity
When Joseph learns that Mary, to whom he is betrothed, is with child, he resolves to put her away privately rather than make her a public example.
— Matthew 1:19 (KJV) (paraphrase) [5]
Matthew shows a betrothed man weighing what public talk would do to the woman he is bound to.
The Kural’s lover faces public talk about his beloved too, though for him the talk is welcome and keeps him alive.
Islam
“And women in the city said, ‘The wife of al-ʿAzeez is seeking to seduce her slave boy; he has impassioned her with love. Indeed, we see her [to be] in clear error.’”
— Qur’an 12:30 (tr. Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an records the gossip of the city’s women about Zulaikha’s love for Joseph.
This is the same alar, a love that has become the talk of the town, though here the gossip is pure censure and does nothing to sustain the lover.
Judaism
“And she lay at his feet until the morning; and she rose up before one could discern another. For he said: ‘Let it not be known that the woman came to the threshing-floor.’”
— Ruth 3:14 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [7]
Boaz takes care that his night meeting with Ruth does not become the talk of the town before he can marry her properly.
Boaz fears exactly the alar the Kural’s lover welcomes, which shows how unusual the Tamil hero’s attitude is.
Buddhism
When rival wanderers spread a slanderous rumour about the Buddha after the murder of the wanderer Sundari, the monks are reviled in the town, and the Buddha tells them the noise will not last long and will vanish after seven days.
— Udana 4.8 (Sundari Sutta) (paraphrase) [8]
The Buddha treats public talk as a passing noise that a calm mind can wait out.
The Kural’s lover clings to the town’s talk as his lifeline; the Buddha teaches that such talk has no lasting power to give life or take it away.
Jainism
Jacobi’s note on the Kapila lecture retells the commentary story: the student Kapila fell in love with the servant girl who waited on him and, to buy her ornaments for a festival, set out at night to beg two pieces of gold, was arrested, and was brought before the king.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 8, Jacobi’s introductory note (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [9]
The Jain commentary uses a young man’s love affair, which ends in public exposure before the king, as the starting point of his conversion.
Like the Kural’s hero, Kapila’s love is exposed to public view; the Jain tale, however, turns that exposure toward renunciation rather than marriage.
Sikhism
“Slander me, slander me - go ahead, people, and slander me. ... The slanderer wishes me long life.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 339, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir welcomes the talk people spread about him, saying that the slanderer, without meaning to, actually wants him to live.
The Kural's lover says the town's gossip keeps his ebbing life from slipping away, though the gossips do not know it; Kabir likewise finds that the slander aimed at him only gives him life.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod warns that Talk (Pheme) is light and easily raised but hard to be rid of, and that talk voiced by many people never wholly dies away, for she too is in some way a goddess.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 760-764 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H.G. Evelyn-White) [11]
For Hesiod, rumour is almost a divine power with a life of its own once many mouths carry it.
The Kural’s lover experiences that self-sustaining life of rumour, but finds in it a power that keeps him alive rather than one to fear.
Philosophy
Stendhal observes that a very small degree of hope is enough to give birth to love, and that love can then survive for a while on almost nothing.
— Stendhal, On Love (De l’amour, 1822), early chapters on the birth of love (paraphrase) [12]
In De l’amour, love lives on hope, and even slight evidence can keep it alive.
The town’s rumour gives the Kural’s lover exactly that small hope, and it is enough to keep his life from slipping away.

Real Life Example

The 'Mitchy boom' greets Crown Prince Akihito's engagement to Michiko Shoda
November 1958-April 1959 · Tokyo, Japan
Crown Prince Akihito met Michiko Shoda, the daughter of a flour-milling businessman, at a tennis match in Karuizawa in the summer of 1957, and the Imperial Household Agency announced their engagement in November 1958. Some conservatives objected to a commoner becoming crown princess, but the public took her to heart: a 'Mitchy boom' swept the country, women's magazines covered her closely, and television sales doubled as people prepared to watch the wedding. They married on 10 April 1959 before crowds of more than half a million along the parade route.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The public's warm talk and enthusiasm surrounded a match that broke with tradition and gave it strong popular support.
In the kural the lover says that the town's gossip about the romance is what keeps him alive; for Akihito and Michiko, the nation's eager talk was a source of strength rather than shame.
