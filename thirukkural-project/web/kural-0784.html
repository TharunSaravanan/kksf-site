Kural 784

Friendship · Natpu · நட்பு

நகுதற் பொருட்டன்று நட்டல் மிகுதிக்கண் மேற்சென்று இடித்தற் பொருட்டு.
Nagudhar poruttandru nattal migudhikkan merchendru idiththar poruttu.

Meaning

We make friends not just to laugh together, but so that when a friend goes the wrong way, we can step in first and correct them.

Elementary

Imagine your friend is about to cross the road without looking. A pretend friend just keeps laughing with them. A real friend grabs their arm and says, ‘Stop! That’s dangerous!’ even if it makes them a bit cross. Friends are for fun, but most of all they are for stopping each other from doing something wrong.

University

This is the chapter’s key statement on the moral purpose of friendship. Nagudhal (நகுதல்) is laughing or merriment; nattal (நட்டல்) is the act of befriending; migudhi (மிகுதி) means excess, that is, a friend’s overstepping or wrongdoing; mēRsenRu (மேற்சென்று) means ‘going ahead, taking the initiative’; and iditthal (இடித்தல், literally ‘striking’) is the classical term for sharp rebuke, related to iḍipparai (இடிப்பார், the rebuking counsellor) in kural 448. Parimelazhagar stresses that the friend must rebuke before the fault grows, without waiting to be asked. Both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print மேற்செனறு, which looks like a shared typing error; the standard reading மேற்சென்று (as in Mu. Varadarasanar’s ‘முற்பட்டுச் சென்று’ gloss) is given here.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“They are abundant, O king, that can always speak agreeable words. The speaker, however, is rare, as also the hearer, of words that are disagreeable but medicinal.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section XXXVII (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura tells Dhritarashtra that flatterers are everywhere, but rare is the one who will speak, or listen to, unwelcome words that heal.
Vidura is the friend the Kural describes, who rebukes for the other’s good rather than merely pleasing him.
Christianity
“Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says a friend’s painful honesty can be trusted, while an enemy’s show of affection cannot.
This is the Kural’s point exactly: a friend is there to strike when needed, not to keep you laughing.
Islam
“Help your brother, whether he is an oppressor or he is an oppressed one. People asked, ‘O Allah’s Messenger! It is all right to help him if he is oppressed, but how should we help him if he is an oppressor?’ The Prophet said, ‘By preventing him from oppressing others.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 2444 [5]
The Prophet teaches that helping a brother who does wrong means stopping him.
As in the Kural, true loyalty means stepping in to stop a friend when he goes too far.
Judaism
“You shall not hate your kinsfolk in your heart. Reprove your kindred but incur no guilt on their account.”
— Leviticus 19:17 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition) [6]
The Torah commands open rebuke instead of silent resentment, so that you do not share in another’s wrongdoing.
Like the Kural, it makes rebuke a duty of love rather than a breach of it.
Buddhism
“Should one find a man who points out faults and who reproves, let him follow such a wise and sagacious person as one would a guide to hidden treasure. It is always better, and never worse, to cultivate such an association.”
— Dhammapada 76 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada tells us to value the person who points out our faults as we would a guide to buried treasure.
This is the rebuking friend of the Kural, and the advice to keep such friends close.
Jainism
“As the virtuous Kitra, for old friendship's sake, loved the king who was attached to sensual pleasures, and as he had at heart his welfare, he spoke to him the following words”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 13.15 (Kitra and Sambhûta; tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The monk Kitra, who had been King Brahmadatta's companion through several past births, meets him again and, out of that old friendship, bluntly warns him that his pleasures lead to suffering and urges him to give them up.
The Kural says friendship exists to step forward and rebuke a friend who goes too far, and Kitra does exactly that, speaking against his friend's excess because he cares about his welfare.
Sikhism
According to the Janamsakhi tradition, Guru Nanak stayed at the inn of Sajjan at Tulamba, a man who posed as pious but robbed his guests. Instead of flattering his host, the Guru sang a hymn exposing hollow shininess (now in the Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 729); Sajjan confessed, gave away his ill-gotten goods and turned his house into a dharamsala.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism, 'Sajjan, Shaikh' and 'Tulamba' (paraphrase of the Janamsakhi tradition) [9]
The story shows the Guru as a guest who took the initiative to confront a host's wrongdoing, and so saved him.
The Kural says friendship exists so that we can step in first and rebuke a friend who goes too far; Guru Nanak's hymn to Sajjan was just such a timely rebuke that turned him back.
Greek Mythology
At the moment Orestes falters before killing his mother, he turns to his silent friend Pylades and asks what to do; Pylades, speaking for the only time in the play, reminds him of Apollo’s command and tells him to hold all men his enemies rather than the gods.
— Aeschylus, Libation Bearers 899-902 [10]
Pylades says nothing through the whole play until his friend wavers, and then speaks firmly to hold him to his duty.
Pylades steps forward at the crucial moment, as the Kural says a friend should.
Philosophy
“If we remonstrate, it should be without bitterness; if we reprove, there should be no word of insult. … ‘There are people who owe more to bitter enemies than to apparently pleasant friends: the former often speak the truth, the latter never.’”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 24 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Laelius insists that friends must reprove one another, gently but honestly, and quotes Cato on the danger of merely pleasant friends.
Cicero and the Kural agree that a friendship that only pleases has failed its purpose.

Real Life Example

Ezra Pound urges T. S. Eliot to cut The Waste Land
1922 · Europe
Before The Waste Land was published in 1922, T. S. Eliot submitted his drafts to his friend and fellow poet Ezra Pound, who had championed Eliot's work since they met in London in 1914. Pound urged Eliot to cut several long passages, among them a long section in heroic couplets imitating Pope, and Eliot accepted much of his advice, though not all of it. The drafts with Pound's markings were later found among the papers of the New York lawyer John Quinn and published in facsimile in 1971.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Pound's blunt criticism is widely credited with shaping a sprawling draft into one of the most influential poems of the twentieth century.
The kural says friends are not only for laughing together but for stepping in first to rebuke one another when one goes too far, and Pound did exactly this, cutting away his friend's excesses.
