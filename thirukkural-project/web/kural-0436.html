Kural 436

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

தன்குற்றம் நீக்கிப் பிறர்குற்றங் காண்கிற்பின் என்குற்ற மாகும் இறைக்கு.
Thankutram neekkip pirarkutrang kaankirpin Enkutra maakum iraikku.

Meaning

If a ruler first fixes his own faults and only then looks at the faults of others, what fault could ever harm him?

Elementary

It is easy to spot what others are doing wrong. This kural says a good leader first looks at and fixes their own mistakes, and only then points out other people’s. A leader who does that has nothing to be blamed for.

University

This is the chapter’s central statement of self-scrutiny and its logic is sequential: தன்குற்றம் நீக்கி (having removed one’s own fault) comes before பிறர்குற்றம் காண் (seeing others’ faults). The verb காண்கிற்பின் (‘if one is able to see’) implies that judging others rightly is a capacity earned by self-correction. The closing rhetorical question, என்குற்றம் ஆகும் இறைக்கு (‘what fault would there be for the king?’), expects the answer ‘none’. For a ruler whose office is to judge, this is the ethics of legitimate authority: the right to correct others rests on having corrected oneself. The couplet is the Tamil counterpart of the ‘mote and beam’ teaching and of the fable of the two wallets, but it is framed positively as a programme for governance rather than as a rebuke to hypocrisy.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Thou seest, O king, the fault of others, even though they be as small as a mustard seed. But seeing, thou noticest not thy own faults even though they be as large as the Vilwa fruit.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section LXXIV (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Shakuntala rebukes King Dushyanta, who denies her, for spotting others’ tiny faults while missing his own huge ones.
Addressed, like the Kural, to a king; it condemns exactly what the Kural says a king must avoid: judging others before seeing one’s own faults.
Christianity
“Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye.”
— Matthew 7:5 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his hearers to remove the plank in their own eye before trying to remove the speck in someone else’s.
Identical order of operations: first remove your own fault, then you can rightly see and correct others’.
Islam
“One of you looks at the mote in his brother's eye while forgetting the stump in his own eye.”
— Al-Adab al-Mufrad 592 (saying of Abu Hurayra) [5]
Abu Hurayra warns against people who notice a speck in a brother’s eye but forget the log in their own.
Same insight as the Kural: self-correction must precede correcting others. Elsewhere the Prophet calls the believer ‘the believer’s mirror’ (Abu Dawud 4918), mutual correction resting on sincerity.
Judaism
“...Reish Lakish says concerning this: Adorn [keshot] yourself and afterward adorn others.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 107b (also Bava Batra 60b) [6]
Reish Lakish teaches that you must correct your own conduct before requiring others to correct theirs; the Talmud applies it to a rabbi who first cut down his own trees before ruling that others must cut theirs.
Same order as the Kural, and like the Kural it is applied to a person with authority to judge others.
Buddhism
“Easily seen is the fault of others, but one's own fault is difficult to see. Like chaff one winnows another's faults, but hides one's own, even as a crafty fowler hides behind sham branches.”
— Dhammapada 252 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that we broadcast others’ faults like chaff in the wind but hide our own, and (Dhp 253) that the fault-finder’s own defilements grow.
The Dhammapada describes the problem the Kural solves: because one’s own faults are hard to see, they must be looked for and removed first.
Jainism
“Fight with your Self; why fight with external foes? He who conquers himself through himself, will obtain happiness.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.35 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
When Indra urges King Nami to subdue the princes who do not acknowledge him, Nami answers that the battle that matters is against oneself, and that only self-conquest brings happiness.
Valluvar tells the ruler to remove his own faults before examining others'; Nami, also a king, likewise turns from the faults and hostility of others to the work of correcting himself.
Sikhism
“Fareed, if you have a keen understanding, then do not write black marks against anyone else. Look underneath your own collar instead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose saloks are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, tells the wise to stop recording others' wrongs and to look first into their own conduct.
The Kural asks the ruler to remove his own faults first and only then examine others'; Farid gives exactly this order of self-scrutiny before judging anyone else.
Greek Mythology
“Jupiter has loaded us with a couple of Wallets: the one, filled with our own vices, he has placed at our backs, {the other}, heavy with those of others, he has hung before. From this circumstance, we are not able to see our own faults: but as soon as others make a slip, we are ready to censure.”
— Phaedrus, Fables IV.10 (tr. H. T. Riley; Aesopic, Perry 266) [10]
The fable says Jupiter hung two bags on every person: others’ faults in front, where we always see them, and our own behind, where we never do.
Explains why the Kural must command the reversal: you have to turn the bag round and deal with your own faults before looking at others’.
Philosophy
“The spirit ought to be brought up for examination daily. It was the custom of Sextius when the day was over... to inquire of his spirit: "What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?”
— Seneca, De Ira (On Anger) III.36 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
Seneca describes reviewing his whole day every night, judging himself and correcting his own faults before sleep.
Seneca’s nightly tribunal is a practical method for the Kural’s first step: finding and removing one’s own faults.

Real Life Example

Pope John Paul II's Day of Pardon
12 March 2000 · St Peter's Basilica, Vatican City
On the first Sunday of Lent in the Jubilee year 2000, Pope John Paul II led a service in St Peter's Basilica in which senior cardinals and bishops confessed categories of sins committed by members of the Catholic Church over the centuries. These included divisions among Christians, the use of violence in the service of truth, hostility toward followers of other religions including Jews, and wrongs against women, the poor and minorities. After each confession the pope asked God's forgiveness.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The ceremony was an unprecedented public act by a major institution examining and confessing its own past faults before addressing those of others.
The kural says a ruler who first removes his own faults and only then examines others' will be free of fault, and the Day of Pardon began with the Church's own record.
