Kural 547

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

இறைகாக்கும் வையகம் எல்லாம் அவனை முறைகாக்கும் முட்டாச் செயின்.
Iraikaakkum Vaiyakam Ellaam Avanai Muraikaakkum Muttaach Cheyin.

Meaning

The king protects the whole world, and justice protects the king, as long as he never fails to do justice.

Elementary

If you always tell the truth and treat your friends fairly, then when someone spreads a nasty rumour about you, your friends stand up for you. Your fairness becomes your shield. It works the same way for rulers.

University

The two halves mirror each other with the verb காக்கும் (guards): இறை காக்கும் வையகம் எல்லாம் (the lord guards the whole world) and அவனை முறை காக்கும் (justice guards him). The condition முட்டாச் செயின், ‘if he does it without lapse’ (முட்டு = obstruction, failure), is the hinge. Parimelazhagar explains that the merit of just rule protects the king from harm that would otherwise come to him; Varadarasan: ‘if he rules without harming justice, that justice itself protects him’. Pappaiah reads முட்டா as qualifying முறை, i.e. ‘his flawless, upright rule protects him’. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads the condition oddly (‘even if they err’), which the grammar does not support. The idea is the political counterpart of the Aram principle that dharma protects those who protect it; compare Manu’s ‘dharmo rakṣati rakṣitaḥ’. Both sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Justice, being violated, destroys; justice, being preserved, preserves: therefore justice must not be violated, lest violated justice destroy us.”
— Manusmriti 8.15 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu teaches the classic principle dharmo rakṣati rakṣitaḥ: justice protects those who protect it and destroys those who violate it.
The Kural’s ‘justice guards him who does not fail it’ is the same maxim.
Christianity
“The king that faithfully judgeth the poor, his throne shall be established for ever.”
— Proverbs 29:14 (KJV) [4]
The proverb promises a lasting throne to the king who judges the poor faithfully.
Just judgment secures the king himself, which is the Kural’s point.
Islam
“Allah will give shade, to seven, on the Day when there will be no shade but His. (These seven persons are) a just ruler, …”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 660 [5]
First among the seven whom God shelters on the Day of Judgment is the just ruler.
The ruler’s justice becomes his protection, here in the next world.
Judaism
“It is an abomination to kings to commit wickedness; For the throne is established by righteousness.”
— Proverbs 16:12 (JPS 1917) [6]
The proverb holds that a king’s throne is made firm by righteousness, not by wickedness.
Righteousness upholding the throne is justice guarding the king.
Buddhism
“Dhamma surely protects one who practices Dhamma.”
— Theragatha 4.10, Dhammika (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The elder Dhammika teaches that Dhamma, well practised, protects the one who practises it and brings happiness.
The Kural applies the same principle to the king: practise justice and justice guards you.
Jainism
“Whenever you die, O king, and leave all pleasant things behind, the Law alone, and nothing else in this world, will save you, O monarch.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Queen Kamalāvatī warns King Ishukāra, who is about to seize property, that neither wealth nor power can protect a ruler; only dharma can.
The Kural says the king guards the world and justice in turn guards the king. The queen tells her king the same thing: the Law alone protects him.
Sikhism
“The mythical bull is Dharma, the son of compassion; this is what patiently holds the earth in its place.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib, Pauri 16, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak reinterprets the myth of the bull that bears the earth: what really holds the world in place is dharma, born of compassion.
The Kural says the king guards the world and justice in turn guards the king; Guru Nanak likewise names righteousness as the power that keeps everything standing.
Greek Mythology
“Next he married bright Themis who bare the Horae (Hours), and Eunomia (Order), Dike (Justice), and blooming Eirene (Peace), who mind the works of mortal men,”
— Hesiod, Theogony 901–903 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod makes Order, Justice and Peace daughters of Zeus and Themis who watch over human affairs.
Justice as a guardian power watching over those who honour it gives the Kural’s idea a mythic form.
Philosophy
Cicero argues that of all means of gaining and keeping power, none is better suited than being loved and none worse than being feared; fear, he says, is a poor guardian of lasting power, while goodwill keeps it safe for ever, and he points to the violent ends of tyrants.
— Cicero, De Officiis II.7.23–24 [11]
For Cicero, a ruler’s best protection is the goodwill won by just and generous conduct, not guards or terror.
The ruler who guards others justly is himself guarded, as the Kural says.

Real Life Example

King Juan Carlos defends Spain's constitution against the 1981 coup
23-24 February 1981 · Madrid, Spain
On 23 February 1981, Civil Guard officers led by Antonio Tejero stormed Spain's Congress of Deputies during the vote to install a new prime minister and held the deputies and members of the government captive for about 18 hours. That night King Juan Carlos appeared on national television to denounce the uprising and call for loyalty to the democratic constitution and the elected government. The coup collapsed, constitutional order was restored, and its leaders were tried and imprisoned.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Because the king stood firmly by the constitution, the lawful order he upheld gave him the authority to stop the coup, and the episode strengthened both Spanish democracy and his own position.
The kural says the king guards the world and justice guards the king, so long as he upholds it without fail, and Juan Carlos's loyalty to the lawful order protected both his country and his crown in 1981.
