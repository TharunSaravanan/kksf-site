Kural 893

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

கெடல்வேண்டின் கேளாது செய்க அடல்வேண்டின் ஆற்று பவர்கண் இழுக்கு.
Ketalventin kelaadhu seyka atalventin aatru pavarkan izhukku.

Meaning

If you want to ruin yourself, act without listening to advice. If you want to be destroyed, do wrong to people who have great power.

Elementary

If you poke a sleeping bear with a stick and ignore everyone shouting stop, you are asking for trouble. Valluvar says, with a wink: if you want to get hurt, go ahead and annoy the strongest one around.

University

This couplet uses irony: keDal veNDin (கெடல் வேண்டின், if you desire ruin), keLaadhu seyga (கேளாது செய்க, do it without listening), izhukku (இழுக்கு, offence, fault) toward aatrupavar (ஆற்றுபவர், those able) aDal veNDin (அடல் வேண்டின், when they wish to slay). The phrase keLaadhu is read differently: Pope’s explanation, following the traditional commentary, takes it as not listening to the righteous dictates of law or the nīti texts, and Solomon Pappaiah likewise speaks of ignoring what the moral treatises say; Kalaignar reads it as not listening to anyone’s advice; Sivayogi Sivakumar reads the first half more freely as a separate clause. The pairing keDal/aDal is a typical Valluvar sound-echo. The text is identical in both sources, which print ஆற்று பவர்கண் with the metrical split.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Yuddha Kanda, Ravana rejects the repeated counsel of his brother Vibhishana to return Sita to Rama, insults him, and drives him away, then goes on to his destruction.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (Vibhishana’s counsel) [3]
Ravana refused to listen to wise advice and persisted in offending a far mightier opponent until his kingdom fell.
This is the Kural’s formula for ruin exactly: do not listen, and wrong the one who can destroy you.
Christianity
“He, that being often reproved hardeneth his neck, shall suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy.”
— Proverbs 29:1 (KJV) [4]
The proverb warns that one who keeps refusing correction will meet sudden and irreparable ruin.
The Kural’s keLaadhu, refusing to listen, is the same road to ruin.
Islam
“And they will say, ‘If only we had been listening or reasoning, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze.’”
— Qur’an 67:10 (Saheeh International) [5]
The people of the Fire lament too late that they refused to listen and reason when warned.
Like the Kural, it traces ruin to the refusal to heed.
Judaism
King Rehoboam rejects the counsel of the elders who served his father Solomon, follows the harsh advice of his young companions, and ten tribes break away from his kingdom.
— 1 Kings 12:1-20 [4]
By refusing to heed experienced counsellors and provoking the people, Rehoboam lost most of his realm.
The Kural’s path to self-ruin, acting without listening, is exactly Rehoboam’s choice.
Buddhism
“Whoever, on account of perverted views, scorns the Teaching of the Perfected Ones, the Noble and Righteous Ones — that fool, like the bamboo, produces fruits only for self destruction.”
— Dhammapada 164 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha compares one who scorns the noble ones to a bamboo whose own fruiting kills it.
As in the Kural, scorning the great is described as a self-chosen path to ruin.
Jainism
“Like a poisonous snake is a great sage of severe austerities, of tremendous piety and power; like a swarm of moths you will rush into a fire, if you beat a monk on his begging tour.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.27 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
When brahmin youths beat the outcaste-born monk Harikeśa, Bhadrā warns them that assaulting such a sage is like moths rushing into a flame, a ruin they bring on themselves.
The Kural says that whoever wants to be ruined need only offend those able to destroy, and Bhadrā's image of moths flying into the fire is that same self-chosen destruction.
Sikhism
“He himself must eat what he has planted.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 280, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In the same passage on slandering the Saint, Guru Arjan says the slanderer reaps exactly the harvest he has sown.
The Kural ironically says that whoever wants ruin need only offend the able, and Gurbani agrees that such a person brings ruin on himself.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides’ Bacchae, King Pentheus ignores the warnings of the seer Tiresias and of his grandfather Cadmus, persecutes the god Dionysus, and is destroyed.
— Euripides, Bacchae [9]
Pentheus refused all counsel and made war on a power far greater than his own.
He follows the Kural’s ironic recipe for ruin to the letter.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Plato describes a ship whose quarrelling sailors ignore and mock the true navigator, so that the voyage is ruined.
— Plato, Republic VI, 488a-489a (Ship of State) [10]
A community that will not listen to those with real skill steers itself toward disaster.
The Kural similarly links ruin to refusing to heed and disrespecting the capable.

Real Life Example

Iraq ignores the UN deadline to leave Kuwait
2 August 1990 - 28 February 1991 · Kuwait and Iraq
Iraqi forces invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990, and the UN Security Council at once demanded their immediate and unconditional withdrawal, then imposed sanctions. In November 1990 Resolution 678 authorized member states to use all necessary means unless Iraq withdrew by 15 January 1991. Iraq did not withdraw, and a coalition of forces from more than 30 nations began air attacks on 16-17 January and a ground offensive on 24 February that liberated Kuwait within about four days.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Iraq's leadership defied repeated warnings from a coalition with overwhelming power and saw its army driven out of Kuwait in days.
The kural says that one who wants ruin need only offend, without heeding anyone, those able to destroy whenever they wish, which Iraq did by ignoring every warning.
