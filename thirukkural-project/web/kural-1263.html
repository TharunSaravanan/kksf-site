Kural 1263

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

உரன்நசைஇ உள்ளம் துணையாகச் சென்றார் வரல்நசைஇ இன்னும் உளேன்.
Urannasaii ullam thunaiyaakach chendraar varalnasaii innum ulen.

Meaning

He went away to win success, with only his own courage for company. I am still alive only because I long to see him come back.

Elementary

A brave soldier leaves home to win a great contest, taking only his courage with him. His wife at home is very sad, but one thing keeps her going: the hope of seeing him walk back through the door. That hope is like a lamp that keeps her heart from going dark.

University

The couplet balances two desires with the same verb, nacai (‘longing’, here in the old verbal form nacaii): he left out of uraṉ nacai, desire for strength or victory, with uḷḷam tuṇaiyāka, his own spirit as his only companion; she lives on out of varal nacai, desire for his return. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar gloss uraṉ as victory (வெற்றி) and uḷḷam as zeal (ஊக்கம்); Solomon Pappaiah adds a note of reproach, reading that he spurned her companionship to make his resolve his companion. Iṉṉum uḷēṉ, ‘I still exist’, implies that only this hope keeps her alive, a recurring motif in the kaṟpu section. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Sita tells Hanuman that she will keep herself alive for one more month in the hope that Rama will come for her, and that she cannot live beyond that.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, Sita’s message to Rama through Hanuman (paraphrase) [3]
Sita’s life in captivity is sustained only by the expectation of her husband’s coming.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Sita says plainly that she lives on only for the day of his return.
Christianity
“But if we hope for that we see not, then do we with patience wait for it.”
— Romans 8:25 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes hope as the patient waiting for what is not yet seen.
The heroine lives in exactly that hope: she cannot see him, but waiting for him keeps her alive.
Islam
“[Jacob] said, ‘Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting. Perhaps Allah will bring them to me all together. Indeed it is He who is the Knowing, the Wise.’”
— Qur’an 12:83 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob, having lost two sons, holds on through patience and the hope that they will be returned to him.
The heroine’s will to live rests on the same hope that the absent one will be brought back.
Judaism
“And Israel said: ‘It is enough; Joseph my son is yet alive; I will go and see him before I die.’”
— Genesis 45:28 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Jacob’s spirit revives at the news that Joseph lives, and seeing him becomes the one thing left to live for.
The heroine too is kept alive by one wish: to see him again.
Buddhism
In the Sambula Jataka, the princess Sambula follows her husband into the forest when he falls gravely ill and tends him faithfully, refusing to abandon him.
— Sambula Jataka (Jataka No. 519, tr. E.B. Cowell et al.; paraphrase) [7]
The Jataka holds up Sambula as a model of a wife whose devotion carries her through hardship.
Sambula’s steadfastness and the heroine’s will to live for her husband’s return are the same devotion under strain.
Jainism
Jacobi notes that Rajimati’s lament for Arishtanemi became the subject of a Jain Sanskrit poem, the Nemidutakavya of Vikrama, in which each stanza ends with a line borrowed from Kalidasa’s Meghaduta, turning her into a separated lover sending a message to her beloved.
— Nemidutakavya of Vikrama, as described in Hermann Jacobi’s note to Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28 (SBE 45) [8]
Jain poets themselves took up the viraha (separation) form to voice Rajimati’s longing for the Jina.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Rajimati in this poem lives in longing for one who has gone away on a lofty purpose of his own.
Sikhism
“Without Him, I cannot live, even for a moment. Without His Name, I die.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 58, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says the soul's life depends entirely on the Lord; without Him there is only death.
The wife in the Kural lives on only because she longs for her husband's return; Guru Nanak too says life itself hangs on the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
While Ceyx is at sea, his wife Alcyone burns incense to the gods every day, weaves garments for his homecoming and prays for his safe return, not knowing he has already drowned.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11 (Ceyx and Alcyone) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s myth shows a wife whose every day is organised around her husband’s expected return.
Alcyone and the Kural’s heroine both live for the moment the absent husband comes home.
Philosophy
Nietzsche writes that if we have our own why of life, we can put up with almost any how.
— Friedrich Nietzsche, Twilight of the Idols, ‘Maxims and Arrows’ 12 (paraphrase) [11]
A clear reason to live makes even hard conditions bearable.
The heroine’s ‘why’ is his return, and it is what lets her endure the ‘how’ of separation.

Real Life Example

Douglas Mawson's extra year in Antarctica and Paquita Delprat's wait
1911-1914 · Commonwealth Bay, Antarctica, and Adelaide, Australia
The geologist Douglas Mawson, engaged to Paquita Delprat, led the Australasian Antarctic Expedition from 1911. On a sledging journey in 1912 his companions Belgrave Ninnis and Xavier Mertz died, and Mawson struggled back alone to his base only to find that the relief ship Aurora had just left. He and six volunteers spent another winter there, he returned in 1914, and he and Paquita married on 31 March 1914 in Melbourne.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mawson went south in pursuit of discovery, and his fiancee had to wait more than two years, including an unplanned extra year, for his return.
The kural's heroine says her husband left in pursuit of achievement with only his resolve for company, and she lives on only in the hope of his return; Paquita Delprat waited through Mawson's long absence in just this situation.
