Kural 817

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

நகைவகைய ராகிய நட்பின் பகைவரால் பத்தடுத்த கோடி உறும்.
Nakaivakaiya raakiya natpin pakaivaraal paththaduththa kodi urum.

Meaning

What you get from your enemies is worth a hundred million times more than the friendship of people who only laugh along with you.

Elementary

A friend who just giggles at everything never tells you there’s spinach in your teeth. Even someone who doesn’t like you might point it out. Sometimes honest criticism helps more than easy laughter.

University

Nakaivakaiyar (நகைவகையர்) are ‘those of the laughing kind’: Mu. Varadarasanar adds ‘laughing outwardly without love within’, Kalaignar ‘those who laugh and talk as a performance’, and Puliyur Kesigan ‘those useful only for idle talk and merriment’. Against them stands what comes from pakaivar (பகைவர், ‘enemies’), worth paththaduththa kodi (பத்தடுத்த கோடி), ‘ten crore’ or a hundred million times more. The exaggeration escalates the kodi of 816. Commentators differ on what an enemy provides: most modern glosses say ‘benefit’, while Kalaignar paradoxically says even the suffering enemies cause is worth more, because it at least makes one alert. The wording agrees across ytamizh and thirukkural.net; the sandhi-joined edition writes ‘நட்பிற் பகைவராற்’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Of a rascal and a serpent, the serpent is the better of the two, for he strikes only at the time he is destined to kill, while the former at every step.”
— Chanakya Niti 3.4 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya prefers an open, predictable danger to the constant harm of a bad person close at hand.
Like the Kural, it ranks the obvious enemy above the false companion.
Christianity
“Judas, betrayest thou the Son of man with a kiss?”
— Luke 22:48 (KJV) [5]
Judas leads the arresting crowd to Jesus and identifies him with a friendly kiss.
The open enemies with swords were less treacherous than the friendly gesture; the Kural likewise ranks enemies above friendship that is all surface.
Islam
“The Religion is sincerity.” We said, “To whom?” He said “To Allah, to His Book, To His Messenger, and to the leaders of the Muslims and their masses.”
— Sahih Muslim 55a [6]
The Prophet makes sincere goodwill, nasiha (often rendered sincere counsel), the core of religion in every relationship.
The Kural’s laughing friends offer the opposite of nasiha: pleasant noise without honest concern.
Judaism
“Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (Hebrew Bible, KJV rendering) [5]
Proverbs holds that honest wounds from a true friend are worth more than an enemy’s pleasant kisses.
Both texts value painful honesty over pleasant surface affection, though they assign the roles differently.
Buddhism
“(iii) he tries to gain one’s favor by empty words”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [7]
Among the marks of the ‘lip-service’ friend, a foe in the guise of a friend, the Buddha lists courting favour with empty talk.
This is the Kural’s nakaivakaiyar, whose friendship is laughter and talk with nothing inside.
Jainism
“It is better to be without the love of those who are without sincerity. Death is preferable to an incurable disease. To kill him at once is more desirable than to vex a man so that he becomes sore at heart, and to abuse is better than to praise one for that which we do not possess.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 22 'The choice of friends', stanza 9 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In a string of 'better than' sayings, the poet holds that it is better to have no love at all than insincere love, and better to be abused than falsely praised.
The Kural says what comes from enemies is worth far more than the friendship of those who only laugh along with you, and the Naladiyar likewise prefers open hostility to hollow affection and flattery.
Sikhism
“If my heart is pure, and I am slandered, then the slanderer washes my clothes. One who slanders me is my friend”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 339, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir says the person who attacks him with slander actually does him good, washing away his faults, so he counts such a critic as a friend.
The Kural says what comes to you from enemies is worth far more than the friendship of those who only laugh along with you; Kabir likewise finds real benefit in his detractors, which no flattering companion can give.
Greek Mythology
Diogenes Laertius reports that Antisthenes, the follower of Socrates, advised paying attention to one’s enemies, because they are the first to notice one’s mistakes.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers, Book 6 (Antisthenes) [10]
For Antisthenes an enemy’s hostile attention is a kind of free audit.
It gives a reason for the Kural’s paradox: an enemy’s scrutiny serves you better than friends who only laugh.
Philosophy
In his essay How to Profit by One’s Enemies, Plutarch argues that an enemy’s constant watchfulness keeps a person careful and self-correcting in ways that indulgent friends do not.
— Plutarch, Moralia, How to Profit by One’s Enemies [11]
Plutarch turns enmity into a moral tool: those who hunt for your faults make you guard against them.
This explains why Valluvar says enemies yield far more than friends who only share a laugh.

Real Life Example

Peter the Great rebuilds his army after defeat at Narva
1700-1709 · Narva, Estonia, and Russia
In November 1700 Charles XII of Sweden routed Peter the Great's much larger Russian army at the Battle of Narva. In the following years Peter regrouped his troops and carried out sweeping military reforms, forming new regiments with modern weapons, uniforms and tactics, often with the help of foreign officers and engineers, and building up foundries and arms works. Russia retook Narva by siege in 1704 and decisively defeated Sweden at Poltava in 1709.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The harsh lesson of defeat by an enemy did more to make Russia a military power than years of comfortable court life had done.
The kural says that what comes from enemies is worth far more than the friendship of those who only laugh along with you, and what Peter learned from the Swedes at Narva became the foundation of his later victories.
