Kural 691

Conduct in the Presence of the King · Mannaraich Serndhozhuthal · மன்னரைச் சேர்ந்தொழுதல்

அகலாது அணுகாது தீக்காய்வார் போல்க இகல்வேந்தர்ச் சேர்ந்தொழுகு வார்.
Akalaadhu anukaadhu theekkaaivaar polka Ikalvendharch serndhozhuku vaar.

Meaning

People who serve kings who are quick to anger should act like people warming themselves by a fire. They should not go too far away or too close.

Elementary

On a cold night you sit near a campfire. Too far away and you shiver; too close and you get burned. Being around a very powerful person is the same: stay close enough to be useful, but never so close that you get burned.

University

The chapter opens the Kural’s treatment of how the minister or courtier (the Amaichiyal section) is to conduct himself with the ruler, and its governing image is the fire (thee) at which one warms oneself (theekkaaivaar). The paired negatives akalaadhu (not withdrawing) and anukaadhu (not approaching) define a calibrated middle distance rather than a fixed rule. Parimelazhagar reads ikalvendhar as kings whose temper easily turns to hostility (ikal = enmity, variance), so the danger is not the king’s wickedness but his volatility: the same fire that warms can burn. The simile closely parallels Kautilya (Arthashastra 5.4), who likens royal service to life in fire, and Dhaumya’s counsel in the Mahabharata that the king is a great fire. The traditional sandhi text reads “அகலா தணுகாது” and “விகல்வேந்தர்”, which divide into the same words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“To dwell with a king is, alas, difficult. ... the king, who is a deity in an embodied form, is as a great fire sanctified with all the mantras.”
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva, Section 4 (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Before the Pandavas go into hiding as servants at King Virata’s court, the priest Dhaumya warns them that living near a king is hard, and he compares the king to a great sacred fire.
Dhaumya and Valluvar use the same image, the king as a fire, to teach the same thing: approach carefully.
Christianity
“When thou art bidden of any man to a wedding, sit not down in the highest room; lest a more honourable man than thou be bidden of him ... But when thou art bidden, go and sit down in the lowest room; that when he that bade thee cometh, he may say unto thee, Friend, go up higher.”
— Luke 14:8-10 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells guests not to grab the seat nearest the host, because they may be moved down in shame. It is better to be invited closer.
Like the Kural, it teaches that pushing too close to the powerful backfires, and that the right nearness is the one you are invited to.
Islam
“He who lives in the desert will become rude; he who pursues the game will be negligent, and he who visits a king will be perverted.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 2859 (graded sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet warns that going to the court of a ruler puts a person’s integrity at risk, because being near power tempts and tests people.
The hadith names the burn that the Kural’s fire image warns about: getting too close to rulers is dangerous.
Judaism
“Be careful [in your dealings] with the ruling authorities for they do not befriend a person except for their own needs; they seem like friends when it is to their own interest, but they do not stand by a man in the hour of his distress.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:3 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabban Gamaliel warns that rulers act friendly when it helps them and drop you when it does not, so deal with them warily.
This is the same careful middle distance the Kural asks for. Rulers can be useful, but you cannot safely get close to them.
Buddhism
“Let him prudently keep not too far from the king nor yet too near to him, and let him stand ready before him, telling something for his lord to hear.”
— Vidhurapandita Jataka (Ja 545), v. 154, The King’s Court (Rājavasati) section [7]
In the Vidhurapandita Jataka, the wise minister Vidhura teaches his family the rules for living at a king’s court, and this is one of them.
This is nearly the Kural’s own wording: not too far and not too near.
Jainism
“A well-behaved monk should not sit on his hams, nor cross his arms, nor stretch out his legs, nor stand (too) close to his teacher.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.19 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The discipline lecture sets out how a disciple keeps a respectful distance and posture before his teacher; a nearby verse (1.34) similarly has the monk stand 'neither too close by nor too far off' when receiving alms.
The Kural pictures courtiers keeping a measured distance from the king, like people warming themselves at a fire; the Jain rule likewise asks for a measured, respectful distance before a superior.
Sikhism
“The kings are tigers, and their officials are dogs; they go out and awaken the sleeping people to harass them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1288, Raag Malaar, Malaar ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes the rulers and officials of his day as predators whose power falls unpredictably on ordinary people.
The Kural warns that changeable, powerful kings must be approached like a fire, neither too near nor too far; Guru Nanak's tigers make the same point about the danger of royal power, though from the side of its victims.
Greek Mythology
Daedalus fits wings to his son Icarus and tells him to fly a middle course: too low and the sea spray will soak the feathers, too high and the sun will melt the wax. Icarus, excited, soars toward the sun and falls into the sea.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.183-235 (Daedalus and Icarus) [10]
The myth shows that safety lies in a middle distance, and that being carried too close to a blazing power ends in disaster.
Icarus flying at the sun is the mythic version of the courtier who comes too near the fire.
Philosophy
“Self-protection shall be the first and constant thought of a wise man; for the life of a man under the service of a king is aptly compared to life in fire.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 5.4, The Conduct of a Courtier (trans. R. Shamasastry) [11]
Kautilya ends his chapter on a courtier’s conduct by saying that serving a king is like living in fire. Fire burns only the body, but a king can ruin or raise a servant’s whole family.
Kautilya and Valluvar build their advice on the same metaphor of royal service as fire.

Real Life Example

Voltaire at the court of Frederick the Great
1750-1753 · Berlin and Potsdam, Prussia (now Germany); Frankfurt
After years of admiring correspondence, Voltaire accepted Frederick II's pressing invitation and set out for Berlin on 28 June 1750, at first enchanted by life at the Prussian court in Berlin and Potsdam. Difficulties soon arose, and when he ridiculed Maupertuis, president of Frederick's Berlin Academy, in the pamphlet Diatribe du docteur Akakia (1752), the enraged king had it burned and gave its author a thorough dressing down. Voltaire left Prussia on 26 March 1753 and, on the journey home, was held under house arrest at an inn in Frankfurt by order of the Prussian resident.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The greatest writer of the age moved from flattering distance into the king's daily circle, and the very closeness that warmed him exposed him to the royal temper.
Voltaire found that a powerful and changeable king is like a fire: welcome at a respectful distance, but scorching to one who settles too close and takes liberties beside it.
