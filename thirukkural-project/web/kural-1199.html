Kural 1199

The Solitary Anguish · Thanippadar Mikudhi · தனிப்படர் மிகுதி

நசைஇயார் நல்கார் எனினும் அவர்மாட்டு இசையும் இனிய செவிக்கு.
Nasai-iyaar nalkaar eninum avarmaattu isaiyum iniya sevikku.

Meaning

Even if the one I long for shows me no kindness, anything I hear about him is still sweet to my ears.

Elementary

Sometimes when you miss someone, just hearing their name makes you smile, even if they haven’t called you. The girl in this couplet feels that way: anything about him sounds like music.

University

Nacaiiyār (with alapeṭai) is ‘the one longed for’; nalkār eṉiṉum, ‘even though he grants no grace’; avar māṭṭu icaiyum, ‘even what is heard of / from him’, is iṉiya cevikku, ‘sweet to the ear.’ The word icai is read two ways: as ‘fame, praise’ (Parimelazhagar’s line, followed by Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan and Kalaignar: hearing others praise him is honey to her), or as ‘word, sound’ (Pappaiah: any word from him is sweet). Pope follows the second: ‘Though he my heart desires no grace accords to me, / Yet every accent of his voice is melody.’ Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Venu Gita, the gopis, apart from Krishna as he grazes the cattle in the forest, describe to one another how the sound of his flute enchants them, the deer, the cows and even the rivers.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.21, Venu Gita (paraphrase) [3]
The gopis find even the distant sound of Krishna’s flute overwhelmingly sweet while they cannot be with him.
As the heroine finds any word of her beloved sweet, the gopis delight in the far-off music that tells them of Krishna.
Christianity
“let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.”
— Song of Solomon 2:14 (KJV) [4]
In the Song of Solomon the lover asks only to hear the beloved’s voice, because it is sweet.
This is the Kural’s claim that the beloved’s voice and words are sweet to the ear whatever else he gives.
Islam
A verse attributed to Majnun says that he passes by the walls of Layla’s dwelling and kisses this wall and that, not for love of the houses, but for love of the one who lives within them.
— Attributed to Qays ibn al-Mulawwah (Majnun Layla), Diwan (paraphrase) [5]
Majnun, kept from Layla, finds sweetness in anything connected with her.
Majnun’s love for her very walls matches the heroine’s delight in anything heard of her beloved, though he gives her nothing.
Judaism
“Hark! my beloved! behold, he cometh, leaping upon the mountains, skipping upon the hills.”
— Song of Songs 2:8 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The maiden’s heart leaps at the mere sound of her beloved before she even sees him.
The sound of the beloved delights her just as, in the Kural, every word of him is sweet to the heroine’s ears.
Buddhism
Nanda, the Buddha’s half-brother, is led into the order on his wedding day, but he cannot stop thinking of his bride, the Beauty of the Land, who called after him to come back soon; he tells the monks he is discontent because her parting words stay with him.
— Udana 3.2, Nanda Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Udana tells how a single remembered word from the beloved kept Nanda’s heart bound to her.
Nanda’s memory of his bride’s words mirrors the heroine’s sense that every word of her beloved is sweet, though the Buddha leads Nanda beyond it.
Jainism
Jain tradition holds that when a Tirthankara preaches in the samavasarana, his divine sound (divya-dhvani) is understood by every listener in its own language and delights gods, humans and even animals.
— Jain doctrine of the samavasarana and divya-dhvani (paraphrase; cf. Tattvartha tradition and the Kalpa Sutra’s account of Mahavira’s preaching) [8]
For Jains the voice of the Jina is the sweetest of all sounds, attracting every being.
The heroine finds her beloved’s words sweet even without his grace; Jainism directs that delight in a voice toward the Jina instead.
Sikhism
“Continually listening to Your Glory, I live.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 388, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that hearing about the Lord's glory is what keeps him alive.
The Kural's wife says that even if her beloved shows her no kindness, every word about him is sweet to her ears; Guru Arjan likewise lives on simply hearing the Beloved's praise.
Greek Mythology
The nymph Echo, able only to repeat the last words she hears, falls in love with Narcissus, who rejects her; she follows him, cherishing and repeating his words, and wastes away until only her voice remains.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 3.356-401 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s Echo lives on the words of a beloved who gives her no love in return.
Echo is the Kural’s heroine taken to its extreme: his words are all she has, and they are still sweet to her.
Philosophy
Pascal writes that the heart has its reasons which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées (Brunschvicg no. 277) (paraphrase) [11]
Pascal recognises that love follows its own logic, which argument cannot overturn.
The heroine’s delight in the words of one who shows her no kindness is just such a reason of the heart.

Real Life Example

Dante writes La Vita Nuova in praise of Beatrice
c. 1274–1293 · Florence, Italy
Dante first saw Beatrice, usually identified as Beatrice Portinari, when he was nine, and he saw her only rarely after that. She married Simone de' Bardi and died in 1290 at about twenty-four. Around 1293 Dante wrote La Vita Nuova, prose and poems tracing his devotion to her, and ended it with a vow to write of her as no woman had been written of before, a vow he fulfilled in the Divine Comedy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dante received almost nothing from Beatrice in life, yet speaking and writing of her became the centre of his poetry.
The kural says that even if the beloved shows no kindness, every word about them is sweet to hear, and Dante filled whole books with words about a woman who gave him little more than a few meetings.
