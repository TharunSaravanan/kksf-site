Kural 506

Selection and Confidence · Therindhu Thelithal · தெரிந்து தெளிதல்

அற்றாரைத் தேறுதல் ஓம்புக மற்றவர் பற்றிலர் நாணார் பழி.
Atraaraith thaerudhal ombuga matravar Patrilar naanaar pazhi.

Meaning

Do not trust people who have no family or community ties. With no one to answer to, they feel no shame about doing wrong.

Elementary

Why do people usually behave well? Partly because they don’t want to let down their family, friends and neighbours. Someone who doesn’t care about anyone might not care if they get in trouble either. So the Kural says: be careful before trusting someone who has no one they care about.

University

அற்றார் means ‘those who are cut off’; the standard commentary (followed by Varadarasan, Pappaiah and Pope) takes it as those without kin (சுற்றம்), who are therefore பற்றிலர், without attachments, and so நாணார் பழி, unashamed of blame. The reasoning links back to 502: shame works through the people whose opinion one values, so someone with no such bonds has no brake. Kalaignar reads அற்றார் as the unprincipled (நெறியற்றவர்), and Sivayogi Sivakumar reverses the sense, reading it as a call to raise up those who have nothing, a reading at odds with the chapter’s context. ஓம்புக here means ‘guard against, avoid’. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him appoint seven or eight ministers whose ancestors have been royal servants, who are versed in the sciences, heroes skilled in the use of weapons and descended from (noble) families and who have been tried.”
— Manusmṛti 7.54 (trans. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Manu tells the king to choose ministers with deep hereditary ties to the court and to noble families, and who have been tested.
Ministers rooted in family and loyalty are the opposite of the unattached people the Kural warns against.
Christianity
“One that ruleth well his own house, having his children in subjection with all gravity; (For if a man know not how to rule his own house, how shall he take care of the church of God?)”
— 1 Timothy 3:4-5 (KJV) [4]
Paul makes a man’s care for his own household a qualification for leading the church.
Family bonds, well kept, show the kind of person who can be trusted with more.
Islam
The Prophet taught that the best of people are those who are best to their families, adding that he was the best of them to his own family.
— Jami‘ at-Tirmidhi 3895 [5]
A person’s treatment of kin is presented as a measure of goodness.
The Kural likewise reads a person’s bonds with kin as evidence of trustworthiness.
Judaism
“…one from among thy brethren shalt thou set king over thee; thou mayest not put a foreigner over thee, who is not thy brother.”
— Deuteronomy 17:15 (JPS 1917) [6]
The Torah requires Israel’s king to be one of their own kin, bound to the people he rules.
Authority goes to someone with ties to the community, as the Kural advises.
Buddhism
In the Rhinoceros Horn discourse the Buddha praises the renunciant who drops all ties of children, wife, parents, wealth and kin and wanders alone like a rhinoceros horn.
— Sutta Nipāta 1.3, Khaggavisāṇa Sutta [7]
For the seeker of liberation, freedom from attachment is an ideal, not a danger.
This is a contrast: the Kural is speaking of state service, where the unattached lack the social brake of shame.
Jainism
“He who has given up his former connections (with his parents, &c.), with his kinsmen and relations, and who is not given to pleasure, him we call a Brâhmana.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 25.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
For the Jain monastic ideal, cutting ties with kin is a mark of spiritual worth rather than of untrustworthiness.
The kural distrusts those without kin because they have nothing binding them to shame; Jainism honours the monk who has deliberately given up kin, an honest contrast in values, though it presumes he is bound instead by his vows.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, do not associate with the faithless cynics; run far away from them.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1371, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir warns against keeping company with the saakat, the person cut off from God, because such a one has no inner bond to hold him back from wrong.
The Kural says not to trust those who are cut off from all ties, since without attachments they feel no shame; Kabir likewise warns against relying on those cut off from the bond that restrains.
Greek Mythology
Nestor warns the Achaean chiefs that a man who loves the horror of civil strife is clanless, lawless and hearthless (aphrētōr, athemistos, anestios).
— Homer, Iliad 9.63-64 [10]
Homer groups being without clan, without law and without hearth as the marks of a dangerous man.
Being without kin and without restraint go together, just as the Kural says.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that man is by nature a political animal, and that one who is by nature without a city is either a bad man or above humanity, like the ‘tribeless, lawless, hearthless one’ Homer condemns.
— Aristotle, Politics 1.2 (1253a) [11]
For Aristotle, ties to household and city are what make ethical life possible.
The rootless person, lacking these ties, is the one the Kural warns us not to trust.

Real Life Example

The Praetorian Guard sells its support to Didius Julianus
March 193 CE · Rome, Roman Empire (now Italy)
After the Praetorian Guard killed the emperor Pertinax in 193, two men sought its backing for the throne, and the wealthy senator Didius Julianus won by promising the soldiers the larger cash payment. The Guard then proclaimed him emperor. Julianus ruled only about two months before he was deposed when Septimius Severus marched on Rome.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Soldiers whose loyalty was tied to no ruler and no community gave the empire to whoever paid most, with no apparent concern for the disgrace.
The kural warns against trusting people with no ties, since having no attachments they feel no shame at blame, and the Guard's sale of its support is a stark case.
