Kural 372

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

பேதைப் படுக்கும் இழவூழ் அறிவகற்றும் ஆகலூழ் உற்றக் கடை.
Pedhaip padukkum izhavoozh arivagatrum Aagaloozh utrak kadai.

Meaning

When fate brings loss, it makes even a clever person act foolishly. When fate brings gain, it opens up a person’s mind and makes it wider.

Elementary

Have you ever had a day when you kept making silly mistakes, and another day when every answer just clicked? This kural says bad fortune can make even smart people act silly, and good fortune can make people seem wiser than usual.

University

The couplet extends the previous one from will to intellect: இழவூழ் (izhavuzh, ‘the fate of loss’) makes one பேதை (a fool), while ஆகலூழ் (aagaluzh, ‘the fate of increase’) அறிவு அகற்றும், widening or enlarging understanding. Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasan read அகற்றும் as ‘to expand’, not ‘to remove’. The idea is that judgement itself is vulnerable to fortune: brilliant people make ruinous decisions when their luck is turning. The same intuition appears across cultures as the sense that those heading for ruin are first robbed of good judgement, and in the biblical account of Ahithophel’s wise counsel being ‘defeated’ by divine design. Philosophically, it raises the question of moral luck: how far our rationality depends on circumstances we do not control.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Krishna traces a downward chain: attachment breeds desire, desire breeds anger, anger breeds delusion, delusion bewilders memory, and when memory is bewildered intelligence is lost and one falls.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62-63 [3]
The Gita shows how a person’s intelligence (buddhi) can be lost step by step, leading to ruin.
Both describe a loss of reason that comes before a fall, though the Gita blames passion rather than fate.
Christianity
“I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and will bring to nothing the understanding of the prudent.”
— 1 Corinthians 1:19 (KJV) [4]
Paul, quoting Isaiah, says God can overturn human cleverness whenever He chooses.
The Kural says a fate of loss makes the wise foolish; here a higher power voids the understanding of the prudent.
Islam
“He gives wisdom to whom He wills, and whoever has been given wisdom has certainly been given much good.”
— Qur'an 2:269 (Saheeh International) [5]
Wisdom is described as a gift God grants to whomever He chooses.
Matches the second half of the Kural: a favourable decree enlarges understanding.
Judaism
“For the LORD had appointed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel, to the intent that the LORD might bring evil upon Absalom.”
— 2 Samuel 17:14 (Hebrew Bible; KJV rendering) [4]
Absalom rejects the sound advice of Ahithophel for worse advice because God has decreed his downfall.
A near-perfect narrative example: a ruler bound for loss is led to choose foolishly.
Buddhism
“To his own ruin the fool gains knowledge, for it cleaves his head and destroys his innate goodness.”
— Dhammapada 72 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
In the wrong person, knowledge itself becomes a tool of self-destruction.
Both texts see that intelligence can end in ruin, though the Dhammapada blames character rather than fate.
Jainism
“Gñânâvaranîya (which acts as an obstruction to right knowledge)” … “Obstruction of knowledge is fivefold (viz. obstruction to): a. Sruta, knowledge derived from the sacred books; b. Âbhinibôdhika, perception; c. Avadhigñâna, supernatural knowledge; d. Manahparyâya, knowledge of the thoughts of other people; e. Kêvala, the highest, unlimited knowledge.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 33.2 and 33.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The first of the eight karmas is knowledge-obscuring karma. It clouds every level of understanding, from book learning up to omniscience, so that how clear or dull a person's mind is depends partly on how much of this karma is active.
Valluvar says a fate of loss makes even a clever person foolish while a fate of gain widens understanding. Jainism likewise explains the dulling and widening of intellect by the rise or loosening of a particular karma.
Sikhism
“Those, whom the Creator Lord would destroy - first He strips them of virtue.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a hymn of the Babarvani, reflecting on the ruin of the rich and powerful in Babar's invasion, Guru Nanak observes that before the Creator brings someone down, He first takes away their goodness and judgement.
The Kural says a fate of loss makes even the clever act foolishly; Guru Nanak names the same pattern, in which ruin begins by stripping a person of the qualities that would have saved them.
Greek Mythology
The chorus in Sophocles’ Antigone sings that evil comes to seem good to the one whose mind a god is leading toward ruin.
— Sophocles, Antigone 620-625 [9]
Greek tragedy often shows ruin arriving through a god-sent blindness of judgement (atē).
The same pattern as இழவூழ் making a person foolish: judgement fails first, then fortune follows.
Philosophy
In the Myth of Er, the soul that draws the first lot hastily chooses the life of a great tyrant through folly and greed, and only later discovers that it includes the fate of devouring his own children.
— Plato, Republic X, 619b-d [10]
Plato shows how a lack of wisdom at the decisive moment locks a soul into a disastrous destiny.
Folly and ruin travel together in both texts, though Plato stresses the soul’s own responsibility.

Real Life Example

Napoleon waits in Moscow
June-December 1812 · Moscow and the road from Moscow to the Niemen River, Russian Empire
In June 1812 Napoleon, then the most successful general in Europe, invaded Russia with a force usually estimated at between 450,000 and 600,000 men. The Russian armies retreated, and after the costly Battle of Borodino in September he entered a largely deserted Moscow, much of which was soon destroyed by fire. He waited there about five weeks for Tsar Alexander I to offer peace, which never came, and left on 19 October; by the end of the winter retreat only a small fraction of his army remained.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The campaign is a classic example of a commander with an unmatched record of victories making a series of misjudgements that destroyed his army and began the end of his empire.
The kural says a fate of loss makes even the clever act foolishly, and Napoleon's long, fruitless wait in Moscow as winter approached is often cited as the moment his famous judgement failed him.
