Kural 886

Internal Enmity · Utpakai · உட்பகை

ஒன்றாமை ஒன்றியார் கட்படின் எஞ்ஞான்றும் பொன்றாமை ஒன்றல் அரிது.
Ondraamai ondriyaar katpatin egngnaandrum pondraamai ondral aridhu.

Meaning

Once people who were united start to split apart, it is always hard for them to escape being destroyed.

Elementary

Think of a team that always played together well. If the players start secretly disliking each other, they stop passing the ball and stop helping, and soon even an easy match is lost. Once a group splits, it is very hard to stop it from falling apart.

University

The couplet is a study in the root ondru (ஒன்று, 'to be one, to unite'): ondraamai (ஒன்றாமை, disunity) arises among ondriyaar (ஒன்றியார், those who were united), and then pondraamai ondral (பொன்றாமை ஒன்றல், 'attaining non-perishing', i.e. escaping ruin) becomes aridhu (அரிது, difficult) egngnaandrum (எஞ்ஞான்றும், at any time). Mu. Varadarasanar reads it as: when enmity arises among one's own people, it is always hard to avoid perishing through that internal enmity; Solomon Pappaiah applies it to a government whose fall cannot then be prevented. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it as the difficulty of ever reuniting once a group is split by another's intrigue. Both sources print the same text, and thirukkural.net transliterates katpatin, reflecting the sandhi of kan + padin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Panchatantra's first book, 'The Separation of Friends', the jackal Damanaka sows mistrust between the lion king Pingalaka and his close friend, the bull Sanjivaka, until the lion kills the bull.
— Panchatantra, Book I (Mitrabheda) [3]
The frame story shows how a strong alliance is destroyed once suspicion is planted between its members.
It illustrates the Kural's teaching that discord among the united makes ruin almost inescapable.
Christianity
“Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house divided against itself shall not stand.”
— Matthew 12:25 (KJV) [4]
Jesus states it as a general law: a divided kingdom, city or household cannot survive.
This is nearly a restatement of the Kural's claim that disunity among the united leads to ruin.
Islam
“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided. And remember the favor of Allah upon you - when you were enemies and He brought your hearts together and you became, by His favor, brothers.”
— Qur'an 3:103 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an commands the community to stay united and reminds it that it was once divided by enmity.
Both texts treat unity as survival and division as the road to ruin.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that the Second Temple fell, though its generation studied Torah and did good deeds, because of sinat chinam, baseless hatred among Jews, which it weighs as equal to idolatry, sexual sin and bloodshed combined.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 9b [6]
Rabbinic tradition locates the cause of national catastrophe in internal enmity rather than in the Roman army.
This is the Kural's lesson: once the united fall into enmity, destruction follows.
Buddhism
When King Ajatasattu plans to attack the Vajjians, the Buddha says they will prosper as long as they meet often and meet, act and disperse in harmony; tradition holds that they were later conquered only after their unity was broken by a planted agent.
— Digha Nikaya 16, Mahaparinibbana Sutta 1.4 [7]
The Mahaparinibbana Sutta makes concord the first condition of a people's survival.
It mirrors the Kural: the united are safe until discord enters, after which ruin is hard to avoid.
Jainism
“But when Mahavira refused to admit his claim, Jamali felt humiliated and finally left him to establish his own order. His order, however, does not appear to have lasted for long.”
— Jain community history, 'Rival Sects' (Nihnavas section), jainworld.com [8]
Jamāli, a monk of Mahāvīra's own order and by tradition his kinsman, caused the first schism (nihnava). His following fell away, and the breakaway group did not survive him.
The Kural says that once discord arises among those who were united, escaping ruin is hard, and the first Jain schism shows a split from within a united community ending in the dissenter's own decline.
Sikhism
After Maharaja Ranjit Singh died in 1839, the Sikh state he had built broke down within six years through palace revolutions and assassinations, and after two wars with the British the Punjab was annexed in 1849.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, 'Sikh Wars' (Britannica Editors) [9]
A kingdom that had been a formidable power under one ruler fell quickly once its own leaders turned against each other.
This bears out the Kural's warning that once discord takes hold among those who were united, escaping destruction becomes very hard.
Greek Mythology
At the close of the Eumenides, Athena prays that civil strife, insatiable of evil, may never roar in Athens and that citizens may not drink one another's blood in vengeance.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides 976-987 [10]
The trilogy ends by naming civil discord as the gravest threat to the newly founded city.
Like the Kural, it sees internal division as the deadliest danger to a united people.
Philosophy
Socrates asks whether there is any greater evil for a city than what tears it apart and makes it many instead of one, or any greater good than what binds it together and makes it one.
— Plato, Republic 462a-b [11]
Plato treats the unity of the city as its highest good and division as its worst evil.
The Kural similarly sees disunity among those once united as the gravest danger.

Real Life Example

Athens and Sparta fight the Peloponnesian War
431-404 BCE · Greece and the Aegean
Athens and Sparta had fought together against the Persian invasions, but rivalry over Athenian power in the Delian League led to the Peloponnesian War, fought from 431 to 404 BCE and ending in Athens' defeat. The Greek city-states continued fighting among themselves for decades afterwards. Weakened by this infighting, they were defeated by Philip II of Macedon at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BCE, after which they lost their independence.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The cities that had united to save Greece from Persia wore each other down until an outside power could master them all.
The kural says that once discord takes hold among those who were united, it is ever hard to escape destruction, as the Greek city-states found.
