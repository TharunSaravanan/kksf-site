Kural 284

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

களவின்கண் கன்றிய காதல் விளைவின்கண் வீயா விழுமம் தரும்.
Kalavinkan kandriya kaadhal vilaivinkan, Veeyaa vizhumam tharum.

Meaning

A strong wish to gain by stealing will, in the end, bring sorrow that never ends.

Elementary

Wanting to cheat is like planting a thorn bush because its flowers look pretty. When it grows up, the thorns keep poking you, and you can never get rid of them.

University

Kaṉṟiya kātal — a desire that has become hardened or habitual — is the subject, and the agricultural verb viḷaivu (ripening, harvest) sets up the karmic metaphor: fraud is a seed whose fruit is vīyā viḻumam, “unperishing affliction.” The emphasis on habit links this couplet to 286 and 288, where Valluvar treats fraud as a character formed by repetition. The imagery of delayed but certain ripening is close to the Dhammapada’s description of evil deeds that seem sweet until they ripen. Commentators split between reading the sorrow as this-worldly (disgrace, punishment) and as extending into later births; the adjective vīyā allows both.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 [5]
Krishna names greed among the three doors to hell and says these passions degrade the self.
The Kural’s ‘hardened craving’ for fraudulent gain is exactly lobha (greed), and its fruit is lasting ruin.
Christianity
“Bread of deceit is sweet to a man; but afterwards his mouth shall be filled with gravel.”
— Proverbs 20:17 (KJV) [6]
Proverbs describes deceitful gain as sweet at first and painful in its aftertaste.
Matches the Kural’s movement from desire to a bitter harvest (viḷaivu).
Islam
“And whoever betrays, [taking unlawfully], will come with what he took on the Day of Resurrection. Then will every soul be [fully] compensated for what it earned, and they will not be wronged.”
— Qur’an 3:161 (Sahih International) [7]
The Qur’an warns that whoever misappropriates goods will carry them to Judgement, where every soul receives its due.
The fraudulent gain becomes a lasting burden, like the Kural’s ‘unperishing affliction’.
Judaism
“For all that do such things, even all that do unrighteously, are an abomination unto the LORD thy God.”
— Deuteronomy 25:16 (JPS 1917) [8]
After commanding honest weights and measures, the Torah declares that anyone who deals dishonestly is an abomination to God.
The lasting harm of the Kural is framed here as enduring estrangement from God.
Buddhism
“So long as an evil deed has not ripened, the fool thinks it as sweet as honey. But when the evil deed ripens, the fool comes to grief.”
— Dhammapada 69 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Dhammapada says the consequences of an evil deed stay hidden until they ripen, and then they bring grief.
Shares the Kural’s harvest metaphor: wrong ‘ripens’ (viḷaivu) into sorrow.
Jainism
“Thus when he takes another's property, and is (after all) not satisfied by the 'colours' (he has obtained), he becomes unhappy, and nobody will protect him.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.29-31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [10]
Lecture 32 traces a chain: craving grows, greed leads a man to take another's property, and the theft brings only deeper misery and no protection. The lecture adds that passion accumulates karman, which in the end produces further misery.
The Kural says an ingrained craving for fraudulent gain ripens into endless sorrow. The Jain text likewise shows craving that ends in theft bearing fruit only in unhappiness, with no one to protect the thief.
Sikhism
“Those who are attached to the poison of greed and sin grab at the wealth of others; they only bring pain on themselves.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 408, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In a hymn in Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan says that people held by greed seize others' wealth and harvest only suffering. Sikh teaching names greed (lobh) as one of the five thieves within.
The Kural warns that a hardened craving for gain by fraud bears fruit in endless sorrow. Guru Arjan likewise traces the grabbing of others' wealth to greed and says it ends in pain for the grabber.
Greek Mythology
In Pindar’s version, Tantalus, a guest at the gods’ table, stole nectar and ambrosia and gave them to his mortal drinking companions; for this he was punished forever, with a huge stone hanging over his head.
— Pindar, Olympian 1.54-64 [12]
A theft driven by craving and pride is repaid with punishment that never ends.
A mythic image of the Kural’s vīyā viḻumam — sorrow that does not die — as the harvest of theft.
Philosophy
In the Gorgias, Socrates argues that doing injustice is worse than suffering it, and that the wrongdoer who escapes punishment is the most wretched of all, because his soul stays diseased.
— Plato, Gorgias 472e-479e [13]
For Plato the real harm of wrongdoing is inside the wrongdoer: a lasting corruption of the soul.
Gives a philosophical account of why the Kural’s sorrow is ‘undying’: the damage is to the self, not just to fortune.

Real Life Example

Cassie Chadwick's lifetime of fraud ends in prison
1905-1907 · Cleveland and Columbus, Ohio, United States
Elizabeth Bigley, later known as Cassie Chadwick, began forging documents as a girl and served a prison term in Ohio for forgery before being released early. From the late 1890s she borrowed large sums from banks, using forged securities bearing Andrew Carnegie's name and letting it be believed she was his secret daughter. Her scheme was exposed in November 1904, she was convicted in March 1905 of conspiracy, including against a bank in Oberlin that collapsed, and she died in the Ohio Penitentiary in 1907.
— Sources: [14] [15] [16]
Her career shows a habit of deception repeated over decades until it ended in disgrace, imprisonment and death behind bars.
The kural says an ingrained craving for gain by fraud, when it ripens, yields sorrow without end, and Chadwick's long habit ripened into ruin for her and her victims.
