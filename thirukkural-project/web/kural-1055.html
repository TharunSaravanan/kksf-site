Kural 1055

Mendicancy · Iravu · இரவு

கரப்பிலார் வையகத்து உண்மையால் கண்ணின்று இரப்பவர் மேற்கொள் வது.
Karappilaar vaiyagaththu unmaiyaal kannindru irappavar merkol vadhu.

Meaning

Some people are willing to stand before others and ask for help only because the world has people who never hold back when asked.

Elementary

People bring their empty buckets to a well because they know the well has water. In the same way, people in need ask because they know there are kind people who will share.

University

The kural explains why anyone begs at all: because karappilaar (கரப்பிலார், 'those without concealment') exist in the world (vaiyagaththu unmaiyaal). Kan nindru (கண்ணின்று, 'standing before the eyes') is read in two ways: Mu. Varadarasanar takes it as standing in front of a giver, while Solomon Pappaiah reads it as asking with the eyes rather than with words, the silent request of the proud poor. Puliyur Kesikan adds that such people beg only to preserve life. The sense of merkolvadhu (மேற்கொள்வது, 'taking up' a practice) suggests begging chosen reluctantly because generous givers are known to exist. The texts on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net agree (மேற்கொள் வது with a space in both).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Bounteous is he who gives unto the beggar who comes to him in want of food and feeble.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.3 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [3]
The Vedic hymn names as truly bountiful the man who gives to a feeble beggar who comes to him.
The beggar comes to him because such a giver exists, which is the logic of the Kural.
Christianity
“And a certain man lame from his mother's womb was carried, whom they laid daily at the gate of the temple which is called Beautiful, to ask alms of them that entered into the temple”
— Acts 3:2 (KJV) [4]
A lame man is carried every day to the temple gate, where worshippers pass and might give.
He is placed where givers are known to pass, which illustrates the Kural's point that asking exists because givers exist.
Islam
“An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign. They do not ask people persistently [or at all].”
— Qur'an 2:273 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes poor people too dignified to ask, who must be recognised by their look rather than by any words.
This is Pappaiah's reading of kan nindru: the poor who ask with their eyes, trusting that a discerning giver will see.
Judaism
“Let thy house be wide open, and let the poor be members of thy household.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:5 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Dr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Yose ben Yochanan teaches that a home should stand open so that the poor come in as if they belonged.
A house whose door is always open is why the needy come to it, which is the Kural's reasoning.
Buddhism
“Alms food is next to nothing, both easy to gain & blameless.”
— Itivuttaka 101 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha counts alms food among the requisites that are easy to get, so a monk can rely on it.
Alms are 'easy to gain' only because there are generous givers, which the Kural gives as the reason people take up asking.
Jainism
After Rishabhanatha renounced the world, people did not know how to give a mendicant proper alms. They offered jewels, horses and even daughters, and he went without food for about a year until Prince Shreyamsa recognised the right way and offered him sugarcane juice, the event remembered on Akshaya Tritiya.
— Hemacandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 1 (life of Rishabha) (tr. Helen M. Johnson) (paraphrase) [8]
The Jain tradition holds that the mendicant's alms round could only work once there were householders who knew how to give.
The story dramatises the Kural's point: asking depends on the existence of givers who know how to give.
Sikhism
“People beg and pray, "Give to us, give to us", and the Great Giver gives His Gifts.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 2, Japji Sahib (Pauri 4), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak describes all creatures constantly asking and God constantly giving; asking makes sense because a Giver exists who answers it.
The Kural says people take up asking only because there are those in the world who never hold back; Guru Nanak likewise grounds all asking in the existence of the Great Giver.
Greek Mythology
“a beggar can always do better in town than country, for any one who likes can give him something.”
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
The disguised Odysseus says a beggar should go where there are many people able to give.
Beggars go where givers are, which is the Kural's explanation of why people take up asking.
Philosophy
“He was known as the ‘Door-opener’ – the caller to whom all doors fly open – from his habit of entering every house and admonishing those within.”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.86 (tr. R. D. Hicks) [11]
The Cynic Crates lived without property and was welcome in every Athenian house.
He could live by asking only because doors opened to him, which is the Kural's point that asking exists because open-handed people exist.

Real Life Example

Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr open Hull-House
1889 · Chicago, Illinois, United States
Inspired by Toynbee Hall in London, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr leased a large house built by Charles Hull in an immigrant district of Chicago in 1889 and moved in to live among their neighbours. They raised money, cared for children, nursed the sick and listened to people in trouble, and by its second year Hull-House was receiving some two thousand people a week, soon adding a public kitchen, kindergarten classes and evening courses.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Once it was known that Hull-House would not turn people away, the needy of the neighbourhood came to its door in great numbers.
The kural says people take up asking only because there are those in the world who never hold back, and the stream of visitors to Hull-House grew precisely because its founders were known to give freely.
