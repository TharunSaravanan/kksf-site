Kural 1272

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

கண்ணிறைந்த காரிகைக் காம்பேர்தோட் பேதைக்குப் பெண்நிறைந்த நீர்மை பெரிது.
Kanniraindha kaarikaik kaamperdhot pedhaikkup penniraindha neermai peridhu.

Meaning

My innocent love, whose beauty fills my eyes and whose shoulders are smooth like bamboo, is full of a deep womanly modesty.

Elementary

Some people are beautiful to look at, but what makes them special is how gentle and kind they are. The poet says his beloved is lovely, but her quiet, modest nature is even bigger than her beauty.

University

The speaker praises the heroine’s kārikai (beauty) that fills the eye (kaṇ niṟainta) and her shoulders like the bamboo (kāmpu ēr tōḷ), a stock Tamil image of smooth, rounded grace; pētai here means an artless, innocent young woman. Her peṇ niṟainta nīrmai (‘a nature full of womanliness’) is said to be peritu, very great. Commentators gloss peṇmai variously: Mu. Varadarasanar as the quality of womanhood, Solomon Pappaiah as an excellence surpassing that found in all women, and Kalaignar as womanliness that is itself her greatest beauty. In the traditional reading associated with Parimelazhagar, the speaker is the husband telling the confidante (tōḻi) that his wife’s modesty is so great that she cannot voice her own feelings, which is why they must be read through signs. The couplet reads identically on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Parvati comes before Shiva and his composure stirs, she betrays her own feeling only through her trembling limbs and stands with her lovely face turned aside, too shy to speak.
— Kalidasa, Kumarasambhava, Canto 3 (paraphrase) [3]
Kalidasa portrays Parvati’s love as visible in her bashful bearing rather than in any words.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Parvati is beautiful but her modesty is what governs how her love can be expressed.
Christianity
“Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; But let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price.”
— 1 Peter 3:3-4 (KJV) [4]
The apostle ranks a gentle, quiet spirit above outward beauty and adornment.
The Kural likewise says the beloved’s modest nature is greater than the beauty that fills the eye.
Islam
“Then one of the two women came to him walking with shyness.”
— Qur’an 28:25 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an notes the modest bearing of the daughter of Madyan who came to invite Moses to her father’s house.
The Kural, too, singles out a young woman’s shyness as her most notable quality.
Judaism
“Grace is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised.”
— Proverbs 31:30 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The poem of the woman of valour ends by placing character above charm and beauty.
The Kural’s speaker reaches the same verdict: her womanly nature is greater than her looks.
Buddhism
Queen Mallika asks the Buddha why some women are beautiful and others plain, and he explains that outward appearance follows from inner qualities such as freedom from anger.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.197, Mallika Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The sutta ties a woman’s beauty to her character rather than treating it as independent of it.
The Kural also looks past beauty to the womanly nature beneath it, though without any teaching on karma.
Jainism
“Now this daughter of an excellent king was virtuous and well looking; she possessed all lucky marks of the body, and shone forth like the lightning Saudâmanî.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana introduces Rajimati, betrothed to Arishtanemi, by naming her virtue before her beauty.
As in the Kural, the bride is beautiful, but her virtue comes first.
Sikhism
“They are also known as 'satee', who abide in modesty and contentment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 787, Raag Soohee, Soohee ki Vaar (salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das redefines the 'satee' (the virtuous wife): she is honoured for her modesty and contentment, not, as in the custom he rejected, for dying on her husband's pyre.
The Kural praises a woman whose modesty is greater even than her beauty; Guru Amar Das likewise says a woman's true honour lies in her modesty.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus, meeting Nausicaa on the shore, compares her to a young palm shoot he once saw springing up beside Apollo’s altar on Delos, and speaks to her with awe.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.160-169 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer pictures a modest young woman’s grace through a slender growing plant.
The Kural’s bamboo shoulders and Odysseus’s palm sapling are parallel plant images for a modest girl’s beauty.
Philosophy
Diotima teaches that the lover should come to value beauty of soul above beauty of the body.
— Plato, Symposium 210b-c (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato’s ladder of love moves from bodily beauty toward the higher beauty of character.
The Kural’s speaker makes the same move, praising her nature as greater than her looks.

Real Life Example

Emily Dickinson's poems are found after her death
1886-1890 · Amherst, Massachusetts, United States
Emily Dickinson lived a quiet, secluded life in Amherst and published only a handful of poems during her lifetime. After her death in 1886, her family found hand-sewn booklets of her poems among her belongings. A first selection was published in 1890, and she is now regarded as one of the two leading American poets of the nineteenth century.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dickinson's reserve was so great that the depth of her inner life became known only after she was gone.
The kural's husband says his wife's modesty is even greater than her beauty, so great that her feelings must be read through signs; Dickinson's retiring nature kept her greatest gift hidden in the same way.
