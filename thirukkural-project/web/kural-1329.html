Kural 1329

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

ஊடுக மன்னோ ஒளியிழை யாமிரப்ப நீடுக மன்னோ இரா.
Ootuka mannoa oliyizhai yaamirappa Needuka mannoa iraa.

Meaning

Let her with the shining jewels sulk a little longer. And let the night grow longer too, so I can keep pleading with her.

Elementary

Sometimes making up with a friend is so much fun that you almost wish the game would go on. Here a husband enjoys coaxing his wife out of her playful sulk so much that he wishes the night would stretch out, so he can keep coaxing.

University

Both lines turn on the optative with the particle maṉṉō (‘O, may it be so!’): ūṭuka (‘may she sulk’) and nīṭuka (‘may it lengthen’), framing the hero’s wish. Oḷi iḻai (‘she of bright jewels’) is a standard epithet for the heroine, and yām irappa (‘so that I may beg, plead’) gives the reason for wanting the night (irā) to stretch on. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan agree in reading the couplet as the husband’s wish that her sulk and the night both continue so that he may keep entreating her; Pope renders it ‘That I may still plead on, O night, prolong thy reign!’ The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Bhagavata Purana says that on the night of the Rasa dance Krishna extended the night so that it lasted as long as a night of Brahma, and the gopis enjoyed his company throughout it.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.33 (the ‘night of Brahma’; paraphrase) [3]
The divine lover stretches time itself so that the night of love will not end.
The Kural’s husband wishes for exactly this, a night prolonged so that the play of love can continue.
Christianity
“I say unto you, Though he will not rise and give him, because he is his friend, yet because of his importunity he will rise and give him as many as he needeth.”
— Luke 11:8 (KJV) [4]
In Jesus’ parable, a friend knocking at midnight is answered because he keeps on asking.
The Kural’s husband, too, keeps on pleading through the night, and enjoys the asking.
Islam
In the hadith of Umm Zar‘, Aisha tells the Prophet a long story of eleven women describing their husbands; he listens to the end and then tells her that he is to her as Abu Zar‘, the kindest husband in the tale, was to Umm Zar‘.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5189, hadith of Umm Zar‘ (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith shows the Prophet patiently enjoying his wife’s company and conversation.
Like the Kural’s husband, who wants the night to last, the Prophet takes pleasure in lingering time with his wife.
Judaism
In the Book of Ruth, Ruth comes to Boaz at night on the threshing floor and asks him to take her under his protection; he tells her to stay the night until morning, and promises to act on her request.
— Ruth 3:7-13 (paraphrase) [6]
The scene is one of the Bible’s tenderest night encounters, full of courteous asking and answering.
As in the Kural, a night of gentle pleading leads to a loving union.
Buddhism
In the Kusa Jataka, the Bodhisatta as King Kusa, whose wife Pabhavati has left him, follows her to her father’s city and humbly works as a potter, basket-maker, gardener and cook, trying again and again to win her back until at last she returns to him.
— Jataka 531, Kusa Jataka (ed. E. B. Cowell; paraphrase) [7]
The Jataka is a story of a lover’s long, humble persistence in pleading.
Kusa’s patient pleading resembles the Kural’s husband, who is happy to keep pleading with his sulking wife.
Jainism
The Tamil Jain epic Jivaka Chintamani follows its hero through a series of romances and marriages, celebrated at length in courtly love poetry, before he finally renounces the world and becomes a Jain monk.
— Tiruttakkatevar, Jivaka Chintamani (paraphrase) [8]
The epic gives full poetic space to married love, while framing it as a stage that ends in renunciation.
Its love poetry shares the Kural’s delight in lovers’ time together, but its ending places that delight below renunciation.
Sikhism
“Fareed, musk is released at night. Those who are sleeping do not receive their share.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Salok Sheikh Fareed (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, whose verses are in the Guru Granth Sahib, teaches that the night hours are precious for the lover of God, who stays awake to seek the Beloved.
The Kural's lover wishes the night would stretch on so he can keep pleading with his beloved; Farid likewise treasures the night as the time for longing and entreaty.
Greek Mythology
“Indeed they would have gone on indulging their sorrow till rosy-fingered morn appeared, had not Minerva determined otherwise, and held night back in the far west, while she would not suffer Dawn to leave Oceanus, nor to yoke the two steeds Lampus and Phaethon that bear her onward to break the day upon mankind.”
— Homer, Odyssey 23 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
On the night Odysseus and Penelope are reunited, Athena holds back the dawn to lengthen their night.
The Kural’s husband prays for the very thing Athena grants the reunited couple, a longer night.
Philosophy
In Amores 1.13, Ovid scolds Dawn for coming too soon and taking him from his beloved, and begs the horses of Night to run slowly (lente currite, noctis equi), a line Marlowe later borrowed for Doctor Faustus.
— Ovid, Amores 1.13 (paraphrase) [11]
Roman love poetry has a whole genre of the lover’s complaint against the dawn.
Ovid’s plea to slow the horses of Night is the Kural’s nīṭuka maṉṉō irā, ‘may the night be long’.

Real Life Example

Antoine Galland publishes The Thousand and One Nights in French
1704-1717 · Paris, France
Between 1704 and 1717 the French scholar Antoine Galland published Les Mille et Une Nuits in twelve volumes, the first European translation of The Thousand and One Nights. In its frame story Shahrazad tells the king a story each evening, leaving it unfinished so that he wishes to hear the end, until he finally abandons his cruel plan. Galland's version caused a craze across Europe and was itself translated into many languages.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Galland's work brought one of the world's great story collections to the West and made Shahrazad's night-after-night storytelling famous.
The lover in this kural wishes the night would grow longer so that he can go on pleading, and the most famous story of a speaker who stretches out the nights with her words is Shahrazad's.
