Kural 1068

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

இரவென்னும் ஏமாப்பில் தோணி கரவென்னும் பார்தாக்கப் பக்கு விடும்.
Iravennum emaappil thoni karavennum paardhaakkap pakku vidum.

Meaning

Begging is like an unsafe little boat. When it hits the rock of someone who hides what they have, it breaks apart.

Elementary

Picture a small paper boat floating on a stream. It looks fine until it bumps into a hard rock and breaks. Asking for help is like that boat, and someone's cold 'no' is the rock that breaks it.

University

The couplet is an extended metaphor (uruvakam): iravu ('begging') is a thoni (தோணி, 'small boat, raft') that is emaappil (ஏமாப்பில், 'without protection, unsafe'), and karavu (கரவு, 'concealment, refusal') is a paar (பார், 'submerged rock, reef'); when it strikes, it pakku vidum ('splits open'). Commentators in the Parimelazhagar line, as in Puliyur Kesikan and Solomon Pappaiah, supply the implied sea: the boat is taken to cross the ocean of poverty. Kalaignar Karunanidhi reads the rock as 'the stone heart that hides what it has and says no'. Pope renders it 'The fragile bark of beggary / Wrecked on denial's rock will lie.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Rig Veda hymn on liberality says that the riches of the generous never waste away, while the one who will not give finds no one to comfort him.
— Rig Veda 10.117.1 (tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith) (paraphrase) [3]
The Vedic hymn contrasts the lasting wealth of the giver with the friendlessness of the refuser.
The Kural shows the other side of that refusal: the asker's fragile hope breaks on it.
Christianity
“And one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?”
— James 2:16 (KJV) [4]
James condemns those who send the needy away with words instead of help.
Such a refusal is the 'rock' on which the Kural's small boat of asking breaks.
Islam
“And let not those who [greedily] withhold what Allāh has given them of His bounty ever think that it is better for them. Rather, it is worse for them. Their necks will be encircled by what they withheld on the Day of Resurrection.”
— Qur'an 3:180 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an warns that those who withhold God's bounty will be burdened with it on the Last Day.
The Kural's karavu, withholding, is the same fault, here seen from the side of the one who is refused.
Judaism
“The pauper speaks in terms of mercy; / The rich person’s answer is harsh.”
— Proverbs 18:23 (The JPS Tanakh: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs observes that the poor plead gently while the rich answer them harshly.
The poor man's plea and the rich man's hard reply are the Kural's frail boat and its rock.
Buddhism
The Parabhava Sutta lists among the causes of a person's downfall having much wealth, gold and food and yet enjoying it alone, and failing to support those who depend on one.
— Parabhava Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.6 (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha names solitary, ungenerous enjoyment of wealth as a road to ruin.
The rich man who hides his wealth is the 'rock' of the Kural, and the Buddha says he wrecks himself as well.
Jainism
“Will those who possess clear understanding follow after such men as constantly revile them, saying, These poor men will become rich through our means; they cannot acquire wealth of themselves?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 1 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet asks whether the wise will run after patrons who mock and despise those who depend on them.
Such scornful patrons are the hard rock on which the Kural's frail boat of asking breaks, so the wise do not set out on it.
Sikhism
“The Guru is the Ladder, the Guru is the Boat, and the Guru is the Raft to take me to the Lord's Name.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 17, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Sri Raag, Guru Nanak pictures the Guru as the safe boat and raft that carries the soul across the world's ocean.
The Kural calls begging an unprotected little boat that splits apart on the rock of refusal; Gurbani uses the same image of a boat but offers the Guru as the vessel that does not fail.
Greek Mythology
In the Odyssey, Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, asks the suitor Antinous for food; Antinous refuses, insults him, and strikes him with a footstool, while the other suitors had at least given him something.
— Homer, Odyssey 17 (paraphrase) [10]
Antinous' violent refusal of a beggar is the Odyssey's emblem of a hard heart.
The beggar's request breaks on Antinous' refusal just as the Kural's boat breaks on the rock.
Philosophy
“He once begged alms of a statue, and, when asked why he did so, replied, ‘To get practice in being refused.’”
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers 6.49 (tr. R. D. Hicks, via Wikisource) [11]
Diogenes the Cynic trained himself to bear refusal by begging from a statue.
Diogenes knew what the Kural knows: begging is fragile and refusal is the rock that breaks it, so he hardened himself against it.

Real Life Example

Japan's racial equality proposal is blocked at the Paris Peace Conference
11 April 1919 · Paris, France
At the Paris Peace Conference Japan proposed adding to the Covenant of the League of Nations a clause affirming the equality of nations and the fair treatment of their nationals. On 11 April 1919 a majority of the League of Nations Commission voted in favour, but the chairman, US President Woodrow Wilson, ruled that it required unanimous support, and with Britain and Australia opposed, the clause was dropped.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The rejection was deeply resented in Japan and is often cited as a turning point in Japan's relations with the Western powers.
The kural compares asking to a frail little boat that splits apart when it strikes the rock of refusal, and Japan's appeal, though supported by a majority, broke on a single ruling.
