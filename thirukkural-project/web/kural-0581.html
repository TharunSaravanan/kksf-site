Kural 581

Detectives · Otraadal · ஒற்றாடல்

ஒற்றும் உரைசான்ற நூலும் இவையிரண்டும் தெற்றென்க மன்னவன் கண்.
Otrum uraisaandra noolum ivaiyirandum Thetrenga mannavan kan.

Meaning

A ruler should know that he has two eyes: his spies, who show him what is really happening, and the respected books of law, which show him what should be done.

Elementary

A captain steering a ship needs two things: a lookout who shouts what he sees on the sea, and a map that shows the right route. With only one, the ship gets lost. This kural says a ruler is the same: he needs people who tell him what is really going on, and good rules that tell him what is right.

University

The chapter title ஒற்றாடல் means ‘the employment (ஆடல், handling) of spies’; one printed text glosses it ஒற்றரை ஆளும் திறம், ‘the skill of managing spies’. It follows directly on கண்ணோட்டம் (ch. 58, ‘the kindly eye’), and Valluvar continues the eye image by naming two organs of royal sight: ஒற்று (the spy, or intelligence generally) and உரைசான்ற நூல் (a treatise ‘full of renown’). Parimelazhagar takes the second to be the science of polity (நீதிநூல்), and the commentators draw the contrast that the treatise shows what should be done while the spy shows what is being done; a king lacking either is half-blind. The verb தெற்றென்க (‘let him know clearly’) turns the saying into a direct order. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar all read it the same way, and the text is identical on ytamizh.com, thirukkural.net and thirukkural-thamizh.org, the last printing it in older sandhi-joined form (ஒற்று முரைசான்ற நூலு மிவையிரண்டுந்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let the king who sees (everything) through his spies, discover the two sorts of thieves who deprive others of their property, both those who (show themselves) openly and those who (lie) concealed.”
— Manusmriti 9.256 (trans. G. Bühler) [4]
Manu describes the ideal king as one who ‘sees through his spies’ (the Sanskrit ideal of the cāra-cakṣus, the spy-eyed ruler) and uses that sight to apply the law against open and hidden wrongdoers.
Spies as the king’s eyes, working together with the law, is exactly the Kural’s pair of ‘two eyes’.
Christianity
“For the word of God is quick, and powerful … and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart. Neither is there any creature that is not manifest in his sight: but all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do.”
— Hebrews 4:12-13 (KJV) [5]
Hebrews joins a searching, discerning word to an all-seeing eye from which nothing is hidden.
A discerning word plus a seeing eye mirrors the Kural’s law-book plus spy, though here both belong to God and not to a king.
Islam
“Indeed, We have revealed to you, [O Muḥammad], the Book in truth so you may judge between the people by that which Allāh has shown you. And do not be for the deceitful an advocate.”
— Quran 4:105 (Sahih International) [6]
The ruler-judge is to decide by the revealed Book and by what God has let him see, and is warned against being fooled into siding with deceivers.
The Book and the ‘showing’ together play the roles of the Kural’s law-text and spy-eye.
Judaism
“A king that sitteth in the throne of judgment scattereth away all evil with his eyes.”
— Proverbs 20:8 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs pictures a king on the seat of justice whose watchful sight alone drives wrongdoing away.
Judgment (law) and the king’s eyes are the two instruments of the Kural’s couplet.
Buddhism
In the Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Sutta, the righteous wheel-turning king is told to rely on the Dhamma alone, to give lawful protection to all classes, and to go regularly to wise ascetics and ask them what is wholesome and what is not, so that he stays informed and on the right path.
— Digha Nikaya 26, Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddhist ideal king governs by two supports: the Dhamma as his law and constant questioning of the wise as his source of knowledge.
Law plus active inquiry is a gentler Buddhist version of the Kural’s law-book plus spy.
Jainism
“Knowledge is fivefold: 1. Sruta, knowledge derived from the sacred books; 2. Âbhinibôdhika, perception; 3. Avadhi, supernatural knowledge; 4. Manahparyâya, knowledge of the thoughts of other people; 5. Kêvala, the highest, unlimited knowledge.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 28.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jain epistemology begins its list of the ways of knowing with two ordinary sources: what is learned from scripture (śruta) and what is learned by direct observation (mati or perception).
The Kural's two eyes, the law-books that show what ought to be done and the spies who report what is actually happening, line up with scripture-knowledge and perception, the first two Jain kinds of knowledge.
Sikhism
“The Guru has given the healing ointment of spiritual wisdom, and dispelled the darkness of ignorance.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 293, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani, salok), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Sukhmani, Guru Arjan pictures the Guru's teaching as a salve (anjan) put on the eyes, so that a person can see what the darkness of ignorance had hidden.
The Kural gives the king two eyes, his spies and the books of statecraft; Guru Arjan likewise treats the Guru's wisdom as what gives true sight, though he means the inner eye, not political intelligence.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that the maiden Dike (Justice) sits beside her father Zeus and tells him of men’s unjust minds, and that the eye of Zeus sees all and understands all.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 256-267 (paraphrase) [10]
In the Greek picture, the king of the gods rules with two helpers: Justice, who reports wrongdoing, and his own all-seeing eye.
Justice and the watching eye beside the throne match the Kural’s law and spy as a king’s two eyes.
Philosophy
Xenophon says that Cyrus the Great, by rewarding everyone who brought him useful information, came to have many so-called ‘King’s eyes’ and ‘King’s ears’ spread across his empire, so that people feared to say or do anything against him.
— Xenophon, Cyropaedia 8.2.10-12 (paraphrase) [11]
Xenophon presents a network of informants as the sense organs of a well-run empire.
The ‘King’s eyes’ are the Kural’s ஒற்று almost word for word.

Real Life Example

U-2 photographs and a lawful quarantine in the Cuban Missile Crisis
14-23 October 1962 · Cuba and Washington, D.C., United States
On 14 October 1962 an American U-2 reconnaissance aircraft photographed Soviet medium-range and intermediate-range ballistic missile sites under construction in Cuba, and analysts at the National Photographic Interpretation Center identified the missiles the next day. President John F. Kennedy and his advisers chose a naval quarantine rather than an immediate attack, using the word quarantine because a blockade would legally imply a state of war, and they sought the backing of the Organization of American States. Kennedy announced the quarantine on 22 October, and it was lifted on 20 November 1962 after the Soviet Union agreed to remove its missiles and, finally, its IL-28 bombers.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The crisis was handled with two kinds of sight working together: hard intelligence about what was actually happening, and careful attention to law and treaty about what the United States could rightfully do.
The kural says a ruler has two eyes, spies who show what is happening and the respected books of law that show what ought to be done, and in October 1962 both eyes were open at once.
