Kural 124

The Possession of Self-restraint · Adakkamudaimai · அடக்கமுடைமை

நிலையின் திரியாது அடங்கியான் தோற்றம் மலையினும் மாணப் பெரிது.
Nilaiyin thiriyaadhu adangiyaan thotram Malaiyinum maanap peridhu.

Meaning

A person who controls themselves and never leaves their proper path in life stands taller than a mountain.

Elementary

A mountain stays put no matter how hard the wind blows. A person who stays calm and humble and keeps doing the right thing, without bragging, looks even ‘taller’ to others than a mountain.

University

Parimelazhagar reads nilai (‘station’) as the householder’s way of life, which fits the couplet’s place in the Illaraviyal (the section on domestic virtue). He takes ‘restraint without swerving’ to mean self-governance while still legitimately using the senses: disciplined participation in ordinary life, not ascetic withdrawal. Manakkudavar instead glosses nilai as one’s varnashrama duty. Thotram, ‘appearance’ or ‘stature,’ is read by Solomon Pappaiah as the impression such a person leaves in others’ minds, and the paradox is deliberate: the one who lowers himself appears the loftiest. The mountain works as an image of both height and immovability, which invites comparison with the Stoic promontory and the Buddhist unshaken rock.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A person who is not disturbed by the incessant flow of desires – that enter like rivers into the ocean, which is ever being filled but is always still – can alone achieve peace, and not the man who strives to satisfy such desires.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.70 [4]
Krishna likens the steady sage to the ocean, which rivers keep pouring into without ever disturbing it.
Where Valluvar uses a mountain, the Gita uses the ocean: both picture the unswerving, restrained person as vast and unmoved.
Christianity
“And whosoever shall exalt himself shall be abased; and he that shall humble himself shall be exalted.”
— Matthew 23:12 (KJV) [5]
Jesus teaches that self-exaltation leads to humiliation and humility to being raised up.
The same paradox as the Kural: the one who holds himself low ends up standing highest.
Islam
“And do not walk upon the earth exultantly. Indeed, you will never tear the earth [apart], and you will never reach the mountains in height.”
— Qur’an 17:37 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an mocks arrogance: however proudly a person struts, he will never be as tall as the mountains.
The Qur’an says arrogance can never reach a mountain’s height; the Kural says restraint rises above it. Both use the mountain to show that true greatness comes from humility.
Judaism
“He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; And he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city.”
— Proverbs 16:32 (JPS 1917) [7]
Proverbs ranks the self-controlled person above the warrior and the conqueror.
Both texts say self-rule gives a greatness beyond the world’s most impressive achievements, a conqueror’s in one case and a mountain’s height in the other.
Buddhism
“Just as a solid rock is not shaken by the storm, even so the wise are not affected by praise or blame.”
— Dhammapada 81 [8]
The Buddha pictures the wise person as a solid rock that storms cannot move.
Like Valluvar’s mountain, the rock stands for someone who stays steady and does not swerve from his course.
Jainism
“By possession of conduct he obtains a stability like that of the king of mountains (viz. Mêru), whereby a houseless monk destroys the four remnants of Karman which even a Kêvalin possesses.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 29.61 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
Mahāvīra's catalogue of spiritual exertions teaches that steady conduct gives the soul the immovability of Mount Meru, the axis of the Jain cosmos, and carries it to liberation.
Valluvar says the stature of one who stays restrained without swerving is greater than a mountain; the Jain text uses the greatest mountain of all, Meru, as the image for the person firm in conduct.
Sikhism
“Through the Word of the Guru's Shabad, they conquer the five thieves.” … “Even as householders, they lovingly attune themselves to the True Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 230, Raag Gauree, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das describes Gurmukhs who master the passions while living the ordinary householder's life.
Parimelazhagar reads the Kural as praising restraint that never leaves the householder's station; Sikhism likewise sets self-mastery within family life, not in renunciation.
Greek Mythology
Back home in disguise as a beggar, Odysseus is mocked by the suitors and the disloyal maids. Though his heart ‘growls’ within him, he tells it to endure, recalling that it once endured worse in the Cyclops’ cave, and holds himself back until the right moment.
— Homer, Odyssey 20.1–30 (paraphrase) [11]
The hero of the Odyssey shows restraint by refusing to strike prematurely, staying true to his purpose despite provocation.
Odysseus stays restrained without swerving from his goal, and that restraint is exactly what makes his eventual victory so great.
Philosophy
“Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.49 (trans. George Long) [12]
Marcus Aurelius tells himself to stand like a rocky headland that waves keep hitting without moving it.
Marcus’ unmoved promontory and Valluvar’s mountain are the same image: a steady, self-governed person standing tall above life’s turmoil.

Real Life Example

George Washington resigns his commission
23 December 1783 · Maryland State House, Annapolis, Maryland, United States
After British troops evacuated New York at the end of the American Revolutionary War, General George Washington, commander-in-chief of the Continental Army, bade farewell to his officers and travelled to Annapolis, where the Continental Congress was meeting. On 23 December 1783, in the Senate chamber of the Maryland State House, he resigned his commission before Congress in an emotional ceremony. He left Annapolis at dawn the next day and reached his home at Mount Vernon on Christmas Eve, a private citizen for the first time in almost nine years.
— Sources: [13] [14]
At the height of his power and popularity, Washington handed military authority back to the civilian government and returned to private life.
The kural says the stature of one who stays restrained and does not swerve from his proper path rises higher than a mountain; Washington's voluntary return to private life is still commemorated in the room where it happened.
