Kural 1091

Recognition of the Signs (of Mutual Love) · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

இருநோக்கு இவளுண்கண் உள்ளது ஒருநோக்கு நோய்நோக்கொன் றந்நோய் மருந்து.
Irunokku ivalunkan ulladhu orunokku noinokkon randhnoi marundhu.

Meaning

Her dark-lined eyes have two kinds of look. One look gives me the ache of love, and the other is the cure for that ache.

Elementary

Think of a new classmate who is very shy. When she looks at you, you feel all fluttery and nervous inside. But when she looks again and gives a small friendly smile, the nervous feeling goes away. The same eyes made the worry and then made it better.

University

Chapter 110 opens the second stage of the kalaviyal (the section on secret, premarital love): after the first sight of ch. 109 (Takaiyananguruttal), the hero now reads the heroine’s kurippu (குறிப்பு, ‘sign, inward intention’) for proof that his love is returned. The chapter shares its title with ch. 71 of Porutpal, where kurippu arithal means reading a king’s mind, so the same art of reading the face is moved from the court to love. Unkan (உண்கண்) means eyes that have ‘eaten’ collyrium (mai), that is, kohl-darkened eyes, and every gloss on both source sites (Mu. Varadarasanar, Puliyur Kesikan, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar) reads the two looks as a nōy nōkku (நோய் நோக்கு, the look that causes love-sickness) and a remedy-look; in the line of Parimelazhagar the second look is her answering sign of consent, which ends his uncertainty. Pope renders: ‘A double witchery have glances of her liquid eye; / One glance is glance that brings me pain; the other heals again.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“A remedy is being applied and it will soon be better. It is plain, sir, that you and she love each other.”
— Kalidasa, Shakuntala, Act III (tr. Arthur W. Ryder, 1912) [3]
When King Dushyanta asks whether Shakuntala’s illness is dangerous, her friend Priyamvada smiles and tells him the cure is already at hand, because the sickness is love for him.
As in the Kural, the one who caused the love-sickness is also its medicine.
Christianity
“Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me.”
— Song of Solomon 6:5 (KJV) [4]
The lover in the Song of Songs begs the beloved to look away because her gaze overwhelms him.
The beloved’s eyes have the same power to wound the lover that the Kural’s first look has.
Islam
“He has impassioned her with love.”
— Qur’an 12:30 (Sahih International) [5]
The women of the city say that the sight of Joseph has filled the wife of al-Aziz with a love she cannot master.
The Qur’an too knows that the mere sight of a person can strike the heart with love like an illness, though it judges this love as error.
Judaism
“Stay ye me with dainties, refresh me with apples; For I am love-sick.”
— Song of Songs 2:5 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The maiden in the Song of Songs says she is sick with love and asks to be revived.
Love as a sickness that longs for its own remedy is the image at the heart of the Kural.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that affection itself breeds sorrow and fear, and that freedom from it ends both.
Buddhism agrees that love brings an ache, but it finds the cure in letting go of attachment rather than in the beloved’s second look.
Jainism
“The mind of those who always live in unfrequented lodgings, who eat low food, and who subdue their senses, will not be attacked by the foe, Love, who is vanquished as disease is by medicine.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.12 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira’s teaching calls love an enemy that can be beaten the way medicine beats a disease, by self-restraint.
Jainism also speaks of love as a sickness with a medicine, but its medicine is restraint of the senses, not the beloved’s answering look.
Sikhism
“Suffering is the medicine, and pleasure the disease, because where there is pleasure, there is no desire for God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Asa, Asa di Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak turns the usual order around: the pain that makes the soul long for God is its cure, while comfort that makes it forget Him is the real sickness.
The Kural's beloved has one look that wounds and another that heals; Guru Nanak likewise sees the ache of longing and its cure as bound together, with the pain itself leading to the remedy.
Greek Mythology
“From whose eyes as they glanced flowed love that unnerves the limbs: and beautiful is their glance beneath their brows.”
— Hesiod, Theogony 910-911 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod says that love pours from the glances of the Graces and weakens whoever receives it.
Like the Kural, Hesiod places the source of love’s power in a glance of the eyes.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus Socrates describes the lover’s soul as aching and stinging when apart from the beloved, and finding relief from its pangs as soon as it looks on the beloved’s beauty again.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251c-e (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato the sight of the beloved both causes the soul’s agony of longing and soothes it.
This is the Kural’s double look: the same beauty gives the pain and the cure.

Real Life Example

Ernest Hemingway and the nurse Agnes von Kurowsky
1918–1919 · Milan, Italy, and Oak Park, Illinois, United States
After the young Ernest Hemingway was wounded on the Italian front in July 1918, he was treated at the American Red Cross hospital in Milan, where he fell in love with the nurse Agnes von Kurowsky. They exchanged daily notes and became engaged. In March 1919, after he had returned home to Oak Park, she ended the engagement by letter, and the rejection hurt him deeply.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The romance stayed with Hemingway and fed into his later fiction, including A Very Short Story and A Farewell to Arms.
The kural says one look of the beloved brings the ache of love and another is its medicine; for Hemingway the same woman was the nurse who cared for him and the source of his heartache.
