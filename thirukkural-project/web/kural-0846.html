Kural 846

Ignorance of Fools · Pullarivaanmai · புல்லறிவாண்மை

அற்றம் மறைத்தலோ புல்லறிவு தம்வயின் குற்றம் மறையா வழி.
Atram maraiththalo pullarivu thamvayin kutram maraiyaa vazhi.

Meaning

If you do not get rid of your faults, then carefully covering your body with clothes is just foolishness.

Elementary

Imagine someone who irons a perfect shirt every morning but is mean to everyone all day. Looking neat on the outside doesn’t hide the meanness. The poet says hiding your body but not fixing your faults is silly.

University

Atram (அற்றம்) here means the parts of the body to be covered, nakedness; maraiththal is covering it. Valluvar sets this against kutram (குற்றம், fault, moral defect) that remains unhidden (maraiyaa), arguing that modesty about the body while one’s faults stay on display is pullarivu, the very ‘petty understanding’ the chapter is named for. Mu. Varadarasanar, Pappaiah and Kalaignar all take ‘covering faults’ to mean removing them (neekkuthal), not concealing them, and Puliyur Kesigan adds the image of the hypocrite fully clothed and walking about ‘as though good’. Sivayogi Sivakumar reads atram differently, as ‘ruin’ or ‘loss’, giving a minority interpretation that concealing one’s downfall is the petty act. Text identical in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One who restrains the senses of action but whose mind dwells on sense objects certainly deludes himself and is called a pretender.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.6 (Prabhupada, As It Is) [3]
Outward restraint without inner change is called self-deception.
Like clothing the body while keeping one’s faults, it is surface without substance.
Christianity
“Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men’s bones, and of all uncleanness.”
— Matthew 23:27 (KJV) [4]
Jesus condemns those who keep the outside polished while the inside stays corrupt.
The kural’s well-dressed fool with uncorrected faults is a whited sepulchre.
Islam
“O children of Adam, We have bestowed upon you clothing to conceal your private parts and as adornment. But the clothing of righteousness - that is best.”
— Qur’an 7:26 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an names bodily clothing and then points to righteousness as the better covering.
Almost Valluvar’s exact contrast: covering nakedness matters less than covering oneself with virtue.
Judaism
After eating from the forbidden tree, Adam and Eve sew fig leaves to cover their nakedness and hide among the trees, then each shifts the blame for the wrongdoing to another.
— Genesis 3:7–13 (Tanakh) [6]
Their first instinct is to cover the body and hide, not to admit and set right the fault.
They hide nakedness while leaving the real fault uncorrected, the folly the kural names.
Buddhism
“What is the use of your matted hair, O witless man? What of your garment of antelope’s hide? Within you is the tangle (of passion); only outwardly do you cleanse yourself.”
— Dhammapada 394 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha mocks the ascetic whose outward dress is perfect while his mind stays tangled.
Same folly: tending the outer covering and leaving the inner fault.
Jainism
Kundakunda's Liṅga-pāhuḍa teaches that what makes a true mendicant is the inner disposition (bhāva-liṅga), and that the outward form (dravya-liṅga), even the Digambara monk's total nudity, does not make anyone a true monk.
— Kundakunda, Liṅga-pāhuḍa (with Bhāva-pāhuḍa), as summarised by Jainpedia, 'Kundakunda' [8]
For the Digambara master, how the body is presented, clothed or naked, is worthless unless the inner faults of passion are removed.
Valluvar calls it folly to cover the body carefully while leaving one's faults exposed, and Kundakunda, from the other side, says that even giving up all clothing achieves nothing while those inner faults remain.
Sikhism
“What should I account as the rewards of this human life, if one does not feel love and devotion to the Lord? Wearing clothes and eating food is useless, if the mind is filled with the love of duality.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1411, Saloks Vaaran Te Vadheek (salok 11), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says that the outward routines of life, even dressing and eating, are worthless while the mind remains full of duality.
The Kural calls it foolish to cover the body carefully while leaving one's faults uncured; Guru Nanak likewise says wearing clothes is useless when the mind is not set right.
Greek Mythology
In Plato’s Republic, Glaucon tells of the shepherd Gyges, whose ring makes him invisible and who then commits injustice freely, to argue that people value seeming just more than being just.
— Plato, Republic II (359d–360b) [10]
The myth separates outward reputation from inner character.
The kural rejects caring for appearances while the fault underneath is untouched.
Philosophy
At the close of the Phaedrus, Socrates prays to Pan to be made beautiful in the inner man and that his outward and inward selves be at one.
— Plato, Phaedrus 279b–c [11]
Socrates asks for inner beauty first and outer agreement with it.
Valluvar likewise demands that the inner person be set right, not just the outer covering.

Real Life Example

Richard Whitney, former head of the Stock Exchange, is exposed as an embezzler
March-April 1938 · New York, United States
Richard Whitney, president of the New York Stock Exchange from 1930 to 1935 and a model of Wall Street respectability, secretly took money entrusted to him, including funds from the Exchange's Gratuity Fund for members' families, the New York Yacht Club, of which he was treasurer, and bonds from his father-in-law's estate. In March 1938 the Exchange discovered that his firm was insolvent and Manhattan District Attorney Thomas E. Dewey charged him with embezzlement. Whitney pleaded guilty and was sentenced to five to ten years in Sing Sing prison.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The fall of the man who had stood for the Exchange's integrity captivated a nation still struggling through the Great Depression.
The kural calls it folly to cover the body carefully while leaving one's faults uncorrected, and Whitney's immaculate public respectability concealed wrongs that he never put right.
