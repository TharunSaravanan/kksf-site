Kural 1262

Mutual Desire · Avarvayin Vidhumpal · அவர்வயின் விதும்பல்

இலங்கிழாய் இன்று மறப்பின்என் தோள்மேல் கலங்கழியும் காரிகை நீத்து.
Ilangizhaai indru marappin-en thoalmael kalangazhiyum kaarikai neeththu.

Meaning

My friend in shining jewels, if I forgot him even now, my arms would lose their beauty and my bangles would slip off.

Elementary

A girl tells her best friend: “Even though missing him hurts, I won’t stop thinking about him. If I tried to forget him, I would only become sadder and thinner, so thin that my bracelets would fall off.” Remembering him, even when it is hard, is what keeps her going.

University

The heroine addresses her friend as ilaṅkiḻāy, ‘you of shining ornaments’ (Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar take the addressee as the tōḻi; Solomon Pappaiah’s gloss ஒளிரும் நகை அணிந்தவனே uses a masculine ending, apparently a slip). Kalam aḻiyum (‘the ornaments come off’) is the stock Tamil sign of a wasting body: the arms (tōḷ) grow thin and the bangles slide off, and kārikai (beauty, grace) departs. The logic is a reversal: one might expect forgetting to ease her pain, but she says that even to try to forget him today would ruin her further, so she chooses to keep remembering. The Drew/Lazarus explanation printed on ytamizh.com reads ‘even in the other life’, reflecting a reading of iṉṟu that some commentators extend; the main modern glosses take iṉṟu simply as ‘today, while I suffer’. The Tamil text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ashoka grove, Hanuman finds Sita pale and thin, wearing a single braid and no ornaments, her thoughts fixed on Rama alone.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki pictures the separated Sita as a wife whose body is wasting while her mind never leaves her husband.
Sita and the Kural’s heroine both lose their outward beauty in separation yet refuse to let the beloved slip from memory.
Christianity
“Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee.”
— Isaiah 49:15 (KJV) [4]
God answers the fear of being forgotten with a promise stronger than a mother’s memory.
The heroine makes the same vow toward her husband: whatever it costs her, she will not forget him.
Islam
Rumi opens the Masnavi with the reed flute, cut from its reed-bed, whose every note is a lament of separation and a longing to return to the source it remembers.
— Jalal al-Din Rumi, Masnavi I.1-18, ‘Song of the Reed’ (tr. R.A. Nicholson; paraphrase) [5]
For Rumi the soul’s song comes from remembering where it belongs; the remembering is both its pain and its life.
Like the reed, the heroine keeps the absent one alive in memory, and that remembering is what sustains her.
Judaism
“If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning. Let my tongue cleave to the roof of my mouth, if I remember thee not; if I set not Jerusalem above my chiefest joy.”
— Psalm 137:5-6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The exiles in Babylon bind their own bodies to their memory of Zion.
The Kural uses the very same structure: if I forget, let my body suffer for it, with arms that lose their beauty and bangles that fall.
Buddhism
Nakula’s father and Nakula’s mother tell the Buddha that they have never been unfaithful even in thought and wish to see each other in this life and in lives to come; the Buddha says that a couple matched in faith, virtue, generosity and wisdom will meet again.
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu; paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha treats a devoted couple’s wish to remain together as something that shared virtue can sustain.
The heroine’s refusal to forget her husband is the same steadfast devotion that the Buddha praises in this model couple.
Jainism
“Firm and decided she cut off her tresses which were black like bees and dressed with a brush and comb.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.30 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Abandoned by Arishtanemi, Rajimati gives up her beauty deliberately and becomes a nun.
Both women lose their adornment in separation, but Rajimati lays it down by choice to follow the path, while the Kural’s heroine fears losing it and clings to memory instead.
Sikhism
“How could I forget Him from my mind, even for an instant? He is the Most Worthy; He is my very life!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 80, Sri Raag, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that forgetting the Lord is unthinkable, because the Lord is the very life of the devotee.
The wife says that if she forgot her husband even now, her arms would lose their beauty; Guru Arjan likewise cannot imagine forgetting the Beloved who is his life.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s letter of Laodamia to Protesilaus at Troy, she says she has no care to comb her hair or wear gold-woven clothes while her husband is at war, and that she lives on thoughts of him.
— Ovid, Heroides 13, Laodamia to Protesilaus (paraphrase) [10]
The mythic wife lets her beauty go untended because her whole mind is with the absent husband.
Laodamia and the Kural’s heroine both let outward grace fall away while holding their husband constantly in mind.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that distance does not break friendship outright but interrupts its activity, and that a long absence seems to make people forget it, citing the saying that lack of converse has broken many a friendship.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 8.5 (tr. W.D. Ross; paraphrase) [11]
Aristotle sees forgetting as the natural danger of long absence.
The heroine sees the same danger and refuses it: she will not let absence turn into forgetting.

Real Life Example

Edward I raises the Eleanor Crosses
1290s · Lincoln to Westminster, England
Eleanor of Castile, wife of King Edward I of England, died at Harby in Nottinghamshire on 28 November 1290. Her funeral procession travelled from Lincoln to Westminster in December, and Edward had a memorial cross built at each place where her body rested overnight, twelve in all. Three of the original crosses survive, at Geddington, Hardingstone and Waltham Cross.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rather than let his grief fade, the king set his love for his wife in stone along the road from Lincoln to London.
The kural's heroine refuses to forget her husband even though remembering costs her; Edward chose lasting remembrance of Eleanor over forgetting.
