Kural 825

Unreal Friendship · Koodaa Natpu · கூடா நட்பு

மனத்தின் அமையா தவரை எனைத்தொன்றும் சொல்லினால் தேறற்பாற்று அன்று.
Manaththin amaiyaa thavarai enaiththondrum sollinaal ther̲ar̲paat̲ru andru.

Meaning

Never trust, in anything at all, the words of people who are not with you in their hearts.

Elementary

If someone keeps promising to share their toys but never does, you learn to stop believing the promise. Words are easy to say. When someone does not really care about you, what they say is not something to lean on.

University

Manaththin amaiyaathavar (மனத்தின் அமையாதவர்) are ‘those not settled with us in mind’; enaiththondrum (எனைத்தொன்றும்) means ‘in anything at all’. Theraṟpaaṯru anru (தேறற்பாற்று அன்று) means ‘it is not fit to be trusted or relied upon’, using theṟal (தேறல், clear conviction), the same root as the chapter Therindhu Thelidhal (51) on testing before trusting. Varadarasanar reads it as not trusting their words in any undertaking, and Kalaignar as not being able to reach any clear decision on such people’s words. The text is identical in ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who trusts the words, true or false, of a foe, meets with destruction like a seeker of honey, in a pit covered with dry grass.”
— Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, Section 139 (Ganguli translation) [3]
In Bhishma’s tale of the bird Pujani and King Brahmadatta, the bird refuses to trust the king’s soothing words after enmity has arisen.
‘The words, true or false, of a foe’ are not to be relied on: the Kural’s exact rule.
Christianity
“My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth.”
— 1 John 3:18 (KJV) [4]
John teaches that real love is shown in action and truth, not in speech alone.
Both texts refuse to treat words as proof of a loving heart.
Islam
“And when you see them, their forms please you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech. [They are] as if they were pieces of wood propped up - they think that every shout is against them. They are the enemy, so beware of them.”
— Qur’an 63:4 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that the hypocrites’ appearance and speech are attractive but hollow, and that they are to be guarded against.
Attractive speech from those not one with you in heart is not to be trusted, as the Kural says.
Judaism
“An enemy dissembles with his speech, / Inwardly he harbors deceit. / Though he be fair-spoken do not trust him, / For seven abominations are in his mind.”
— Proverbs 26:24-25 (Revised JPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs warns that fair speech from someone hostile within must not be trusted.
‘Though he be fair-spoken do not trust him’ is virtually the Kural restated.
Buddhism
“In four ways, young householder, should one who renders lip-service be understood as a foe in the guise of a friend: (i) he makes friendly profession as regards the past, (ii) he makes friendly profession as regards the future, (iii) he tries to gain one's favor by empty words, (iv) when opportunity for service has arisen, he expresses his inability.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [7]
The Buddha describes the friend whose friendship lives only in words.
The lip-service friend’s ‘empty words’ are exactly what the Kural says not to rely on.
Jainism
“Our intimacy with those to whom we are not bound by the chain of friendship, O king of the hills, the sides of which are covered with groves of tall sandal-trees! though it seem day by day to increase, will be dissolved as instantaneously as fire catches straw.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 24 ('Improper Friendship'), st. 4 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Nālaṭiyār warns that closeness with people who are not truly bound to us can look as if it is growing, yet it vanishes in an instant.
Valluvar says nothing at all should be trusted from those not one with us in heart; the Nālaṭiyār agrees that a bond without a real 'chain of friendship' cannot be relied on, however warm it seems.
Sikhism
“Whatever is in the mind, comes forth; spoken words by themselves are just wind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 474, Raag Aasaa, Asa Ki Vaar (salok 2 before pauri 22), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok in Asa Ki Vaar, Guru Angad teaches that what counts is what is in the mind, while words cut off from it are only air.
The Kural says the words of those not one with you in heart cannot be relied on; Guru Angad likewise calls words empty when the heart is not behind them.
Greek Mythology
“Him do I hate even as the gates of hell who says one thing while he hides another in his heart”
— Homer, Iliad, Book 9 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Achilles rejects Agamemnon’s gift-laden embassy, refusing to be won over by words that do not match the heart behind them.
Achilles, like Valluvar, will not rely on the words of one whose heart is elsewhere.
Philosophy
“Friendship, in which, unless you both see and show an open breast, to use a common expression, you can neither trust nor be certain of anything--no, not even of mutual affection, since you cannot be sure of its sincerity.”
— Cicero, De Amicitia 26 (trans. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Cicero says that without openness of heart, nothing a friend says can be relied on.
Where the heart is not open and one with ours, the Kural likewise says words cannot be trusted.

Real Life Example

Shivaji escapes from Aurangzeb's detention at Agra
1666 · Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India
Under the Treaty of Purandar, made after his defeat by the Mughal general Jai Singh, the Maratha leader Shivaji agreed to visit the court of Emperor Aurangzeb, and he arrived at Agra in May 1666 with Jai Singh's promise that he would be allowed to return home safely. The audience went badly, and Shivaji was kept under guard rather than allowed to leave. Later that year he escaped with his son Sambhaji, reached his own territory again and resumed his struggle against the Mughals.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Shivaji learned that the assurances given in a treaty with an opponent counted for little once he was in that opponent's power.
The kural says the words of those who are not one with you in heart should not be relied on in any matter, and Shivaji's trust in the promises of safe conduct ended in detention at Agra.
