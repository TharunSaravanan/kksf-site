Kural 135

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

அழுக்கா றுடையான்கண் ஆக்கம்போன்று இல்லை ஒழுக்க மிலான்கண் உயர்வு.
Azhukkaaru udaiyaan-kan aakkampoandru illai Ozhukkam ilaan-kan uyarvu.

Meaning

Just as riches never stay with jealous people, greatness never comes to people without good behaviour.

Elementary

A jealous kid spends so much time being upset about what others have that he never enjoys his own things. In the same way, someone who behaves badly never really rises high, no matter what.

University

The couplet works by analogy (ponru, ‘just as’). Azhukkaaru, envy, is so incompatible with aakkam (increase, prosperity) that its absence is proverbial, and uyarvu (elevation, rank) is just as surely absent from a person without ozhukkam. Envy gets a whole later chapter (17). Pappaiah reads uyarvu as ‘high lineage’, keeping the chapter's theme of birth, while Varadarasanar reads it as elevation in life generally. The pairing of envy with misconduct is deliberate: Tamil ethics treats envy as the model inner corruption that eats away at outward fortune, which a later couplet (Kural 168) develops. The claim is about incompatibility more than reward: certain dispositions cannot coexist with certain goods.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Seeing the splendour of the Pandavas' palace and the wealth brought to Yudhishthira's Rajasuya sacrifice, Duryodhana is consumed by envy. That envy drives him to the rigged dice game, which ends in the destruction of his whole line.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Dyuta Parva), tr. K. M. Ganguli [3]
In the Mahabharata, envy of another's prosperity sets off Duryodhana's moral collapse and his eventual ruin.
Duryodhana has birth and wealth, yet envy and misconduct keep true eminence from him, as the Kural predicts.
Christianity
“For where envying and strife is, there is confusion and every evil work.”
— James 3:16 (KJV) [4]
James warns that envy breeds disorder and every kind of wrongdoing.
Both treat envy as corrosive and incompatible with anything good growing.
Islam
“Avoid envy, for envy devours good deeds just as fire devours fuel or (he said) ‘grass.’”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 4903 [5]
The Prophet compares envy to fire that burns up whatever good a person has stored.
Like the Kural, it makes envy the thing that consumes a person's gains.
Judaism
“envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:21 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar Ha-kappar names envy first among the traits that destroy a person.
Both see envy as ruining one's place in the world.
Buddhism
“Not by mere eloquence nor by beauty of form does a man become accomplished, if he is jealous, selfish and deceitful.”
— Dhammapada 262 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says jealousy and deceit cancel out every outward advantage.
This echoes the Kural's point that the envious and ill-conducted cannot reach true eminence.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Uttarādhyayana teaches that prosperity won through wrong conduct does not last, because the passions that produced it also undo it.
The Kural says that, as prosperity never settles on the envious, so eminence never comes to one without conduct; this verse makes the related point that gains without right conduct do not endure.
Sikhism
“One whose heart is filled with jealousy of others, never comes to any good.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 308, Raag Gauri, Gauri ki Vaar, Salok Mahala 4, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das warns that a person who harbours envy of others will never prosper.
The Kural builds on the proverb that envy and prosperity never meet; Gurbani states that same incompatibility outright.
Greek Mythology
Minerva sends Invidia (Envy) to poison Aglauros, who is consumed by jealousy of her sister's good fortune with Mercury. When she tries to block the god's way, she is turned to stone.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.708–832 [10]
Envy eats Aglauros from the inside and leaves her with nothing, frozen and ruined.
This vividly shows the Kural's premise that envy and prosperity cannot coexist.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines envy as a form of hatred that makes a person pained at another's good fortune and glad at their misfortune. For him, every such sad passion lessens a person's power of acting.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions 23 [11]
Envy is not only unpleasant but actually weakens the one who feels it.
This helps explain why the Kural says the envious cannot prosper.

Real Life Example

Boris Onishchenko is disqualified for a rigged epee at the Montreal Olympics
1976 · Montreal, Canada
During the fencing event of the modern pentathlon at the 1976 Olympic Games, the epee of the Soviet pentathlete Boris Onishchenko registered hits on opponents it did not appear to have touched. The British team asked for the weapon to be inspected, and it was found to have been modified so that it could register a hit even when none had been made. Onishchenko was disqualified, the Soviet team was eliminated, and Great Britain went on to win the team gold medal.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The case remains one of the most notorious acts of cheating in Olympic history, and it showed that a rise won by deceit can collapse in an instant once the means are exposed.
The kural holds that true eminence never comes to a person without right conduct, just as prosperity never settles on the envious; Onishchenko's rigged weapon brought him not honour but lasting disgrace.
