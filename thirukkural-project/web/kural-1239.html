Kural 1239

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

முயக்கிடைத் தண்வளி போழப் பசப்புற்ற பேதை பெருமழைக் கண்.
Muyakkidaith thanvali poazhap pasapputra paedhai perumazhaik kan.

Meaning

When even a little breeze slipped in between us as we hugged, the big, cool eyes of my innocent wife turned pale.

Elementary

Think of two friends sitting so close on a cold night that not even the wind can get between them. If a tiny gust slips in, one of them shivers. The husband here remembers that even a little breeze between them made his wife look sad, so he knows a long trip away must be very hard for her.

University

The husband’s recollection continues and intensifies 1238: now not his arms but a cool breeze (taṇ vaḷi) ‘split’ (pōḻa, ‘to cleave, pierce’) their embrace (muyakku iṭai, ‘in the midst of the embrace’), and at that hair’s-breadth separation her eyes, not her forehead, were touched with pacappu. Perumaḻaik kaṇ, ‘large rain-eyes’, is glossed by Mu. Varadarasanar as ‘large eyes like rain’ (cool and moist) and by Kalaignar as ‘wide, long eyes’; the image may suggest both their coolness and their readiness for tears. Solomon Pappaiah ends his gloss with the husband’s worried question, ‘how must they be now?’, which captures the a fortiori logic of 1238-1240. Pope: ‘As we embraced a breath of wind found entrance there; / The maid’s large liquid eyes were dimmed with care.’ Ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, when Krishna suddenly vanishes from the circle of dancing gopis at night, they are overwhelmed at once, searching for him from tree to tree and asking the creepers and deer where he has gone.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.30 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata shows a moment’s disappearance of the beloved producing instant, overwhelming distress.
The Kural’s wife, too, is stricken by the smallest break in closeness, a breeze slipping between them.
Christianity
“Again, if two lie together, then they have heat: but how can one be warm alone?”
— Ecclesiastes 4:11 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher observes plainly that two together keep each other warm, and one alone cannot.
The Kural’s cold breeze slipping between the lovers is exactly the chill the verse describes.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an presents spouses as a source of tranquillity for each other, bound by affection and mercy.
The Kural shows how fragile that tranquillity feels to a loving wife, broken even by a breath of air between them.
Judaism
“When our love was strong, we could have slept on a bed that was the width of a sword. Now that our love is not strong, a bed of sixty cubits is not sufficient for us.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 7a (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
A Talmudic proverb measures love by how little space a couple needs between them.
In the Kural the lovers’ closeness is so complete that even a breeze in the gap is felt as a wound.
Buddhism
In the Canda-Kinnara Jataka, a pair of kinnaras are separated for a single night when a sudden flood fills the stream between them; they spend the night calling to each other, and for the rest of their long lives they grieve over that one lost night.
— Canda-Kinnara Jataka (Jataka 485) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Jataka makes a single night’s separation the cause of lifelong lament between devoted mates.
The Kural’s wife reacts the same way to the slightest gap, a breeze in the embrace.
Jainism
In the Jain Ramayana, Pavananjaya, encamped on a campaign, sees a female chakravaka bird crying all night for her mate, from whom she is parted till dawn; realising that his neglected wife Anjana must suffer far more, he secretly flies back to her that very night.
— Hemachandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 7, story of Anjana and Pavananjaya (paraphrase; cf. tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
The Jain narrative makes a bird’s single night of separation open a husband’s eyes to his wife’s pain.
Like Pavananjaya, the Kural’s husband reasons from a small separation, a breeze in an embrace, to how much his wife must suffer in a long absence.
Sikhism
“When I could not be with You for just one moment, the Dark Age of Kali Yuga dawned for me.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 96, Raag Maajh, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Shabad Hazare, Guru Arjan says that even a moment away from the beloved Guru felt like the darkest age of the world.
In the Kural even a cool breeze slipping between the embracing couple was enough to make the wife's eyes turn pale; Guru Arjan likewise finds a single moment's separation unbearable.
Greek Mythology
When Ceyx resolves to sail to consult an oracle, his wife Alcyone turns pale, weeps and begs him not to go, or at least to take her with him, since even a short parting by sea terrifies her.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.410-443 (Ceyx and Alcyone) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s devoted wife cannot bear the thought of even a brief separation from her husband.
The Kural’s wife pales at a breath of wind between them; Alcyone pales at the mere mention of a voyage.
Philosophy
Lucretius says lovers clasp and press each other close, mouth to mouth, yet can take nothing from each other, since body cannot pass into body, and so even the tightest embrace leaves a gap that desire cannot close.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1105-1111 (paraphrase; cf. tr. W.H.D. Rouse) [11]
The Epicurean poet sees in lovers’ embraces a longing for a total union that bodies cannot achieve.
The Kural’s breeze slipping into the embrace is the gap Lucretius points to, felt by the wife as a wound.

Real Life Example

Captain Scott's last letter to his wife Kathleen
1912 · Antarctica
During the fatal return journey from the South Pole in 1912, Captain Robert Falcon Scott wrote last letters from his tent, including one to his wife Kathleen addressed To my widow. He wrote of the cold, 70 degrees below zero, and asked her to make their son Peter, then about three, interested in natural history if she could. The letter was given to the Scott Polar Research Institute at Cambridge and put on public display in 2007.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Written as he faced death, the letter shows a husband's last thoughts turning to his wife and child far away.
In this kural the husband, far from home, remembers his wife's delicacy and worries how she will bear the separation, and Scott's last letter turns from his own plight to her and their son's future.
