Kural 1289

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

மலரினும் மெல்லிது காமம் சிலர்அதன் செவ்வி தலைப்படு வார்.
Malarinum mellidhu kaamam silaradhan sevvi thalaippadu vaar.

Meaning

Love is softer than a flower. Only a few people know how to enjoy it at its very best.

Elementary

A flower petal is so soft that if you squeeze it, it gets crushed. The poet says love is even softer than that, so you must be gentle and patient with it. Not many people learn how to care for it properly.

University

Malariṉum melliti kāmam: ‘love (kāmam) is softer/more delicate than a flower’. Puliyur Kesikan specifies the flower as aṉiccam, the proverbially delicate flower that withers even at a breath (cf. kural 90 and 1111). Cevvi means ‘ripeness, right moment, perfection’ and talaippaṭuvār ‘those who attain/grasp’; so few attain love’s cevvi. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses ‘few know this truth and obtain its good fruit’; Solomon Pappaiah stresses having the right place, time and circumstances; Kalaignar ‘few enjoy it with the same delicacy’. The couplet is often quoted independently as the Kural’s most celebrated statement on love, and here it explains why both partners must set aside sulking: a love so delicate is easily bruised. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net (ytamizh joins சிலர்அதன்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kama, the god of love, carries a bow of sugarcane strung with bees and five arrows tipped with flowers; in the Kumarasambhava he aims his flower-arrow at Shiva to awaken love for Parvati.
— Kalidasa, Kumarasambhava, canto 3 (paraphrase) [3]
Indian tradition imagines love’s weapons as flowers, signalling its delicacy and sweetness.
The Kural calls love softer than a flower; the image of Kama’s flower-arrows makes the same association.
Christianity
“As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters.”
— Song of Solomon 2:2 (KJV) [4]
The lover in the Song compares his beloved to a lily, delicate and rare among thorns.
The Kural, too, joins love with the delicacy of a flower and its rarity among the many.
Islam
The Prophet taught that God is gentle and loves gentleness, and grants for gentleness what He does not grant for harshness.
— Sahih Muslim 2593 (paraphrase) [5]
The hadith praises gentleness (rifq) as a quality God loves and rewards.
The Kural says love must be handled with more gentleness than a flower; the hadith sets gentleness as a divine ideal.
Judaism
“I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem: Why should ye awaken, or stir up love, until it please?”
— Song of Songs 8:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The Song’s refrain warns against rousing love before its proper time.
The Kural’s cevvi, love’s right moment, is exactly what the Song guards: love is delicate and must not be forced.
Buddhism
“Upon a heap of rubbish in the road-side ditch blooms a lotus, fragrant and pleasing.”
— Dhammapada 58 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada uses the rare lotus blooming in a ditch as an image of the few who rise to wisdom.
The Kural, too, joins a delicate flower with the rarity of the few who attain its essence, though the Buddhist lotus stands for wisdom, not love.
Jainism
The Tattvartha Sutra lists among the ten supreme virtues mardava, literally ‘softness’, the humility and gentleness that dissolves pride.
— Tattvartha Sutra 9.6 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Nathmal Tatia) [8]
Jain ethics names softness of heart as one of the ten dharmas practised to stop the influx of karma.
The Kural calls love softer than a flower; Jainism prizes softness of heart, though as a renunciant virtue rather than a quality of love.
Sikhism
“The path they take is sharper than a two-edged sword, and finer than a hair.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 918, Raag Raamkalee, Anand Sahib (pauri 14), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Anand Sahib Guru Amar Das says the way of the devotees is so fine and demanding that few can walk it.
The Kural says love is more delicate than a flower and few grasp its finest moment; Guru Amar Das likewise says the path of love for God is finer than a hair and walked by few.
Greek Mythology
Sappho compares a maiden to a sweet apple reddening on the topmost bough, which the apple-pickers missed, or rather could not reach.
— Sappho, fragment 105a (paraphrase) [10]
Sappho’s image presents beauty and love as rare, ripe and out of reach for most.
The Kural, too, speaks of love’s ripeness (cevvi) that only a few manage to attain.
Philosophy
Agathon, in Plato’s Symposium, praises Love as the most tender of the gods, who walks not on the earth but in the softest of places, the hearts and souls of gods and men, and who dwells among flowers.
— Plato, Symposium 195c-196b (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato’s Agathon describes Love as supremely soft and delicate, living only where there is gentleness.
This is close to the Kural’s claim that love is softer than a flower.

Real Life Example

Wagner wakes Cosima with the Siegfried Idyll
25 December 1870 · Tribschen, near Lucerne, Switzerland
Richard Wagner secretly composed a gentle piece for small orchestra as a birthday present for his new wife, Cosima. On Christmas morning 1870, when they were to celebrate her birthday, a small group of musicians directed by Wagner played it on the stairs outside her bedroom to wake her. The piece, first named after their home at Tribschen, became known as the Siegfried Idyll, after their young son.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Wagner, known for vast and stormy operas, chose an intimate, quiet gesture for the right moment.
The kural says love is more delicate than a flower and few grasp its perfect moment; the Siegfried Idyll is a rare documented case of a lover choosing exactly the right, tender moment to express love.
