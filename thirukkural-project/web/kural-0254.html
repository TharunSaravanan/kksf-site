Kural 254

Flesh Renunciation · Pulaan Maruthal · புலான் மறுத்தல்

அருளல்ல தியாதெனிற் கொல்லாமை கோறல் பொருளல்ல தவ்வூன் தினல்.
Arulalla thiyaathenir kollaamai koral, Porulalla thavvoon thinal.

Meaning

If you ask what kindness is and what it is not: not killing is kindness, and killing is not. Eating the flesh of what was killed is wrong.

Elementary

What is kindness? Not hurting or killing. What is unkindness? Killing. So eating the meat that came from killing isn’t right either — because it depends on the unkind act.

University

This couplet has a compressed catechism structure that Parimelazhagar unpacks as: அருள் யாது? கொல்லாமை; அருளல்லது யாது? கோறல் — “What is grace? Non-killing. What is not grace? Killing.” The final clause, பொருளல்லது அவ்வூன் தினல் (“eating that flesh is not porul”), extends the moral judgement from the act of killing to the act of eating, closing the loophole that the eater is innocent because someone else killed. பொருள் here is best read as “righteousness, what is proper” (Varadarasan: அறம் அல்லாதது). The argument prefigures chapter 33 (கொல்லாமை, Non-killing), making flesh-renunciation the dietary corollary of ahimsa.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Meat can never be obtained without injury to living creatures, and injury to sentient beings is detrimental to (the attainment of) heavenly bliss; let him therefore shun (the use of) meat.”
— Manusmriti 5.48 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu reasons that because meat always requires harming a living being, and harm blocks spiritual reward, one should avoid meat.
Same syllogism as the Kural: killing is contrary to virtue, meat requires killing, so eating meat is not right.
Christianity
“And God said, Behold, I have given you every herb bearing seed, which is upon the face of all the earth, and every tree, in the which is the fruit of a tree yielding seed; to you it shall be for meat. And to every beast of the earth… I have given every green herb for meat: and it was so.”
— Genesis 1:29-30 (KJV) [4]
In the original creation, God gives humans and animals plants — not each other — as food.
The Edenic ideal of a world without killing for food reflects the Kural’s identification of non-killing with goodness.
Islam
“And there is no creature on [or within] the earth or bird that flies with its wings except [that they are] communities like you.”
— Quran 6:38 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran declares that animals and birds form communities just like human ones.
Recognising animals as fellow communities grounds the Kural’s claim that killing them is the opposite of compassion.
Judaism
“Meat was not permitted to Adam, the first man, for consumption”
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 59b (William Davidson ed.) [6]
The Talmud reads Genesis 1:29-30 as meaning that the first human was vegetarian; meat was permitted only after the Flood.
Rabbinic tradition thus preserves the ideal that a world without killing for food was the original good — the Kural’s standard.
Buddhism
“I undertake the precept to refrain from taking life”
— Five Precepts (pāṇātipātā veramaṇī), as summarised in scholarship on Buddhist vegetarianism [7]
The first of the Five Precepts binds every Buddhist not to kill living beings.
Matches the Kural’s definition of compassion as non-killing; whether it extends to eating meat is debated within Buddhism.
Jainism
“A wise man should study them with all means of philosophical research. All beings hate pains; therefore one should not kill them. This is the quintessence of wisdom: not to kill anything.”
— Sutrakritanga I.11.9-10 (also at I.1.4.10) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga sums up the whole path in one rule: because every being hates pain, wisdom comes down to not killing anything.
Valluvar answers 'what is compassion?' with 'not killing'; the Sutrakritanga answers 'what is wisdom?' with the same reply.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, they oppress living beings and kill them, and call it proper. When the Lord calls for their account, what will their condition be?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1375, Salok Bhagat Kabir 199 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir rejects the claim that killing a creature by force becomes acceptable because it is labelled proper, and warns that God will call such killers to account.
The Kural flatly calls killing the opposite of grace and says eating what was killed is not right; Kabir likewise refuses to let any label turn killing into righteousness.
Greek Mythology
“For the son of Cronos has ordained this law for men, that fishes and beasts and winged fowls should devour one another, for right is not in them; but to mankind he gave right which proves far the best.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 276-280 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod says animals eat each other because they lack justice, while Zeus gave justice to humans.
Like the Kural, Hesiod separates the human moral order from the law of devouring — though he applies it to human conduct generally, not to diet.
Philosophy
“Will ye not cease from this ill-sounding slaughter? See ye not that ye are devouring one another in the thoughtlessness of your hearts?”
— Empedocles, fr. 136 DK (trans. J. Burnet) [11]
Empedocles, believing souls pass between humans and animals, calls animal slaughter and eating a form of cannibalism.
Like the Kural, he treats killing and eating as one continuous wrong born of a heedless heart.

Real Life Example

Emperor Wu of Liang requires Chinese Buddhist monks to give up all meat
c. 517-520 CE · Jiankang (now Nanjing), Liang dynasty, China
Emperor Wu of Liang, a devoted Buddhist patron, called assemblies of monks and pressed on them a rule that monastics must abstain from all meat, citing the Nirvana Sutra. Earlier monastic rules had allowed monks to eat so-called clean meat, from animals not killed specifically for them, and some monks objected that the old discipline did not forbid meat. The emperor's rule was enforced and became the lasting norm for Chinese Buddhist monks and nuns.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The decree closed the loophole that eating meat is innocent so long as someone else did the killing, and it shaped Chinese Buddhist practice for the following fifteen centuries.
The kural says not killing is compassion and adds that eating the flesh of what was killed is also not right, which is exactly the loophole Emperor Wu's rule closed.
