Kural 814

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

அமரகத்து ஆற்றறுக்கும் கல்லாமா அன்னார் தமரின் தனிமை தலை.
Amarakaththu aatrarukkum kallaamaa annaar thamarin thanimai thalai.

Meaning

It is better to be alone than to have friends who are like an untrained horse that throws its rider off in the middle of a battle.

Elementary

If you ride a bike with wobbly brakes, it might fail just when you’re going downhill. A friend who leaves you at the scariest moment is like that bike, and it’s safer to walk on your own.

University

The image is martial: amarakam (அமரகம்) is ‘the battlefield’, kallaa maa (கல்லா மா) ‘a horse that has not been trained’, and aatrarukkum (ஆற்றறுக்கும்) describes it cutting the rider off midway, whether by throwing him (Mu. Varadarasanar, Kalaignar) or bolting from the field (Puliyur Kesigan, who widens maa to ‘an untaught beast’). Thamar (தமர்) means ‘one’s own people, intimates’, and the verdict is thanimai thalai (தனிமை தலை): solitude is supreme. The couplet sharpens the chapter’s logic: a bad friend is not merely useless but dangerous at exactly the moment you depend on him. The texts agree; the sandhi-joined edition writes ‘அமரகத் தாற்றறுக்குங் கல்லாமா வன்னார் தமரிற் றனிமை தலை’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is better to be without a kingdom than to rule over a petty one; better to be without a friend than to befriend a rascal”
— Chanakya Niti 6.13 (Sri Chanakya Niti-sastra, English translation) [4]
Chanakya ranks having no friend above having a bad one, in a series of ‘better without’ comparisons.
This is the Kural’s thanimai thalai, solitude over unworthy company, stated almost word for word.
Christianity
“Confidence in an unfaithful man in time of trouble is like a broken tooth, and a foot out of joint.”
— Proverbs 25:19 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs compares trusting an unfaithful person in a crisis to leaning on a body part that gives way under load.
Like the untrained horse in battle, the unfaithful man fails exactly when you put your weight on him.
Islam
“But when the two armies sighted each other, he turned on his heels and said, ‘Indeed, I am disassociated from you.’”
— Qur’an 8:48 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse describes Satan encouraging the Quraysh before Badr and promising to protect them, then abandoning them as battle was joined.
A protector who vanishes when the armies meet is the Kural’s horse that throws its rider on the battlefield.
Judaism
“Nittai the Arbelite used to say: keep a distance from an evil neighbor, do not become attached to the wicked, and do not abandon faith in [divine] retribution.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:7 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
The Mishnah advises keeping one’s distance from bad neighbours and not attaching oneself to the wicked.
Both teach that distance, even isolation, is better than attachment to people who will fail or harm you.
Buddhism
“If for company you cannot find a wise and prudent friend who leads a good life, then, like a king who leaves behind a conquered kingdom, or like a lone elephant in the elephant forest, you should go your way alone.”
— Dhammapada 329 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada advises walking alone rather than settling for unworthy companions.
This is the Kural’s thanimai thalai in Buddhist form: solitude over bad company.
Jainism
“If he does not meet with a clever companion who surpasses or equals him in virtue, he should live by himself, abstaining from sins and not devoted to pleasures.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 32.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
The Jain canon tells the monk to choose a companion at least his equal in virtue, and to live alone if none can be found.
The Kural says it is better to be alone than to have companions who, like an untrained horse, throw you in the midst of battle, and the Uttaradhyayana likewise prefers solitude to an unworthy companion.
Sikhism
Before the battle of Bhangani (1686 or 1688), a body of Pathans who had taken service with Guru Gobind Singh on the recommendation of Pir Buddhu Shah were bought over by the hill raja Bhim Chand with promises of plunder, and most of the Udasis also deserted. Hearing of this, Pir Buddhu Shah came with his sons and several hundred followers to fight at the Guru's side, and two of his sons were killed.
— Wikipedia, 'Battle of Bhangani', citing Harjinder Singh Dilgeer and the Bachittar Natak (paraphrase) [10]
The episode sets companions who abandoned the Guru on the eve of battle against an ally who arrived when it mattered and paid for it with his sons' lives.
The Kural says it is better to be alone than to have companions like an untrained horse that throws its rider in mid-battle; the hired Pathans at Bhangani were exactly such companions, while Pir Buddhu Shah showed what a true ally is.
Greek Mythology
“Never travel with a friend who deserts you at the approach of danger.”
— Aesop, The Bear and the Two Travelers (tr. G. F. Townsend) [11]
In Aesop’s fable, one traveler climbs a tree and leaves his companion to face a bear alone; the survivor says the bear ‘whispered’ this advice to him.
The deserting friend is the untrained horse of the Kural, and the moral is the same: better to go alone.
Philosophy
Seneca urges Lucilius to withdraw into himself as far as he can and to associate only with people who will make him better, since bad company infects the character.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 7 [12]
For Seneca, keeping one’s own company is preferable to company that weakens or corrupts.
It shares the Kural’s preference for solitude over unreliable companions, though on moral rather than practical grounds.

Real Life Example

Mir Jafar's cavalry stands aside at the Battle of Plassey
23 June 1757 · Plassey (Palashi), West Bengal, India
Before the battle, Robert Clive of the East India Company and the Jagat Seth bankers secretly promised Mir Jafar, one of the commanders of Siraj-ud-Daulah, nawab of Bengal, that he would become nawab if Siraj were defeated. At Plassey the nawab's army greatly outnumbered Clive's, but Mir Jafar, commanding a large body of the nawab's cavalry, kept his troops out of the fighting. Clive won, and Mir Jafar was installed as nawab, ruling largely as a puppet of the British.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Plassey was decided less by fighting than by a commander who abandoned his own side in the middle of the battle, and it opened the way to British rule in Bengal.
The kural warns against companions who are like an untrained horse that throws its rider in the midst of battle, and Mir Jafar left his nawab exposed at the very moment of combat.
