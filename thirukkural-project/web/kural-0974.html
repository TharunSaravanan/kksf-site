Kural 974

Greatness · Perumai · பெருமை

ஒருமை மகளிரே போலப் பெருமையும் தன்னைத்தான் கொண்டொழுகின் உண்டு.
Orumai magalire polap perumaiyum thannaiththaan kondozhugin undu.

Meaning

A faithful wife stays true by guarding herself. In the same way, greatness lasts only for a person who guards and controls themselves.

Elementary

A promise only stays a promise if you keep it, even when nobody is watching. Valluvar says greatness is the same: it is not a prize someone hands you, it stays with you only while you keep guarding your own good behaviour.

University

The simile turns on orumai magalir (ஒருமை மகளிர், 'women of oneness'), which the commentators uniformly read as women of undivided chastity (கற்பு, karpu): Mu. Varadarasanar says ஒரு தன்மையான கற்புடைய மகளிர், and Puliyur Kesikan describes women whose minds do not waver and who guard themselves without lapsing. The point of comparison is thannaiththaan kondozhugin (தன்னைத்தான் கொண்டொழுகின், 'if one conducts oneself holding oneself'), that is, self-guarding: greatness, like chastity, cannot be imposed from outside and is lost by a single lapse. The gendered simile reflects the period; Sivayogi Sivakumar reads it more inwardly as being true to oneself. Pope's 'Like single-hearted women, greatness too, / Exists while to itself is true' keeps the ambiguity. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“One must deliver himself with the help of his mind, and not degrade himself. The mind is the friend of the conditioned soul, and his enemy as well.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that each person must raise himself and guard against bringing himself down.
Like the Kural, it makes a person's elevation depend on his own self-guarding.
Christianity
“Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life.”
— Proverbs 4:23 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs urges constant guarding of one's inner self as the source of everything one becomes.
The Kural likewise says greatness lasts only while one keeps guard over oneself.
Islam
“He has succeeded who purifies it, And he has failed who instills it [with corruption].”
— Qur'an 91:9-10 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an ties success or failure to how a person tends their own soul.
Like the Kural, it makes standing depend on keeping oneself pure and guarded.
Judaism
“Who is mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination, as it is said: ‘He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that rules his spirit than he that takes a city’ (Proverbs 16:32).”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Ben Zoma redefines might as mastery over one's own impulses.
Like the Kural, it locates true greatness in holding oneself in check.
Buddhism
“One truly is the protector of oneself; who else could the protector be? With oneself fully controlled, one gains a mastery that is hard to gain.”
— Dhammapada 160 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says no one else can guard a person; self-control yields a rare mastery.
Close to the Kural: greatness exists only for the one who guards himself.
Jainism
In the Rathanemi lecture, the nun Rajimati, sheltering in a cave after being caught in rain, is approached by the monk Rathanemi, who loses his composure; she stays firm, rebukes him for wavering in his vows, and her steadfastness brings him back to the discipline.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 22 (Rathanemi) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati, revered in Jain tradition for her constancy, guards herself and even restores another's resolve.
She embodies both halves of the Kural's simile: single-hearted faithfulness and the greatness that comes from guarding oneself.
Sikhism
“You search for your Husband Lord; you must have some fault in your body. Those who are known as happy soul-brides, do not look to others.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1384, Saloks of Sheikh Farid, salok 114 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In these saloks the faithful soul-bride (suhagan) is the one whose devotion is undivided and who does not look elsewhere. Gurbani uses this image for the disciplined soul that keeps itself wholly for God.
Valluvar compares the greatness of the self-guarding person to a woman of single-hearted faithfulness, and the Granth's happy soul-bride who does not look to others is the same image of guarded, undivided loyalty.
Greek Mythology
In the underworld scene of the Odyssey, the shade of Agamemnon praises Penelope for keeping faith with Odysseus through twenty years of pressure from the suitors, and declares that the fame of her virtue will never die.
— Homer, Odyssey 24 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Penelope's renown rests entirely on her steady self-keeping.
She is the Greek counterpart of the Kural's single-hearted woman whose honour lasts because she guards herself.
Philosophy
In Republic IV Socrates explains temperance as an ordering and mastery of pleasures and desires, the condition people point to when they call someone 'master of himself'.
— Plato, Republic IV, 430e-431b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato treats self-mastery as the inner order on which every other virtue depends.
Like the Kural, it makes excellence a matter of keeping hold of oneself.

Real Life Example

Lou Gehrig plays 2,130 consecutive games and then takes himself out of the lineup
1 June 1925 to 2 May 1939 · New York, United States
From 1 June 1925 the New York Yankees first baseman Lou Gehrig played in every one of his team's games for almost fourteen years, a record of 2,130 consecutive games that stood until 1995. A quiet and modest man often overshadowed by his flamboyant teammate Babe Ruth, he was remembered by a teammate as someone who simply did his job every day. When his play declined badly at the start of the 1939 season because of the illness later diagnosed as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Gehrig himself took his name out of the lineup on 2 May 1939, ending the streak, and he never played again.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gehrig's greatness rested on steady self-discipline sustained over years, and he guarded his own standards even to the point of ending his record himself.
The kural says that greatness, like a woman's single-hearted faithfulness, belongs only to one who guards himself, and Gehrig's record was the fruit of a self-discipline no one else imposed on him.
