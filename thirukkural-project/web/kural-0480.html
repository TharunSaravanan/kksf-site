Kural 480

The Knowledge of Power · Valiyarithal · வலியறிதல்

உளவரை தூக்காத ஒப்புர வாண்மை வளவரை வல்லைக் கெடும்.
Ulavarai thookkaadha oppuravu aanmai Valavarai vallaik kedum.

Meaning

Giving away money without thinking about how much you really have will soon use up everything you own.

Elementary

Helping others is wonderful, but if you give away more than you actually have, soon you’ll have nothing left, not even enough to keep helping.

University

Oppuravu is the civic virtue of beneficence to which Valluvar devotes chapter 22 (Oppuravu Arithal), where he says that even ruin incurred through beneficence is worth buying by selling oneself (220). Here he adds the prudential counterweight. Ulavarai thookkaadha describes beneficence that does not weigh (thookku, the same balance-verb as in 471) the extent of what one has, and it rapidly (vallai) destroys the limits of one’s wealth (valavarai). The chapter thus ends where it began, with weighing. Even virtue has to be measured against capacity if it is to last.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Karṇa, famous for never refusing a request, is approached by Indra disguised as a brahmin, who asks for his natural armour and earrings. Although his father Sūrya has warned him that they make him invulnerable, Karṇa cuts them from his body and gives them away. This leaves him exposed in the war that follows.
— Mahābhārata, Vana Parva (Kuṇḍalāharaṇa sub-parva; tr. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Karṇa’s generosity ignores what he himself needs to survive and contributes to his eventual defeat.
It illustrates the Kural’s warning about beneficence that does not weigh one’s own limits. The epic, however, honours Karṇa’s gift as noble.
Christianity
“But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.”
— 1 Timothy 5:8 (KJV) [5]
Paul insists that duties to one’s own household come first and may not be sacrificed.
It supports the Kural’s limit on beneficence: generosity that ignores one’s own resources and dependents is a failure. Elsewhere, though, the gospels praise the widow who gave all she had (Mark 12:41–44).
Islam
“And they ask you what they should spend. Say, ‘The excess [beyond needs].’”
— Qur’an 2:219 (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur’an answers the question of how much to give by pointing to what is surplus to one’s needs.
This is the Kural’s ulavarai thookku, weighing what one has before giving.
Judaism
“One who scatters should not scatter more than one-fifth, lest he render himself destitute and need the help of other people.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 50a [7]
The rabbis at Usha set an upper limit on charitable giving so that the giver does not become poor himself.
It gives the Kural’s principle as a rule: beneficence that does not weigh one’s means ends in destitution.
Buddhism
Prince Vessantara, perfecting generosity, gives away his kingdom’s auspicious rain-bringing white elephant, which leads to his exile. He then gives away his chariot, his children and even his wife.
— Vessantara Jātaka (Jātaka No. 547) [8]
The Jātaka presents boundless giving as the Bodhisatta’s perfection (dāna-pāramī), even though it brings exile and loss.
It is a deliberate counterpoint to the Kural. Vessantara’s giving takes no account of his resources and ruins his estate, which is exactly what Valluvar warns against in a householder, while the Buddhist story praises it as a spiritual ideal.
Jainism
“Tyāga: Charity is of three kinds, namely, gift of pure food (āhāradāna), gift of dispelling fear (abhayadāna), and gift of right knowledge (jñānadāna). The giving of these to others according to one's capacity is charity.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.24, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018; wisdomlib.org) [9]
Among the sixteen practices that lead to rebirth as a Tirthankara, Pujyapada's commentary defines charity as giving according to one's capacity, and austerity likewise.
The Kural says beneficence must be weighed against one's resources, and the Jain definition builds the same limit into charity itself.
Sikhism
Sikhs practice dasvandh, setting aside a tenth of their earnings in the Guru's name for the community's common resources. The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism traces the idea to Guru Nanak's line on earning and sharing (Ang 1245) and records that under Guru Ram Das Sikhs were asked to set apart at least ten per cent of their income for the common pool.
— The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (Punjabi University, Patiala), "Dasvandh" by Wazir Singh (paraphrase) [10]
Dasvandh gives Sikh generosity a set proportion, so that giving is regular and tied to what one actually earns.
The Kural warns that generosity that does not weigh one's resources soon exhausts wealth; dasvandh ties giving to a fixed share of income.
Greek Mythology
Timon of Athens pours out his fortune on friends, flatterers and hangers-on without counting the cost. When his wealth is gone, those he helped abandon him, and he withdraws in bitterness to live as a labourer outside the city.
— Lucian, Timon the Misanthrope (tr. H. W. and F. G. Fowler) [11]
Timon’s generosity, which never weighed his means, destroys his wealth and leaves him friendless.
This is a close illustration of the Kural: oppuravu that does not weigh one’s limits ruins one’s wealth quickly.
Philosophy
Cicero lists cautions for acts of kindness: they must not harm the recipient or others, must not exceed our means, and must suit the recipient’s worth. He argues that those who want to be more open-handed than their circumstances allow wrong their own next of kin, and are often tempted into taking wrongly from others to fund their generosity.
— Cicero, De Officiis I.42–44 (tr. Walter Miller) [12]
Cicero makes the limit of one’s means a strict condition of true liberality.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s ulavarai thookkaadha oppuravu: beneficence that ignores one’s means is self-destructive and ends up being unjust.

Real Life Example

Mansa Musa's pilgrimage through Cairo
1324 · Cairo, Egypt
Mansa Musa, ruler of the Mali Empire, made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 with a very large caravan carrying great quantities of gold. When he stopped in Cairo, he spent and gave away gold so lavishly that its value there fell, and the market had still not recovered some twelve years later.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Generosity on such a scale became famous across the Mediterranean world but also showed that even vast wealth can be poured out faster than is wise.
The kural warns that giving without weighing the limits of one's resources quickly exhausts them, and Mansa Musa's journey is the best-known case of giving on a scale beyond measure.
