Kural 542

The Right Sceptre · Sengonmai · செங்கோன்மை

வானோக்கி வாழும் உலகெல்லாம் மன்னவன் கோல்நோக்கி வாழுங் குடி.
Vaanokki Vaazhum Ulakellaam Mannavan Kolnokki Vaazhung Kuti.

Meaning

All living things on earth look up to the sky for rain. In the same way, the people of a country look to their king’s fair rule in order to live.

Elementary

Plants in a garden wait for rain, and without it they dry up. The people of a country are a bit like that garden: they are waiting for their leaders to be fair and kind. When the leader rules well, it is like a good rain, and everyone grows.

University

The couplet is built on an exact parallel: வான் நோக்கி வாழும் உலகு (the world that lives looking to the sky) matches கோல் நோக்கி வாழும் குடி (the citizenry that lives looking to the sceptre). As commentators note, வான் here stands for rain by metonymy, which echoes the opening chapter on rain (வான்சிறப்பு, Ch. 2), where rain sustains all life. Parimelazhagar reads உலகெல்லாம் as ‘all living beings’ and குடி as the settled subjects of the kingdom. Just as he reads கோல் throughout the chapter, the sceptre means just rule, not simply power. Varadarasan and Pappaiah keep the rain comparison; Kalaignar makes it ‘good government is as necessary to citizens as rain is to living things’. The comparison already hints at 545 and 559 (in the next chapter), where just rule and rainfall are causally linked. Both sources agree on the text; வாழுங் is the sandhi form of வாழும் before குடி.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For, when these creatures, being without a king, through fear dispersed in all directions, the Lord created a king for the protection of this whole (creation),”
— Manusmriti 7.3 (trans. G. Bühler) [3]
Manu explains that kingship was created because, without a king, people scattered in fear and could not live securely.
The subjects ‘live looking to the sceptre’ because, without it, ordinary life cannot go on.
Christianity
“He shall come down like rain upon the mown grass: as showers that water the earth. In his days shall the righteous flourish; and abundance of peace so long as the moon endureth.”
— Psalm 72:6–7 (KJV) [4]
The psalm prays that the just king will be for his people what rain is to the cut grass, bringing new life and peace.
The same picture as the Kural: the righteous ruler is to his people what rain is to the earth.
Islam
“Surely! Everyone of you is a guardian and is responsible for his charges: The Imam (ruler) of the people is a guardian and is responsible for his subjects;”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 7138 [5]
The Prophet describes the ruler as a shepherd whose flock depends on him and for whom he must answer.
The flock looking to its shepherd matches the people looking to the sceptre.
Judaism
“When the righteous are increased, the people rejoice; but when the wicked beareth rule, the people sigh.”
— Proverbs 29:2 (JPS 1917) [6]
The proverb says the people’s condition follows the character of those who rule them.
Because the people’s welfare hangs on the ruler’s justice, they live ‘looking to the sceptre’.
Buddhism
“When cattle cross a river, if the bull goes straight, they all go straight, because their leader is straight. So it is for humans: when the one deemed chief does the right thing, what do you expect the rest to do? The whole nation sleeps at ease, when the king is just.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.70, Adhammika Sutta (trans. Bhikkhu Sujato) [7]
The Buddha compares a people to a herd crossing a river that follows the lead bull, straight or crooked.
The whole nation ‘sleeps at ease’ under a just king, just as the people live by looking to the straight sceptre.
Jainism
When, through the fault of time, the power of the wish-fulfilling trees declined and the twins began to break the old laws, the people went to Rishabha, told him of the wrongdoing, and begged him to be their king, saying no one else like him could be found among them.
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book 1, ch. 2, part 8 ‘Coronation as king’ (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [8]
In Jain sacred history, kingship begins when nature's free provision fails. People who once lived from the wishing-trees now have to look to a ruler to keep order and sustain them.
The Kural says that as all life looks to the sky for rain, subjects look to the king's sceptre. Hemacandra's account describes the moment the people of the first age turned from the failing wishing-trees to Rishabha's rule for their survival.
Sikhism
“Leaving the water, the fish cannot live; the rainbird cannot live without the raindrops from the clouds.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 708, Raag Jaithsree (Jaithsree ki Vaar), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan uses the rainbird's total dependence on rain from the clouds as an image of the soul's dependence on God.
The Kural says the people live looking to the sceptre as all living things look to the sky for rain; Guru Arjan uses the same picture of life hanging on rain from above, though for him the dependence is on God, not a king.
Greek Mythology
“All the people look towards him while he settles causes with true judgements:”
— Hesiod, Theogony 84–86 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod describes the king favoured by the Muses, on whom the whole people fix their eyes as he gives straight verdicts.
Almost the Kural’s own phrase: the people ‘look to’ the king (நோக்கி) as he judges rightly.
Philosophy
“He is the bond which fastens the commonwealth together, he is the breath of life to all those thousands, who by themselves would become merely an encumbrance and a source of plunder if that directing mind were withdrawn”
— Seneca, Of Clemency (De Clementia), Book I, ch. 4 (tr. Aubrey Stewart) [11]
Writing to Nero, Seneca compares the ruler to the mind that directs the body and says the great multitude lives by him and would fall into ruin without him.
Like the Kural's image of all life looking to the rain, Seneca makes the ruler the very breath by which the people live.

Real Life Example

Hammurabi sets up his law code on a public stela
c. 1750s BCE · Babylon (present-day Iraq)
Toward the end of his reign, King Hammurabi of Babylon had 282 of his legal decisions, covering matters from prices and trade to family and criminal law, inscribed on a diorite stela set up in a temple. In the epilogue, Hammurabi invites any oppressed person who has a case at law to come and stand before his image, read the inscription and learn what is just. The best-preserved stela was found at Susa in 1901 and is now in the Louvre in Paris.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By placing the law in public view, the king presented justice as something his subjects could look to and rely on, not merely the ruler's private will.
The kural says the people of a land look to the king's sceptre as living things look to the sky for rain, and Hammurabi's stela explicitly invited every wronged subject to look to the king's justice.
