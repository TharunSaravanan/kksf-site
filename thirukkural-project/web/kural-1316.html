Kural 1316

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

உள்ளினேன் என்றேன்மற் றென்மறந்தீர் என்றென்னைப் புல்லாள் புலத்தக் கனள்.
Ullinaen endraen-mat ren-marandheer endrennaip pullaal pulaththak kanal

Meaning

When I said, "I thought of you," she asked, "So you had forgotten me?" Then, instead of hugging me, she began to sulk.

Elementary

If you tell your friend, ‘I remembered you today!’ a teasing friend might say, ‘Only today? So you forgot me yesterday?’ The wife plays the same word game with her husband.

University

Uḷḷiṉēṉ means ‘I recalled (you)’; the wife argues, as Mu. Varadarasanar puts it, that recalling presupposes forgetting (niṉaippukku muṉ maṟappu uṇṭu), so she asks eṉ maṟantīr (‘why did you forget?’), and, though about to embrace him (pullāḷ, ‘she did not embrace’), turns to sulking (pulattakkaṉaḷ). The logic is the same as in 1314: a loving word is pressed for an implication it never had. Solomon Pappaiah notes that she had begun to embrace him and stopped; Pope: ‘“Each day I called to mind your charms,” “O, then, you had forgot,” she cried, and then her opened arms, forthwith embraced me not.’ The line division reads மற் றென்மறந்தீர் on both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s Shakuntala, King Dushyanta, under a curse, forgets his wife Shakuntala, and only the sight of the ring he gave her brings his memory and grief back.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Acts 5-6 (paraphrase) [3]
The drama turns on the anguish of a beloved who has been forgotten and the joy of being remembered again.
The Kural’s wife treats even a passing lapse of memory as a small version of Shakuntala’s wound.
Christianity
“Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee.”
— Isaiah 49:15 (KJV) [4]
God promises a love that never forgets, even more constant than a mother’s.
The wife demands this kind of never-forgetting love, so that ‘I remembered you’ already sounds like a confession of forgetting.
Islam
“I know when you are pleased with me or angry with me… When you are pleased with me, you say, ‘No, by the Lord of Muhammad,’ but when you are angry with me, then you say, ‘No, by the Lord of Abraham.’” Aisha replied, “by Allah, O Allah’s Messenger, I leave nothing but your name.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5228 (sunnah.com) [5]
Aisha’s anger at the Prophet only went as far as leaving out his name, while her love stayed whole.
The Kural’s wife likewise withholds only her embrace for a moment, while her love remains underneath the sulk.
Judaism
“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had turned away, and was gone. My soul failed me when he spoke. I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Songs 5:6 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
In the Song of Songs, the beloved hesitates to open the door, and by the time she does, her lover has gone.
As in the Song, a moment of holding back between lovers, here the wife’s withheld embrace, interrupts their meeting.
Buddhism
“‘He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.’ Those who harbor such thoughts do not still their hatred.”
— Dhammapada 3 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that dwelling on supposed slights keeps resentment alive.
The wife holds onto an imagined slight, ‘you forgot me’, and so keeps the sulk going, though only in play.
Jainism
Mahavira’s teaching tells the ascetic not to watch the beauty, laughter and glances of women nor to keep a recollection of them in his mind.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45; paraphrase) [8]
For the Jain monk, deliberately not remembering the beloved is part of the discipline of chastity.
The Kural’s scene runs the other way: for the loving wife, any gap in remembering is itself an offence.
Sikhism
“Chanting it, I live; forgetting it, I die.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 9, So Dar (Raag Aasaa), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak says remembering the Name is life and forgetting it is death.
The Kural's wife hears 'I thought of you' and asks, 'So you had forgotten me?'; Gurbani makes the same link between remembrance and forgetting central to love for God.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus finally returns, Penelope holds back from embracing him until he proves himself by the secret of their bed built around an olive tree; only then does she run to him.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.173-206 (paraphrase) [10]
Penelope deliberately withholds her embrace, testing her husband before she yields.
The Kural’s wife likewise stops short of embracing her husband, holding back to test him with her question.
Philosophy
Aristotle lists among the things that make people angry being slighted by those who forget us, even forgetting our names, because forgetting seems a sign that we do not matter to them.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric II.2, 1379b (paraphrase) [11]
Aristotle sees forgetting as a form of slight that naturally provokes anger.
The wife’s mock anger works on the same logic: if he had to ‘remember’ her, he must once have forgotten.

Real Life Example

William Jones publishes Sacontala, or The Fatal Ring
1789 · Calcutta (Kolkata), India
In 1789 Sir William Jones published in Calcutta an English translation of Kalidasa's Sanskrit and Prakrit drama Shakuntala under the title Sacontala; or, The Fatal Ring. The play's Sanskrit title means Shakuntala recalled through recognition, and it turns on a king who must be brought to remember his wife with the help of a ring. Jones's translation was soon reprinted in London and Edinburgh and became widely influential in Europe.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The translation introduced Europe to one of the great love dramas of India, a story built around forgetting and remembering a beloved.
The wife in this kural turns I remembered you into So you had forgotten me, and Shakuntala is the classic Indian drama of a lover who forgets and must be made to remember.
