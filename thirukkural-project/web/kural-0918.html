Kural 918

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

ஆயும் அறிவினர் அல்லார்க்கு அணங்கென்ப மாய மகளிர் முயக்கு.
Aayum arivinar allaarkku anangenba maaya magalir muyakku.

Meaning

For people without the sense to look closely, the hug of tricky, false women is said to be like being grabbed by a demon that leads them to harm.

Elementary

In fairy tales, a magic song can make people forget who they are and walk into trouble. Valluvar says that people who do not stop to think can be ‘enchanted’ by false affection in the same way, while thoughtful people can see the trick.

University

Anangu (அணங்கு) in Sangam usage is a dangerous divine power, often a female spirit that seizes and afflicts; Mu. Varadarasanar glosses it as anangu-thaakku, ‘the attack of an anangu’, and as ‘Mohini’s bewitchment’, and Solomon Pappaiah as ‘a deity who stirs desire and takes life’. Maaya magalir (மாய மகளிர்) are women skilled in maayam (மாயம், deception, illusion). The decisive phrase is aayum arivinar allaar (ஆயும் அறிவினர் அல்லார்), ‘those who are not of examining intelligence’: only they experience this as a supernatural seizure, whereas the discerning see an ordinary deception. Enba (என்ப, ‘they say’) marks this as received opinion. Sivayogi Sivakumar alone reads anangu in the positive sense of ‘divine’, as what the foolish mistake such embraces for. Both sources agree on the text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At the churning of the ocean, Vishnu takes the form of the enchantress Mohini; the asuras, dazzled by her beauty, hand her the pot of amrita to share out, and she gives it to the devas while the asuras are left with nothing.
— Bhagavata Purana 8.8-8.9 [3]
The Mohini episode shows how enchantment makes the undiscerning give away what matters most.
Kalaignar and Mu. Varadarasanar themselves gloss Kural 918’s anangu as ‘Mohini mayakkam’, the Mohini bewitchment.
Christianity
“But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed.”
— James 1:14 (KJV) [4]
James locates the power of temptation in a person’s own desire.
Valluvar likewise implies that the ‘spirit’ works only on those who do not examine themselves.
Islam
“...And what is the worldly life except the enjoyment of delusion.”
— Qur’an 57:20 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an calls worldly attractions a pleasure that deceives.
Kural 918’s maayam (illusion) is one form of that deceiving enjoyment.
Judaism
Delilah is paid in silver by the Philistine lords to discover the secret of Samson’s strength; she presses him with claims of love until he tells her, and he is captured and blinded.
— Judges 16:4-21 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
The story is the Hebrew Bible’s classic example of a strong man undone by paid, deceptive affection.
Samson, mighty but undiscerning, is the man of Kural 918 who cannot see through maaya affection.
Buddhism
“Those who are lust-infatuated fall back into the swirling current (of samsara) like a spider on its self-spun web.”
— Dhammapada 347 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada says infatuation traps a person in a web of their own making.
In Kural 918, the ‘spell’ that grips the undiscerning is likewise partly their own doing.
Jainism
“As (men by baiting) with a piece of flesh a fearless single lion get him into a trap, so women may capture an ascetic though he be careful.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga presents seduction as a snare that catches even the strong and wary.
Kural 918 compares the embrace of deceitful women to seizure by an enchanting spirit (anangu) that ruins those who do not look closely, the same image of a man captured.
Sikhism
“The mad-man’s mind is enticed by Maya, the enticer; he has taken the intoxicating drug of falsehood.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1213, Raag Saarang, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan calls Maya mohani, the enchantress who bewitches the mind until it acts as though drugged. The word is the same one the Tamil commentators reach for when they gloss the Kural’s anangu as Mohini’s spell.
The Kural compares the deceitful embrace to seizure by an enchanting spirit, and Guru Arjan’s enchantress Maya, who leaves the mind drugged and foolish, is a close Sikh counterpart.
Greek Mythology
“First you will come to the Sirens who enchant all who come near them. If any one unwarily draws in too close and hears the singing of the Sirens, his wife and children will never welcome him home again...”
— Homer, Odyssey 12 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Circe warns Odysseus that the Sirens’ sweet song draws careless sailors to their death; he survives by having himself bound to the mast.
The Sirens are a Greek anangu: fatal only to those who come close ‘unwarily’, without the foresight Valluvar calls aayum arivu.
Philosophy
“If you are struck by the appearance of any promised pleasure, guard yourself against being hurried away by it; but let the affair wait your leisure, and procure yourself some delay.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 34 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus advises pausing to examine a tempting impression before acting on it.
This pause is precisely the aayum arivu (examining intelligence) that Kural 918 says protects against enchantment.

Real Life Example

Ludwig I of Bavaria's infatuation with Lola Montez ends his reign
1846-1848 · Munich, Bavaria, Germany
In 1846 the dancer and adventuress Lola Montez performed in Munich, and King Ludwig I became so attached to her that he made her Countess of Landsfeld. Her influence on the government was such that the cabinet was nicknamed the Lola ministry, and the affair further reduced the king's popularity. When the revolutions of 1848 broke out, Ludwig abdicated in favour of his son in March 1848.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A king who had reigned for more than twenty years lost his throne in part because of an infatuation his subjects would not accept.
The kural says that for those who lack discerning judgment, attachment to such women is like being seized by a spirit that leads to ruin, and Ludwig's attachment helped cost him his crown.
