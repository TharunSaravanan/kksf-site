Kural 1110

Delight in Union · Punarchi Makizhthal · புணர்ச்சி மகிழ்தல்

அறிதோறு அறியாமை கண்டற்றால் காமம் செறிதோறும் சேயிழை மாட்டு.
Aridhoru ariyaamai kandatraal kaamam seridhorum seyizhai maattu.

Meaning

The more you learn, the more you see how much you do not know. In the same way, each time I come close to my jeweled love, my love for her feels new again.

Elementary

When you read one good book about space, you find out there are a hundred more things about space you never knew. The more you learn, the more there is to learn! The lover says love is like that: the more time he spends with the one he loves, the more new things he finds to love.

University

The chapter ends with a celebrated simile from learning: aṟitōṟu aṟiyāmai kaṇṭaṟṟāl (‘like discovering one’s ignorance each time one learns’), so is kāmam (love) each time he draws close (ceṟitōṟum) to the cēyiḻai (சேயிழை, ‘she of red, i.e. gold, jewels’). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah specify learning from books (nūl), and Solomon Pappaiah reads the second half as love’s savour appearing ever new; Kalaignar likewise has ‘knowing new things again and again’. The verse links the Kamattuppal to the Porutpal chapters on learning (ch. 40-43), and turns the humility of the scholar into a picture of inexhaustible love. Pope’s second line slightly shifts the sense: ‘The more men learn, the more their lack of learning they detect; / ’Tis so when I approach the maid with gleaming jewels decked.’ The ytamizh.com ‘Couplet Explanation’ reads it as repeated union only creating desire for more, a narrower gloss than the Tamil commentators give. Text identical on both source sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Kena Upanishad says that Brahman is truly known by the one who thinks he does not know it, and is not known by the one who thinks he knows it; it is unknown to those who know and known to those who do not.
— Kena Upanishad 2.3 (tr. F. Max Müller, SBE 1) [3]
The Upanishad teaches that the highest reality always exceeds what one thinks one has grasped.
Like the Kural, it finds that deeper knowing reveals how much remains unknown.
Christianity
“And if any man think that he knoweth any thing, he knoweth nothing yet as he ought to know.”
— 1 Corinthians 8:2 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that whoever thinks he knows has not yet begun to know as he should.
This is the first half of the Kural’s simile: true knowing reveals how much is still unknown.
Islam
“Say, ‘If the sea were ink for [writing] the words of my Lord, the sea would be exhausted before the words of my Lord were exhausted, even if We brought the like of it in [continual] supplement.’”
— Qur’an 18:109 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says that God’s words are inexhaustible, so that all the oceans as ink could never write them all.
Like the Kural’s endless discovery, the Qur’an points to a depth that can never be reached by learning.
Judaism
“Ben Bag Bag said: Turn it over, and [again] turn it over, for all is therein. And look into it; And become gray and old therein.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:22 (tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
The sage teaches that the Torah can be studied again and again for a lifetime and still yield new meaning.
The Kural’s love, which finds something new at every meeting, is like the Torah that is never exhausted by study.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that knowing the extent of one’s ignorance is the beginning of wisdom.
This is the learning half of the Kural’s simile: wisdom grows as one sees what one does not know.
Jainism
“How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira teaches that sense-desire is never satisfied, however much it enjoys.
The Kural finds joy in a love that is always new and never used up; Jainism sees the same endlessness as the misery of a desire that can never be filled.
Sikhism
“The more you say about them, the more there still remains to be said.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 5, Japji Sahib (pauri 24), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak says the greatness and virtues of God can never be fully described: every attempt only shows how much more there is.
The Kural says that, as with learning, every meeting with the beloved shows how much more there is to discover; Guru Nanak says the same about the endless Beloved, God.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus and Penelope are reunited, Athena holds back the Dawn at the edge of the Ocean and lengthens the night, so that the couple can share their joy and tell each other all that has happened.
— Homer, Odyssey 23.241-246, 300-343 [10]
The goddess stretches the night because the lovers’ delight in each other has no natural end.
The Kural’s love, which finds something new at every meeting, is matched by a reunion so rich that the night itself must be lengthened.
Philosophy
“Well, although I do not suppose that either of us knows anything really beautiful and good, I am better off than he is,—for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know.”
— Plato, Apology 21d (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates concludes that his only wisdom is knowing that he does not know.
This is the classic statement of the first half of the Kural’s simile, the learner who sees his own ignorance.

Real Life Example

Will and Ariel Durant write The Story of Civilization together
1968 (Pulitzer Prize) · United States
Will Durant met Ariel when he was her teacher at the Ferrer Modern School, and they married in 1913. She began as his research aide on The Story of Civilization, a many-volume history of the world, became his collaborator, and from the seventh volume onward was credited as co-author. In 1968 they won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction for Rousseau and Revolution; Ariel died on 25 October 1981 and Will less than two weeks later.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The Durants' history became one of the most widely read popular histories of the twentieth century, and it was the work of a marriage as much as of one author.
The kural says love keeps revealing something new, just as each new piece of learning shows how much more there is to know; the Durants joined their marriage to a lifetime of learning, volume after volume, until both lives ended within days of each other.
