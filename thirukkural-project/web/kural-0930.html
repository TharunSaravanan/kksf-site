Kural 930

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

கள்ளுண்ணாப் போழ்திற் களித்தானைக் காணுங்கால் உள்ளான்கொல் உண்டதன் சோர்வு.
Kallunnaap pozhdhir kaliththaanaik kaanungaal ullaankol undadhan sorvu.

Meaning

When a drinker is sober and sees someone else who is drunk, do they not think that they look just as bad when they drink?

Elementary

Have you ever laughed at someone acting silly, then realised you act just like that sometimes? The Kural asks why a drinker, seeing another person drunk and stumbling, doesn't think, ‘Oh no, that's me when I drink!’

University

The closing couplet is a rhetorical question marked by kol (கொல்). When the drinker, in kallunnaap pozhdhu (கள்ளுண்ணாப் போழ்து, 'the time he is not drinking'), sees a kaliththaan (களித்தான், a drunk man), will he not reflect, ullaan (உள்ளான், 'will he not consider'), on the sorvu (சோர்வு, slackness, lapse) that comes from his own drinking? The appeal is to self-recognition. Commentators agree on this reading: Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah, Kalaignar and Puliyur Kesikan all take it as a sober drinker seeing his own condition in another. Mu. Varadarasanar's printed gloss reads மயங்கினவளை ('the intoxicated woman') on both base sites, apparently a typographical slip for மயங்கினவனை. The text is identical on ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The learned Sukra, having been deceived while under the influence of wine, and remembering the total loss of consciousness that is one of the terrible consequences of drink, and beholding too before him the handsome Kacha whom he had, in a state of unconsciousness, drunk with his wine, then thought of effecting a reform in the manners of Brahmanas.”
— Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Section 76 (K. M. Ganguli trans.) [3]
Once sober, Shukra looks back at what drink made him do and acts on it.
Shukra does what the Kural hopes every sober drinker will do: reflect and change.
Christianity
“And take heed to yourselves, lest at any time your hearts be overcharged with surfeiting, and drunkenness, and cares of this life, and so that day come upon you unawares.”
— Luke 21:34 (KJV) [4]
Jesus calls on his hearers to watch themselves so that drunkenness does not catch them unprepared.
‘Take heed to yourselves’ is the same self-examination the Kural asks of the sober drinker.
Islam
“Satan only wants to cause between you animosity and hatred through intoxicants and gambling and to avert you from the remembrance of Allāh and from prayer. So will you not desist?”
— Qur'an 5:91 (Sahih International) [5]
After setting out the harms of intoxicants, the Qur'an ends with a pointed question.
‘So will you not desist?’ has the same rhetorical form as the Kural's ‘will he not reflect?’
Judaism
“‘They struck me, but I felt no hurt; They beat me, but I was unaware; As often as I wake, I go after it again.’”
— Proverbs 23:35 (JPS Tanakh 1985, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs gives the drinker's own voice: he wakes, feels nothing, and goes straight back to the cup.
This is the failure the Kural laments: the drinker who, even when sober, will not reflect.
Buddhism
In the Kumbha Jataka, Sakka appears before King Sabbamitta carrying a jar of liquor and describes in verse everything the drink will do to him; the king, seeing the effects laid out before he drinks, gives up liquor.
— Kumbha Jataka (Jataka 512, Cowell ed.) [7]
A vivid picture of drunkenness's effects leads a king to renounce it while still sober.
This is the reflection the Kural hopes for: seeing the effects and applying them to oneself.
Jainism
“When one thus knows that a (virtuous) monk or householder will be gladdened (by his gain), how, then, should a man, whilst he is losing (his chance), not be conscious of his losing it?”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.22 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
After the parable of the three merchants, the Uttarādhyayana asks how a person who sees others gaining by virtue can fail to notice his own loss.
Valluvar closes by asking whether the drinker who sees another man reeling drunk does not recognise his own state; the Jain verse asks the same kind of question, urging a person to see his own loss by comparison with others.
Sikhism
“Fareed, if you have a keen understanding, then do not write black marks against anyone else. Look underneath your own collar instead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid 6 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid tells the clever person not to keep a record of other people’s faults but to bow his head and look at his own. Seeing someone else’s failing is meant to send a person back to examine himself.
The Kural asks whether the sober drinker, seeing a drunk man, will think he looks the same himself, and Farid’s ‘look underneath your own collar’ is that same turn to self-examination.
Greek Mythology
“the wine must have been doing you a mischief, as it does with all those drink immoderately. It was wine that inflamed the Centaur Eurytion …”
— Homer, Odyssey 21-22 (Butler trans.) [10]
The suitor Antinous lectures the disguised Odysseus on the ruin wine brings, while drinking heavily himself; in the next book he is shot down with the cup at his lips.
Antinous sees drink's folly in others but never in himself, the blindness the Kural asks about.
Philosophy
Plutarch reports that the Spartans would make helots drink unmixed wine and bring them into the common messes, to show the young men what drunkenness looked like.
— Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 28 [11]
Spartan education used the sight of a drunk man to teach sobriety.
This puts into practice the Kural's hope that seeing another drunk will make a person reflect.

Real Life Example

Bill Wilson seeks out a fellow alcoholic in Akron and Alcoholics Anonymous begins
May-June 1935 · Akron, Ohio, United States
Bill Wilson, a New York stockbroker who had stopped drinking after a hospital stay in late 1934, found himself alone and tempted to drink in an Akron hotel in 1935 after a business deal failed. Having learned that talking with other alcoholics kept him sober, he telephoned a local clergyman and was put in touch with Robert Smith, a physician who was still drinking, and Wilson stayed on in Smith's house for several months. Smith stopped drinking soon after, and the two men's work with other alcoholics grew into Alcoholics Anonymous.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Wilson discovered that facing another drinker's condition was what kept him from returning to his own, and that insight became the basis of a worldwide movement.
The kural asks whether a sober drinker, seeing another man drunk, does not recognise his own state when drinking, and Wilson kept himself sober precisely by seeing his own condition in another alcoholic.
