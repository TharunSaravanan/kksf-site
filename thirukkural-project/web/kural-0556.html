Kural 556

The Cruel Sceptre · Kodungonmai · கொடுங்கோன்மை

மன்னர்க்கு மன்னுதல் செங்கோன்மை அஃதின்றேல் மன்னாவாம் மன்னர்க் கொளி.
Mannarkku mannuthal sengonmai akhthindrel Mannaavaam mannark koli.

Meaning

Fair rule is what makes a king’s fame last. Without it, his glory will not stay.

Elementary

A trophy you won by cheating doesn’t feel like yours, and people stop cheering once they find out. Valluvar says a king’s glory is like that: only fair, honest rule keeps it shining, and without fairness it fades away.

University

The couplet repeats the root மன்- ‘to endure’ for effect: மன்னர் (kings), மன்னுதல் (enduring) and மன்னா (will not endure). ஒளி (light, lustre) means renown. Parimelazhagar supplies the instrumental case lost by elision: ‘kings’ fame endures by the straight sceptre’. He cites Nāṉmaṇikkaṭikai 17 (‘if you wish to endure in this world, plant fame’) to show that fame is what makes a person endure. He explains the plural (ஒளி taken as many forms of fame, from victory, generosity and so on) and concludes that if their fame does not last, the kings themselves do not last. Some continuous-text editions read the sandhi form அஃதின்றேன் and மன்னாவா மன்னர்க் for அஃதின்றேல் மன்னாவாம். Manakkudavar puts this couplet first in the ‘faults’ series, taking the loss of glory as the first penalty of misrule.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhishma tells how the first true king, Prithu, was called rājā because he gladdened (rañjana) all the people by protecting them, the earth yielding abundantly under his righteous rule.
— Mahābhārata, Śānti Parva, Section LIX (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
The very title ‘king’ is said to come from pleasing and protecting the people.
As the Kural says, a king’s standing and fame rest wholly on righteous rule.
Christianity
“The king that faithfully judgeth the poor, his throne shall be established for ever.”
— Proverbs 29:14 (KJV) [5]
The proverb ties the permanence of a throne to just judgement of the powerless.
This is the Kural’s claim: just rule is what makes a king endure.
Islam
“O David, indeed We have made you a successor upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth and do not follow [your own] desire, as it will lead you astray from the way of Allah.”
— Qur’an 38:26 (Saheeh International) [6]
David’s authority as ruler is given on the condition that he judges with truth.
Like the Kural, it makes just judgement the condition of lasting rule.
Judaism
“There are three crowns: the crown of Torah, the crown of priesthood, and the crown of royalty, but the crown of a good name supersedes them all.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:13 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Rabbi Shimon ranks a good name above every office, kingship included.
The Kural likewise holds that royal glory is empty unless the good name that comes from just rule upholds it.
Buddhism
The Jātakas list ten royal virtues (dasa rāja-dhamma): generosity, moral conduct, self-sacrifice, honesty, gentleness, self-restraint, freedom from anger, non-violence, patience and non-opposition to the right. A king who keeps them is praised and his reign prospers.
— Jātaka No. 534, Mahāhaṃsa Jātaka (ed. E. B. Cowell) [8]
In Buddhist tradition, a king’s greatness is measured by these ten virtues.
They spell out the ‘straight sceptre’ that the Kural says alone makes a king’s fame last.
Jainism
“Sânti, a universal monarch of great power, the bringer of peace to the world, gave up Bharatavarsha and reached perfection.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 18.38 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Gardabhāli's sermon lists righteous universal monarchs, such as Bharata, Sagara and Śānti, who are remembered for their virtue and for renouncing their power.
The Kural says only just rule makes a king's fame endure, and the Jain list keeps the names of kings who brought peace to the world, though it values their renunciation more than their reigns.
Sikhism
“They alone find peace, and they are the true kings, whose dealings are true.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 962, Raag Raamkalee (Raamkalee ki Vaar), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Arjan defines true kingship not by power or display but by truthful dealing.
The Kural says only just rule makes a king's glory endure; Guru Arjan likewise grants the title of true king only to those whose dealings are true.
Greek Mythology
In Plato’s closing myth, Minos, Rhadamanthys and Aeacus, kings remembered for their justice in life, are appointed after death to judge the dead, while the souls of tyrants are the most scarred and incurable.
— Plato, Gorgias 523e-526c (trans. B. Jowett) [11]
Only just kings gain an honour that lasts forever; unjust rulers become eternal warnings.
It matches the Kural’s point that only just rule makes a king’s glory endure.
Philosophy
Seneca tells Nero that a ruler’s one impregnable defence is the love of his citizens, and that clemency secures a lasting good name where cruelty leaves only a hated memory.
— Seneca, De Clementia I.19 [12]
The Stoic argues that mercy and justice, not force, make a reign secure and its memory honoured.
It parallels the Kural’s claim that only the straight sceptre makes a ruler’s glory last.

Real Life Example

Stalin's body is removed from Lenin's Mausoleum
31 October 1961 · Red Square, Moscow, Soviet Union
After Stalin's death in 1953 his embalmed body was placed beside Lenin's in the mausoleum on Red Square. During the de-Stalinization campaign led by Nikita Khrushchev, the body was removed on 31 October 1961 and reburied by the Kremlin Wall. The same year the city of Stalingrad was renamed Volgograd.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Within a decade of his death, the honours given to one of history's most feared rulers were being taken down by his own successors.
The kural says just rule is what makes a king's fame last and that without it his glory will not endure, and the removal of Stalin from the mausoleum shows such glory fading.
