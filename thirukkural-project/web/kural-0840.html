Kural 840

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

கழாஅக்கால் பள்ளியுள் வைத்தற்றால் சான்றோர் குழாஅத்துப் பேதை புகல்.
Kazhaaakkaal palliyul vaiththatraal saandror kuzhaaaththup pedhai pugal.

Meaning

A fool walking into a gathering of good and worthy people is like putting unwashed feet on a clean bed.

Elementary

Imagine coming in from a muddy football field and climbing straight into a freshly made bed with dirty feet. Everything gets messy. The poet says a fool barging into a group of wise people spoils things in the same way.

University

The closing simile sets kazhaaak kaal (கழாஅக்கால், unwashed foot) in palli (பள்ளி) against the fool’s pugal (புகல், entering) into saandror kuzhaaam (சான்றோர் குழாஅம், the gathering of the noble). The lengthened forms kazhaaa- and kuzhaaa- are isai-nirai alapedai (இசைநிறை அளபெடை), vowel prolongation that fills out the metre. Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar take palli as a bed (as do Pope and Drew), but Puliyur Kesigan reads it as a place of worship (‘தொழுகைக்குரிய பள்ளி’), making the image one of defiling a sanctuary; both senses of palli are attested in Tamil. The text is identical in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari’s Nitishataka calls silence the cover the Creator gave to hide ignorance, and says it is the ornament of the unlearned above all in an assembly of the learned.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka (verse beginning ‘svayattam ekantagunam’) [3]
The Sanskrit verse advises the ignorant to keep quiet when among the wise.
Valluvar sees the fool’s presence in a learned gathering as a defilement; Bhartrhari offers the fool his only remedy.
Christianity
“And when the king came in to see the guests, he saw there a man which had not on a wedding garment: And he saith unto him, Friend, how camest thou in hither not having a wedding garment? And he was speechless.”
— Matthew 22:11-12 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the wedding feast, a guest who enters unprepared is challenged and has no answer.
Like the unwashed foot on the bed, he brings into a pure gathering what does not belong there.
Islam
“O you who have believed, when you rise to [perform] prayer, wash your faces and your forearms to the elbows and wipe over your heads and wash your feet to the ankles.”
— Qur’an 5:6 (Sahih International; excerpt) [5]
The Qur’an requires washing, including the feet, before standing in prayer.
On Kesigan’s reading of palli as a place of worship, the Kural’s image is the very impurity this washing prevents.
Judaism
“Therefore the ungodly shall not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous.”
— Psalm 1:5 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
The first psalm says the wicked have no place in the gathering of the righteous.
Valluvar likewise sees the fool as out of place in the assembly of the wise.
Buddhism
“Though all his life a fool associates with a wise man, he no more comprehends the Truth than a spoon tastes the flavor of the soup.”
— Dhammapada 64 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada says a fool can sit among the wise for a lifetime and absorb nothing.
Both texts see the fool in wise company as out of place and unchanged.
Jainism
“If one who has grown up in ignorance enter the assembly of the excellent in learning, in the earth, and sits down, it will be like the sitting down of a dog; and though, not remaining quiet, he should say anything, it will be like the barking of a dog.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 26 ('The want of understanding'), st. 4 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaṭiyār mocks the ignorant man who enters a gathering of the learned: his sitting there is like a dog's, and his speech is like barking.
Valluvar compares a fool entering the assembly of the wise to putting unwashed feet on a clean bed; the Nālaṭiyār uses an equally earthy image for the same intrusion, a dog sitting and barking among the learned.
Sikhism
“Seeing the swans swimming, the herons became envious. But the poor herons drowned and died, and floated with their heads down, and their feet above.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 585, Raag Vadahans, Vadahans Ki Vaar (salok 3 before pauri 1), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Amar Das tells of herons who envied the swans and tried to swim among them, only to drown in disgrace.
The Kural says a fool entering the assembly of the wise is like unwashed feet on a clean bed; Guru Amar Das's herons venturing among the swans are likewise out of place.
Greek Mythology
When the mountain god Tmolus judged the music contest between Apollo and Pan and gave the prize to Apollo, Midas alone objected; Apollo gave him the ears of an ass for his dull hearing.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.146-193 [9]
Ovid shows a fool pushing his opinion into a contest judged by the divine and the wise, and being marked for it.
Like Valluvar’s fool entering the assembly of the wise, Midas spoils the gathering and disgraces himself.
Philosophy
In the Phaedo, Socrates says that it is not permitted for the impure to lay hold of the pure, which is why the soul must be cleansed before it can grasp truth.
— Plato, Phaedo 67b [10]
Plato treats purification as a precondition for entering into contact with what is pure.
The Kural’s unwashed foot on a clean bed is a homely image of the same incompatibility.

Real Life Example

George Psalmanazar's false Formosa deceives learned London
1703-1764 · London, England
A young Frenchman who called himself George Psalmanazar arrived in London around 1703 claiming to be a native of Formosa (Taiwan), and in 1704 published a description of the island, which he had never visited, complete with an invented alphabet and language. He was received in learned circles, including the Royal Society and the Bishop of London, until other experts showed his claims to be false and he became a target of satire. In his memoirs, published in 1764 after his death, he admitted the imposture.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
An impostor with no real knowledge entered the gatherings of scholars and, once exposed, became a byword for fraud.
The kural likens a fool entering an assembly of the wise to setting unwashed feet on a clean bed, and Psalmanazar's appearance among London's learned societies brought falsehood into a place meant for knowledge.
