Kural 23

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

இருமை வகைதெரிந்து ஈண்டுஅறம் பூண்டார் பெருமை பிறங்கிற்று உலகு.
Irumai vakaitherindhu eendaram poondaar, Perumai pirangitru ulagu.

Meaning

Nothing in this world shines brighter than the greatness of those who have weighed the two kinds of life and chosen to live a life of goodness here.

Elementary

Some people look at two roads and can actually tell which one leads somewhere good and which one just looks fun for a little while. This kural says the people who can tell the difference — and then actually start walking the good road today, not someday — are the brightest people in the whole world.

University

“Irumai” — “twoness” — is the pivot, and the commentators do not agree on what the two are. Parimelazhagar takes them as this birth and the next (iham and param); Manakkudavar and several modern readers take them as birth and release from birth, the round of rebirth against liberation; a third reading makes them pleasure and good, the transient and the abiding. Valluvar leaves the ambiguity intact, which is characteristic: what matters is not which pair you name but that you have actually distinguished (“vakai therindhu,” sorted out the kinds) rather than drifted. Against that discernment he sets the sharply temporal “eendu” — here, in this place, in this life — so that the verse refuses to let the insight be postponed to a future existence. “Pirangitru” (from pirangu-, to shine forth, to be conspicuous) makes the resulting greatness visible in the world rather than hidden in it; the renunciant's virtue is not an inward secret but the brightest public fact the world contains.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man. It is well with him who clings to the good; he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end.”
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2 (trans. Max Müller, Sacred Books of the East vol. 15) [4]
Death himself teaches Nachiketas that the preyas (the pleasant) and the shreyas (the good) present themselves together, and that everything depends on being able to tell them apart before choosing.
This is the Kural's “irumai vakai therindhu” almost word for word: discerning the two kinds, then binding oneself to the better one.
Christianity
“While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal.”
— 2 Corinthians 4:18 (KJV) [5]
Paul contrasts two orders of reality — the visible and passing against the invisible and lasting — and says the Christian's whole orientation follows from keeping that distinction in view.
Same structure as the Kural: two kinds are distinguished, and greatness lies in ordering one's present life by that distinction.
Islam
“But you prefer the worldly life, While the Hereafter is better and more enduring.”
— Qur'an 87:16-17, Al-A'la (Sahih International) [6]
The Qur'an rebukes the human habit of choosing the immediate over the lasting, and names the two options plainly so that the choice cannot be made in ignorance.
The verse presents exactly the two kinds the Kural says the great have discerned, and identifies which one the wise take up.
Judaism
“Rabbi Jacob said: this world is like a vestibule before the world to come; prepare yourself in the vestibule, so that you may enter the banqueting-hall.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:16 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Dr. Joshua Kulp) [7]
The Mishnah's image makes the present world real but preliminary, and makes preparation in the here and now the only access to what follows.
Both the two-order distinction and the Kural's insistence on “eendu,” acting here rather than later, are present in a single sentence.
Buddhism
“Lead a righteous life; lead not a base life. The righteous live happily both in this world and the next.”
— Dhammapada 169, Lokavagga (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada's chapter on the world sets the two possible ways of living side by side and reports the outcome across both existences.
Like the Kural, it makes the choice of dharma here the thing that determines standing in both orders of existence.
Jainism
“He who, in the world, comprehends and renounces these causes of sin, is called a reward-knowing sage (muni).”
— Acaranga Sutra I.1.1.7 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The very first lesson of the Acaranga Sutra sets out the twofold knowledge (parijna): first understanding the causes of sin and rebirth, then renouncing them. Only the person who does both is called a true sage (muni).
Valluvar's renunciant first discerns the two orders of existence ('vakai therindhu') and then takes up virtue here and now. That is the same two-step the Acaranga sets at the head of the Jain canon, where comprehension has to pass into renunciation in this life.
Sikhism
“In the Company of the Holy, one is happy here and hereafter.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 272, Raag Gauri, Sukhmani Sahib, Ashtpadi 7, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Sukhmani often pairs this world and the next (halat palat). Spiritual discernment lived out now, in the company of the holy, brings good in both.
The Kural praises those who have discerned the two orders of existence and live virtuously here and now. This line speaks to the same two worlds and to living them well now.
Greek Mythology
Plato closes the Republic with the myth of Er, a soldier who dies, travels to the place where souls are judged and choose their next lives, and returns to tell the living what he saw — the point of the account being that a person who has seen both worlds will choose the just life rather than the merely attractive one.
— Plato, Republic X (the Myth of Er, 614b-621d) [11]
The story is designed to give the reader the vantage point of someone who has surveyed both existences at once, so that the choice between them can be made knowingly.
It dramatises exactly the Kural's claim that greatness begins with having actually discerned the two orders, not merely heard of them.
Philosophy
“For I deem that the true disciple of philosophy is likely to be misunderstood by other men; they do not perceive that he is ever pursuing death and dying.”
— Plato, Phaedo 64a (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [12]
Socrates argues on his last day that philosophy just is the practice of separating oneself from the body's demands, so that the philosopher has been rehearsing for the other order of existence all along.
Like the Kural's renunciant, the philosopher is great precisely because he has taken the measure of both states and arranged his present life around the better one.

Real Life Example

Emperor Charles V abdicates and retires to the monastery of Yuste
1555-1557 · Brussels (Belgium) and Yuste (Spain)
In Brussels on 25 October 1555, Charles V, ruler of the Holy Roman Empire, Spain and the Netherlands, began giving up his realms, handing the Netherlands and Spain to his son Philip and the imperial title to his brother Ferdinand. He sailed to Spain in 1556 and in February 1557 moved to the monastery of Yuste. He lived there in relative seclusion until his death on 21 September 1558.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The most powerful monarch in Europe chose to lay down his crowns while still alive and end his days beside a monastery, an almost unique act among rulers of his age.
The kural praises those who have weighed this world against the other and taken up the path of virtue here and now, and Charles V, having held more of the world than almost anyone, chose to leave it for a life of retreat.
