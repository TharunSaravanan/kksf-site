Kural 373

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

நுண்ணிய நூல்பல கற்பினும் மற்றுந்தன் உண்மை யறிவே மிகும்.
Nunniya noolpala karpinum matrundhan Unmai yarive migum.

Meaning

However many deep books a person studies, in the end the understanding that fate has given them is what wins out.

Elementary

You can give a pencil to every child in class, but each one will still draw in their own way. This kural says that reading many clever books helps, but in the end a person’s own natural understanding decides how they really think and act.

University

The key phrase is உண்மை அறிவு (unmai arivu): literally ‘the knowledge that truly is’ one’s own, which commentators gloss as the understanding given by ஊழ், as opposed to what is acquired from books (நூல்). Valluvar is not dismissing learning; he devotes whole chapters to கல்வி (learning). He is noting that learning is filtered through an innate capacity it cannot fully override. The Gita makes a parallel claim that even the learned act according to their own nature (prakṛti), and Heraclitus’ fragment that character is a person’s daimon points the same way. Modern debates on nature versus nurture, and on the limits of education, are a secular version of the same observation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Even a man of knowledge acts according to his own nature, for everyone follows the nature he has acquired from the three modes. What can repression accomplish?”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.33 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna says that learning does not override the nature (prakṛti) a person has acquired.
Almost the same claim as the Kural: however learned, a person’s own innate nature wins out.
Christianity
“For to one is given by the Spirit the word of wisdom; to another the word of knowledge by the same Spirit … dividing to every man severally as he will.”
— 1 Corinthians 12:8, 11 (KJV) [4]
Paul teaches that wisdom and knowledge are distributed as gifts, each person receiving a different share.
Like the Kural’s ‘own true understanding’, wisdom here is a given portion, not something merely studied into existence.
Islam
“We raise in degrees whom We will, but over every possessor of knowledge is one [more] knowing.”
— Qur'an 12:76 (Saheeh International) [5]
Degrees of knowledge are raised by God’s will, and no one’s learning is final.
Knowledge levels are set from above, which echoes the Kural’s view that one’s true understanding is allotted.
Judaism
The Talmud relates that the angel appointed over conception brings the drop before God and asks whether it will become strong or weak, wise or foolish, rich or poor, but does not ask whether it will be righteous or wicked.
— Babylonian Talmud, Niddah 16b [6]
Wisdom, strength and wealth are fixed before birth; only moral character is left to free choice.
It directly supports the Kural: wisdom (or foolishness) is set before any study begins.
Buddhism
“Though all his life a fool associates with a wise man, he no more comprehends the Truth than a spoon tastes the flavor of the soup.”
— Dhammapada 64 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
Being around wisdom does not give understanding to someone who lacks the capacity for it.
Exposure to wisdom, like reading many books, cannot replace the understanding that is truly one’s own.
Jainism
“The dispassionate man who has performed all duties will quickly remove the obstructions to right knowledge and to right faith, and whatever Karman produces obstruction (to righteousness). Then he knows and sees all things, he is free from delusion and hindrances.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.108–109 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
In Jainism, perfect knowledge (kevala jñāna) is the soul's own nature. It is not built up from study but revealed once the obstructing karmas are removed, and book learning (śruta) is only one limited, partly covered form of it.
Valluvar says that however many subtle books one studies, the knowledge that is truly one's own prevails in the end. The Jain idea of an innate knowledge, uncovered rather than acquired, offers a parallel to that 'true knowledge' (unmai arivu).
Sikhism
“You may read and read loads of books; you may read and study vast multitudes of books.” … “O Nanak, only one thing is of any account: everything else is useless babbling and idle talk in ego.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 467, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak says that no quantity of reading counts in the end; only one thing, the Lord's Name and praise, is of account, and the rest is ego talking.
The Kural says that however many subtle books one studies, only the understanding truly one's own prevails; Guru Nanak likewise ranks bookish learning below the one thing that really counts.
Greek Mythology
In Aeschylus’ Prometheus Bound, Prometheus, the giver of all human arts, admits that craft is far weaker than Necessity, and says Necessity is steered by the three Fates and the Furies.
— Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 514-518 [10]
Even the god of forethought and skill cannot out-think what is fated.
Learning and skill, however subtle, are weaker than the portion fate assigns, which is the Kural’s point.
Philosophy
Heraclitus’ fragment ‘ēthos anthrōpōi daimōn’ is usually rendered ‘character is a person’s destiny’.
— Heraclitus, fragment DK 22 B119 [11]
A person’s inner character, not outside instruction, is what shapes their fate.
Both put a person’s inner, given nature above what is learned from outside.

Real Life Example

Ramanujan writes to G. H. Hardy
16 January 1913 · Madras (Chennai), India, and Trinity College, Cambridge, England
Srinivasa Ramanujan, who had no university degree, taught himself higher mathematics largely from G. S. Carr's Synopsis, a dated book that listed thousands of results with only the briefest proofs, and then went far beyond it with his own discoveries. On 16 January 1913, while working as a clerk in Madras, he wrote to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, enclosing around 120 theorems. Hardy, though puzzled by the absence of conventional proofs, recognised his genius and arranged for him to come to Cambridge, where Ramanujan was later elected a Fellow of Trinity College.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ramanujan's mathematics came from a powerful insight of his own that no textbook had given him, and it astonished scholars trained in far more learning.
The kural says that however many subtle books a person studies, it is the understanding that is truly their own that prevails, and Ramanujan's original genius, nourished by a single old book, outran what formal learning could teach.
