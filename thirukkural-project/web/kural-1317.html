Kural 1317

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

வழுத்தினாள் தும்மினேன் ஆக அழித்தழுதாள் யாருள்ளித் தும்மினீர் என்று.
Vazhuththinaal thumminen aaka azhiththazhudhaal yaarullith thummineer endru

Meaning

When I sneezed, she first wished me a long life. Then she took it back and cried, asking, "Who was thinking of you, that you sneezed?"

Elementary

Some people say that when you sneeze, someone somewhere is thinking about you. Here the wife first says ‘Bless you!’, then suddenly cries, ‘Wait, who was thinking about you?’

University

The couplet turns on a second Tamil belief about sneezing: that one sneezes when someone is thinking of (uḷḷi) one. She first blesses him (vaḻuttiṉāḷ), as custom requires, then cancels it (aḻittu) and weeps (aḻutāḷ), asking yār uḷḷit tummiṉīr (‘thinking of whom did you sneeze?’, or ‘at whose thought’). Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar gloss the blessing as the wish of ‘a hundred years’ (nūṟāṇṭu). Pope: ‘She hailed me when I sneezed one day; but straight with anger seized, she cried; “Who was the woman, pray, thinking of whom you sneezed?”’ The text matches on ytamizh.com (which omits the closing full stop) and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Bhagavad Gita traces how dwelling on something breeds attachment, attachment breeds desire, thwarted desire breeds anger, and anger clouds judgement and memory.
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62-63 (paraphrase) [3]
The Gita describes how quickly a loving mind can slide into anger and confusion.
The wife’s swift slide from blessing to suspicious tears is a small, comic instance of that chain.
Christianity
“Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not so to be.”
— James 3:10 (KJV) [4]
James notes how the same mouth can bless and then turn to reproach.
The wife does just that: her mouth blesses her husband and, a moment later, accuses him.
Islam
“O you who have believed, avoid much [negative] assumption. Indeed, some assumption is sin.”
— Qur’an 49:12 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns against building suspicion on mere guesses about others.
The wife builds a whole rival out of one sneeze, the kind of guesswork the verse warns against, though in playful form.
Judaism
“Joshua ben Perahiah used to say: appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion and judge all men with the scale weighted in his favor.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:6 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah teaches giving others the benefit of the doubt.
The wife does the reverse in jest, weighting the scale against her husband over a simple sneeze.
Buddhism
“Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.”
— Dhammapada 1 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that what we experience is shaped first by the mind that meets it.
A sneeze is just a sneeze; it is the wife’s loving, anxious mind that turns it into a sign of a rival.
Jainism
“Pleasant things (by themselves) do not cause indifference nor emotions (as anger, &c.); but by either hating or loving them, a man undergoes such a change through delusion.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.101 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching holds that our passions come not from things themselves but from the love or aversion we bring to them.
The sneeze itself is neutral; the wife’s love turns it, in an instant, from a cause for blessing into a cause for tears.
Sikhism
“Only idiots and fools worry about these omens and days.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 843, Raag Bilaaval (Vaar Sat), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das dismisses worry over omens and auspicious days as foolishness.
The Kural's wife reads a sneeze as a sign that another woman is thinking of her husband; Gurbani rejects reading meaning into such signs.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, Procris hears that her husband Cephalus, resting after the hunt, keeps calling to ‘Aura’; she takes the word for a woman’s name, though he is only calling to the cooling breeze, and her jealous mistake ends in tragedy.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.796-862 (paraphrase) [10]
A harmless word, misheard as the name of a rival, sets off a wife’s jealous grief.
The Kural’s wife does the same with a harmless sneeze, hearing in it the thoughts of another woman.
Philosophy
Spinoza describes ‘vacillation of mind’ as the state in which we are moved by contrary feelings, such as love and hatred, toward the same object at once.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Prop. 17 and Scholium (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, the mind can be pulled by opposite emotions toward one person in the same moment.
The wife’s blessing and her tears, toward the same husband in the same breath, are a lively picture of such vacillation.

Real Life Example

Murasaki Shikibu writes The Tale of Genji
c. 1008-1010 · Heian-kyo (Kyoto), Japan
Murasaki Shikibu, a lady serving at the Japanese imperial court, wrote The Tale of Genji in the early eleventh century; her own diary shows she was writing it in 1008, and she probably completed it about 1010. Most of its 54 chapters concern the loves of the handsome courtier Genji, and each of the women in his life is drawn with great sensitivity. It is often called the world's first novel.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The work is a landmark of world literature and a detailed record of the emotional lives, including the hurt feelings and suspicions, of lovers at the Heian court.
The kural's wife first blesses her husband and then weeps, wondering which other woman is thinking of him, and Murasaki's novel is the classic early portrait of women who must share the attention of one admired man.
