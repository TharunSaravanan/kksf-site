Kural 1201

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

உள்ளினும் தீராப் பெருமகிழ் செய்தலால் கள்ளினும் காமம் இனிது.
Ullinum theeraap perumakizh seydhalaal kallinum kaamam inidhu.

Meaning

Love is sweeter than strong drink. Drink makes you happy only while you drink it, but love gives great joy that never runs out, even when you only remember it.

Elementary

A sweet drink makes you happy only while you are sipping it. But think of a wonderful day with someone you love, and you smile all over again. That is why the poet says love is sweeter than any drink: even the memory of it makes you glad.

University

Chapter 121, ninaintavar pulampal (‘the lament of those who remember’), stands in the karpiyal (wedded love) sequence on separation, where the absent partner is sustained and tormented by memory. The opening couplet sets the chapter’s premise by comparison with kaḷ (toddy, palm wine): uḷḷiṉum, ‘even when merely thought of’, love yields tīrā perumakiḻ, a great joy that does not run out, whereas kaḷ delights only when consumed, as Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both gloss. Solomon Pappaiah, following the traditional assignment, hears the husband speaking of past union with his wife, while modern readers often extend it to both lovers. Pope renders: ‘From thought of her unfailing gladness springs, / Sweeter than palm-rice wine the joy love brings.’ The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the second canto of Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda, Radha, left alone in a bower after Krishna has wandered off with other cowherd girls, dwells in song on her memories of him, and her heart takes delight in recalling his play even as she grieves.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 2 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Barbara Stoler Miller) [3]
Jayadeva’s Radha shows how, in the bhakti love lyric, remembering the beloved is itself a source of rapture.
Radha’s memory-song illustrates the Kural’s claim that love delights even when it is only thought of.
Christianity
“We will remember thy love more than wine: the upright love thee.”
— Song of Solomon 1:4 (KJV) [4]
The bride of the Song of Solomon says that she and her companions will remember the beloved’s love more than wine.
Both texts set the remembrance of love above the pleasure of wine, the Kural’s exact comparison.
Islam
The Sufi poet Ibn al-Farid opens his Wine Ode by saying that, in remembrance of the Beloved, he and his companions drank a wine that made them drunk before ever the vine was created.
— Ibn al-Farid, al-Khamriyya (The Wine Ode), opening verse (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Arberry and R.A. Nicholson) [5]
In the Khamriyya, the intoxication of love is a wine that comes from remembering the Beloved, needing no cup.
Like the Kural, Ibn al-Farid makes love-remembered a better intoxicant than any drink, though his Beloved is divine.
Judaism
“How fair is thy love, my sister, my bride! How much better is thy love than wine!”
— Song of Songs 4:10 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover of the Song of Songs ranks his bride’s love above wine.
The Kural’s verdict that love is sweeter than toddy is the same judgment, made in a Tamil key.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada traces grief and fear to endearment and praises freedom from it.
Where the Kural calls remembered love an unfailing joy, the Buddha warns that such endearment is the very root of sorrow, a deliberate contrast.
Jainism
In the dialogue of the priest’s sons, the youths tell their father that pleasures bring a moment’s happiness but long and intense suffering, and so are a hindrance to liberation.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 14.13 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana treats every sensual joy, love included, as brief sweetness bought with long pain.
The Kural praises love for a joy that never runs out; Jain teaching answers that such joy is fleeting and binding, a candid contrast.
Sikhism
“I drink in the sublime essence of the Lord. With the taste of this essence, I have forgotten all other tastes.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 337, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir says the sweetness of the Lord's Name is so great that every other drink and pleasure fades beside it.
The Kural says love is sweeter than toddy, because its joy does not run out even when it is only remembered; Kabir likewise rates the taste of divine love above every intoxicant.
Greek Mythology
Sappho says that some call an army of horsemen or of ships the loveliest thing on the dark earth, but she says it is whatever one loves, and she recalls the absent Anactoria, whose lovely walk and shining face she would rather see than all the chariots of Lydia.
— Sappho, fragment 16 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Anne Carson) [10]
Sappho ranks the beloved above every rival splendour, and does so by remembering someone far away.
Like the Kural’s speaker, Sappho finds love, recalled in absence, sweeter than anything else the world offers.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that lovers take pleasure in talking, writing and composing about the absent beloved, because in remembering him they seem to perceive him, and that love begins when one delights not only in the beloved’s presence but in remembering him when absent.
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 1.11, 1370b (paraphrase; cf. tr. W. Rhys Roberts) [11]
For Aristotle, remembered love is a genuine pleasure and even the mark that love has begun.
This is the Kural’s point in analytic form: love gives joy even when it is only thought of.

Real Life Example

Mozart's travel letters to Constanze
April 1789 · Prague and Dresden, on the journey from Vienna to Berlin
In the spring of 1789, short of money, Mozart travelled to Prague, Dresden, Leipzig and Berlin hoping to earn from concerts and court appearances. Along the way he wrote affectionate letters to his wife Constanze; from Prague on 10 April he called her his dearest, best little wife, sent kisses to her and their son Karl, and hoped to find a letter from her in Dresden. He carried a small portrait of her, and in a letter of 13 April he described greeting the portrait each day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
On a hard, uncertain business trip, the mere thought of his wife, kept alive by a portrait and letters, was clearly one of Mozart's chief pleasures.
The kural says love is sweeter than wine because it gives joy even when only remembered, and Mozart's playful letters show the delight he took simply in thinking of Constanze while far away.
