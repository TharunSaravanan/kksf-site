Kural 368

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

அவாஇல்லார்க் கில்லாகுந் துன்பம் அஃதுண்டேல் தவாஅது மேன்மேல் வரும்.
Avaa illaarkku illaagum thunbam aqdhundel Thavaaadhu menmel varum.

Meaning

People without desire have no sorrow. Wherever desire exists, sorrow keeps coming, one after another, without fail.

Elementary

Every time you want something you can’t have, you feel a little sad. If you want a hundred things, you get a hundred little sads. Want nothing, and the sads have nowhere to come from.

University

The couplet is a strict pair of conditions. For those without desire (avaa illaarkku), suffering ceases to exist (illaagum); if desire exists (aqdhundel), suffering comes thavaaadhu menmel, without fail, one upon another. The doubled menmel (“more and more, upon and upon”) imitates the way craving compounds, since each satisfied desire breeds new ones. This is the Kural’s closest approach to a formal statement of the Buddhist second noble truth, but Valluvar stays practical rather than metaphysical: he describes the lived economy of wanting, not a theory of dependent origination.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“While contemplating the objects of the senses, a person develops attachment for them, and from such attachment lust develops, and from lust anger arises. From anger, complete delusion arises, and from delusion bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, intelligence is lost, and when intelligence is lost one falls down again into the material pool.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.62-63 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna traces a chain reaction: attachment breeds desire, desire breeds anger, and each stage brings a further downfall.
Both show desire setting off an unending cascade of suffering, each trouble bringing the next.
Christianity
“But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows.”
— 1 Timothy 6:9-10 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that craving riches leads into snares and “many sorrows”.
This matches the Kural closely: where desire is, sorrows pile up.
Islam
“So as for he who transgressed And preferred the life of the world, Then indeed, Hellfire will be [his] refuge.”
— Qur’an 79:37-39 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that the one who prefers worldly desires ends in suffering.
Both link giving in to worldly desire with inevitable suffering. The Qur’an places that suffering in the hereafter, the Kural in this life.
Judaism
“He that is greedy of gain troubleth his own house; but he that hateth gifts shall live.”
— Proverbs 15:27 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that greed brings trouble on one’s own household, while the one who rejects bribes will live.
Both say that desire itself produces trouble and that freedom from it brings life.
Buddhism
“Just as a tree, though cut down, sprouts up again if its roots remain uncut and firm, even so, until the craving that lies dormant is rooted out, suffering springs up again and again.”
— Dhammapada 338 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that as long as the root of craving remains, suffering keeps growing back.
This is almost identical to the Kural: where craving remains, suffering comes “again and again” (menmel).
Jainism
“Thus the objects of the senses and of the mind cause pain to passionate men, but they never in the least cause any pain to the dispassionate.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.100 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
After going through each of the senses in turn, the lecture concludes that suffering comes not from the objects themselves but from passionate attachment to them. The dispassionate meet the same objects and feel no pain.
Valluvar says those without desire have no sorrow, while for those with desire sorrow comes without fail. The Jain verse states the same two-sided rule: pain for the passionate, none for the dispassionate.
Sikhism
“The fire of desire is deep within; unsatisfied, people remain hungry and thirsty.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 138, Raag Maajh (Vaar Maajh, salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Angad says that desire is a fire burning inside, and that those who carry it stay hungry and thirsty however much they get.
The Kural says that where desire exists sorrow comes without fail, one upon another; Guru Angad describes the same endless hunger and thirst that desire brings.
Greek Mythology
In the underworld, Tantalus stands in a pool beneath fruit-laden trees. Whenever he stoops to drink the water drains away, and whenever he reaches for fruit the wind whirls the branches out of reach, so his torment never ends.
— Homer, Odyssey 11.582-592 (paraphrase) [9]
Tantalus suffers forever because his desire is endless and never satisfied.
He embodies the second half of the Kural: where desire remains, suffering keeps coming without end.
Philosophy
Socrates tells a parable in which the craving part of a foolish soul is like a leaky jar or sieve. It must be filled again and again and is never satisfied, so its owner lives in constant distress, unlike the orderly person whose jars are sound and full.
— Plato, Gorgias 493a-494a (paraphrase) [10]
Plato argues that a life of ever-renewed desire is a life of ceaseless labor and pain.
This mirrors the Kural’s claim that where desire exists, suffering keeps coming, while the desire-free are at rest.

Real Life Example

Ludwig II of Bavaria's building debts and deposition
1869-1886 · Neuschwanstein, Linderhof and Herrenchiemsee, Bavaria, Germany
King Ludwig II of Bavaria poured vast sums into fantastic palaces, including Linderhof, Herrenchiemsee, modelled on Versailles, and the fairy-tale castle of Neuschwanstein. Although heavily in debt, he kept on building, and from 1885 foreign banks threatened to seize his property. In June 1886 the Bavarian government had him declared unfit to rule and deposed him, a step not provided for in the Bavarian constitution, and his uncle Luitpold became regent.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Each new project led to more debt and more conflict with his ministers, until the king lost his throne and his castles were opened to paying visitors.
The kural says that where desire exists, sorrow follows without fail, one upon another, and Ludwig's unending building brought a chain of debts, crises and finally the loss of his crown.
