Kural 385

The Greatness of a King · Iraimaatchi · இறைமாட்சி

இயற்றலும் ஈட்டலுங் காத்தலும் காத்த வகுத்தலும் வல்ல தரசு.
Iyatralum eettalung kaaththalum kaaththa Vaguththalum valla tharasu.

Meaning

A good ruler knows how to create sources of money, collect it, keep it safe, and then share out what was kept safe in a wise way.

Elementary

Think of a farmer who plants seeds, harvests the crop, stores it safely in a barn, and then shares it out so everyone has food all year. A good ruler does the same with the country’s wealth: make it, collect it, protect it, and share it fairly.

University

This is the Kural’s compact theory of public finance, expressed as four verbs: iyatral (generating new sources of revenue), eettal (collecting), kaaththal (guarding), and vaguththal (apportioning or allocating). The sequence is cyclical — well-allocated spending produces the prosperity from which new revenue is generated — and it treats wealth as a means of governance rather than a royal possession. Commentators note that vaguththal implies planned allocation among competing ends (defence, welfare, religious and civic works), not merely generosity. The same logic appears in Kalidasa’s praise of King Dilipa, who taxed his people only as the sun draws up water, in order to return it a thousandfold as rain.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa says King Dilipa collected taxes from his subjects only for their own welfare, just as the sun draws up moisture only to return it a thousandfold as rain.
— Kalidasa, Raghuvamsha 1.18 [3]
A famous Sanskrit poem compares a good king to the sun, which takes water from the earth only to give it back as much more rain.
Captures the Kural’s full cycle: gathering wealth (eettal) only in order to distribute it wisely (vaguththal).
Christianity
“And the Lord said, Who then is that faithful and wise steward, whom his lord shall make ruler over his household, to give them their portion of meat in due season?”
— Luke 12:42 (KJV) [4]
Jesus praises the wise manager who distributes food to the household at the right time.
Matches the guarding (kaaththal) and timely distribution (vaguththal) of resources.
Islam
“[Joseph] said, ‘Appoint me over the storehouses of the land. Indeed, I will be a knowing guardian.’”
— Qur’an 12:55 (Sahih International) [5]
Prophet Joseph asks the king of Egypt to put him in charge of the nation’s storehouses, promising to guard them wisely.
‘Knowing guardian’ of the storehouses is precisely the Kural’s kaaththal (safeguarding) joined to wise management.
Judaism
Joseph advises Pharaoh to collect a fifth of the harvest during seven years of plenty, store it in the cities under guard, and distribute it during the seven years of famine (Genesis 41:34-36, 41:47-57).
— Genesis 41 (KJV) [4]
Joseph saves Egypt by collecting grain in good years, keeping it safe, and handing it out during the famine.
Enacts all of the Kural’s stages — gathering, storing, guarding, and distributing wealth.
Buddhism
In the Kutadanta Sutta, a king troubled by banditry is advised not to raise taxes or punish, but to give seed and fodder to farmers, capital to traders, and wages to officials; the country then prospers and the treasury fills.
— Digha Nikaya 5 (Kutadanta Sutta) [6]
The Buddha tells of a king who fixed crime and poverty by investing in farmers, merchants, and workers, which made the whole kingdom richer.
Illustrates iyatral — generating new sources of wealth — and wise allocation (vaguththal).
Jainism
“If there were numberless mountains of gold and silver, as big as Kailâsa, they would not satisfy a greedy man; for his avidity is boundless like space.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.46–48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
When Indra tells King Nami to multiply his gold, jewels and treasury 'then you will be a true Kshattriya', Nami answers that no amount of wealth can satisfy craving. For laypeople, Jainism directs wealth through the vow of limiting possessions (parigraha-parimana) rather than through endless accumulation.
Valluvar sets out how a government should create, collect, guard and distribute wealth. The Jain text, taking the renunciant's view, warns about the greed that a full treasury can feed, which is a counterweight to this kural, not a parallel.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang (Saarang ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak says the true path is to live by honest labour and to share part of what one earns, the basis of the Sikh ideals of kirat karni and vand chhakna.
The Kural's theory of government runs from producing and gathering wealth to distributing it wisely; Guru Nanak gives the same cycle of earning and sharing at the level of the individual householder.
Greek Mythology
King Midas wishes that everything he touches turn to gold, and nearly starves because his wealth cannot be used; he begs Dionysus to undo the gift.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 [9]
A king who only wanted to pile up gold found that wealth that cannot be used is worthless.
A cautionary contrast: acquisition without right use fails, whereas the Kural demands that gathered wealth be distributed.
Philosophy
The Aristotelian Oeconomica holds that the manager of a household or estate must be able to acquire wealth, to preserve it, and to make proper use of it.
— Pseudo-Aristotle, Oeconomica I.6 (1344b) [10]
An ancient Greek treatise on management says good managers must know how to earn, keep, and use wealth.
Nearly the same sequence of competences as the Kural’s generate-gather-guard-distribute.

Real Life Example

The Inca state's network of storehouses
15th-early 16th century · Andes (present-day Peru and neighbouring countries)
The Inca Empire built large numbers of state storehouses, called qollqa or colcas, along its road network and near administrative centres. Surplus food, cloth, raw materials and other goods produced through labour owed to the state were stored in them and recorded by officials using knotted-string quipu. The stores supplied armies and state workers and were used to support communities when harvests failed.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The storehouse system turned the empire's production into a reserve that could be protected and redistributed where it was needed. It helped the empire survive poor harvests and sustain its armies across great distances.
Valluvar describes good government as creating wealth, collecting it, guarding it and distributing it wisely, and the Inca storehouses carried out each of these four tasks.
