Kural 287

The Absence of Fraud · Kallaamai · கள்ளாமை

களவென்னும் காரறி வாண்மை அளவென்னும் ஆற்றல் புரிந்தார்கண் இல்.
Kalavennum kaarari vaanmai alavennum, Aatral purindhaarkan il.

Meaning

Stealing is a dark, sneaky skill. People who have chosen the strength of living within proper limits do not have it.

Elementary

Some people are clever in a sneaky, shadowy way. But a person who has learned to be happy with a fair share has a brighter kind of strength, and the sneaky cleverness simply has no room to live in them.

University

Kār aṟivu — literally “black knowledge” — is Valluvar’s striking name for fraud: an intelligence that is real but darkened, directed at deception rather than understanding. He opposes to it aḷavu eṉṉum āṟṟal, “the power called measure,” so that restraint is framed not as weakness but as a form of strength. The contrast anticipates Aristotle’s distinction between practical wisdom and mere cleverness (deinotēs), which can serve bad ends. Textual note: some online editions print the last word-group as புரிந்தார்கண்ட இல்; the reading புரிந்தார்கண் இல் given here follows Tamil Surangam and thirukkural-thamizh.org and fits the metre.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Among all modes of purification, purity in (the acquisition of) wealth is declared to be the best; for he is pure who gains wealth with clean hands, not he who purifies himself with earth and water.”
— Manusmriti 5.106 (tr. G. Bühler) [5]
Manu ranks honesty in acquiring wealth above every kind of ritual purity.
The person of ‘measure’ in the Kural is the one with clean hands in money matters; dark cleverness has no place there.
Christianity
“But godliness with contentment is great gain.”
— 1 Timothy 6:6 (KJV) [6]
Paul says true profit is godliness joined to contentment, not accumulation.
Contentment is the ‘strength of measure’; it removes the motive for the dark cleverness of fraud.
Islam
“And give full measure when you measure, and weigh with an even [i.e., honest] balance. That is the best [way] and best in result.”
— Qur’an 17:35 (Sahih International) [7]
The Qur’an commands honest measure and calls it both morally best and best in its outcome.
Affirms, like the Kural, that honest measure is a strength rather than a loss.
Judaism
“Who is rich? He who rejoices in his lot”
— Pirkei Avot 4:1 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [8]
Ben Zoma redefines wealth as contentment with one’s portion.
The contented person has the ‘strength of measure’ and so no use for fraud’s dark cleverness.
Buddhism
“Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth.”
— Dhammapada 204 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [9]
The Dhammapada ranks contentment above every material possession.
Contentment is the Kural’s aḷavu; where it is present, the urge to defraud does not arise.
Jainism
“A Nirgrantha who has taken possession of some ground, should always take possession of a limited part of it and for a fixed time. The Kevalin says: If a Nirgrantha who has taken possession of some ground, should take possession of an unlimited part of it and for an unfixed time, he might take what is not given.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.15, third great vow, third clause (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884, p. 206) [10]
One of the five clauses that protect the vow against stealing requires the monk to keep a strict limit on what he occupies and for how long. Going beyond that limit is treated as sliding into theft.
Valluvar sets 'measure' (aḷavu) against the dark cleverness of fraud. The Jain clause makes the same link institutional: keeping to a fixed measure is what keeps one from taking what is not given.
Sikhism
“Hundreds of thousands of clever tricks, but not even one of them will go along with you in the end.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Pauri 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [11]
In the first pauri of Japji, Guru Nanak dismisses cleverness (siaanap) as a way to reach truth. Only walking in the Hukam, God's order, removes the wall of falsehood.
The Kural calls fraud a 'dark cleverness' that is absent in those who have chosen the strength of measure. Japji likewise sets worldly cunning against the true discipline it can never replace.
Greek Mythology
In the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, the newborn god uses his cunning to steal Apollo’s cattle and lie about it. When Zeus orders a reconciliation, Hermes gives Apollo the lyre, receives honours over herds in exchange, and swears he will never steal from Apollo again.
— Homeric Hymn 4, To Hermes [12]
The trickster’s dark cleverness is turned into lawful exchange once he accepts a fixed and agreed share.
Shows the Kural’s point from the other side: fraudulent cunning ends once someone accepts measure (an agreed, fair portion).
Philosophy
Epictetus tells his students to behave in life as at a banquet: when a dish is passed to you, reach out and take a moderate share; if it passes you by, do not grab after it; if it has not yet reached you, do not send your desire ahead of it.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 15 [13]
The Stoic ideal is a disciplined measure in taking, grounded in accepting one’s portion.
The Kural’s aḷavu is the banquet-guest’s moderation, which leaves no room for grabbing by stealth.

Real Life Example

Ishida Baigan teaches honest dealing to Kyoto's merchants
1729 onward · Kyoto, Japan
Ishida Baigan, a farmer's son who had worked as an apprentice in a Kyoto merchant house, began giving public lectures in his home in 1729, founding the Shingaku (heart learning) movement. He taught that merchants' fairly earned profit was legitimate, that a true merchant must benefit his customer as well as himself, and that honesty and frugality were linked virtues. After his death, disciples spread his teaching to schools across Japan.
— Sources: [14] [15]
Baigan gave Japan's merchant class an ethic that treated honest restraint, not clever deception, as the source of a merchant's strength and standing.
The kural says the dark cleverness of fraud is not found in those who have chosen the strength of measure, and Baigan taught merchants to choose exactly that measured honesty.
