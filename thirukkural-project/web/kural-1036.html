Kural 1036

Agriculture · Uzhavu · உழவு

உழவினார் கைம்மடங்கின் இல்லை விழைவதூஉம் விட்டேம்என் பார்க்கும் நிலை.
Uzhavinaar kaimmadangin illai vizhaivadhooum vittaemen paarkkum nilai.

Meaning

If farmers stop working, even people who say, 'We have given up all our desires,' cannot keep living their way of life.

Elementary

Even people who have given up toys, money and fancy things to live a simple, holy life still need a bowl of rice each day. If farmers stopped working, those holy people would go hungry too. So everyone, even a saint, depends on the farmer.

University

uzhavinaar kai madangin (உழவினார் கை மடங்கின்) means 'if the ploughmen's hands fold (stop working)'. Then illai ... nilai (இல்லை ... நிலை): there is no standing even for those who say vizhaivadhooum vittaem (விழைவதூஉம் விட்டேம், 'we have renounced even what is desired'), that is, the ascetics (thuRavigaL). Puliyur Kesikan, in Parimelazhagar's line, takes vizhaivadhu as the food everyone desires and reads nilai as the renunciant's steadiness in his dharma; Solomon Pappaiah agrees, and Kalaignar says even renouncers must look to the farmer's hand. The couplet links back to the Aram section's chapter on ascetics (Neeththaar Perumai, ch. 3) and to rain as the support of austerity (Kural 19), placing the household economy beneath the renunciant. The Tamil text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; the alapedai lengthening -ooum is printed in both.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As all living creatures subsist by receiving support from air, even so (the members of) all orders subsist by receiving support from the householder.”
— Manusmṛti 3.77 (tr. Georg Bühler, SBE 25, sacred-texts.com) [3]
Manu teaches that students, hermits and ascetics all live on the support of the householder, whose order is therefore the most excellent.
If the householder-farmer's hands stop, the renunciant orders fail, which is exactly the Kural's claim.
Christianity
“he that ploweth should plow in hope; and that he that thresheth in hope should be partaker of his hope. If we have sown unto you spiritual things, is it a great thing if we shall reap your carnal things?”
— 1 Corinthians 9:10-11 (KJV) [4]
Paul argues that those who serve spiritually may rightly be fed by the material harvest of those they serve.
Spiritual workers live on others' ploughing and threshing, the dependence of renouncers on farmers that the Kural names.
Islam
“There were two brothers during the time of the Messenger of Allah (s.a.w). One of them used to come to the prophet (s.a.w), and the other had some business. The businessman among them complained to the Prophet (s.a.w) about his brother, so he said: ‘Perhaps you are provided for because of him.’”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2345 (sunnah.com; graded Sahih by Darussalam) [5]
When the working brother complains about supporting his devout brother, the Prophet suggests that his provision may come for the sake of that devotion.
The hadith shows the same pairing of a worker supporting a devotee that the Kural describes, though it stresses the devotee's spiritual worth.
Judaism
“Where there is no bread, there is no Torah; where there is no Torah, there is no bread.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:17 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah teaches that sacred study cannot survive without material sustenance, and the reverse.
Without bread, the life of study and holiness cannot stand, which is the Kural's point about renunciants and farmers.
Buddhism
“I, contemplative, plow & sow. Having plowed & sown, I eat. You, too, contemplative, should plow & sow. Having plowed & sown, you (will) eat.”
— Kasi Bharadvaja Sutta, Sn 1.4 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, accesstoinsight.org) [7]
A brahman farmer challenges the Buddha, who has come for alms, to plough before he eats. The Buddha replies that his own 'ploughing' is faith, austerity and discernment, which bears the deathless as its fruit.
The sutta stages the Kural's question of how renunciants live off farmers, and the Buddha answers that they repay the farmer with a spiritual harvest.
Jainism
“I am a chaste Sramana, controlling myself; I have no property, nothing belonging to me, and do not cook my food; I have come for food which is dressed for somebody else at the time when I call. … know that I subsist by begging; let the mendicant get what is left of the rest.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.9-10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Jain monk Harikesa explains that a renunciant owns nothing and does not cook, and lives only on food that householders have prepared for themselves.
The Jain mendicant depends entirely on householders' food, so if the farmers' hands folded, his discipline could not continue, as the Kural says.
Sikhism
“Then Baba (Nanak) returned to Kartarpur where he put aside his attire of a Recluse.”
— Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 1, Pauri 38, English translation as given in the BaniDB database [9]
Bhai Gurdas records that after his long travels Guru Nanak settled at Kartarpur and laid aside the dress of an udasi (renunciant), living thereafter as a householder among a working community. Bhai Gurdas's Vaaran are early and highly respected exegesis, not scripture.
The Kural warns that even those who claim to have renounced all desire cannot survive if the farmers stop work; Guru Nanak's own choice to put off the renunciant's garb and live by settled labour expresses the Sikh preference for the working householder over the dependent ascetic.
Greek Mythology
“In the fields the oxen drew many a curved plough in vain, and much white barley was cast upon the land without avail. So she would have destroyed the whole race of man with cruel famine and have robbed them who dwell on Olympus of their glorious right of gifts and sacrifices”
— Homeric Hymn to Demeter 305-312 (tr. H.G. Evelyn-White, sacred-texts.com) [10]
When grieving Demeter halts the harvest, humanity faces famine and even the gods lose the offerings they live by.
When the plough fails, even those above ordinary life lose their support, which is the Kural's point about renunciants.
Philosophy
“such operations as the breaking up of fallow with the plough, the sowing of seed, the planting of trees, the pasturing and herding of flocks, are one and all open-air employments on which the supply of products necessary to life depends.”
— Xenophon, Oeconomicus ch. 7 (tr. H.G. Dakyns, Project Gutenberg) [11]
Xenophon's Ischomachus names ploughing and sowing as the outdoor work on which the whole household's necessities depend.
Every life, whatever its station, depends on the ploughing hand, and the Kural extends this even to renunciants.

Real Life Example

The Bardoli farmers withhold their revenue and the government gives way
1928 · Bardoli, Gujarat, India
In 1928 the peasants of Bardoli taluka in Gujarat, led by Vallabhbhai Patel, refused to pay a sharply increased land-revenue assessment and held out in a disciplined, nonviolent campaign despite seizures of land and property. The campaign ended in success for the farmers, and Patel's leadership earned him the title Sardar.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The campaign showed how much those in authority depend on the cultivators, and it made Patel a national leader.
The kural says that if farmers fold their hands even those who have renounced everything cannot stand, and Bardoli showed a government that could not stand firm once its farmers held back.
