Kural 187

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

பகச்சொல்லிக் கேளிர்ப் பிரிப்பர் நகச்சொல்லி நட்பாடல் தேற்றா தவர்.
Pagachchollik kelirp pirippar nagachcholli, Natpaadal thetraa thavar.

Meaning

People who do not know how to make friends through warm, friendly talk will say things that split people apart, even their own close friends.

Elementary

Some people know how to make friends by saying kind, fun things. Others only know how to say “Did you hear what she said about you?” — and that makes friends fight. This kural says people who can’t make friends with nice words end up breaking friendships with nasty ones.

University

The verse is built on the chiastic pair paka-c-colli (“speaking so as to split”) and naka-c-colli (“speaking so as to make [people] laugh/rejoice”). Valluvar diagnoses divisive speech as a failure of social skill as much as of morality: those who cannot tēṟṟu (be sure of, master) the art of naṭpāṭal, friendship-making through genial conversation, fall back on the easier currency of tale-bearing. The object kēḷir means kin or intimates, so the damage falls on the speaker’s own circle — Parimelazhagar notes they alienate even their friends from themselves. This connects the chapter to the book’s later treatment of friendship (naṭpu, chapters 79–83) and recalls the Buddhist category of pisuṇā vācā, speech that splits people apart. The couplet implies a positive programme: cultivate the speech that unites, and the temptation to divide loses its hold.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, the maid Manthara whispers to Queen Kaikeyi that Rama’s coronation will ruin her and her son Bharata, turning a queen who loved Rama into the cause of his exile.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda 7–9 (paraphrase) [3]
Manthara’s poisonous tale-bearing breaks apart a royal family that had been united and loving.
Manthara is the Kural’s divider: her words split kin who had no quarrel until she spoke.
Christianity
“A froward man soweth strife: and a whisperer separateth chief friends.”
— Proverbs 16:28 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs observes that a whisperer — a gossip — breaks up even the closest friends.
Nearly identical to the Kural: divisive whispering separates intimates.
Islam
“And do not obey every worthless habitual swearer [And] scorner, going about with malicious gossip -”
— Qur’an 68:10–11 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an warns against following the one who goes about spreading namīm — tale-bearing that sows discord.
Both texts point to the gossip who circulates tales between people as someone to reject.
Judaism
“What is different and notable about a leper, that the Torah states: ‘He shall dwell alone; outside of the camp shall be his dwelling’? He replied: By speaking malicious speech he separated between husband and wife and between one person and another”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 16b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
The Talmud explains that the slanderer is made to live alone because his words had separated spouses and friends.
Both identify the slanderer as one who splits apart people who belong together — and who ends up alone.
Buddhism
“What he has heard here he tells there to break those people apart from these people here. What he has heard there he tells here to break these people apart from those people there.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 10.176, Cunda Kammaraputta Sutta (Thanissaro) [7]
The Buddha defines divisive speech (pisuṇā vācā) as carrying tales back and forth to break people apart.
This is exactly the Kural’s ‘speaking to split’ — the canonical Buddhist description of it.
Jainism
“A monk or a nun, well considering, should not use speech whether truth or untruth, or truth mixed with untruth, if it be sinful, blamable, rough, stinging, coarse, hard, leading to sins, to discord and factions, to grief and outrage, to destruction of living beings.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.4.1.6 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
The Ācārāṅga forbids any speech, even true speech, that leads to discord and factions or causes grief.
Valluvar condemns words that split people apart and drive away even close friends, and the Ācārāṅga bans speech that leads to discord and factions even when what it says is true.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, speaking insipid words, the body and mind become insipid.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa di Vaar Guru Nanak teaches that harsh, graceless speech dries up the speaker himself, body and mind.
The Kural says those who cannot win friends with pleasant talk fall into divisive speech that drives off even their own; Guru Nanak likewise warns that harsh speech leaves the speaker sour and estranged.
Greek Mythology
“But abhorred Strife bare painful Toil and Forgetfulness and Famine and tearful Sorrows, Fightings also, Battles, Murders, Manslaughters, Quarrels, Lying Words, Disputes, Lawlessness and Ruin”
— Hesiod, Theogony 226–232 (Evelyn-White) [10]
In Hesiod’s family tree of the gods, Eris (Strife) gives birth to Lying Words and Disputes along with violence.
Myth places divisive, lying speech in the family of Strife; the Kural shows that kind of speech tearing friends apart.
Philosophy
“Many are the friendships that have been cut short, many the households set by the ears, because slander has found ready credence.”
— Lucian, Slander, a Warning §1 (Fowler) [11]
Lucian opens his essay on slander by listing the friendships and families it has destroyed.
Lucian and Valluvar both describe slander as the force that tears apart friends and kin.

Real Life Example

Rousseau breaks with David Hume
1766 · Wootton, Staffordshire, England, and Paris, France
In 1766 the philosopher David Hume brought Jean-Jacques Rousseau, then facing persecution on the Continent, to England and found him a refuge at Wootton in Staffordshire. Rousseau became convinced, wrongly, that Hume was at the centre of a plot to mock and dishonour him, and spread accounts of Hume's bad faith in letters that circulated in Paris. Hume answered by publishing their correspondence as A Concise and Genuine Account of the Dispute, and the friendship ended in a public quarrel.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Suspicion and accusatory talk turned a protector into an enemy and made the quarrel the talk of literary Europe.
The kural says those who cannot build friendship through warm, cheerful words fall back on words that divide and drive away even their own friends, and Rousseau's accusations cost him his greatest benefactor.
