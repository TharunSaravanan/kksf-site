Kural 1017

Shame · Naanudaimai · நாணுடைமை

நாணால் உயிரைத் துறப்பர் உயிர்ப்பொருட்டால் நாண்துறவார் நாணாள் பவர்.
Naanaal uyiraith thurappar uyirpporuttaal naanthuravaar naanaal pavar.

Meaning

People who live by shame will give up their life to keep it, but they will never give up shame to save their life.

Elementary

Some heroes in stories are asked to do something really wrong, like betray a friend, or else they'll be hurt. And they say no, even though it's scary. Valluvar says people with real shame would rather lose everything than do something shameful.

University

The kural is built as a chiasmus. Naanaal uyiraith thurappar (நாணால் உயிரைத் துறப்பர், 'for shame's sake they renounce life') is mirrored by uyir poruttaal naan thuravaar (உயிர்ப்பொருட்டால் நாண்துறவார், 'for life's sake they do not renounce shame'). Naan aalpavar (நாணாள்பவர், 'those who wield or rule by shame') are its subject. Kesikan frames it as a dilemma: when shame and life cannot both be kept, the noble let life go. Kalaignar substitutes maanam (honour), linking the kural to ch. 97 (Maanam), where similar sentiments appear (e.g. kural 969 on the yak that dies on losing its hair). Pope: ‘The men of modest soul for shame would life an offering make.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for a respectable person, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that for an honoured man, disgrace is worse than dying.
This is the Kural's ranking exactly: losing one's shame or honour is worse than losing one's life.
Christianity
“And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell.”
— Matthew 10:28 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his disciples to value faithfulness above bodily survival.
Like the Kural, it ranks bodily life below the inner good one must never surrender.
Islam
“He said, ‘My Lord, prison is more to my liking than that to which they invite me. And if You do not avert from me their plan, I might incline toward them and [thus] be of the ignorant.’”
— Qur'an 12:33 (Saheeh International) [5]
Pressed to give in to seduction or face prison, Joseph (Yusuf) chooses the dungeon over a shameful act.
Yusuf would give up his freedom rather than his modesty, the Kural's choice made concrete.
Judaism
The Talmud rules that for three sins, idol worship, forbidden sexual relations and bloodshed, a person must let himself be killed rather than transgress (yehareg ve'al ya'avor).
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 74a [6]
Rabbinic law sets certain boundaries that may not be crossed even to save one's life.
This is the Kural's principle as law: life is given up before the deepest integrity is.
Buddhism
The Buddha tells his monks that even if bandits were to saw them limb from limb with a two-handled saw, anyone who let his mind turn to hatred would not be following his teaching.
— Majjhima Nikaya 21, Kakacupama Sutta (The Simile of the Saw) [7]
The Saw Simile demands that a monk keep his inner discipline even at the cost of a terrible death.
Like the Kural, it ranks keeping one's moral self above keeping one's body alive.
Jainism
“Fie upon you, famous knight, who want to quaff the vomited drink for the sake of this life; it would be better for you to die.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.42 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45); the same verse appears in Dasavaikalika Sutra 2 [8]
The nun Rajimati rebukes the monk Rathanemi when he tempts her to break their vows, telling him death would be better than such shamelessness. Shamed, he returns to the Law.
Rajimati states the Kural's principle outright: better to die than to give up shame for the sake of this life.
Sikhism
“those who merely live shall depart in dishonor; everything they eat is impure.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 142, Raag Majh, Majh ki Vaar (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Majh ki Vaar, Guru Nanak says that a life that goes on after its honour (pat) is lost is worthless, and everything it consumes is tainted.
The Kural says those who live by shame will give up life rather than shame; Guru Nanak says that life kept at the cost of honour is not worth living.
Greek Mythology
After Troy falls, the captive princess Polyxena is led to be sacrificed at Achilles' tomb. She refuses to be held and goes freely to her death rather than live as a slave, and even as she falls she takes care to fall modestly.
— Euripides, Hecuba 342-378, 546-570 [10]
Euripides presents Polyxena as choosing an honourable death over a shameful life and keeping her modesty to the end.
Polyxena is the Kural's naan-aalpavar: she gives up life for shame's sake and never gives up shame for life's sake.
Philosophy
At his trial Socrates says that a man worth anything should not weigh the chances of living or dying. He should ask only whether he is acting rightly or wrongly. Achilles chose death rather than disgrace.
— Plato, Apology 28b-d (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates refuses to save his life by giving up philosophy or begging shamefully.
His stance is the Kural's: life may be given up, integrity may not.

Real Life Example

Giordano Bruno refuses to recant
February 1600 · Rome, Papal States (Italy)
After years of imprisonment and trial by the Roman Inquisition, the philosopher Giordano Bruno declared that he had nothing to retract. Pope Clement VIII ordered him sentenced as an impenitent heretic; the sentence was read to him on 8 February 1600, and he was executed in the Campo de' Fiori on 17 February 1600.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Bruno is remembered as a person who gave up his life rather than disown the convictions he held, and a statue was raised to him at the place of his execution in 1889.
The kural says those who live by shame will give up life for its sake but never give it up to save life, and Bruno would not recant even to escape death.
