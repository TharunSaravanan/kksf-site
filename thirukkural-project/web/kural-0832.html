Kural 832

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

பேதைமையுள் எல்லாம் பேதைமை காதன்மை கையல்ல தன்கட் செயல்.
Pedhaimaiyul ellaam pedhaimai kaadhanmai kaiyalla thankat seyal.

Meaning

The worst foolishness of all is wanting to do things you should not do.

Elementary

A goldfish might dream of climbing trees like a squirrel, but if it jumps out of its bowl to try, it will only get hurt. The silliest mistake is wanting badly to do things that are not right for who you are.

University

The couplet ranks follies and puts at the top kaadhanmai (காதன்மை, fond desire, love) for kaiyalla (கையல்ல, what is not within one’s proper hand or conduct). Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar take kaiyalla as acts unsuited to one’s own moral conduct (ஒழுக்கம்), which matches G. U. Pope’s ‘deeds which to your station unbefitting prove’ and Drew’s ‘delight in doing what is forbidden’. Kalaignar instead reads it as deeds beyond one’s ability, Solomon Pappaiah as desiring things that do one no good, and Puliyur Kesigan as both choosing an undesirable act and doing it unfittingly; the line thus spans a moral reading (forbidden) and a prudential one (beyond one’s scope). The wording is the same in ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“It is far better to discharge one’s prescribed duties, even though faultily, than another’s duties perfectly. Destruction in the course of performing one’s own duty is better than engaging in another’s duties, for to follow another’s path is dangerous.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.35 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna warns Arjuna against taking up a role that is not his own, however attractive it seems.
The Kural similarly calls it the height of folly to love deeds that do not belong to one’s own path.
Christianity
“But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed. Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death.”
— James 1:14-15 (KJV) [4]
James traces wrongdoing back to a desire that pulls a person toward what he should not do.
Valluvar likewise locates the worst folly in desire (kaadhanmai) fixed on unfitting deeds.
Islam
“And do not walk upon the earth exultantly. Indeed, you will never tear the earth [apart], and you will never reach the mountains in height.”
— Qur’an 17:37 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an rebukes the person who strides about as though he could do what is far beyond his measure.
Both warn against reaching for what lies outside one’s rightful scope.
Judaism
“LORD, my heart is not haughty, nor mine eyes lofty: neither do I exercise myself in great matters, or in things too high for me.”
— Psalm 131:1 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
The psalmist claims a quiet humility that does not grasp at matters beyond his reach.
It describes the wise opposite of the Kural’s fool, who sets his heart on what does not befit him.
Buddhism
“The fool seeks undeserved reputation, precedence among monks, authority over monasteries, and honor among householders.”
— Dhammapada 73 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada’s chapter on the fool describes him as hungry for positions and honours that do not fit him.
Valluvar’s fool, too, sets his heart on what is not properly his.
Jainism
“The presumptuously saying, We are those who will do what should not be done, and the deferring and putting aside that which ought to be done at once, verily these two things will cause affliction instantly, even to ascetics, who have renounced the pleasures of the domestic state.”
— Nālaṭiyār, ch. 24 ('Improper Friendship'), st. 5 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Nālaṭiyār names two ruinous habits: boldly taking up what should not be done, and putting off what should be done at once, and it says they bring trouble even to renunciants.
Valluvar calls the greatest folly a fond desire for deeds unfitting for oneself; the Nālaṭiyār likewise puts eagerness to do 'what should not be done' first among the habits that bring swift affliction.
Sikhism
“Like the monkey with a handful of grain, who will not let go because of greed - just so, all the deeds committed in greed ultimately become a noose around one’s neck.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 336, Raag Gauri, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Kabir uses the monkey trapped because it will not let go of a fistful of grain to show how deeds done from greed tighten into a noose.
The Kural calls it the worst folly to set one's heart on unfitting deeds; Kabir's monkey clinging to what traps it shows that attachment.
Greek Mythology
Bellerophon, after his victories, tried to ride the winged horse Pegasus up to the dwelling of the gods and was thrown back down to earth.
— Pindar, Isthmian 7.44-47 [9]
Pindar uses the story to warn that reaching for what belongs to the gods ends in a fall.
It pictures the Kural’s chief folly: desiring a deed that is not one’s own to do.
Philosophy
In the Republic, Socrates defines justice as each person doing his own work and not meddling in what belongs to another.
— Plato, Republic IV, 433a-b [10]
Plato treats staying within one’s proper task as the basis of a well-ordered soul and city.
Valluvar likewise counts it the worst folly to love deeds that are not one’s own.

Real Life Example

Muhammad ibn Tughluq moves his capital to Daulatabad
1327 · Delhi and Daulatabad (Deogiri), Maharashtra, India
Sultan Muhammad ibn Tughluq of Delhi moved his capital to Deogiri in the western Deccan, renamed Daulatabad, in order to strengthen his hold on the south, and large numbers of people from Delhi were moved there. Soon after he was established in the Deccan, rebellions broke out in the north, on the western frontier and in Bengal, and he had to return to Delhi. His transfer of the capital and his introduction of a token currency are both counted among his ambitious schemes that failed and caused great hardship.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The sultan's projects were bold, but they were poorly executed and beyond what his government could carry out, and they stirred opposition across his realm.
The kural calls it the worst folly to set one's heart on deeds that are not fitting for oneself, read by some commentators as deeds beyond one's ability, and the forced move of the capital was such an undertaking.
