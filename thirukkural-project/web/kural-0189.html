Kural 189

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

அறன்நோக்கி ஆற்றுங்கொல் வையம் புறன்நோக்கிப் புன்சொல் உரைப்பான் பொறை.
Arannokki aatrungol vaiyam purannokkip, Punsol uraippaan porai.

Meaning

The earth still carries the person who waits until someone is gone and then says mean things about them. Is that only because the earth cares about being good?

Elementary

The ground holds up everyone who walks on it — good people and mean people. This kural jokes that the Earth must be very patient and kind to keep carrying a gossip who waits for people to leave so he can say nasty things about them.

University

The couplet is one of Valluvar’s sharpest ironies. Vaiyam, the earth, is imagined as a moral agent who bears (āṟṟu) the poṟai (burden, weight) of the slanderer, and the poet wonders whether it does so only aṟaṉ nōkki — out of regard for dharma, since bearing all beings is its duty. The mirrored phrases aṟaṉ nōkki and puṟaṉ nōkki are carefully placed: the earth looks toward virtue, while the slanderer looks toward the back, watching for his victim’s absence before he speaks puṉ col, base words. The image draws on the Indian idea of the earth as the patient bearer of all beings, whose forbearance (poṟai also means patience) contrasts with the slanderer’s impatience to wound. Valluvar thus makes the slanderer a dead weight on the world, tolerated only by a patience he does not deserve.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, the Earth (Bhūmi), weighed down by the burden of wicked kings, takes the form of a cow and goes weeping to Brahmā to ask for relief.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.1 (paraphrase) [3]
Hindu myth pictures the Earth as a patient mother who carries the weight of the wicked until it becomes too much.
The Kural uses the same image: the earth patiently bearing the slanderer’s weight, only out of dharma.
Christianity
“for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”
— Matthew 5:45 (KJV) [4]
Jesus points to God’s patience, which sustains even the wicked, as the model for loving enemies.
Both texts marvel at a forbearance that keeps supporting even those who don’t deserve it.
Islam
“And if Allah were to impose blame on the people for what they have earned, He would not leave upon the earth any creature. But He defers them for a specified term.”
— Qur’an 35:45 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an says only God’s forbearance keeps wrongdoers on the earth at all.
As in the Kural, the wrongdoer stays on the earth only because of a patience he has not earned.
Judaism
“He and I cannot dwell together in the world.”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 15b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
Rav Hisda, citing Mar Ukva, says God declares this about anyone who speaks lashon hara, reading Psalm 101:5.
The Talmud and the Kural are equally astonished that the world has room for the slanderer at all.
Buddhism
“Just as when people throw what is clean or unclean on the earth — feces, urine, saliva, pus, or blood — the earth is not horrified, humiliated, or disgusted by it”
— Majjhima Nikaya 62, Maha-Rahulovada Sutta (Thanissaro) [7]
The Buddha tells Rahula to make his mind like the earth, which accepts whatever is thrown on it without disgust.
Both use the earth as the model of boundless forbearance; the Kural ironically applies it to carrying the slanderer.
Jainism
“Though life cannot be prolonged, as the saying is, still foolish people sin recklessly; a foolish man is filled to the brim (as it were) with sins. Considering this a sage is not conceited.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.2.2.21 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga pictures the reckless fool as a vessel filled to the brim with sin, a heavy load of karma he has made for himself.
Valluvar wonders whether the earth bears the slanderer's weight only out of duty, and the Jain verse likewise pictures the reckless sinner as loaded to the brim with sin.
Sikhism
“The mythical bull is Dharma, the son of compassion; this is what patiently holds the earth in its place.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib, Pauri 16, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Guru Nanak reinterprets the myth of the bull that carries the earth: what really holds the world up is dharma, born of compassion.
The Kural wonders whether the earth bears the weight of the backbiter only out of regard for dharma; Japji says it is dharma itself that patiently holds the earth up.
Greek Mythology
“the gods by means of Helen's loveliness embroiled Troy and Hellas, causing death thereby, that they might lighten mother Earth of the outrage done her by the increase of man's number.”
— Euripides, Orestes (Apollo’s closing speech) (E. P. Coleridge) [10]
Apollo explains that the Trojan War was sent to relieve Mother Earth of the weight of too many mortals.
Greek myth, like the Kural, imagines the earth as burdened by humans who weigh on her.
Philosophy
“falling upon that earth out of which my father collected the seed … which bears me when I tread on it and abuse it for so many purposes.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.4 (George Long) [11]
Marcus Aurelius reflects with gratitude on the earth that patiently bears him even as he misuses it.
Both texts marvel at the earth’s endurance of those who abuse it.

Real Life Example

Pietro Aretino, the scourge of princes
1527-1556 · Venice, Republic of Venice (now Italy)
The writer Pietro Aretino settled in Venice in 1527 after making many enemies in Rome. His satirical attacks on the powerful often amounted to blackmail, and he called himself the scourge of princes. Kings and nobles who feared his satire or wanted his praise sent him gifts, and he grew wealthy and lived in style in Venice until his death in 1556.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Aretino prospered for decades by trading on what he might say about people, a puzzle that his contemporaries and later historians have noted.
The kural wonders whether the earth bears the weight of the man who speaks base words of others only out of its own patience, and Aretino lived comfortably for decades on such words.
