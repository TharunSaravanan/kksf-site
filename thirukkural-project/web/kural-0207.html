Kural 207

Dread of Evil Deeds · Theevinaiyaccham · தீவினையச்சம்

எனைப்பகை யுற்றாரும் உய்வர் வினைப்பகை வீயாது பின்சென்று அடும்.
Enaippakai yutraarum uyvar vinaippakai, Veeyaadhu pinsendru adum.

Meaning

People can escape even the worst enemies. But the enemy made by their own bad deeds never goes away. It follows them and destroys them.

Elementary

You can hide from a bully, run away, or make friends again. But the bad things you did are like a shadow-monster that follows you everywhere. You can’t hide from it or run away from it, because it comes from you.

University

The contrast is between பகை (enmity) from others and வினைப்பகை (‘the enmity of deeds’) — Valluvar’s striking personification of karma as a relentless personal foe. Human enemies can be escaped, appeased or outlived (உய்வர், ‘they survive’); the enemy that is one’s own past action ‘does not perish’ (வீயாது), ‘goes behind’ (பின்சென்று) and ‘kills’ (அடும்). The image of pursuit is shared with the Greek Erinyes, who hound Orestes across the world, and with the Mahabharata’s calf that unfailingly finds its mother among a thousand cows. The couplet thus answers a common hope of wrongdoers — that they might outrun consequences.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As the young calf is able to recognise its dam from among a thousand cows, so does the previous acts of a man pursue him (in all his different transformations).”
— Mahabharata, Anusasana Parva, Section VII (Ganguli) [4]
Bhishma teaches Yudhishthira that past deeds track down their doer as unerringly as a calf finds its mother.
Same image of deeds pursuing their doer without fail.
Christianity
“But if ye will not do so, behold, ye have sinned against the LORD: and be sure your sin will find you out.”
— Numbers 32:23 (KJV) [5]
Moses warns the tribes that a broken commitment will not stay hidden: the sin itself will track them down.
Sin as a pursuer that will find the sinner — the Kural’s ‘enmity of deeds’.
Islam
“Paradise is not [obtained] by your wishful thinking nor by that of the People of the Scripture. Whoever does a wrong will be recompensed for it, and he will not find besides Allah a protector or a helper.”
— Qur’an 4:123 (Sahih International) [6]
No identity or wishful hope shields a wrongdoer from the consequences of his wrong.
Like the Kural, denies any escape from the recompense of one’s deeds.
Judaism
“Moreover he saw a skull floating on the face of the water. He said to it: because you drowned others, they drowned you. And in the end, they that drowned you will be drowned.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:6 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
Hillel reads a floating skull as proof that violent deeds pursue their doers through a chain of consequences.
Deeds track down those who commit them, generation after generation.
Buddhism
“Neither in the sky nor in mid-ocean, nor by entering into mountain clefts, nowhere in the world is there a place where one may escape from the results of evil deeds.”
— Dhammapada 127 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha lists every conceivable hiding place and declares none of them safe from the fruit of evil kamma.
Exactly the Kural’s claim: one may escape anything except one’s own evil deeds.
Jainism
“As the burglar caught in the breach of the wall perishes by the work the sinner himself had executed, thus people in this life and the next cannot escape the effect of their own actions.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [9]
The burglar is crushed in the very hole he dug. In the same way, the Uttarādhyayana says no one escapes the results of his own deeds, in this life or the next.
Valluvar says other enmities can be survived but the enmity of one's own evil deeds never dies; the Jain verse makes the same point with a man destroyed by his own handiwork.
Sikhism
“Whatever you yourself plant, that is what you shall have to eat; nothing else can be said about this.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 755, Raag Soohee, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In a hymn in Raag Soohee, Guru Amar Das states that a person must eat the fruit of his own planting, and that there is no arguing with this law.
The Kural says one may survive any human enmity, but the enmity of one's own evil deeds never dies; Guru Amar Das likewise presents the return of one's deeds as a law from which there is no escape.
Greek Mythology
After killing his mother Clytemnestra, Orestes is hunted across Greece by the Erinyes (Furies), who track him by the scent of blood and will not relent until his case is judged at Athens.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides (paraphrase) [11]
The Furies are the Greek personification of guilt’s relentless pursuit of a wrongdoer.
A living image of Valluvar’s ‘enmity of deeds’ that follows and destroys.
Philosophy
“The difficulty, my friends, is not in avoiding death, but in avoiding unrighteousness; for that runs faster than death.”
— Plato, Apology 39a–b (Jowett) [12]
Condemned, Socrates says death — the slower runner — has caught him, but unrighteousness — the faster — has overtaken his accusers.
Wrongdoing as a swift pursuer that overtakes its doers — close to the Kural’s image.

Real Life Example

Adolf Eichmann is found in Argentina and brought to trial
11 May 1960 (capture); 1961 (trial) · Buenos Aires, Argentina; Jerusalem, Israel
Adolf Eichmann, the SS officer who had organised the deportation of Jews to the Nazi camps, escaped justice after the Second World War and was living in Argentina under the name Ricardo Klement. Israeli agents seized him near his home in Buenos Aires on 11 May 1960 and flew him to Israel. His trial in Jerusalem began on 11 April 1961, he was found guilty on all counts in December 1961, and he was executed in 1962.
— Sources: [13] [14]
Fifteen years of hiding on another continent did not end the consequences of what Eichmann had done.
The kural says people may escape any human enmity, but the enmity of their own evil deeds never dies and follows them to destroy them, and Eichmann's past pursued him across the world.
