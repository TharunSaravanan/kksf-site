Kural 183

Slander Avoidance · Purangkooraamai · புறங்கூறாமை

புறங்கூறிப் பொய்த்துயிர் வாழ்தலின் சாதல் அறங்கூற்றும் ஆக்கத் தரும்.
Purangoorip poiththuyir vaazhdhalin saadhal, Arangootrum aakkath tharum.

Meaning

It is better to die than to live by saying bad things behind people's backs and being fake to their faces. Dying gives the real gain that the teachings of goodness speak of.

Elementary

Imagine you could stay popular only by telling mean secrets about your friends and pretending to be nice to them. This kural says it’s better to lose everything than to live that way — being a good, honest person matters more than anything you could win by gossip.

University

The couplet raises the stakes from comparison of vices to a comparison between a way of life and death itself. Puṟaṅkūṟip poyttu uyir vāḻtal — “living by backbiting and falsehood” — names a whole livelihood, the courtier’s or social climber’s art of advancing by undermining others. Against this Valluvar sets cātal, death, which paradoxically yields ākkam (prosperity, gain) of the kind that aṟam-kūṟṟu, the pronouncements of the moral treatises, recognise. Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasan gloss the death as dying in poverty for having refused such conduct — a reading that turns the verse into a choice between material survival bought by slander and moral gain secured by refusing it. The structure echoes a theme found across classical ethics: that there are lives not worth living, and that moral ruin is a worse fate than death.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for a respectable person, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 (As It Is) [3]
Krishna warns Arjuna that for an honourable person, living on in disgrace is worse than dying.
Both say a dishonourable life is worse than death; the Kural names backbiting as one such dishonourable life.
Christianity
“Backbiters, haters of God, despiteful, proud, boasters … they which commit such things are worthy of death”
— Romans 1:30, 32 (KJV) [4]
Paul lists backbiters among those whose deeds, by God’s judgment, deserve death.
Paul names backbiting specifically and links it to death; the Kural too weighs a backbiter’s life against death.
Islam
“one of them never saved himself from being soiled with his urine while the other used to go about with calumnies (to make enmity between friends).”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 216 [5]
Passing two graves, the Prophet revealed that one man was being punished after death for spreading tales (namīmah) that set friends against each other.
The hadith shows that a life lived by slander carries its cost beyond death — the Kural’s point that such a life is not worth its gains.
Judaism
“Third speech, i.e., malicious speech about a third party, kills three people. It kills the one who speaks malicious speech, and the one who accepts the malicious speech when he hears it, and the one about whom the malicious speech is said.”
— Talmud Bavli, Arakhin 15b (Koren–Steinsaltz) [6]
The Sages of the Land of Israel taught that backbiting is lethal — even to the one who speaks it.
If slander ‘kills’ the slanderer, then, as the Kural says, living by it is already a kind of death, worse than an honest one.
Buddhism
“Easy is life for the shameless one who is impudent as a crow, is backbiting and forward, arrogant and corrupt.”
— Dhammapada 244 (Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada notes that the backbiter’s life is easy, while the next verse says the modest, pure life is hard.
Both texts admit slander can make life easier — and both say that easy life is the wrong one to choose.
Jainism
“Indifferent and pure with regard to every kind of control, a Śramaṇa should walk about; he who entertains pure thoughts during his whole life, dies as a worthy and wise man.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.2.2.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga holds that a life kept pure in thought ends in a worthy death, so the quality of a death depends on how the life was lived.
Valluvar says death is better than a life kept going by backbiting and falsehood, and the Jain verse likewise judges a life by whether it can end in a pure and worthy death.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, no one else is truly alive; those who merely live shall depart in dishonor”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 142, Raag Maajh (Vaar Maajh, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok of Vaar Maajh, Guru Nanak teaches that only one whose mind holds the Lord is truly alive, and that a life which loses honour is not worth having.
The Kural says it is better to die than to stay alive by backbiting and falsehood; Guru Nanak likewise rates a dishonoured life as no true life at all.
Greek Mythology
Ajax, shamed after his madness, tells his wife Tecmessa that a man of noble birth must either live with honour or die with honour, and then takes his own life rather than live disgraced.
— Sophocles, Ajax 479–480 (paraphrase) [10]
Sophocles’ tragic hero decides that a dishonourable life is not worth keeping.
Ajax embodies the Kural’s ‘death is better than a base life,’ though his disgrace comes from madness, not slander.
Philosophy
“The difficulty, my friends, is not to avoid death, but to avoid unrighteousness; for that runs faster than death.”
— Plato, Apology 39a–b (Jowett) [11]
Condemned to die, Socrates tells the jury that wrongdoing is the real danger to flee, not death.
Socrates and Valluvar agree: death is a lesser evil than living by injustice such as slander and falsehood.

Real Life Example

Irena Sendler refuses to give the Gestapo any names
October 1943 · Warsaw, German-occupied Poland
Irena Sendler, a Warsaw social worker who headed the children's section of the underground Council to Aid Jews (Zegota), helped smuggle Jewish children out of the Warsaw Ghetto and place them in hiding. Arrested by the Gestapo in October 1943, she was interrogated under harsh mistreatment and pressed to give up the names of the organisation's leaders and the hidden children, but she never revealed the names of the children or her colleagues. She was sentenced to death, but Zegota bribed an official to release her, and she resumed her underground work.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Sendler could have bought her life by speaking against others, and she chose to face death instead.
The kural says it is better to die than to live by speaking against others, and Sendler accepted a death sentence rather than save herself by betraying the people who depended on her.
