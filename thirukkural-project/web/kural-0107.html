Kural 107

Gratitude Recognition · Seynnanri Arithal · செய்ந்நன்றி அறிதல்

எழுமை எழுபிறப்பும் உள்ளுவர் தங்கண் விழுமந் துடைத்தவர் நட்பு.
Ezhumai ezhupirappum ulluvar thangan Vizhuman thudaiththavar natpu.

Meaning

Good people remember, through all seven births (all their lives to come), the friendship of those who wiped away their sorrow.

Elementary

If someone wiped away your tears when you were really hurting, you should remember them for a very, very long time. The kural says: for seven whole lifetimes!

University

விழுமம் denotes grave affliction, and துடைத்தல், 'to wipe away', evokes the wiping of tears, an image that G. U. Pope's verse translation foregrounds. எழுமை எழுபிறப்பும் is variously parsed: traditional commentary reads it within the South Asian doctrine of rebirth as 'through sevenfold births', while modern commentators such as Kalaignar treat it as hyperbole meaning 'without any limit of time'. Either way, the couplet turns gratitude from a transaction into a form of memory (உள்ளுவர், 'they will keep in mind') whose duration exceeds a single life. The motif of long-lived or even inherited gratitude, such as David's kindness to Jonathan's son, Rama's avowed debt to Hanuman, and the Buddha's thought of the five companions after his awakening, shows how widely traditions have treated rescuers as creditors whose claim outlives the moment of rescue.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Uttara Kanda, as Hanuman takes leave, Rama tells him that he would give his life for each single service Hanuman has done and still remain in his debt, and that he wishes these favours to stay unrepaid within him, since a return is needed only when a benefactor falls into misfortune.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda (Rama's farewell to Hanuman; chapter numbering varies by edition, commonly 7.40) [3]
Rama tells Hanuman he can never finish repaying him, and even hopes he never gets the chance, because that would mean Hanuman was in trouble.
Rama's gratitude to the one who wiped away his sorrow is lifelong and open-ended, just like the Kural's 'seven births'.
Christianity
“I thank my God upon every remembrance of you,”
— Philippians 1:3 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells the church at Philippi, which had supported him from the start, that every time he remembers them he thanks God.
Gratitude that returns with every memory is the Kural's 'they will keep in mind', applied to those who helped in hardship.
Islam
“And lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy and say, “My Lord, have mercy upon them as they brought me up [when I was] small.”
— Qur'an 17:24 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an tells believers to be humble to their aged parents and to pray for them, remembering how they cared for them as little children.
Gratitude here reaches back across a whole lifetime to care received long ago, like the Kural's unending remembrance.
Judaism
“And David said, Is there yet any that is left of the house of Saul, that I may shew him kindness for Jonathan’s sake?”
— 2 Samuel 9:1 (KJV) [4]
After his friend Jonathan died, King David searched for any surviving relative so he could repay Jonathan's friendship, and found and cared for Jonathan's disabled son Mephibosheth.
David's gratitude to the friend who protected him outlived the friend himself, the kind of remembrance the Kural stretches across 'seven births'.
Buddhism
After his awakening, when the Buddha considered whom to teach first, he thought of his former teachers Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta (both had died), and then of the group of five monks who had attended him during his years of striving, recalling that they had been very helpful to him.
— Majjhima Nikaya 26, Ariyapariyesana Sutta [6]
The first people the newly enlightened Buddha wanted to share his discovery with were those who had helped him through his hardest years.
Even at the moment of enlightenment, the Buddha's mind turned first to those who had eased his hardships, as the Kural says the good always do.
Jainism
“Prince Pārśva pulled a log from the ascetic Kaṭha's fire and saved the serpent burning inside it; reborn as Dharaṇa, king of the Nāgas, the serpent came when Pārśva was later assailed in meditation, raised him on a lotus, sheltered him with seven hoods, and told the attacker, “I am the servant of the Compassionate.”
— Hemacandra, Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Book IX (Pārśvanāthacaritra), ch. III, part 9 'Attacks by Meghamālin' (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; wisdomlib.org) [7]
In Hemacandra's life of Pārśvanātha, a benefit received in one birth is remembered and repaid in the next, when the rescued serpent, now a Nāga king, shields his rescuer from Meghamālin's flood.
Valluvar says the good remember, through seven births, those who wiped away their distress, and Jain narrative gives exactly that: gratitude that survives death and rebirth.
Sikhism
“A hundred times a day, I am a sacrifice to my Guru; He made angels out of men, without delay.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 462, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Opening the Asa di Vaar, Guru Nanak expresses unending gratitude to the Guru who transforms ordinary people; the disciple's thankfulness is renewed again and again.
As the Kural's good remember through seven births the one who wiped away their sorrow, the Sikh offers ceaseless gratitude to the Guru who lifted him out of his lowly state.
Greek Mythology
As Odysseus leaves Phaeacia, the princess Nausicaa, who found him naked and shipwrecked on the shore and brought him to safety, asks him to remember her, since he owes her his life; Odysseus answers that if he reaches home he will pray to her as to a goddess all his days.
— Homer, Odyssey 8.457-468 [9]
The young princess who rescued Odysseus from his worst moment asks him not to forget her, and he promises to honour her for the rest of his life.
Lifelong remembrance of the one who ended your affliction is exactly the Kural's teaching.
Philosophy
Marcus Aurelius opens his Meditations with an entire book listing, person by person, what he owes to his grandfather, mother, teachers, friends, his adoptive father Antoninus, and the gods, naming the specific good each gave him.
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book 1 [10]
The Roman emperor began his private notebook by writing down everyone who had helped him become who he was, and exactly how.
Book 1 is a written act of the long remembrance the Kural prescribes: keeping benefactors in mind across a whole life.

Real Life Example

Turkey repays Japan for the Ertugrul rescue
16 September 1890; March 1985 · Kushimoto, Wakayama, Japan; Tehran, Iran
In September 1890 the Ottoman frigate Ertugrul, returning from a goodwill visit to Japan, broke up in a typhoon off the island of Kii Oshima near Kushimoto, and islanders worked through the night to rescue 69 survivors; local physicians later declined Turkey's offer to pay for their care. In March 1985, during the Iran-Iraq War, more than 200 Japanese were stranded in Tehran after Iraq warned it would target aircraft over Iran, and at Japan's request Turkey sent airliners that carried 215 Japanese nationals to safety. When Japan expressed its thanks, Turkish officials cited their nation's debt of gratitude from the Ertugrul rescue 95 years earlier.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A rescue by poor villagers was taught to generations of Turks and repaid almost a century later, when the original rescuers' countrymen were themselves in danger.
The kural says the good remember through all their births those who wiped away their sorrow; Turkey remembered Kushimoto's kindness across nearly a hundred years and repaid it.
