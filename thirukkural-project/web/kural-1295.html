Kural 1295

Expostulation with Oneself · Nenjodu Pulathal · நெஞ்சொடு புலத்தல்

பெறாஅமை அஞ்சும் பெறின்பிரிவு அஞ்சும் அறாஅ இடும்பைத்தென் நெஞ்சு.
Peraaamai anjum perinpirivu anjum araaa idumpaiththen nenju.

Meaning

When he is away, my heart fears it will not have him. When he is here, it fears he will leave. So my heart is never free from pain.

Elementary

Think of a child waiting for a favourite toy: before getting it, she worries she never will, and after getting it, she worries she will lose it. The wife’s heart is like that with her husband: worried when he is gone, and worried when he is home that he will go again.

University

The couplet is built on parallel clauses: peṟāamai añcum (‘it fears not having him’) and peṟiṉ pirivu añcum (‘having him, it fears separation’), leading to aṟāa iṭumpaittu eṉ neñcu, ‘my heart is (a thing) of unceasing distress’. Peṟāamai and aṟāa again show metrical vowel lengthening (aḷapeṭai). Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan agree on the reading; Solomon Pappaiah makes the setting explicit, ‘when my husband was away… when he has come’. Pope renders ‘I fear I shall not gain, I fear to lose him when I gain; / And thus my heart endures unceasing pain.’ Within the chapter this explains why the heart cannot afford to sulk: even in his presence it already fears his next departure. The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrihari observes that every worldly good comes with its own fear: in enjoyment there is fear of disease, in family pride fear of a fall, in wealth fear of kings, and so on, so that only detachment is free of fear.
— Bhartrihari, Vairagya Shataka, verse beginning bhoge roga-bhayam (paraphrase) [3]
The verse lists how each thing we hold brings the dread of losing it.
The wife’s heart lives this exactly: having her husband brings only the fear of losing him.
Christianity
“There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love.”
— 1 John 4:18 (KJV) [4]
John teaches that perfect love leaves no room for fear, because fear itself is a torment.
The wife’s heart is the opposite case: its love is full of fear, and so it lives in torment.
Islam
“In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you. And Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful -”
— Qur’an 57:23 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches calm both over what one lacks and over what one has.
The wife’s heart has neither calm: it frets over what it lacks and over what it might lose.
Judaism
“Hope deferred maketh the heart sick; but desire fulfilled is a tree of life.”
— Proverbs 13:12 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs observes that waiting for what one longs for wears the heart down.
The first half of the Kural, the heart afraid while he is away, is this heartsickness; the Kural adds that even fulfilment brings fear.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful. Therefore hold nothing dear, for separation from the dear is painful. There are no bonds for those who have nothing beloved or unloved.”
— Dhammapada 210-211 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that loving someone brings pain both in absence and in the threat of separation.
This names both halves of the wife’s distress: pain at not seeing him and dread of parting from him.
Jainism
“How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that attachment brings unhappiness at every stage, in getting, keeping and losing.
The wife’s heart suffers at every stage of her love, whether he is absent or present.
Sikhism
“Without Him, I cannot survive even for an instant, and my life-night passes in anguish.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 35, Siree Raag, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das voices the soul-bride's constant pain while she is apart from her Lord.
The Kural's heart is never free of fear, dreading his absence and then dreading his leaving; Guru Amar Das likewise describes a soul in unbroken anguish over separation.
Greek Mythology
Sappho calls Eros the loosener of limbs, a bittersweet creature against whom there is no defence.
— Sappho, fragment 130 (paraphrase; cf. tr. D.A. Campbell, Greek Lyric I) [10]
Sappho coined the idea of love as sweet and bitter at the same time.
The wife’s love is bittersweet in just this way: even its sweetness carries pain.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines hope and fear as unsteady pleasure and pain arising from an uncertain outcome, and adds that there is no hope unmingled with fear, and no fear unmingled with hope.
— Baruch Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Definitions of the Emotions XII-XIII (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, hope and fear always come together because both rest on uncertainty.
The wife’s heart never escapes fear because its joy in her husband is always shadowed by uncertainty about losing him.

Real Life Example

Mary Livingstone rejoins David Livingstone on the Zambezi
1852–1862 · Shupanga (Chupanga), on the Zambezi River, Mozambique
Mary Moffat married the missionary-explorer David Livingstone in 1845 and travelled with him on many journeys in southern Africa, until in 1852 he sent her and their four children back to Britain for their health and safety. She lived there in near poverty during his long years of exploration. Determined to return to Africa with him, she joined his Zambezi expedition in 1862 but caught malaria and died at Shupanga in April 1862.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Mary Livingstone suffered first from long separation and then, when reunited, lost her husband again within months through her own death.
The kural’s heart fears not having the beloved when he is away and fears parting when he is near, and Mary Livingstone’s story moved from years of separation to a reunion that was itself cut short.
