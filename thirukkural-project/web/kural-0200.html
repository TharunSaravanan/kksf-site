Kural 200

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

சொல்லுக சொல்லிற் பயனுடைய சொல்லற்க சொல்லிற் பயனிலாச் சொல்.
Solluka sollir payanudaiya sollarka, Sollir payanilaach sol.

Meaning

Of all the words you could say, say only the useful ones. Never say the ones that are useless.

Elementary

Before you speak, ask yourself one simple question: “Will this help?” If the answer is yes, say it. If the answer is no, don’t. That’s the whole rule, and this kural says it in the simplest way possible.

University

The chapter closes with one of the most famous sound-patterns in the Kural. Forms of சொல் (sol, ‘word/speak’) occur five times: சொல்லுக (‘speak’), சொல்லில் (‘among words’, twice), சொல்லற்க (‘do not speak’) and சொல் (‘word’). The only thing that changes between the positive and negative halves is பயனுடைய / பயனிலா (‘useful / useless’). The form enacts the content: a couplet made almost entirely of the word ‘word’ shows perfect economy, with nothing wasted. Solomon Pappaiah reads பயன் as the three classical aims of life (aram, porul, inbam), which ties the rule to the whole structure of the Kural. The maxim sums up the chapter as a single test that can be applied to every utterance, much like Epictetus’s ‘speak merely what is necessary’ and the hadith ‘speak good or keep quiet’.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Silence, it is said, is better than speech, if speak you must, then it is better to say the truth; if truth is to be said, it is better to say what is agreeable; and if what is agreeable is to be said, then it is better to say what is consistent with morality.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, section 36 (Vidura Niti) (trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Vidura, quoting the sage Atreya, sets out a graded ladder in which silence is the baseline and each kind of speech must improve on it.
In both texts, speech is only justified when it adds value that silence would not. The Kural makes benefit the test, and Vidura adds truth, agreeableness and dharma.
Christianity
“Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.”
— Ephesians 4:29 (KJV) [4]
Paul makes the hearer’s benefit (edification) the test of Christian speech.
Both texts give the same two-part rule: speak only what builds up and withhold everything else.
Islam
“Anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should talk what is good or keep quiet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6018 (trans. Muhsin Khan, sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet gives a simple binary test for all speech: say what is good (khayr) or stay silent.
The hadith and the Kural state the same two-part rule: speak the beneficial and never the rest.
Judaism
“One who engages in idle chatter … violates a positive commandment, as it is stated: And you shall talk of them; talk of them and not of other matters.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 19b (William Davidson / Koren-Steinsaltz trans., Sefaria) [6]
Rava reads Deuteronomy 6:7 (‘you shall talk of them’) as a command to fill speech with Torah and a prohibition of devarim beteilim (idle words).
Both texts give a positive command to speak what is valuable and a matching ban on idle words.
Buddhism
“Better than a thousand useless words is one useful word, hearing which one attains peace.”
— Dhammapada 100 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada measures words entirely by their usefulness.
Both texts rest on the same useful/useless contrast.
Jainism
“Well considering (what one is to say), speaking with precision, one should employ language in moderation and restraint.”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra II.4.1.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [8]
The Ācārāṅga's lecture on modes of speech requires that every word be thought over first and spoken precisely, sparingly and with restraint.
Valluvar closes the chapter with the rule to speak only useful words and never useless ones, and the Ācārāṅga sets out the same rule for monks: think first, then speak precisely, sparingly and with restraint.
Sikhism
“Those words are acceptable, which, when spoken, bring honor. Harsh words bring only grief. Listen, O foolish and ignorant mind!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 15, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn in Sri Raag, Guru Nanak sets two kinds of speech side by side: words that bring honour are approved, while fikaa words bring only ruin and grief.
The Kural closes the chapter by pairing a command to speak only useful words with a ban on useless ones; Guru Nanak builds the same two-sided rule, approving one kind of speech and rejecting the other.
Greek Mythology
“The best treasure a man can have is a sparing tongue, and the greatest pleasure, one that moves orderly; for if you speak evil, you yourself will soon be worse spoken of.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 719-721 (trans. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod’s farmer-poet counts a sparing, well-ordered tongue as a person’s best possession.
Both texts praise economy and order in speech as the governing rule.
Philosophy
“Be for the most part silent, or speak merely what is necessary, and in few words.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 33 (trans. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus gives the Stoic student a rule for speech: silence by default, with only necessary words.
Both texts lay down a rule of speech governed by necessity and usefulness.

Real Life Example

Winston Churchill's memorandum on Brevity
9 August 1940 · London, England, United Kingdom
In the middle of the Battle of Britain, Prime Minister Winston Churchill issued a War Cabinet memorandum titled Brevity, now held at the UK National Archives as CAB 67/8/11. He asked ministers and officials to make their reports much shorter and to set out the real point concisely. He argued that the time saved would be great and that the discipline of stating the real point briefly would help clearer thinking.
— Sources: [12] [13]
At a moment of national danger, Churchill treated wasted words as a real cost to the war effort.
The kural's rule is to speak only the words that are useful and never those that are useless, which is almost exactly what Churchill ordered his officials to do.
