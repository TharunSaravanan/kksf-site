Kural 624

Hopefulness in Trouble · Idukkan Azhiyaamai · இடுக்கண் அழியாமை

மடுத்தவா யெல்லாம் பகடன்னான் உற்ற இடுக்கண் இடர்ப்பாடு உடைத்து.
Matuththavaa yellaam pakatannaan utra Itukkan itarppaatu utaiththu.

Meaning

Some people are like an ox that keeps pulling a cart through every rut and muddy patch. The trouble that meets them is the one that ends up suffering.

Elementary

Picture a strong ox pulling a heavy cart down a muddy road. Every time a wheel gets stuck, the ox just leans in and pulls harder until it’s free. If you keep going like that ox, your problems get tired before you do.

University

Maṭuttavāy ellām means ‘in all the places where (the cart) sinks or is blocked’, from maṭu, a pit or deep hollow, and the image is completed by pakaṭu, a draught ox or bull that pulls the cart out without flinching; Pope’s explanation renders pakaṭu as ‘buffalo … through deep mire’, reflecting a traditional alternative gloss for the animal. The second line again personifies trouble: the iṭukkaṇ that such a person meets ‘itself suffers hardship’ (iṭarppāṭu uṭaittu). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both stress persistence without losing heart, while Kalaignar expands the image into success through unbroken effort, linking this couplet to the doctrine of ūkkam and āḷviṉai in chapters 60 and 62. The text is the same in both sources; ytamizh prints the sandhi form மடுத்தவா யெல்லாம் exactly as thirukkural.net does.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.48 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna asks Arjuna to keep doing his work steadily, unmoved by whether each step succeeds or fails.
The Kural’s ox that keeps pulling through every rut is a picture of the Gita’s steady, unattached worker.
Christianity
“No man, having put his hand to the plough, and looking back, is fit for the kingdom of God.”
— Luke 9:62 (KJV) [4]
Jesus uses the farmer’s plough to teach that once a task is begun, one must keep pressing forward without looking back.
Both texts borrow the image of plough and draught animal to praise forward-pressing perseverance.
Islam
“O mankind, indeed you are laboring toward your Lord with [great] exertion and will meet it.”
— Qur’an 84:6 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes human life as a hard, toiling journey (kadḥ) that nonetheless arrives at its destination.
Like the Kural’s ox straining through the mire, the Qur’an pictures life as exertion that pushes steadily to its goal.
Judaism
“It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you at liberty to neglect it;”
— Pirkei Avot 2:16 (tr. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Tarfon teaches that one must keep at a vast task even when its completion is out of sight.
The Kural’s ox does not ask how long the road is; it keeps pulling, just as Tarfon forbids setting the burden down.
Buddhism
“Delight in heedfulness! Guard well your thoughts! Draw yourself out of this bog of evil, even as an elephant draws himself out of the mud.”
— Dhammapada 327 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha uses the image of a great elephant heaving itself out of a swamp to describe determined spiritual effort.
The Kural’s ox dragging the cart out of the mire and the Dhammapada’s elephant pulling free of the mud are the same picture of strength against being stuck.
Jainism
“… he was free like a bird; he was always waking like the fabulous bird Bhâruṇḍa, valorous like an elephant, strong like a bull, difficult to attack like a lion, steady and firm like Mount Mandara, deep like the ocean … like the earth he patiently bore everything …”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §118 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
During his years as a wandering ascetic, the Kalpa Sutra describes Mahavira with a string of similes, among them the strength of a bull and the earth's patience in bearing everything.
The Kural's image of the ox that pulls the cart through every rut is echoed in the Jain portrait of Mahavira as strong like a bull and bearing everything like the earth.
Sikhism
“The mythical bull is Dharma, the son of compassion; this is what patiently holds the earth in its place.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib (pauri 16), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Japji Sahib, Guru Nanak takes the old myth of a bull bearing the earth and reinterprets it: the bull is dharma, born of compassion, which patiently bears the world's weight.
The Kural pictures the steadfast person as an ox hauling the cart through every rut and mire; Japji Sahib turns the bull that patiently carries the load into an image of dharma.
Greek Mythology
Stranded in the Libyan desert, the Argonauts lifted the Argo onto their shoulders and carried it overland for twelve days and nights across the sand until they reached Lake Tritonis and could sail again.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4 [10]
Instead of despairing when their ship could not float, the heroes became beasts of burden and hauled it through the obstacle.
Like the Kural’s ox dragging the cart through every rut, the Argonauts simply shoulder the load and haul it past the blockage.
Philosophy
Epictetus observes that when a lion attacks, it is the bull alone that knows its own strength and charges forward to protect the whole herd, using the bull as a model for the person who rises to hard circumstances.
— Epictetus, Discourses 1.2 [11]
For Epictetus, difficulty reveals the strong character that is already in us, as danger reveals the bull’s power.
The Kural too takes a bull as its model of strength that shows itself precisely when the road gets hard.

Real Life Example

The Overland Telegraph is pushed through Australia's wet season
1870-22 August 1872 · Port Augusta to Darwin, Australia
In 1870 South Australia began building a telegraph line of about 3,200 kilometres (some 2,000 miles) from Port Augusta to Darwin under Superintendent of Telegraphs Charles Todd. In the northern section the tropical wet season flooded creeks, halted the work teams and put the project months behind schedule, and Todd travelled north himself in early 1872 to push the work on. The line, with some 36,000 poles, was completed on 22 August 1872 and was soon linked to the undersea cable from Java, cutting communication time with Europe from months to hours.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Despite floods, spoiled supplies and a collapsed contract, the builders kept hauling the line forward, and Australia's isolation from the rest of the world ended.
The kural pictures an ox that drags the cart through every rut and mire until the trouble itself suffers, and the telegraph builders kept dragging their line through every flooded creek until the obstacles gave way.
