Kural 433

Error Correction · Kutrankaditthal · குற்றங்கடிதல்

தினைத்துணையாங் குற்றம் வரினும் பனைத்துணையாக் கொள்வர் பழிநாணு வார்.
Thinaiththunaiyaang kutram varinum panaiththunaiyaak Kolvar pazhinaanu vaar.

Meaning

People who are afraid of being blamed treat even a fault as small as a millet seed as if it were as big as a palmyra palm (a very tall tree).

Elementary

A millet seed is tiny, and a palm tree is huge. People who really care about doing right treat even a tiny mistake as if it were a giant one, so they fix it straight away instead of letting it grow.

University

The couplet turns on a vivid scale contrast between தினை (foxtail millet, among the smallest grains) and பனை (the palmyra palm, among the tallest trees). பழிநாணுவார், ‘those who are ashamed before blame’, are defined not by having no faults but by how they perceive them: they magnify their own errors in their own eyes. This is a moral psychology of vigilance. Shame (நாண்) works here as a protective faculty, the Tamil counterpart of Greek aidōs, that makes a small lapse feel intolerable before it can grow. The commentators divide on whether the sense is ‘they grieve over it as if it were huge’ (Puliyur Kesikan) or ‘they guard against it as if it were huge’ (Varadarasan, Karunanidhi); both readings share the principle of disproportionate self-scrutiny.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for a respectable person, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 [3]
Krishna reminds Arjuna that, for someone of honour, a disgraced name is harder to bear than death.
The Kural’s ‘those who dread disgrace’ are exactly the people for whom, as the Gita says, dishonour weighs more than death, which is why they treat even small faults as huge.
Christianity
“He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much: and he that is unjust in the least is unjust also in much.”
— Luke 16:10 (KJV) [4]
Jesus teaches that how someone handles very small things reveals how they will handle big ones.
Both texts refuse to treat small faults as trivial; the millet-sized fault matters because character is built on the least things.
Islam
“A believer sees his sins as if he were sitting under a mountain which, he is afraid, may fall on him; whereas the wicked person considers his sins as flies passing over his nose and he just drives them away like this.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6308 (saying of ‘Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud) [5]
Ibn Mas‘ud contrasts the believer, who feels even his sins as a mountain about to fall, with the wicked person, who brushes them off like flies.
Almost the same image as the Kural: the conscientious person sees a small fault as a mountain (palmyra) rather than as something tiny (millet, a fly).
Judaism
“And be careful with a light commandment as with a grave one, for you did know not the reward for the fulfillment of the commandments.”
— Pirkei Avot 2:1 [6]
Rabbi Judah the Prince teaches that small duties deserve as much care as big ones, since we cannot know their true weight.
The Kural’s ‘treat the small as great’ is the Mishnah’s ‘be as careful with the light as with the grave’.
Buddhism
“Think not lightly of evil, saying, 'It will not come to me.' Drop by drop is the water pot filled. Likewise, the fool, gathering it little by little, fills himself with evil.”
— Dhammapada 121 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha warns that small wrongdoings, repeated, fill a person with evil just as drops of water fill a pot.
Both say that no fault is small enough to ignore; the wise person, like the Kural’s shame-sensitive person, treats the drop as seriously as the flood.
Jainism
“One should not be complacent with a small debt, slight wound, spark of fire and slight passion, because what is small (today) may become bigger (later).”
— Saman Suttam 134 (English tr., jainworld.com) [8]
Jain teaching treats even a slight stirring of passion (kashāya) as dangerous, comparing it to a small debt, wound or spark that grows if neglected.
Those who dread disgrace, says Valluvar, treat a millet-sized fault as if it were as large as a palmyra; the Jain verse gives the same reason for refusing to belittle a small fault: what is small today grows large tomorrow.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, I am the worst of all. Everyone else is good.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1364, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir models a humility that looks hard at one's own failings and refuses to rank oneself above anyone else.
The Kural praises those who see a millet-sized fault in themselves as large as a palmyra; Kabir's self-judgement magnifies his own faults in the same spirit.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod describes how, in the degenerate Iron Age, Aidōs (Shame) and Nemesis (Righteous Indignation) will abandon humankind and depart to the gods, leaving no defence against evil.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 197-201 [10]
In Greek myth, Shame is a goddess, and when she leaves the human world, people lose what kept them from wrongdoing.
The Kural’s பழிநாணுவார் are those in whom Aidōs still lives: their sense of shame is the faculty that makes even small faults feel large.
Philosophy
“Shame should not be described as a virtue; for it is more like a feeling than a state of character. It is defined, at any rate, as a kind of fear of dishonour...”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.9 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle analyses shame as a fear of disgrace that restrains people, especially the young, from doing wrong.
The Kural’s ‘those who dread disgrace’ are defined by exactly this fear of dishonour, which drives them to magnify their own faults.

Real Life Example

Hugh Dalton resigns over a budget leak
12-13 November 1947 · Palace of Westminster, London, United Kingdom
On his way into the House of Commons to deliver his budget, Chancellor of the Exchequer Hugh Dalton stopped to talk with a lobby journalist and mentioned some of his tax plans. The journalist's evening paper printed them shortly before Dalton reached that part of his speech. Although the slip was brief and had little practical effect, Dalton apologised to the House and offered his resignation, which Prime Minister Clement Attlee accepted the next day.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Dalton treated a small indiscretion as a grave breach of his duty to Parliament and gave up one of the highest offices in government because of it.
The kural says those who dread disgrace treat a fault as small as a millet seed as if it were as tall as a palmyra palm, and Dalton resigned over a few leaked lines.
