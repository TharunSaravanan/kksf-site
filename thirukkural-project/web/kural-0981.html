Kural 981

Perfectness · Saandraanmai · சான்றாண்மை

கடன்என்ப நல்லவை எல்லாம் கடன்அறிந்து சான்றாண்மை மேற்கொள் பவர்க்கு.
Kadanenpa nallavai ellaam kadanarindhu saandraanmai merkol pavarkku.

Meaning

For people who know their duty and choose to live with perfect character, all good things are said to be simply part of their duty.

Elementary

Some children help clear the table only when someone asks and then want a sticker for it. Others just do it every day because that is who they are. Valluvar says truly good people are like the second kind: being kind and helpful is not an extra for them, it is just their everyday job.

University

The chapter title saandraanmai (சான்றாண்மை, from saandror, 'the full, the accomplished', and aanmai, 'mastery, the bearing of a quality') names the fullness of noble character; the related noun saalbu (சால்பு, 'abundance, completeness') recurs throughout the chapter. The opening couplet turns on kadan (கடன், 'debt, duty'): for one who knows his kadan and undertakes (merkol, மேற்கொள்) saandraanmai, all good things (nallavai ellaam) are themselves called duty. Mu. Varadarasanar glosses that for such a person all good things become 'natural duty' (இயல்பான கடமை), and Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan both render it as good qualities becoming one's very nature, the line of interpretation that follows the traditional commentary of Parimelazhagar, in which virtue is no longer a burden chosen case by case but an obligation already accepted. Pope's 'All goodly things are duties to the men, they say' keeps the reported-speech force of enpa (என்ப, 'they say'), marking this as received wisdom. Sivayogi Sivakumar on ytamizh reverses the predicate ('the obligatory duty is the good things'), a minor variation in emphasis rather than in sense. The chapter follows Perumai (Greatness, ch. 98) in the Kudiyiyal and is followed by Panbudaimai (Courtesy, ch. 100). The Tamil text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Therefore, without being attached to the fruits of activities, one should act as a matter of duty, for by working without attachment one attains the Supreme.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.19 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is, A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda) [3]
Krishna teaches that right action should be done simply because it is one's duty, not for what it brings.
The Kural likewise describes the perfected person as one for whom doing good is simply what is owed, not a bargain.
Christianity
“So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.”
— Luke 17:10 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his disciples that even after doing everything commanded, they should regard it as no more than their duty.
Like the Kural, it treats all good deeds as simply what is owed, leaving no room for self-congratulation.
Islam
“Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct and giving to relatives and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression. He admonishes you that perhaps you will be reminded.”
— Qur'an 16:90 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an presents justice, good conduct and generosity as standing obligations upon the believer.
As in the Kural, the whole range of good conduct is framed as something owed, not optional.
Judaism
“Ben Azzai said: Be quick in performing a minor commandment as in the case of a major one, and flee from transgression; For one commandment leads to another commandment, and transgression leads to another transgression; For the reward for performing a commandment is another commandment and the reward for committing a transgression is a transgression.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:2 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Ben Azzai teaches that each good deed done as duty draws the next one after it, so that goodness becomes a way of life.
This is how, in the Kural's words, all good things come to be the natural duty of one who has taken up the noble life.
Buddhism
“As from a great heap of flowers many garlands can be made, even so should many good deeds be done by one born a mortal.”
— Dhammapada 53 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha urges that a human life be used to do as many good deeds as possible, as a garland-maker uses every flower.
Like the Kural, it treats the doing of all good things as what a human being owes.
Jainism
“Having adopted (the law), one should not hide it, nor forsake it.”
— Acaranga Sutra I.4.1.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
The Acaranga teaches that once the law of non-harm has been taken up, one must hold to it openly and never abandon it.
The Kural's noble person likewise 'takes up' perfection (merkol) and then treats every good as a binding duty.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Sarang, Vaar Sarang, Salok Mahala 1, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak sums up the Sikh way of life in two ordinary habits, kirat karni (living by honest work) and vand chhakna (sharing what one earns). The person who does both as a matter of course is the one who knows the Path.
The Kural says that for those who know what is due from them every good act is simply duty done as a matter of course, and Guru Nanak likewise finds the true path in goodness made into an everyday habit.
Greek Mythology
In Prodicus' fable of the Choice of Heracles, the young hero at a crossroads is approached by Vice, who offers an easy life of pleasure, and by Virtue, who offers a hard path of labour and duty; Heracles chooses Virtue.
— Prodicus' Choice of Heracles, as retold in Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34 [10]
The story presents the noble life as something a person deliberately chooses and then lives out through toil.
Heracles' choice mirrors the Kural's person who knowingly takes up perfection and accepts all good things as duty.
Philosophy
“For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that moral virtues are formed by habit, so that doing good repeatedly makes goodness part of one's character.
This explains how, for the Kural's noble person, all good things become natural duty once perfection has been taken up.

Real Life Example

Aristides de Sousa Mendes issues visas to refugees in Bordeaux
June 1940 · Bordeaux, France
In June 1940, as German forces advanced through France, Portugal's consul general in Bordeaux, Aristides de Sousa Mendes, defied his government's instructions restricting visas and began issuing transit visas to refugees, many of them Jews, giving them free to those who could not pay. Helped by two of his sons, he worked for days with little rest, and Yad Vashem records 1,575 visas issued between 15 and 22 June alone. Recalled to Lisbon, he was dismissed from the diplomatic service and died in poverty in 1954; Yad Vashem recognised him as Righteous Among the Nations in 1966.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Sousa Mendes treated helping desperate strangers as something his conscience simply required of him, even though it cost him his career and his family's security.
The kural says that for those of perfected character every good deed is simply a duty owed, and Sousa Mendes acted on that duty as a matter of course rather than as a calculated sacrifice.
