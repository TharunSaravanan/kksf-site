Kural 74

The Possession of Love · Anbudaimai · அன்புடைமை

அன்புஈனும் ஆர்வம் உடைமை அதுஈனும் நண்பு என்னும் நாடாச் சிறப்பு.
Anbu eenum aarvam udaimai adhu eenum, Nanbu ennum naadaach chirappu.

Meaning

Love makes us eager to be close to others. That eagerness brings friendship, a great treasure that comes to us without our looking for it.

Elementary

Love works like a seed that grows into a plant that makes more seeds. First you care about somebody. Then you want to be around them and do things for them. Then, before you notice, you have a friend — and friendship is one of the best things anyone can have.

University

The couplet is constructed as a genealogy, with ஈனும் (eenum, "begets, gives birth to") repeated to stage a two-step generation: அன்பு → ஆர்வம் → நண்பு. ஆர்வம் (aarvam) is warm eagerness or attentive desire toward another — the same root that gave ஆர்வலர் in Kural 71 — and Valluvar places it as the middle term that converts a disposition into a relationship. The closing phrase நாடாச் சிறப்பு is the verse's most striking move: நாடா means "unsought" or "not to be searched out," so friendship is an excellence that cannot be acquired by pursuit and arrives only as the by-product of love already given. This anticipates the entire நட்பியல் (the book of friendship, chapters 79–87) in the Poruṭpāl, and it separates Valluvar from purely transactional accounts of alliance: friendship here is emergent, not negotiated.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That very ancient science of the relationship with the Supreme is today told by Me to you because you are My devotee as well as My friend.”
— Bhagavad Gītā 4.3, trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that the highest teaching is being entrusted to him not because of rank or learning but because of the affectionate friendship (sakhya) between them.
Devotional love is what produces the friendship, and the friendship is what makes the greatest good available — the Kural's sequence exactly.
Christianity
“Henceforth I call you not servants; for the servant knoweth not what his lord doeth: but I have called you friends; for all things that I have heard of my Father I have made known unto you.”
— Gospel of John 15:15, King James Version [4]
Jesus reclassifies his disciples from servants to friends, and the reclassification follows directly on his command that they love one another.
Friendship is presented, as in the Kural, as the crowning outcome of love rather than as a separate arrangement.
Islam
“None of you will have faith till he wishes for his brother what he likes for himself.”
— Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 13 (Book of Belief), narrated by Anas ibn Mālik [5]
The Prophet makes an outward-turning desire for another's good — not merely refraining from harm — a condition of faith itself.
This is precisely Valluvar's middle term ஆர்வம்: love turned into active eagerness for another's welfare, which is what makes people friends.
Judaism
“And it came to pass, when he had made an end of speaking unto Saul, that the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul.”
— 1 Samuel 18:1, King James Version [4]
The text describes friendship arising spontaneously and involuntarily out of love — Jonathan does not decide to befriend David; the bond simply forms.
This is the Kural's நாடாச் சிறப்பு, the unsought excellence: friendship that is not gone looking for but is begotten by love.
Buddhism
“Admirable friendship, admirable companionship, admirable camaraderie is actually the whole of the holy life.”
— Upaḍḍha Sutta, Saṃyutta Nikāya 45.2, trans. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu [6]
Correcting Ānanda's guess that good friendship is half the spiritual life, the Buddha says it is the entirety of it, since the whole path unfolds from it.
Both texts rank friendship at the very top of the scale of goods — Valluvar's "excellence beyond seeking" and the Buddha's "whole of the holy life."
Jainism
“Benevolence (maitrī) towards all living-beings (sattva), joy (pramoda) at the sight of the virtuous (guṇādhika), compassion and sympathy (kāruṇya) for the afflicted (kliśyamāna), and tolerance (mādhyasthya) towards the insolent and ill-behaved (avineya) are the other observances.”
— Tattvartha Sutra 7.11 (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [7]
Umâsvâti lists four attitudes to cultivate, starting with maitrî (friendliness toward all beings), and Pûjyapâda's commentary defines pramoda as 'fervent affection' toward the virtuous.
Valluvar's sequence of love, then warmth toward others, then friendship has a Jain counterpart in maitrî and pramoda, which the sutra presents as cultivated dispositions supporting the vows.
Sikhism
“No one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger. I get along with everyone.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299, Raag Kaanraa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan describes the change that comes from the company of the holy (sadh sangat): once love has replaced jealousy, everyone becomes a friend and no one remains an outsider.
As in the Kural's chain from love to warm eagerness to friendship, friendship here is not pursued but arrives as the by-product of a heart already changed by love.
Greek Mythology
Pythias, condemned to death by the tyrant Dionysius of Syracuse, is allowed to go home to settle his affairs only because Damon offers to stand as hostage and die in his place; Pythias returns just in time, and the astonished tyrant releases them both and asks to be admitted to their friendship.
— The legend of Damon and Pythias, recounted in Cicero, De Officiis III.45 and Valerius Maximus, Memorable Doings and Sayings IV.7 [9]
The legend's whole point is that a bond of affection produced a degree of mutual trust the tyrant had assumed impossible between human beings.
The tyrant's request to join them dramatises Valluvar's நாடாச் சிறப்பு — friendship as an excellence that cannot be obtained by asking for it.
Philosophy
“For without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.1, 1155a, trans. W. D. Ross [10]
Aristotle opens his two books on friendship by ranking philia above every other external good, arguing it is both a virtue and the condition of a liveable life.
Aristotle's valuation matches the Kural's: friendship is not one good among many but the excellence that makes the rest worth having.

Real Life Example

Jesse Owens and Luz Long become friends at the Berlin Olympics
August 1936 · Berlin, Germany
At the 1936 Berlin Olympics the American Jesse Owens won the long jump gold medal and the German Luz Long took silver. Owens later wrote that what he remembered most was the friendship he struck up with Long, and the two corresponded by letter until the war broke out in 1939. Long was killed in the Second World War in 1943, and after the war Owens kept up the friendship through correspondence with Long's son.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
A warm regard between two rivals, formed in the most politically charged Games of the century, grew into a friendship that outlasted a war and a death.
The kural traces a lineage from love to warm eagerness to friendship, an unsought excellence, and the Owens and Long friendship arose unlooked-for out of goodwill between competitors.
