Kural 1165

Complaint · Padar Melindhirankal · படர் மெலிந்திரங்கல்

துப்பின் எவனாவர் மன்கொல் துயர்வரவு நட்பினுள் ஆற்று பவர்.
Thuppin evanaavar mankol thuyarvaravu natpinul aatru pavar.

Meaning

If he can bring me such sadness while we are lovers, what would he do to me if he were my enemy?

Elementary

Imagine your best friend forgets your birthday and it really hurts. You might say, ‘If this is how much my friend can hurt me, I would hate to see what an enemy could do!’ The wife is half-joking, half-hurt in the same way about her husband who has gone away.

University

The couplet is ironic reproach: the husband ‘accomplishes the coming of sorrow’ (tuyar varavu āṟṟupavar, துயர்வரவு ஆற்றுபவர்) even within naṭpu (நட்பு, friendship, intimacy), so what (evaṉ, எவன்) would he become in tuppu (துப்பு)? The commentators divide over tuppu: Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan take it as ‘strength’, the power needed against a foe, while Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar take it directly as ‘enmity’ (pakai), and the sense is the same either way. Maṉ kol (மன்கொல்) adds wondering doubt. The reproach is affectionate rather than bitter, in keeping with the karpu ideal in which the wife complains of, but does not renounce, the absent husband. Pope: ‘Who work us woe in friendship’s trustful hour, / What will they prove when angry tempests lower?’ The text is identical on both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Shakuntala comes to Dushyanta’s court, the king, his memory clouded by a curse, denies he ever married her, and she reproaches him for treating with such cruelty the woman he had won with tender words.
— Kalidasa, Abhijnanashakuntalam, Act 5 (paraphrase) [3]
The very man who loved her becomes the source of her deepest wound.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Shakuntala finds that the one bound to her by love can hurt her more than any enemy.
Christianity
“Yea, mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me.”
— Psalms 41:9 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist laments that the wound came not from a stranger but from his trusted companion.
The Kural’s heroine makes the same complaint: the hurt comes from the one bound to her in love.
Islam
“Oh, woe to me! I wish I had not taken that one as a friend.”
— Qur’an 25:28 (Sahih International) [5]
On the Day of Judgment the wrongdoer laments the friendship that led him to ruin.
The Kural’s heroine, more gently, also counts the sorrow that comes through a bond of friendship.
Judaism
“For it was not an enemy that taunted me, Then I could have borne it; Neither was it mine adversary that did magnify himself against me, Then I would have hid myself from him. But it was thou, a man mine equal, My companion, and my familiar friend.”
— Psalms 55:13-14 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist says he could have endured an enemy’s hostility; it is the hurt from a close companion that is unbearable.
This is the Kural’s comparison of friend and foe turned the same way: the wound from one who loves is the hardest to bear.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that the beloved can cause pain just as surely as the disliked, simply by being absent.
The Kural’s heroine finds the same thing: the beloved, by being away, causes as much pain as an enemy could.
Jainism
In Kapila’s verses a monk is told to quit his former connections and set his affection on nothing, for one who loves not even those who love him will be free from sin and hatred.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 8.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [8]
Jain teaching sees affection itself as the channel through which suffering enters, and so advises withdrawing it.
The Kural’s heroine discovers that the bond of love is what lets her husband hurt her; the Jain text draws the renunciant’s conclusion from that same fact.
Sikhism
“O my Husband Lord, You are angry with me in Your Mind. The fault is with me, and not with my Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 794, Raag Soohee, Sheikh Farid (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid, speaking as the soul-bride, feels the Husband Lord's displeasure but lays the blame on her own failings, not on Him.
The Kural's heroine reproaches her husband, asking what he would do as an enemy if he brings such sorrow as a lover; Farid's bride feels the same estrangement but turns the reproach on herself.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid’s Heroides, Ariadne, abandoned on the island of Naxos by Theseus, writes that she saved his life and was repaid with desertion, so that the man she helped has wounded her worse than any foe.
— Ovid, Heroides 10 (Ariadne to Theseus) (paraphrase) [10]
Ariadne’s complaint is that her pain comes from the very person she loved and served.
She voices the Kural’s question: if love does this, what could enmity do?
Philosophy
In Cicero’s On Friendship, Laelius reflects that friendships which break down often turn into bitter enmities, and that the hurt is the sharper because of the intimacy that preceded it.
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia (On Friendship) (paraphrase) [11]
Cicero notes that closeness gives a friend unusual power to wound.
The heroine’s rueful question rests on the same insight: love gives the beloved the power to cause the deepest pain.

Real Life Example

Heloise writes her first letter to Abelard
c. 1132 · Abbey of the Paraclete, near Nogent-sur-Seine, France
Around 1130 the philosopher Peter Abelard wrote a long letter of consolation to a friend, known as the Historia calamitatum, describing his misfortunes. Heloise, by then abbess of the Paraclete, wrote to him after it came into her hands, complaining that he had written to comfort a friend but had neglected her, and asking him to write to her of his dangers and of the convent he had founded. This letter opened one of the most famous correspondences of the Middle Ages.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Heloise's letter is a loving reproach: she does not renounce Abelard but asks why the man she loves has left her without a word.
The kural's heroine asks, half in reproach and half in love, what her husband would do as an enemy if he brings such grief as a lover, and Heloise's complaint of neglect has that same tender anger.
