Kural 831

Folly · Pedhaimai · பேதைமை

பேதைமை என்பதொன்று யாதெனின் ஏதங்கொண்டு ஊதியம் போக விடல்.
Pedhaimai enbadhondru yaadhenin edhangkondu oodhiyam poga vidal.

Meaning

What is foolishness? It is grabbing what hurts you and letting go of what would help you.

Elementary

Imagine someone offers you a choice between a shiny toy that will break in a day and a bicycle you could ride for years, and you pick the toy and walk away from the bike. That swap, keeping the bad thing and dropping the good one, is exactly what the poet calls being foolish.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a definition rather than an example: pedhaimai (பேதைமை) is the act of taking up edham (ஏதம், harm, loss) and letting oodhiyam (ஊதியம், gain, profit) go. Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar read the pair as a ledger of consequences: the fool does not merely fail to gain, he actively trades gain for loss, which is why folly is placed in the Natpiyal section right after the chapters on bad friendship. Puliyur Kesigan sharpens this into a cost-benefit reading (accepting the trouble that comes with a task while throwing away the profit it would bring), whereas Sivayogi Sivakumar reads edhangkondu as ‘out of fear of a small hardship’, a softer psychological gloss. Kalaignar stresses the failure of discernment: the fool never clarifies which is harmful and which is beneficial. The text is identical in ytamizh and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Katha Upanishad, Yama tells Nachiketas that the good (shreyas) and the pleasant (preyas) both approach a person; the wise examine and choose the good, while the dull-witted choose the pleasant for the sake of getting and keeping.
— Katha Upanishad 1.2.1-2 [3]
The Upanishad frames life’s basic test as a choice between what truly benefits and what merely attracts, and calls the person who picks wrongly a fool.
Valluvar defines folly the same way: choosing what harms over what profits.
Christianity
“Lest there be any fornicator, or profane person, as Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright.”
— Hebrews 12:16 (KJV) [4]
The letter to the Hebrews holds up Esau as a warning: a man who traded something of lasting worth for a moment’s appetite.
Esau is the textbook case of taking the small harm and letting the great gain go.
Islam
“Those are the ones who have purchased error [in exchange] for guidance, so their transaction has brought no profit, nor were they guided.”
— Qur’an 2:16 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an describes the self-deceived as bad traders who swap guidance for error and walk away with no profit.
Both texts use the language of profit and loss to define the fool’s bargain.
Judaism
“Then Jacob gave Esau bread and pottage of lentiles; and he did eat and drink, and rose up, and went his way: thus Esau despised his birthright.”
— Genesis 25:34 (Tanakh, KJV translation) [4]
Genesis tells how Esau, hungry from the field, sold his birthright for a bowl of stew and walked off unconcerned.
The story dramatizes the Kural’s definition: grabbing a passing want and letting a lasting good go.
Buddhism
“If by renouncing a lesser happiness one may realize a greater happiness, let the wise man renounce the lesser, having regard for the greater.”
— Dhammapada 290 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Dhammapada advises giving up a small pleasure when doing so secures a larger one.
It is the wise mirror image of the fool in the Kural, who gives up the greater gain for the lesser.
Jainism
“As a man for the sake of one Kâkinî (risks and) loses a thousand (Kârshâpanas), or as the king lost his kingdom (and life) by eating a mango-fruit which he was strictly forbidden (by his physician)”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
In the 'Parable of the Ram' Mahāvīra's teaching shows the fool trading something vast for a trifle, like a man who loses a thousand coins for a single small one, or a king who loses his kingdom for a forbidden mango.
Valluvar defines folly as grasping what harms you and letting real gain slip away; the Uttarādhyayana's man who loses a thousand coins for one small coin shows that very trade.
Sikhism
“The nights are wasted sleeping, and the days are wasted eating. Human life is such a precious jewel, but it is being lost in exchange for a mere shell. … You fool - you shall regret and repent in the end!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 156, Raag Gauri Bairagan, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak calls it folly to trade the jewel of human life for a worthless shell, and warns that the fool will regret it.
The Kural defines folly as grabbing what harms you and letting gain slip away; Guru Nanak's fool who swaps a jewel for a shell makes exactly this losing trade.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod tells how Epimetheus, though warned by Prometheus never to accept a gift from Zeus, took in Pandora and understood the harm only after it was already his.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 83-89 [9]
Epimetheus, whose name means ‘afterthought’, accepts the gift that carries every evil into the world.
Like Valluvar’s fool, he takes in the harm and loses the good he had been warned to keep.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Protagoras, Socrates argues that people choose worse over better because they mismeasure pleasures and pains, and that the remedy is an art of measurement; the failure he calls ignorance.
— Plato, Protagoras 356a-357e [10]
For Socrates, choosing the larger harm for the smaller benefit is a failure of knowledge, not of desire.
Valluvar likewise defines folly as a mistaken weighing of harm against gain.

Real Life Example

Decca Records turns down the Beatles
1 January 1962 · London, England
Through their manager Brian Epstein, the Beatles were given an audition at Decca Records' studios in north London on New Year's Day 1962, recording about fifteen songs. Decca turned them down and instead signed Brian Poole and the Tremeloes, another group that auditioned that day. Later in 1962 the Beatles were signed by EMI's Parlophone label under producer George Martin and went on to become the most successful band in popular music.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
The decision became one of the best-known missed opportunities in business history, though Decca's executives acted on their judgement of a young, unproven group.
The kural defines folly as taking hold of what brings loss and letting slip what would bring profit, and Decca chose the lesser act and let the Beatles go to a rival.
