Kural 488

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

செறுநரைக் காணின் சுமக்க இறுவரை காணின் கிழக்காம் தலை.
Serunaraik Kaanin Sumakka Iruvarai Kaanin Kizhakkaam Thalai.

Meaning

When you meet your enemies, put up with them and even show them respect. When their time to fall comes, they will fall headfirst.

Elementary

Sometimes you have to put up with someone difficult who is stronger than you right now. If you stay patient, a time will come when they lose their power. Wait for that time instead of starting a fight you can’t win.

University

Serunar are enemies, and sumakka means ‘carry’ or ‘bear’. Parimelazhagar understands this as even showing them deference, carrying them ‘on one’s head’ as it were. Iruvarai is the limit or end-time, the point at which the enemy’s fortune has run out. Kizhakkaam thalai, ‘the head will turn downward’, comes from kizh (below). Parimelazhagar reads kizhakku here as keezh, ‘downward’, not the homonym meaning ‘east’. The advice is openly realpolitik: submit outwardly while weak and strike when the enemy’s time has come. It closely echoes Kanika’s counsel to Dhritarashtra in the Mahābhārata to carry the enemy on one’s shoulder and then smash him like a pot on a stone. Both sources give identical text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The minister Kanika advises Dhritarashtra that as long as time is unfavourable, a king should carry his foe on his shoulder. When the right time comes, he should dash the foe to pieces like an earthen pot thrown on a stone.
— Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva, Section 142 (Kanika’s counsel; tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Kanika’s Machiavellian counsel, given as the Kauravas turn against the Pandavas, recommends outward submission until the moment to destroy.
‘Carry him on your shoulder’ is sumakka, ‘bear him’. The pot smashed on the stone is the head that falls at iruvarai.
Christianity
“Let both grow together until the harvest: and in the time of harvest I will say to the reapers, Gather ye together first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn them: but gather the wheat into my barn.”
— Matthew 13:30 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the wheat and the tares, the householder refuses to pull up the weeds too early and waits for harvest, when they can be removed safely.
Enduring what is hostile until its appointed end is the Kural’s sumakka … iruvarai.
Islam
In an early Meccan sura, the Prophet is told to bear patiently what his opponents say and to part from them gracefully. God says to leave the wealthy deniers to Him and to give them a little respite.
— Qur’an 73:10–11 [5]
The weak early community is told to endure hostility and not to retaliate, because the opponents’ end is fixed.
Bearing the enemy now and trusting that his downfall will come in its time is the Kural’s counsel.
Judaism
“David said furthermore, As the LORD liveth, the LORD shall smite him; or his day shall come to die; or he shall descend into battle, and perish.”
— 1 Samuel 26:10 (KJV) [4]
David, standing over the sleeping Saul, refuses to kill him. He is sure that Saul’s appointed end will come by itself.
‘His day shall come’ is very nearly the Kural’s iruvarai, the boundary-time of the enemy’s fall.
Buddhism
Devadatta repeatedly plotted against the Buddha. He tried to have him killed by assassins, a boulder and a maddened elephant. The Buddha never retaliated. Tradition says Devadatta was finally swallowed by the earth as the result of his own deeds.
— Vinaya, Cullavagga VII; Dhammapada Commentary on verse 17 [6]
The Buddha bore his enemy’s hostility, and the enemy fell by the working of his own karma.
This shares the Kural’s pattern of enduring now until the enemy’s end, without the Kural’s idea of striking back.
Jainism
“There is not the least hatred in me, neither now, nor before, nor in future. The Yakshas attend upon me, therefore they have beaten the boys.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 12.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The beaten monk Harikesa bears abuse and blows without hatred, and his attackers are nevertheless brought low, not by him but by the guardian spirits.
The kural advises bearing one's foes until their time of downfall comes; in this story the monk too bears his enemies and they fall, but Jainism insists that he wanted no revenge at all.
Sikhism
“Fareed, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists. Kiss their feet, and return to your own home.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 7) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Sheikh Farid tells the seeker not to return blows but to answer aggressors with deference and go home in peace.
The Kural says to bear with foes and even honour them until their time of downfall comes; Farid also counsels bearing foes and honouring them.
Greek Mythology
Returning to Ithaca disguised as a beggar, Odysseus endures the suitors’ insults, a stool thrown at him, and the taunts of his own disloyal servants. He waits until the contest of the bow puts a weapon in his hands. Then he throws off his rags and kills them all.
— Homer, Odyssey, Books 17–22 [9]
The king bears humiliation from his enemies in his own hall until the moment of their ruin comes.
This is sumakka followed by the falling heads, in the most famous revenge story of Greek epic.
Philosophy
After Rome’s disasters against Hannibal, Quintus Fabius Maximus refused pitched battle and shadowed the invader from the hills. He endured public mockery as ‘Hannibal’s pedagogue’ and ‘the Delayer’ (Cunctator) while Hannibal’s strength wore down. Plutarch praises his judgment, which Rome’s later victory vindicated.
— Plutarch, Life of Fabius Maximus 5–10 [10]
Plutarch holds up Fabius as the model of the statesman who bears an enemy’s presence and his own disgrace until time turns the balance.
Fabius ‘carried’ Hannibal in Italy for years until the invader’s power ran out, as the Kural advises.

Real Life Example

Tokugawa Ieyasu serves Hideyoshi, then wins Japan
1584-1603 · Japan
After fighting Toyotomi Hideyoshi without success in 1584, Tokugawa Ieyasu agreed to become his vassal and served him for the rest of Hideyoshi's life, strengthening his own domain around Edo and avoiding Hideyoshi's costly invasions of Korea. Hideyoshi died in 1598, leaving a young heir, and in 1600 Ieyasu defeated the rival coalition at the Battle of Sekigahara, becoming the undisputed master of Japan. He received the title of shogun in 1603, founding the Tokugawa shogunate.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
Ieyasu bowed to a stronger rival for more than a decade and moved only when that rival's power had passed.
The kural advises bearing with one's foes, even showing them deference, until their time runs out, and Ieyasu's long service to Hideyoshi ended in his own supremacy.
