Kural 1122

Declaration of Love's Special Excellence · Kaadhar Sirappuraithal · காதற் சிறப்புரைத்தல்

உடம்பொடு உயிரிடை என்னமற் றன்ன மடந்தையொடு எம்மிடை நட்பு.
Udambodu uyiridai ennamat ranna madandhaiyodu emmidai natpu.

Meaning

Whatever the bond is between the body and the life inside it, that is the bond between this young woman and me.

Elementary

Your body and the life inside it always go together; you can't send one to school and leave the other at home. The poet says he and the person he loves belong together in that same way.

University

The hero now states the inseparability of the lovers through the classic Indian pairing of udambu (உடம்பு, body) and uyir (உயிர், life or soul). The word chosen for the relationship is natpu (நட்பு, 'friendship, intimacy'), the same term that heads the Porutpaal chapters on friendship (ch. 79 onward), so the love is presented as a perfect friendship. Madandhai (மடந்தை) denotes a young woman, conventionally of marriageable age. Modern commentators agree on the sense: Mu. Varadarasanar ('as the ties between body and life'), Solomon Pappaiah ('the relation between body and life'), Kalaignar ('life and body never live apart; such is our relationship'); Pope translates 'Between this maid and me the friendship kind / Is as the bonds that soul and body bind.' Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Rigvedic wedding hymn the couple pray that the gods and the waters join their two hearts together into one.
— Rigveda 10.85.47 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Ralph T.H. Griffith) [3]
The verse is still recited at Hindu weddings as the moment the two lives are declared united.
Like the Kural, it describes the bond of lovers as a union so close that two become one.
Christianity
“So ought men to love their wives as their own bodies. He that loveth his wife loveth himself.”
— Ephesians 5:28 (KJV) [4]
Paul tells husbands that a wife is as much a part of a man as his own body.
The Kural likewise makes the beloved as inseparable from the lover as life is from the body.
Islam
“They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them.”
— Qur'an 2:187 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an describes husband and wife as garments for each other: closest to the body, protecting and covering one another.
Both texts pick the thing nearest the self, clothing or the body itself, to show how close the lovers are.
Judaism
“This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh … and they shall be one flesh.”
— Genesis 2:23-24 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
On first seeing the woman, Adam recognises her as part of his own body, and the Torah adds that husband and wife become one flesh.
The Kural's lover too finds the beloved to be part of his very being.
Buddhism
“If both husband & wife want to see one another not only in the present life but also in the life to come, they should be in tune [with each other] in conviction, in tune in virtue, in tune in generosity, and in tune in discernment.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 4.55, Samajivina Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
When the devoted couple Nakula's father and mother ask to be together in future lives, the Buddha tells them how.
The Kural's bond as close as body and life finds its Buddhist counterpart in a couple joined across lives by shared virtue.
Jainism
The Sutrakritanga refutes the materialists who hold that the soul is the same as the body, insisting that soul and body are distinct, like a sword that can be drawn from its scabbard.
— Sutrakritanga 2.1 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
For Jainism the soul (jiva) is only temporarily bound to a body by karma, and liberation means separating them for good.
The Kural's ideal of a bond like body and life is, for a Jain, the very bond to be loosened, so the parallel is one of sharp contrast.
Sikhism
“O mind, love the Lord, as the lotus loves the water.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 59, Sri Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak asks for a love so bound to the Lord that, like the lotus that cannot live apart from water, the soul draws its very life from Him.
The Kural's bond of body and life (udambu and uyir) finds its Sikh counterpart in the lotus and the water, a pairing in which one cannot survive without the other.
Greek Mythology
“human nature was originally one and we were a whole, and the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love.”
— Plato, Symposium 192e-193a, speech of Aristophanes (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
In Aristophanes' myth, humans were once double beings that Zeus split in two, and love is each half seeking its other half.
The Kural's lovers belong together as body and life do; Aristophanes says lovers are literally two halves of one being.
Philosophy
Asked what a friend is, Aristotle is reported to have answered: a single soul dwelling in two bodies.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 5.20 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.D. Hicks) [11]
The saying sums up Aristotle's view that perfect friendship makes two people share one life.
The Kural even calls the love natpu, friendship, and makes it as close as soul and body.

Real Life Example

The pair statue of King Menkaure and his queen
c. 2490–2472 BCE; found January 1910 · Giza, Egypt; now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, United States
During Egypt's Fourth Dynasty, sculptors carved a nearly life-size statue in greywacke showing King Menkaure standing beside a queen, probably his principal wife. The queen stands with one arm around his waist and her other hand on his arm, and the two figures share one base and back pillar. Excavators led by George Reisner found the statue in Menkaure's valley temple at Giza in January 1910, and it is now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The statue is a masterpiece of Old Kingdom art and one of the earliest images of a couple shown as an affectionate pair.
The kural says the bond between the lovers is like the bond between body and life, and this statue, carved more than 4,000 years ago, presents king and queen as one joined form, standing together for eternity.
