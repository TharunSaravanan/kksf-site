Kural 28

The Greatness of Ascetics · Neethaar Perumai · நீத்தார் பெருமை

நிறைமொழி மாந்தர் பெருமை நிலத்து மறைமொழி காட்டி விடும்.
Niraimozhi maandhar perumai nilaththu, Maraimozhi kaatti vidum.

Meaning

The greatness of wise people whose words always come true is shown on earth by the holy sayings they leave behind.

Elementary

Some people say things that just stay true, long after they have gone — you can still hear them repeated hundreds of years later. This kural says you do not need to have met such a person to know how great they were; the words they left behind tell you.

University

The couplet turns on a pair of near-rhyming compounds. “Nirai-mozhi” is full speech — words so complete that what they say comes about; “marai-mozhi” is hidden or sacred speech, the mantra, the word set apart. Valluvar's claim is evidential: the greatness of such people is not something we take on trust but something the surviving sayings “kaatti vidum,” show up, display, on the earth (“nilaththu”). The verse encodes an old South Asian doctrine of the efficacious utterance — the satya-vakya or act of truth, in which a perfected person's word alters reality because it proceeds from a life that contains no falsehood. What is distinctly Valluvar's is the inversion: rather than arguing that great people produce powerful words, he argues from the words back to the people, making the surviving text the standing proof. The line has an obvious reflexivity for a reader holding the Kural itself, and Tamil tradition has not missed it.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Vedic tradition holds that the hymns were not composed but seen — each sukta is assigned to a named rishi who is its mantra-drashta, its seer — so that the surviving mantra is the standing evidence of the seer's realisation, and the anukramani indexes preserve those names alongside the verses.
— Rig Veda and the rishi-attribution tradition of the Sarvanukramani [4]
In this framework the sage does not own the mantra and the mantra does not flatter the sage; the relationship is evidential, with the enduring efficacious word testifying to the state of the one who saw it.
This is the Kural's exact logic — the sacred saying left on earth is what displays the greatness of the person of full speech.
Christianity
“Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away.”
— Matthew 24:35 (KJV) [5]
Jesus claims a durability for his sayings greater than that of the physical cosmos, making the words rather than any monument the lasting evidence of who spoke them.
Like the Kural, it treats surviving speech as the thing that carries a person's greatness forward into the world.
Islam
“Many a person with disheveled hair and covered with dust is turned away from the doors (whereas he is held in such a high esteem by Allah) that if he were to adjure in the name of Allah (about anything) Allah would fulfil that.”
— Sahih Muslim 2622 (Book of Virtue, chapter on the virtue of the weak and downtrodden) [6]
The hadith describes a person whose outward appearance invites contempt but whose word, sworn before God, is honoured — his unregarded speech is the exact index of his standing.
This is the Kural's doctrine of full speech: the word of the renunciant is effective, and its effectiveness is what reveals a greatness the world had missed.
Judaism
The Talmud draws from a verse about one who rules over people justly the astonishing corollary that the righteous rule over the fear of God: God decrees and the tzaddik may annul the decree.
— Babylonian Talmud, Mo'ed Katan 16b [7]
The rabbis take the efficacy of a righteous person's word to such a limit that it is said to override heaven's own pronouncement, making speech the visible measure of sanctity.
Both traditions read a holy person's greatness directly off the power their words are found to carry.
Buddhism
Sent to help a woman in obstructed labour, the former bandit Angulimala performs an act of truth, declaring that since he was born in the noble birth he has never intentionally taken a life — and on the strength of that true statement the woman and her child are delivered safely.
— Angulimala Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 86 [8]
The saccakiriya works only because the statement is true of the speaker; the power belongs not to the formula but to the life that can say it without falsehood.
Exactly the Kural's claim — the efficacy of the surviving utterance is what discloses what kind of person stood behind it.
Jainism
“That alone is true Scripture which is the word of a Tirthamkara, which cannot be over-ridden in disputation, nor falsified by perception, reason or testimony, which reveals the nature of things, which is helpful to men, animals and all other kinds of beings, and which is potent enough to destroy all forms of falsehood.”
— Ratnakaranda Shravakachara v. 9 (tr. Champat Rai Jain, The Householder's Dharma, 1917) [9]
Samantabhadra grounds the authority of Jain scripture in the person who spoke it. A Tirthankara, free of every fault and omniscient, utters words that cannot be refuted and that benefit every living being.
The Kural says the sacred sayings left on earth by people of perfected speech show their greatness. Jain tradition makes the same tie between the perfected person and the sacred word, though it argues from the speaker to the scripture rather than from the scripture back to the speaker.
Sikhism
“The Word, the Bani is Guru, and Guru is the Bani. Within the Bani, the Ambrosial Nectar is contained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 982, Raag Nat, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Sikhism the Gurus' recorded words are the Guru. That is why Guru Arjan compiled them into the Adi Granth (1604), and why the scripture is honoured today as the living Guru.
The Kural says the greatness of perfected people is shown by the sacred words they leave behind. Sikhism goes further and treats those words as the Guru's presence itself.
Greek Mythology
Tiresias, the blind Theban seer, is the one figure in Greek myth whose pronouncements always prove true — Oedipus rages at him and disbelieves him, and every word he has spoken comes about regardless, while he alone among the dead retains his mind and prophesies to Odysseus in the underworld.
— Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 300-462; cf. Homer, Odyssey 11.90-151 [11]
Greek tradition marks out the seer precisely by the fact that his speech is not opinion but something that happens, and his standing is established retrospectively by the fulfilment of his words.
As in the Kural, the greatness of the man is displayed by the words rather than the words by the man.
Philosophy
“And the Sibyl, with raving lips uttering things mirthless, unbedizened, and unperfumed, reaches over a thousand years with her voice, thanks to the god in her.”
— Heraclitus, Fragment DK B92 (trans. John Burnet) [12]
Heraclitus contrasts the Sibyl's unadorned, unattractive speech with its reach, arguing that her words outlast a millennium precisely because they carry something other than ornament.
It is the Kural's point in a single sentence: the plain sacred utterance persists, and its persistence is what testifies to the one who spoke it.

Real Life Example

The Diamond Sutra, the world's earliest dated printed book
868 (printed); found in the early 1900s · Dunhuang, China
A Chinese copy of the Diamond Sutra, a Buddhist scripture, was printed from carved wooden blocks and dated by its colophon to the equivalent of 11 May 868. It was found in the early 20th century among tens of thousands of scrolls in a sealed cave at the Mogao Buddhist cave site near Dunhuang. It is the earliest dated complete printed book known and is now held by the British Library.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The words of a teacher of renunciation were thought important enough to become the subject of the first dated printed book, and they have survived more than eleven centuries.
The kural says the greatness of those whose words are full of power is shown by the sacred sayings they leave behind, and the Diamond Sutra is a striking case of such sayings being preserved and spread.
