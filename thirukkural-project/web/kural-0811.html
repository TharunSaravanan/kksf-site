Kural 811

Evil Friendship · Thee Natpu · தீ நட்பு

பருகுவார் போலினும் பண்பிலார் கேண்மை பெருகலிற் குன்றல் இனிது.
Parukuvaar poalinum panpilaar kenmai perukalir kundral inidhu.

Meaning

Even when people with bad character act so loving that they seem to want to drink you up, it is better for that friendship to shrink than to grow.

Elementary

Some people hug you so tightly and say so many nice things that it feels wonderful. But if they are not kind or honest underneath, it is better to let that friendship get smaller instead of bigger, just like you would slowly back away from a puppy that wags its tail and also bites.

University

The chapter on tee natpu (தீ நட்பு, ‘evil friendship’) opens with its sharpest image: parukuvaar (பருகுவார்), ‘those who would drink you up’, describing a show of affection so intense it seems to swallow the friend whole. The defect is panpu-ilaar (பண்பிலார்), ‘those without good character’, so the warmth is not the test; character is. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both stress that the excess is appearance (‘தோன்றினாலும்’, ‘நடிக்கும்’), and Kalaignar speaks of being drowned in a flood of affection; all agree the right course is kundral (குன்றல்), letting the bond dwindle, rather than an abrupt break. This sets the chapter’s practical tone: Valluvar is less interested in condemning bad friends than in teaching how to reduce their hold. The text is identical in ytamizh and thirukkural.net; the sandhi-joined edition at thirukkural-thamizh.org prints ‘குன்ற லினிது’, a difference of word division only.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Hitopadesha’s first book, a jackal makes friends with a deer, flattering him and staying close, because he hopes to eat the deer’s flesh; he leads the deer into a farmer’s snare, and only the deer’s old friend, a crow, saves him.
— Hitopadesha, Book I (Mitralabha), the deer, the crow and the jackal [4]
The jackal’s friendship is all warmth on the surface and appetite underneath, and the deer nearly dies of it.
This is the Kural’s ‘friend who would drink you up’ made literal: affection masking a wish to consume.
Christianity
“He that hateth dissembleth with his lips, and layeth up deceit within him; When he speaketh fair, believe him not: for there are seven abominations in his heart.”
— Proverbs 26:24-25 (KJV) [5]
Proverbs warns that pleasant speech can be a cover for hostility, so fair words alone should not win trust.
Like the Kural, it tells the reader to judge by the heart, not by the show of warmth.
Islam
“And of the people is he whose speech pleases you in worldly life, and he calls Allah to witness as to what is in his heart, yet he is the fiercest of opponents.”
— Qur’an 2:204 (Sahih International) [6]
The verse describes a person whose words charm and who swears to his sincerity, yet who is in fact a bitter adversary.
Both texts warn that pleasing, affectionate speech can hide a character that works against you.
Judaism
Absalom, plotting against his father King David, would take the hand of anyone who came to bow before him and kiss him, and so, the narrator says, he stole the hearts of the men of Israel.
— 2 Samuel 15:5-6 (Hebrew Bible) [5]
Absalom’s overflowing affection was a political tool in service of a rebellion, not a sign of good character.
It shows exactly the Kural’s danger: lavish closeness from someone whose character does not deserve trust.
Buddhism
“The friend who appropriates, the friend who renders lip-service, the friend that flatters, the friend who brings ruin, these four as enemies the wise behold, avoid them from afar as paths of peril.”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), tr. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha tells the young Sigala that some who look like friends are really foes and should be kept at a distance.
The Kural likewise refuses to let the appearance of friendship decide the matter and counsels reducing contact.
Jainism
“Our intimacy with those to whom we are not bound by the chain of f'riendship, O king of the hills, the sides of which are covered with groves of tall sandal-trees! though it seem day by day to increase, will be dissolved as instantaneously as fire catches straw.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 24 'Improper Friendship', stanza 4 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The poet warns that an intimacy not grounded in real friendship may seem to grow day by day, yet it vanishes as fast as straw catches fire.
The Kural says it is sweeter for the effusive friendship of people of poor character to shrink than to grow, and the Naladiyar likewise sees no value in such growing intimacy, since it will burn away at once.
Sikhism
“Friendship with the self-willed Manmukhs lasts for only a few short days. This friendship is broken in an instant; this friendship leads to corruption.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 587, Raag Vadhans (Vadhans ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das warns that friendship with the manmukh, the person ruled by his own ego, is short-lived and drags one toward vice however warm it seems.
The Kural says that even when people of poor character seem fond enough to drink you in, it is better for that friendship to shrink than grow; Guru Amar Das explains why, since such friendship is brittle and corrupting.
Greek Mythology
“How handsome is the Crow,” he exclaimed, “in the beauty of her shape and in the fairness of her complexion!”
— Aesop, The Fox and the Crow (tr. G. F. Townsend) [10]
In Aesop’s fable the Fox praises the Crow lavishly only to make her open her beak and drop the meat he wants.
The Fox’s gushing praise is the Kural’s ‘as though they would drink you up’: warmth that serves an appetite.
Philosophy
“for the flatterer is a friend in an inferior position, or pretends to be such and to love more than he is loved”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.8 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle notes that the flatterer performs a love greater than he receives, and that most people enjoy this because they want to be loved.
The Kural’s friend of poor character likewise wins us by an excess of displayed love, which is exactly why it must be watched.

Real Life Example

Ferdinand Ward's firm ruins Ulysses S. Grant
1880-1884 · New York City, United States
After his presidency, Ulysses S. Grant became a partner in Grant & Ward, the Wall Street firm of his son Buck and the young financier Ferdinand Ward, whose apparent ability to produce large returns Grant admired, and the Grant family put most of its savings into it. Ward was secretly running what would now be called a Ponzi scheme, and in May 1884 the firm collapsed, leaving Grant nearly penniless and owing a $150,000 personal loan from William Vanderbilt. Ward was convicted of fraud and sent to Sing Sing prison, while Grant, already ill with cancer, wrote his Personal Memoirs to provide for his family.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Grant's warm trust in a charming partner of bad character grew year by year until it destroyed his fortune, and his memoirs were written in the shadow of that ruin.
The kural says that when people of poor character seem to love you intensely, it is better for that friendship to shrink than to grow, and Grant's closeness to Ward grew until it consumed nearly everything he had.
