Kural 261

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

உற்றநோய் நோன்றல் உயிர்க்குறுகண் செய்யாமை அற்றே தவத்திற் குரு.
Utra noi nondral uyirkku urukan seyyaamai Atre thavaththirku uru.

Meaning

Patiently bearing the pain that comes to you, and never hurting any other living thing: this is what true penance (a life of strict self-control) really is.

Elementary

Imagine someone bumps into you and it hurts. Penance, says Valluvar, is two simple things: you put up with the hurt without making a fuss, and you never hurt anyone back or hurt anyone at all. That is what being a real ‘tapas’ person looks like.

University

Valluvar opens the chapter with a definition that is striking for what it leaves out: no fire rituals, no fasting regimens, no forest hermitage, only a two-part ethical formula — endurance (நோன்றல், noṉṟal) of suffering that befalls oneself, and non-infliction (செய்யாமை) of suffering on other lives. The emphatic அற்றே (‘just that much’) deliberately reduces tapas from spectacle to disposition. Parimelazhagar notes that the suffering endured includes both what arises naturally (hunger, cold, illness) and what others inflict, which aligns the verse closely with the Jain and Buddhist redefinition of austerity as patience plus ahiṃsā. The move is thus both conservative (it keeps the prestigious word ‘tapas’) and reformist (it ethicizes it), and it frames every later claim in the chapter about the ‘power’ of tapas as resting on this moral foundation.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of the body consists in worship of the Supreme Lord, the brāhmaṇas, the spiritual master, and superiors like the father and mother, and in cleanliness, simplicity, celibacy and nonviolence.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.14 [3]
The Gita breaks tapas into bodily, verbal and mental kinds, and places nonviolence (ahiṃsā) inside the very definition of bodily austerity.
Like the Kural, the Gita makes not-harming part of what tapas is; Valluvar goes further by making it half the whole definition and pairing it with endurance.
Christianity
“For what glory is it, if, when ye be buffeted for your faults, ye shall take it patiently? but if, when ye do well, and suffer for it, ye take it patiently, this is acceptable with God.”
— 1 Peter 2:20 (KJV) [4]
Peter tells believers that patiently enduring undeserved suffering, without striking back, is what pleases God.
Both texts define spiritual worth as bearing pain patiently while refusing to retaliate.
Islam
“Fasting is a shield (or a screen or a shelter). So, the person observing fasting should avoid sexual relation with his wife and should not behave foolishly and impudently, and if somebody fights with him or abuses him, he should tell him twice, ‘I am fasting.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 1894 [5]
The Prophet describes sawm not merely as going hungry but as a discipline in which the faster absorbs provocation without answering it with harm.
The Islamic fast, like Valluvar’s tapas, joins bodily endurance with a refusal to hurt others even when provoked.
Judaism
“About those who are insulted and do not insult, who hear their shame and do not respond, who act out of love and are joyful in suffering, the verse says: ‘And they that love Him are as the sun going forth in its might’.”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 88b (Koren–Steinsaltz trans.) [6]
The Talmud praises those who absorb insult without returning it and who accept suffering with joy, likening them to the rising sun.
Endure what comes, harm no one in return — the same two pillars Valluvar names as the whole of tapas.
Buddhism
“Enduring patience is the highest austerity. ‘Nibbana is supreme,’ say the Buddhas. He is not a true monk who harms another, nor a true renunciate who oppresses others.”
— Dhammapada 184 [7]
The Buddha redefines tapas as patient endurance and declares that anyone who harms others is not a genuine renunciant.
Almost a restatement of the Kural: austerity = patience + not harming others.
Jainism
“Suffering from insects a great sage remains undisturbed. … Tolerate living beings, do not kill them, though they eat your flesh and blood.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.10-11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
In the lecture on the twenty-two troubles (parīṣaha) a monk must bear, Mahavira tells him to endure even the bites of insects without driving them off or harming them.
Valluvar defines penance as bearing one's own suffering while causing none to others; this verse joins exactly those two halves, patient endurance and refusal to injure even the creatures causing the pain.
Sikhism
“Fareed, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists. Kiss their feet, and return to your own home.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Salok Sheikh Farid 7 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Shaikh Farid, whose saloks are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, tells the seeker to take blows without hitting back, to answer them with humility, and to return to inner peace.
Valluvar defines true penance as bearing what one suffers while causing no suffering to others; Farid asks for exactly that, patience under blows and no retaliation.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus, disguised as a beggar in his own house, bears the suitors’ insults and blows night after night, commanding his own heart to endure rather than lash out.
— Homer, Odyssey Book 20 [10]
Homer’s hero is celebrated for polytlas — much-enduring — self-command under humiliation.
Mirrors the endurance half of Valluvar’s definition; the non-harm half fails, since Odysseus later slaughters the suitors.
Philosophy
“These two words, he said, are ἀνέχου (bear) and ἀπέχου (forbear).”
— Epictetus in Aulus Gellius, Attic Nights 17.19.5-6 (trans. J. C. Rolfe) [11]
Epictetus taught that the two worst faults are failing to endure what must be endured and failing to refrain from what must be avoided; ‘bear and forbear’ would make a person almost sinless.
The Stoic two-word rule is a near twin of Valluvar’s two-part definition of tapas.

Real Life Example

The Doukhobors burn their weapons and bear persecution without resistance
1895-1899 · Transcaucasus, Russian Empire (now Georgia, Azerbaijan and Turkey), and Saskatchewan, Canada
In 1895 thousands of Doukhobors, a Russian Christian sect committed to pacifism, piled up their own weapons in their villages in the Transcaucasus and burned them, refusing to bear arms or do military service. The Russian authorities responded with renewed persecution, and the Doukhobors did not take up arms in return. With help from Leo Tolstoy and from English Quakers, about 7,500 of them emigrated to Canada in 1899 and settled in what became Saskatchewan.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The Burning of Arms has been called one of the first mass pacifist protests of modern times, and the community chose to absorb punishment and exile rather than harm anyone.
The kural defines true penance as bearing whatever suffering comes to oneself while inflicting none on others, which is exactly the two-part stance the Doukhobors took.
