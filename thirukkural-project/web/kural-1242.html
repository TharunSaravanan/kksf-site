Kural 1242

Soliloquy · Nenjodu Kilathal · நெஞ்சொடு கிளத்தல்

காதல் அவரிலர் ஆகநீ நோவது பேதைமை வாழியென் நெஞ்சு.
Kaadhal avarilar aakanee noavadhu paedhaimai vaazhiyen nenju.

Meaning

Long live you, my heart! He has no love for us, so your suffering over him is just foolish.

Elementary

Imagine you keep waiting by the door for a friend who has forgotten all about you. You might say to yourself, “Silly heart, why are you sad about someone who isn’t even thinking about you?”

University

The heroine now scolds her heart: kātal avar ilar āka (‘he being without love’), nī nōvatu (‘your aching’) is pētaimai (‘folly, simple-mindedness’, from pētai ‘simpleton’). The interjection vāḻi eṉ neñcu (‘long may you live, my heart!’) is a conventional blessing that here carries gentle irony, as Solomon Pappaiah’s rendering ‘நீ வாழ்ந்து போ’ makes explicit. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar both gloss pētaimai as aṟiyāmai (ignorance), and Pope translates ‘thy smart is folly, fare thee well my heart’. The traditional reading, which modern commentators follow, takes the accusation of lovelessness as the wife’s overstatement in grief rather than a settled judgment of her husband. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“For him who has conquered the mind, the mind is the best of friends; but for one who has failed to do so, his very mind will be the greatest enemy.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.6 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna teaches that the mind can be either one’s closest ally or one’s worst foe, depending on whether it is mastered.
The Kural’s heroine discovers this double nature: her heart is both her only confidant and the foolish source of her pain.
Christianity
“Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life.”
— Proverbs 4:23 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs urges a person to guard the heart carefully, since everything in life flows from it.
The Kural’s heroine, seeing her heart ache foolishly, is in effect trying to keep watch over it.
Islam
“But perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allah Knows, while you know not.”
— Qur’an 2:216 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an warns that what the heart loves is not always good for it, since human knowledge is limited.
The Kural’s heroine tells her heart that loving one who does not love back is folly, a love that is ‘bad for you’.
Judaism
“Why art thou cast down, O my soul? And why moanest thou within me? Hope thou in God; for I shall yet praise Him for the salvation of His countenance.”
— Psalms 42:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist questions his own downcast soul as if it were another person, challenging its sorrow.
This is the same inner dialogue the Kural stages: the speaker addresses her heart and asks why it aches.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha says that recognising one’s own foolishness is already the beginning of wisdom.
By calling her own heart foolish (pētai), the Kural’s heroine shows exactly this first glimmer of self-knowledge.
Jainism
“If you owned the beauty of Vaiśramaṇa, the pleasing manners of Nalakūbara, if you were like Purandara himself, I should have no desire for you.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.41 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rājīmatī, now a nun, tells Rathanemi, who has fallen for her, that his longing is hopeless and recalls him to his vows.
The Kural’s heroine tells her heart the same hard truth: aching for someone who does not return your love is folly.
Sikhism
“I am ungraceful and ill-mannered, full of endless faults. How can I go to enjoy my Husband Lord?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 762, Raag Soohee (Kuchajji), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the hymn known as Kuchajji, Guru Nanak speaks as a soul-bride who blames her own faults, not her Husband, for the distance between them.
The Kural's wife tells her heart that its pain is folly because he has no love for them; Gurbani turns the charge around, teaching that the divine Husband never lacks love and the gap lies in the bride's own failings.
Greek Mythology
Back in his own palace, Odysseus lies awake seething, strikes his breast and rebukes his own heart, telling it to endure, since it once endured something worse, the day the Cyclops devoured his companions.
— Homer, Odyssey 20.17-21 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer shows a hero talking sternly to his own heart (kradiē) to master an overwhelming emotion.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Odysseus speaks to his heart as to a separate person, reproaching it for what it feels.
Philosophy
Pascal observes that the heart has its own reasons, which reason does not know.
— Blaise Pascal, Pensées, no. 277 (Brunschvicg numbering; 423 in Lafuma) (paraphrase) [11]
Pascal sees the heart as a faculty that loves and judges by its own logic, often against what reason advises.
The Kural’s heroine faces exactly this: her reason calls the heart’s ache folly, yet the heart goes on aching.

Real Life Example

Charlotte Brontë's unanswered letters to Constantin Héger
1844-1845 · Haworth, England, and Brussels, Belgium
After studying and teaching in Brussels, Charlotte Brontë kept writing to her former teacher Constantin Héger, a married man, making her feelings plain in January 1845. Héger barely replied and by the end of 1845 had stopped replying altogether; in one letter she wrote that a friend had brought her neither letter nor message from him. Four of her letters survive because Héger's wife retrieved the torn letters from the wastebasket and mended them, and his family gave them to the British Library in 1913.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The letters reveal the private heartbreak behind one of England's greatest novelists and show how long she went on hoping for an answer that never came.
The kural's heroine tells her heart that since he has no love for them, its suffering is mere foolishness, and Brontë kept pouring out her feelings to a man who would not reply.
