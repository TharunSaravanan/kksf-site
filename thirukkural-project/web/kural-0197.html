Kural 197

Not Speaking Profitless Words · Payanila Sollaamai · பயனில சொல்லாமை

நயனில சொல்லினுஞ் சொல்லுக சான்றோர் பயனில சொல்லாமை நன்று.
Nayanila sollinum solluka saandror, Payanila sollaamai nandru.

Meaning

Good people may say things that are not pleasant if they must. But it is good for them never to say things that are useless.

Elementary

A good coach may sometimes say something that stings, like “You need to practise harder!” That isn’t nice to hear, but it helps. What a good coach should never do is waste the team’s time with pointless chatter. This kural says: a hard word that helps is okay, but a useless word is not.

University

This is the chapter’s most philosophically daring couplet, because it ranks the two standards of speech against each other. நயனில (nayanila) is glossed by Kalaignar as ‘not sweet or pleasant’, by Pappaiah as ‘unjust’ and by Mu. Varadarasan as ‘lacking aram’. On the milder reading, Valluvar concedes that the சான்றோர் (saandror, the noble or accomplished) may speak unpleasantly, but insists that they must never speak uselessly. So usefulness (பயன்) is made the non-negotiable floor of speech, and pleasantness (நயன்) is a desirable but negotiable quality. This differs from Manu’s well-known rule against ‘disagreeable truth’, and it lines up closely with the Buddha’s criteria in the Abhaya Sutta, where beneficial but unwelcome words may be spoken at the right time and unbeneficial words never.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Let him say what is true, let him say what is pleasing, let him utter no disagreeable truth, and let him utter no agreeable falsehood; that is the eternal law.”
— Manusmriti 4.138 (trans. G. Bühler, SBE 25) [3]
Manu’s famous rule makes pleasantness a requirement of dharmic speech alongside truth.
This is an instructive contrast. Manu forbids ‘disagreeable truth’, while the Kural permits unpleasant words if they are useful. Both refuse to let empty pleasantness replace substance.
Christianity
“Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.”
— Proverbs 27:6 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs values honest words that hurt over flattering ones that betray.
Both texts accept painful words when they serve a real purpose.
Islam
“O you who have believed, fear Allah and speak words of appropriate justice.”
— Qur’an 33:70 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an asks believers for qawlan sadīdan, speech that is straight, sound and on target.
Both texts define good speech by soundness and aim rather than by softness. A straight word may not please, but it is never empty.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him.”
— Leviticus 19:17 (KJV) [4]
The Torah commands tochacha (rebuke), which is unwelcome speech that is still a duty because it benefits the hearer.
Both texts license unpleasant words when they are genuinely beneficial.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, unbeneficial, unendearing & disagreeable to others, he does not say them. … In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them.”
— Majjhima Nikaya 58, Abhaya Sutta (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
The Buddha sets out a grid of truth, benefit and pleasantness: unbeneficial words are never spoken, while beneficial but unpleasant words may be spoken at the right time.
This is almost exactly the Kural’s ranking: unpleasant words may be spoken if they are beneficial, and unbeneficial words are never spoken.
Jainism
“Wise, fearless monks consider even a rough instruction as a benefit, but the fools hate it, though it produces patience and purity of mind.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.29 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
The Uttarādhyayana teaches that a teacher's harsh but useful words are a real benefit to the wise, even if fools resent them.
Valluvar allows the worthy to say unpleasant things if they must but never useless ones, and the Jain text values rough words that do real good over pleasant ones that do none.
Sikhism
“Nanak speaks the Word of Truth; he proclaims the Truth at this, the right time.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 723, Raag Tilang, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In a hymn in Raag Tilang, sung during Babur's invasion, Guru Nanak openly voiced painful and unwelcome truths about the violence he saw, and closes by saying he speaks the truth because the hour requires it.
The Kural allows the worthy to say what is not pleasing, but never what is useless; Guru Nanak's hymn is a model of speech that is hard to hear yet full of purpose.
Greek Mythology
The blind seer Tiresias at first refuses to speak, then tells King Oedipus the painful truth that he himself is the polluter of Thebes, and is abused for it.
— Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 300-462 [9]
Tiresias’ words are deeply unwelcome, but they are the only words that can save the city.
Tiresias shows the Kural’s concession in action: the wise may say what is unpleasant, provided it carries real weight.
Philosophy
“I am that gadfly which God has given the state and all day long and in all places am always fastening upon you, arousing and persuading and reproaching you.”
— Plato, Apology 30e (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Socrates defends his irritating questions as a benefit to Athens, like a gadfly stinging a sluggish horse.
Socrates’ speech is famously unpleasant yet purposeful, which fits the Kural’s ideal of the sage who may sting but never babble.

Real Life Example

Ignaz Semmelweis orders doctors to wash their hands
1847 · Vienna General Hospital, Vienna, Austria
At the First Obstetrical Clinic of Vienna General Hospital, where women attended by doctors and medical students died of childbed fever far more often than those in the midwives' clinic, the Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis concluded that doctors coming from autopsies were carrying the cause on their hands. In 1847 he required doctors and students to disinfect their hands with a chlorinated lime solution before examining patients, and the death rate fell sharply. His message was unwelcome to many colleagues, his ideas were largely rejected during his lifetime, and he returned to Pest in 1850; they were accepted only after his death.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Semmelweis told his profession something it did not want to hear, and it saved lives wherever it was put into practice.
The kural allows the worthy to say what is unpleasant but never what is useless, and Semmelweis's unpopular instruction was entirely unpleasant to its hearers and entirely useful.
