Kural 245

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

அல்லல் அருளாள்வார்க்கு இல்லை வளிவழங்கும் மல்லன்மா ஞாலங் கரி.
Allal arulaalvaarkku illai valivazhangum, Mallanmaa gnaalang kari.

Meaning

Suffering never comes to people who live with kindness. This great, rich earth, where the wind blows, is the proof.

Elementary

Look around the whole wide world, where the wind blows over every field and sea. Valluvar says the whole world can see that kind people are not the ones who end up miserable. The earth itself is the witness.

University

Valluvar calls the whole wind-swept earth as a witness (kari), a legal image in which the world itself testifies to a moral regularity. Parimelazhagar understands the witness as the people of the world, who can see for themselves that the compassionate do not suffer. Kalaignar offers a physical metaphor instead: as the moving wind sustains the vast earth, so the inner movement of arul sustains a person through hardship. The claim, that the kind escape sorrow, is empirically bold and can be read two ways: as an external promise of protection, or as an internal truth that allal (inner affliction) cannot take root in a mind at peace with all beings. The second reading brings the kural close to the Stoic claim that genuine benevolence is invincible and to the Buddhist list of the benefits of mettā.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those devotees are very dear to Me who are free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate... equipoised in happiness and distress, and ever-forgiving.”
— Bhagavad Gita 12.13-14 (trans. Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna links the compassionate temperament (karuṇa) with evenness of mind in joy and sorrow.
The compassionate are not overthrown by sorrow, which is a psychological version of the kural’s allal illai.
Christianity
“for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.”
— Matthew 5:45 (KJV) [4]
Jesus points to sun and rain, the ordinary workings of nature, as evidence of God’s impartial kindness, which disciples should imitate.
Like Valluvar’s wind-swept earth, nature itself bears witness to universal benevolence; Jesus stresses God’s generosity rather than the compassionate being spared sorrow.
Islam
“But My mercy encompasses everything.”
— Quran 7:156 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
God tells Moses that divine mercy extends over all creation, and that He will ordain it for those who shun evil.
The whole of creation is held within mercy, a cosmic backdrop like the earth that witnesses to the security of the compassionate.
Judaism
“For I have said, Mercy shall be built up for ever”
— Psalm 89:2 (KJV) [4]
The Hebrew olam chesed yibaneh is read in Jewish tradition as ‘the world is built by lovingkindness’, making chesed the structure on which the world stands.
The world itself rests on and testifies to lovingkindness, much as the kural calls the great earth to witness.
Buddhism
“One sleeps easily, wakes easily, dreams no evil dreams. One is dear to human beings, dear to non-human beings. The devas protect one. Neither fire, poison, nor weapons can touch one.”
— Metta Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 11.16 (trans. Thanissaro) [6]
The Buddha lists eleven benefits for one who cultivates the release of mind through good will.
An explicit catalogue of the kural’s promise: the one who lives by compassion is shielded from affliction.
Jainism
Umasvati's Tattvartha Sutra lists compassion (anukampa) for all living beings and for those who keep vows, together with charity, self-restraint with some attachment, forbearance and purity, as the causes of the inflow of pleasant-feeling (sata-vedaniya) karma.
— Tattvartha Sutra 6.12 (Digambara numbering; 6.13 in the Svetambara recension), as given with commentary by Vijay K. Jain (wisdomlib.org) [7]
In Jain karma theory, compassion attracts the karma that later ripens as pleasant experience, while causing pain to others attracts karma that ripens as suffering.
Valluvar says sorrow does not come to those who live by compassion; the Tattvartha Sutra gives the Jain mechanism, in which compassion binds karma whose fruit is pleasant feeling rather than pain.
Sikhism
“The mythical bull is Dharma, the son of compassion; this is what patiently holds the earth in its place.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 3, Japji Sahib, Pauri 16, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Japji reinterprets the myth of the bull that bears the world: what really holds up the earth is dharma, born of compassion.
Valluvar calls the wind-swept earth to witness that the compassionate do not suffer; Guru Nanak goes further and makes compassion, through dharma, the very support of the earth.
Greek Mythology
Hesiod says that where people deal straight judgements to strangers and citizens alike, the city flourishes: peace settles on the land, the earth bears plenty, oaks bear acorns, and flocks are heavy with wool.
— Hesiod, Works and Days 225-237 [9]
In Hesiod the fertility of the land visibly reflects the righteousness of its people.
The abundant earth bears witness to virtue, a close echo of mallanmaa gnaalam (the great, fertile world) as witness; Hesiod’s virtue is justice rather than compassion.
Philosophy
“a good disposition is invincible, if it be genuine, and not an affected smile and acting a part.”
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.18 (trans. George Long) [10]
Marcus asks what even the most violent man can do to someone who remains kind toward him.
Genuine benevolence cannot be defeated by others’ harm, a Stoic form of ‘sorrow does not come to the compassionate’.

Real Life Example

The public rallies to Mary Seacole after her bankruptcy
1856-1857 · London, England
Mary Seacole, a Jamaican-born businesswoman and healer, ran a hotel and store near the front in the Crimean War, where she fed and cared for British soldiers. At the war's end she returned to England destitute and was declared bankrupt. Military admirers raised funds for her, a benefit festival in 1857 attracted thousands of people, and her memoir Wonderful Adventures of Mrs Seacole in Many Lands, published the same year, sold well.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The kindness she had shown to others was publicly remembered, and the public's support lifted her out of ruin.
The kural says sorrow does not stay with those who live by compassion and that the wide world stands witness, and in Seacole's case the people of the world themselves came to her aid.
