Kural 374

Fate · Uzhi · ஊழ்

இருவேறு உலகத்து இயற்கை திருவேறு தெள்ளிய ராதலும் வேறு.
Iruveru ulagaththu iyarkai thiruveru Thelliya raadhalum veru.

Meaning

Fate works in two different ways in the world. Becoming rich is one thing, and becoming wise is quite another.

Elementary

Some kids are great at sports and others are great at math, and being good at one doesn’t make you good at the other. This kural says money and wisdom work the same way: being rich and being wise come from different kinds of luck, so one doesn’t guarantee the other.

University

The couplet makes an observation anyone can check: wealth (திரு, thiru, also the name of the goddess of fortune) and clear wisdom (தெள்ளியர் ஆதல்) are handed out on separate tracks. The rich are often not wise, and the wise are often not rich. Commentators read இருவேறு இயற்கை as two distinct operations of ஊழ். Indian folk tradition expresses the same idea as the old rivalry between Lakshmi (wealth) and Saraswati (learning). Ecclesiastes’ remark that bread is not always to the wise nor riches to the understanding, the Talmudic teaching on sustenance depending on mazal rather than merit, and Aristotle’s puzzle about fortunate fools all record the same gap between intelligence and worldly reward.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Among women I am fame, fortune, fine speech, memory, intelligence, steadfastness and patience.”
— Bhagavad Gita 10.34 (tr. Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna lists fortune (śrī) and intelligence (medhā) as separate divine manifestations.
Wealth and wisdom appear as distinct gifts, matching the Kural’s ‘two different natures’.
Christianity
“The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all.”
— Ecclesiastes 9:11 (KJV) [4]
The Preacher notes that wisdom and understanding do not guarantee bread or riches.
An almost exact match: riches and wisdom are separate lots, and one does not bring the other.
Islam
“It is We who have apportioned among them their livelihood in the life of this world and have raised some of them above others in degrees [of rank].”
— Qur'an 43:32 (Saheeh International) [5]
God Himself apportions people’s livelihoods and ranks.
Worldly wealth is a separately apportioned lot, as the Kural says.
Judaism
Rava teaches that life, children and sustenance depend not on merit but on mazal (fortune). The Talmud illustrates this with two righteous sages, Rabba and Rav Ḥisda: one household enjoyed plenty while the other struggled for bread.
— Babylonian Talmud, Moed Katan 28a [6]
Equal virtue and learning did not produce equal prosperity, because sustenance follows fortune.
A vivid illustration of the Kural: being wise and being rich are separate lots.
Buddhism
In the Shorter Exposition of Kamma, the Buddha explains that beings are owners and heirs of their kamma, and that kamma is what separates beings into rich and poor, and into wise and foolish, each through its own specific causes.
— Majjhima Nikaya 135 (Cula-kammavibhanga Sutta) [7]
Wealth comes from one kind of past action (generosity) and wisdom from another (inquiry), so the two are distributed separately.
Close to the Kural: wealth and wisdom come from two different workings of past deeds.
Jainism
“Antarâya is fivefold as preventing: 1. gifts; 2. profit; 3. momentary enjoyment; 4. continuous enjoyment; and 5. power.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 33.15 (cf. 33.2–4) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Jainism keeps the karma that blocks profit and enjoyment (antarāya) separate from the karma that obscures knowledge (jñānāvaraṇīya, 33.4). Because they are distinct karmas, a person's share of wealth and share of wisdom are governed separately.
Valluvar observes that fate works in two different ways, one making people rich and the other making them wise. The Jain list of karmas likewise assigns gain and understanding to two different karmic causes.
Sikhism
“By His Command, some are high and some are low; by His Written Command, pain and pleasure are obtained.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1, Japji Sahib, Pauri 2, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the second pauri of Japji Guru Nanak teaches that hukam, the divine order, assigns people different stations and different shares of pain and pleasure.
The Kural observes that fate deals out wealth and wisdom on two separate tracks; Japji likewise sees people's unequal portions as handed out by the one divine order rather than earned by merit alone.
Greek Mythology
In the myth Protagoras tells, Epimetheus hands out distinct endowments to each creature: speed to some, strength to others. He runs out before reaching humans, so Prometheus steals craft-wisdom and fire for them.
— Plato, Protagoras 320c-322a [10]
Gifts are handed out one at a time, and no creature receives everything.
Like the Kural’s two separate operations of fate, endowments are allotted separately rather than all together.
Philosophy
Aristotle observes that some foolish people succeed in many things where luck is in control, and asks whether good fortune (eutuchia) is a natural gift quite separate from practical wisdom.
— Aristotle, Eudemian Ethics VIII.2 (1246b-1248b) [11]
Aristotle notices that success often lands on the unwise, and he tries to explain luck as something other than intelligence.
The same observation as the Kural: fortune and wisdom run on different tracks.

Real Life Example

Spinoza declines the chair of philosophy at Heidelberg
1673 · The Hague, Dutch Republic (now the Netherlands)
Baruch (Benedict de) Spinoza, expelled from Amsterdam's Jewish community in 1656, supported himself by grinding optical lenses and lived in modest rented rooms. In 1673 he was offered the chair of philosophy at the University of Heidelberg but declined it, out of concern that he would not have complete freedom to teach as he wished. He died in 1677, and his Ethics, published after his death, became one of the most influential works of Western philosophy.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One of the great minds of the seventeenth century lived and died with very little money, while his ideas proved far more valuable than any fortune.
The kural says fate works in two different ways, so that becoming wealthy is one thing and becoming wise is another, and Spinoza's life of modest lens-grinding alongside his towering philosophy shows how separate the two can be.
