Kural 777

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

சுழலும் இசைவேண்டி வேண்டா உயிரார் கழல்யாப்புக் காரிகை நீர்த்து.
Suzhalum isaivendi vendaa uyiraar Kazhalyaappuk kaarigai neerththu.

Meaning

For people who want fame that spreads across the world and do not worry about their own lives, wearing the warrior’s ankle ring is a true decoration.

Elementary

A medal looks most beautiful on someone who earned it by being brave for others. The warrior’s anklet in this verse is like that medal: it is beautiful because of the courage of the one who wears it.

University

Suzhalum isai is fame that “circles” or spreads around the world; vendaa uyiraar are those who do not desire (cling to) life; kazhal yaappu is the tying on of the kazhal, the heroic anklet worn by warriors in Tamil tradition; and kaarigai neerththu means it has the nature of a true ornament. The point is that the badge of valour is fitting only on those whose courage is real: on others it would be mere decoration. Solomon Pappaiah glosses isai as the fame that stays on earth after death, unlike the hero who departs. Both base texts agree on the wording; the sandhi யாப்புக் = yaappu + k.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“People will always speak of your infamy, and for a respectable person, dishonor is worse than death.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.34 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna warns Arjuna that the shame of retreat would follow him forever.
This is the obverse of the Kural’s fame-seeking warrior who values renown over life.
Christianity
“I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith: Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness”
— 2 Timothy 4:7-8 (KJV) [4]
Near death, Paul looks back on his life as a completed contest and looks forward to a crown from God.
Paul’s crown is like the Kural’s anklet, a mark of honour, but it is awarded by God, not by worldly fame.
Islam
“And never think of those who have been killed in the cause of Allah as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision,”
— Qur’an 3:169 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an assures that those who die for God’s cause live on with Him.
The Kural promises the fallen hero lasting fame among men; the Qur’an promises life with God instead, a different kind of immortality.
Judaism
“A good name is better than fragrant oil, and the day of death than the day of birth.”
— Ecclesiastes 7:1 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet values a good name above luxuries and sees its full worth only at death.
Like the Kural, it ranks reputation above the ornaments and comforts of life.
Buddhism
“Self-conquest is far better than the conquest of others. Not even a god, an angel, Mara or Brahma can turn into defeat the victory of a person who is self-subdued and ever restrained in conduct.”
— Dhammapada 104-105 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada points to the one victory that no one can take away.
The Kural prizes a fame that circles the world; Buddhism prizes a victory that cannot be undone, a different idea of what lasts.
Jainism
“But famous warriors, leaders of heroes at the time of the battle, do not look behind them; (they think) what if all end with death?”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga 1.3.3.6 (cf. 1.3.3.7) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Unlike the coward who looks for a hiding-place, the true warrior fights without caring for his life, and the monk is told to 'exert himself in a similar way' (1.3.3.7).
The Kural says the hero's anklet belongs only to those who do not cling to life, and the Sutrakritanga uses the same warrior's indifference to death as its model of resolve.
Sikhism
“If you desire to play this game of love with Me, then step onto My Path with your head in hand. When you place your feet on this Path, give Me your head, and do not pay any attention to public opinion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412, Salok Vaaraan Thay Vadheek, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak asks the seeker to come ready to give up life itself; Sikh tradition links this salok to the Khalsa's founding call for heads in 1699.
The Kural says the warrior's anklet suits only those who do not cling to life; Guru Nanak says the path is open only to one who comes with his head on his palm.
Greek Mythology
Achilles tells the embassy that his mother foretold two fates: if he stays at Troy he will die young but his glory (kleos) will be imperishable; if he goes home he will live long without fame.
— Homer, Iliad 9.410-416 (paraphrase) [10]
The choice between long life and undying fame is the defining dilemma of the Greek hero.
The Kural’s warriors who “do not want life” but seek world-wide fame have made Achilles’ choice.
Philosophy
Aristotle identifies a “political” or citizen courage, closest to true courage, in which soldiers face danger because of the honours that come to the brave and the disgrace that comes to cowards.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.8 (paraphrase) [11]
Aristotle sees honour-driven courage as admirable but a step below courage chosen purely because it is noble.
The Kural’s fame-seeking warrior fits this honour-driven courage, and Aristotle helps show both its worth and its limit.

Real Life Example

Queen Victoria institutes the Victoria Cross for valour
29 January 1856; first investiture 26 June 1857 · London, England, United Kingdom
A royal warrant of 29 January 1856 created the Victoria Cross as Britain's highest award for gallantry in the face of the enemy, backdated to cover acts in the Crimean War. Unlike earlier honours, it was open to all ranks. At the first investiture, in Hyde Park on 26 June 1857, Queen Victoria personally presented the cross to 62 recipients before large crowds.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The decoration was deliberately reserved for proven courage rather than rank or birth, so that wearing it meant one had risked one's life.
The kural says the warrior's anklet is a true ornament only on those who seek lasting fame and do not cling to life, and the Victoria Cross was designed as just such a badge, fitting only on those whose valour is real.
