Kural 329

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

கொலைவினைய ராகிய மாக்கள் புலைவினையர் புன்மை தெரிவா ரகத்து.
Kolaivinaiya raagiya maakkal pulaivinaiyar Punmai therivaa ragaththu.

Meaning

People who kill for a living are seen as doers of shameful deeds by those who understand what truly low behavior is.

Elementary

Some jobs make you proud, like helping people or growing food. Valluvar says that a job built on killing living things makes wise people think less of you, because they can see how it hardens the heart.

University

The couplet names the kolaiviṉaiyar, those whose “occupation” (viṉai) is killing, and uses the word mākkaḷ — often reserved for people who have lost human discernment — to describe them. In the minds of those who discern baseness (puṉmai terivār), they are pulaiviṉaiyar, doers of polluted, degraded work. The verse is a moral judgement rather than a caste statement, though the vocabulary of pulai (pollution) reflects the social idiom of its time and should be read critically today. Its underlying insight — that habitual killing corrodes compassion — is shared by the Buddha’s list of wrong livelihoods and by Thomas More’s Utopians, who kept citizens from butchery lest it destroy the feeling of mercy.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He who permits (the slaughter of an animal), he who cuts it up, he who kills it, he who buys or sells (meat), he who cooks it, he who serves it up, and he who eats it, (must all be considered as) the slayers (of the animal).”
— Manusmriti 5.51 (G. Bühler trans.) [4]
Manu extends the guilt of killing to everyone in the chain of slaughter and trade.
Like the Kural, it condemns those whose work is bound up with killing.
Christianity
“Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer: and ye know that no murderer hath eternal life abiding in him.”
— 1 John 3:15 (KJV) [5]
John teaches that the murderer, and even the hater, is cut off from the life of God.
Shares the Kural’s verdict that the killer is spiritually degraded.
Islam
“But whoever kills a believer intentionally - his recompense is Hell, wherein he will abide eternally, and Allāh has become angry with him and has cursed him and has prepared for him a great punishment.”
— Qur’an 4:93 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an pronounces a curse and severe punishment on the deliberate killer of a believer.
Like the Kural, it marks the killer as degraded and cursed in the eyes of the just.
Judaism
“What hast thou done? the voice of thy brother’s blood crieth unto me from the ground.”
— Genesis 4:10–12 (KJV) [5]
After Cain murders Abel, God curses him to be a fugitive and a wanderer on the earth.
The first killer becomes the first outcast — the degradation the Kural attributes to killers.
Buddhism
“Monks, a lay follower should not engage in five types of business. Which five? Business in weapons, business in human beings, business in meat, business in intoxicants, and business in poison.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 5.177, Vanijja Sutta (Thanissaro Bhikkhu trans.) [7]
The Buddha lists trades that are wrong livelihood because they depend on harming or killing.
Directly parallels the Kural’s kolaiviṉaiyar — those whose livelihood is killing are on the wrong path.
Jainism
“Be without fear, O king; but grant safety to others also; in this transient world of living beings, why are you addicted to cruelty?”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 18.2 and 18.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
King Sañjaya, out hunting, killed a frightened deer and then met the monk Gardabhāli, who rebuked his habit of killing; the king renounced his throne.
The Kural brands those who make killing their pursuit as doers of base deeds; the monk names the hunting king's killing 'cruelty' and turns him from it.
Sikhism
“Those who suck the blood of human beings-how can their consciousness be pure?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 140, Raag Maajh, Maajh ki Vaar, Salok, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak declares that those who live by preying on others cannot have a pure mind, whatever outward rituals they keep.
The Kural calls those who make killing their work doers of base deeds in the eyes of the discerning; Guru Nanak likewise judges those who live off others' blood as inwardly impure.
Greek Mythology
Ixion, remembered as the first mortal to shed kindred blood, was shunned by gods and men so completely that no one would purify him, until Zeus himself did — and Ixion later ended bound on a fiery wheel.
— Pindar, Pythian 2.21–48 [10]
Pindar makes Ixion the archetype of the killer who becomes an outcast.
Shows the killer as vile and shunned in the eyes of the discerning, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
In More’s Utopia, citizens are not allowed to slaughter animals themselves, because the Utopians believe that butchery gradually kills the feeling of mercy, the gentlest affection of human nature.
— Thomas More, Utopia, Book 2 [11]
Thomas More imagines a society that protects its citizens’ compassion by keeping them away from killing as a trade.
Shares the Kural’s insight that the work of killing degrades the one who does it.

Real Life Example

Constantine issues a law against gladiatorial shows
325 CE · Roman Empire
In 325 the emperor Constantine issued a law, preserved in the Theodosian Code, stating that bloody spectacles had no place in a time of civil peace and domestic quiet and prohibiting gladiators. Criminals who would previously have been condemned to fight as gladiators were to be sent to work in the mines instead. The law was not fully enforced, and gladiatorial games continued in some places until they were finally suppressed decades later.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
An emperor publicly declared the trade of killing for entertainment unfit for a peaceful society, even though the games took decades more to disappear.
The kural says those who make killing their work are seen as base by the discerning, and Constantine's law declared the gladiator's bloody trade unfit for a civilised peace.
