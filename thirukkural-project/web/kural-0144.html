Kural 144

Not Coveting Another's Wife · Piranil Vizhaiyaamai · பிறனில் விழையாமை

எனைத்துணையர் ஆயினும் என்னாம் தினைத்துணையும் தேரான் பிறனில் புகல்.
Enaiththunaiyar aayinum ennaam thinaiththunaiyum, Theraan piranil pugal.

Meaning

However great a man may be, what is his greatness worth if he goes after another man’s wife without thinking even a little about how wrong it is?

Elementary

Imagine the best player on a team, who is famous and loved by everyone. If that player cheated in a very unfair way without stopping to think, all their fame would be worth nothing. This kural says the same about a person who goes after someone else's husband or wife: being important cannot save them.

University

The couplet plays two measures against each other: எனைத்துணை ('however great a measure') and தினைத்துணை ('a millet grain's measure'), the smallest unit in Tamil idiom. A man may possess vast greatness, yet if he does not reflect (தேரான்) for even a millet grain's worth before entering another's home, that greatness is worthless. The rhetorical question என்னாம் ('what does it become?') leaves the answer, nothing, to the listener. The emphasis on failing to reflect links to the chapter's theme of folly: the danger lies in the thoughtless moment in which rank and reputation are thrown away, a pattern visible in stories of kings and heroes across many cultures.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Mahabharata, Kichaka, the powerful commander of King Virata's army, becomes infatuated with Draupadi, the wife of the Pandavas, who is living disguised as a maid at the court. Ignoring her refusals and warnings, he pursues her and is killed at night by Bhima.
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva (narrative summary; trans. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
Kichaka's power and position did him no good once he recklessly pursued another man's wife.
This is the Kural's point: however great one is, entering another's home for his wife without a moment's thought ends in ruin.
Christianity
“For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?”
— Mark 8:36 (KJV) [4]
Jesus asks what worldly success is worth if a person loses their soul in the process.
Same rhetorical move as the Kural: 'however great he may be, what is it worth?' if he destroys himself through wrongdoing.
Islam
“And do not approach unlawful sexual intercourse. Indeed, it is ever an immorality and is evil as a way.”
— Qur'an 17:32 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an forbids not only the act but even approaching it, calling it an evil path.
The Kural blames the man who steps into another's home without a moment's reflection; the Qur'an tells believers to stop well before the first step.
Judaism
“And Nathan said to David, Thou art the man. Thus saith the LORD God of Israel, I anointed thee king over Israel, and I delivered thee out of the hand of Saul; ... thou hast killed Uriah the Hittite with the sword, and hast taken his wife to be thy wife.”
— 2 Samuel 12:7-9 (KJV) [4]
The prophet Nathan confronts King David: God had given him a kingdom, yet he took another man's wife and arranged her husband's death.
David is the Kural's 'however great' man: a king whose greatness could not cover his taking of another's wife.
Buddhism
“Who plays with dice and drinks intoxicants, goes to women who are dear unto others as their own lives, associates with the mean and not with elders — he declines just as the moon during the waning half.”
— Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31 (trans. Narada Thera) [6]
The Buddha says that a man who goes to women cherished by others loses his standing, fading away like the waning moon.
Both texts say whatever brightness a man has, he loses it by going after another's cherished wife.
Jainism
“Some men of great intelligence who perform their duties as supporters of women, get into their power … (The adulterers’) hands and feet are cut off, their skin and flesh are torn off, they are roasted alive, and acid is poured into their wounds.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.4.1.20–21 (cf. 22) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [7]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga warns that even men of great intelligence are brought down by illicit passion and then suffer the brutal punishments of the day, and yet, it adds, they 'will not promise not to do the same again'.
Valluvar asks what a man's greatness is worth if he enters another's home without weighing the wrong even a millet-grain's worth, and the Sūtrakṛtāṅga shows great intelligence counting for nothing once a man is in passion's power.
Sikhism
“Ahalyaa was the wife of Gautam the seer. Seeing her, Indra was enticed. When he received a thousand marks of disgrace on his body, then he felt regret in his mind.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1344, Raag Prabhati, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak retells how Indra, king of the gods, lusted after a sage's wife and was marked with disgrace, regretting it only afterwards.
The Kural asks what greatness is worth if a man enters another's home without a moment's thought; Indra's story shows the highest rank undone by exactly that thoughtlessness.
Greek Mythology
“Ill deeds do not prosper, and the weak confound the strong. See how limping Vulcan, lame as he is, has caught Mars who is the fleetest god in heaven.”
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 8 (trans. Samuel Butler) [9]
When Ares (Mars) is caught with Hephaestus's (Vulcan's) wife Aphrodite in a hidden net, the other gods laugh that even the swiftest, strongest god was trapped.
Just as the Kural says, great strength and rank were worth nothing to a god who went after another's wife without thinking.
Philosophy
“Now in everything the pleasant or pleasure is most to be guarded against; for we do not judge it impartially. We ought, then, to feel towards pleasure as the elders of the people felt towards Helen.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.9 (trans. W. D. Ross) [10]
Aristotle warns that pleasure clouds our judgement, and says we should treat it as the wise Trojan elders treated Helen: admiring her beauty, yet wishing her sent away to avoid ruin.
The Kural blames the man who does not reflect for even a moment; Aristotle says pleasure is exactly what stops us reflecting, so it must be kept at a distance.

Real Life Example

The O'Shea divorce case ends Parnell's leadership
1890 · London, United Kingdom, and Ireland
Charles Stewart Parnell, leader of the Irish Parliamentary Party, was at the height of his popularity and often called Ireland's uncrowned king when Captain William O'Shea filed for divorce from his wife Katharine at the end of 1889, naming Parnell as co-respondent. The case was not defended and the divorce was granted in November 1890. When Parnell refused to step down, Gladstone made clear that the Liberal alliance would end, the Catholic hierarchy declared against him, and in December 1890 most of his party withdrew from his leadership; he died in October 1891 still fighting to regain it.
— Sources: [11] [12]
The split divided Irish nationalism and damaged the cause of Home Rule for years, showing how one private choice could undo a great public career.
The kural asks what a man's greatness is worth if he enters another's home for his wife; Parnell's immense standing proved worthless once the affair became public.
