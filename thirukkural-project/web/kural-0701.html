Kural 701

The Knowledge of Signals · Kurippu Arithal · குறிப்பறிதல்

கூறாமை நோக்கக் குறிப்பறிவான் எஞ்ஞான்றும் மாறாநீர் வையக் கணி.
Kooraamai nokkak kurippu-arivaan enjnjaandrum Maaraaneer vaiyak kani.

Meaning

A person who can look at someone and know what they mean without a word being said is always a jewel to this world surrounded by the sea.

Elementary

Imagine your friend comes to school looking quiet and droopy. Nobody says anything, but you notice, sit next to them, and share your snack. You understood without being told. Valluvar says a person who can do that is like a jewel the whole world wears.

University

The chapter opens a sequence in the Amaicciyal (section on ministers) that follows directly on ‘Conduct in the Presence of the King’ (ch. 70): having learned how near to stand, the minister must now learn to read the ruler’s unspoken kurippu (intention, inner purpose shown by outward sign). Parimelazhagar reads the subject as the minister and the object as the king: kooraamai (without his saying it) and nokka (by looking, i.e., at the face and eyes) he knows the intent, and such a man is ani (ornament, jewel) to the vaiyam (earth) whose maaraaneer (never-failing water, the ocean) surrounds it; enjnjaandrum means ‘at all times’. The hyperbole of ‘ornament to the world’ rather than ‘to the court’ signals that this faculty is rare and universally prized. The same title, Kurippu Arithal, reappears as chapter 110 in the Kamattuppal, where lovers read each other’s glances; commentators note the deliberate echo between political and amorous sign-reading. The standard text reads nokka; one online sandhi edition prints நோக்ககிக், evidently a misprint for நோக்கக் (some editions give நோக்கிக், with the same sense).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He shall also endeavour to read the mind and appearance of the king.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 5.5 (‘Time-Serving’), trans. R. Shamasastry [3]
In the chapter on how a courtier serves the ruler, Kautilya makes reading the king’s mood from his looks and movements a standing duty of the minister.
Kautilya names the same skill Valluvar praises: knowing the ruler’s intent from his appearance before he speaks.
Christianity
“Wherefore the king said unto me, Why is thy countenance sad, seeing thou art not sick? this is nothing else but sorrow of heart.”
— Nehemiah 2:2 (KJV) [4]
King Artaxerxes sees his cupbearer Nehemiah’s face and correctly reads grief in his heart before Nehemiah has said anything.
A royal court scene in which the unspoken heart is read from the face, as the Kural describes.
Islam
“An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign. They do not ask people persistently [or at all].”
— Qur’an 2:273 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an describes needy people too dignified to beg; the careless think they are fine, but the perceptive recognise their need from outward signs.
The praised person is the one who knows a need that is never spoken, just as the Kural praises reading the unsaid.
Judaism
“And Joseph came in unto them in the morning, and saw them, and, behold, they were sad. And he asked Pharaoh’s officers ... saying: ‘Wherefore look ye so sad to-day?’”
— Genesis 40:6-7 (JPS 1917) [6]
Joseph, a prisoner, notices from their faces that Pharaoh’s butler and baker are troubled, and that attentiveness leads to his interpreting their dreams and later to his rise at court.
Joseph’s rise begins with reading unspoken trouble on a face, the very skill the Kural says makes a man a jewel.
Buddhism
“‘Such is your thinking, here is where your thinking is, thus is your mind.’ And however much he may read, that’s exactly how it is, and not otherwise.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.60, Sangarava Sutta, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
The Buddha lists reading another’s mind, first of all by means of a sign, as one of three recognised wonders, and says the accurate reader is never wrong.
Buddhism likewise treats accurately knowing another’s unspoken mind as a rare and admired power.
Jainism
“A monk who, on receiving an order from his superior, walks up to him, watching his nods and motions, is called well-behaved.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana opens its lecture on discipline by defining the good disciple as one who attends to his teacher's unspoken nods and gestures, not only to his words.
Like the Kural, it treats reading a superior's silent signs as the mark of the person most worth having in the presence of authority.
Sikhism
“He alone is a Sikh, a friend, a relative and a sibling, who walks in the Way of the Guru's Will.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 601, Raag Sorath, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das defines the true disciple as one who lives inside the Guru's bhana (will), already moving with it rather than waiting to be told.
The Kural praises the one who grasps a master's intention without a word being said; Gurbani honours the Sikh who is so attuned that he walks within the Guru's will.
Greek Mythology
“No one noticed his distress except Alcinous, who was sitting near him, and heard the heavy sighs that he was heaving.”
— Homer, Odyssey 8 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Odysseus hides his tears under his cloak while the bard sings of Troy; only King Alcinous perceives his grief and tactfully ends the song.
Alcinous is the Kural’s ornament in action: he understands a guest’s silent sorrow and acts on it without being asked.
Philosophy
Seneca tells Lucilius that a person’s character can be gathered from the smallest things: the way they walk, move a hand, answer, or carry their face.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 52 (trans. R. M. Gummere) [11]
For the Stoic, outward signs are constant evidence of the inner man for anyone who pays attention.
Seneca supports the Kural’s premise that the attentive can know a mind without being told.

Real Life Example

Jane Goodall watches David Greybeard make and use a tool
1960 · Gombe Stream, Tanzania
In mid-1960 Jane Goodall, a 26-year-old without formal scientific training, began a field study of wild chimpanzees at Gombe on Lake Tanganyika for the paleoanthropologist Louis Leakey. After months of patient watching she gained the trust of an older male she named David Greybeard, and late in 1960 she saw him strip a twig and use it to fish termites from a mound, evidence that chimpanzees make and use tools. Her long study went on to reveal chimpanzee personalities, emotions and social bonds, and she became one of the best-known naturalists in the world.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By reading the gestures and expressions of animals that could tell her nothing in words, Goodall changed how science understood both chimpanzees and humans.
The kural praises the person who understands what others intend without a word being said as an ornament to the world, and Goodall's wordless understanding of the chimpanzees made her a figure honoured worldwide.
