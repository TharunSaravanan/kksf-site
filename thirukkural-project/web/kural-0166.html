Kural 166

Anti-envy · Azhukkaaraamai · அழுக்காறாமை

கொடுப்பது அழுக்கறுப்பான் சுற்றம் உடுப்பதூஉம் உண்பதூஉம் இன்றிக் கெடும்.
Koduppadhu azhukkaruppaan sutram uduppadhoovum Unbadhoovum indrik kedum.

Meaning

A person who is jealous when others receive gifts will be ruined, and their family with them, left with no clothes to wear and no food to eat.

Elementary

Imagine a kid who gets upset every time a teacher gives stickers to others. Eventually, nobody wants to share with that kid or their friends either. Being jealous of other people's gifts leaves you, and the people around you, with nothing.

University

This couplet narrows envy to a specific form: resentment of generosity itself (கொடுப்பது, “what is given”), whether one objects to a gift going to someone else or tries to stop it. The punishment is social and material: not only the envier but his சுற்றம் (kin, dependents) are left without food or clothing, since the grudging spirit cuts the household off from the web of mutual giving. The idea has a close counterpart in the Buddha's teaching that whoever obstructs another's gift harms three parties, beginning with himself. Pirkei Avot likewise names the giver who wishes others would not give as having an “evil eye” toward what belongs to others.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When King Bali prepares to grant three steps of land to the dwarf Vamana, his preceptor Shukracharya urges him to withhold the gift. Bali refuses, gives anyway, and is ultimately blessed; later retellings add that Shukra lost an eye trying to block the gift.
— Bhagavata Purana 8.19-20 [3]
Shukra tries to stop a gift from being given, and his obstruction fails.
The Kural condemns the one who resents or blocks another's giving; Shukra's attempt is a well-known illustration of that failure.
Christianity
“Is it not lawful for me to do what I will with mine own? Is thine eye evil, because I am good?”
— Matthew 20:15 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the vineyard workers, the owner answers those who resent his generosity to latecomers: are they envious (“evil eye”) because he is generous?
Jesus confronts exactly the envy the Kural condemns: resenting what is given to someone else.
Islam
“Who are stingy and enjoin upon [other] people stinginess and conceal what Allāh has given them of His bounty - and We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment -”
— Qur'an 4:37 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an condemns those who are stingy themselves and also urge others not to give.
Both texts condemn those who obstruct others' generosity and warn of humiliating loss.
Judaism
“There are four types of charity givers. He who wishes to give, but that others should not give: his eye is evil to that which belongs to others.”
— Pirkei Avot 5:13 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah singles out the person who resents others' giving as having an “evil eye” toward what belongs to them.
This is the Kural's exact target: resentment of what others give.
Buddhism
“Whoever prevents another from giving a gift creates three obstructions, three impediments. Which three? He creates an obstruction to the merit of the giver, an obstruction to the recipient's gains, and prior to that he undermines and harms his own self.”
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.57 (Vaccha Sutta), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu [7]
The Buddha tells Vacchagotta that blocking someone else's gift harms the giver, the receiver, and, first of all, the one who blocks it.
Both texts teach that begrudging another's giving ends in the begrudger's own ruin.
Jainism
“Laying obstacles (vighna) (in way of charity, etc.) is the cause of the influx of obstructive (antarāya) karmas.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 6.27, with Pūjyapāda's Sarvārthasiddhi (tr. Vijay K. Jain, 2018) [8]
Jain doctrine holds that obstructing another's giving, gain or enjoyment binds antarāya karma, which in turn obstructs the obstructer's own future charity, gain, and enjoyment of food, clothing and other goods.
Valluvar warns that one who resents what is given to others will end without food or clothing, and the Tattvārtha's karma law says the same: blocking another's gift blocks one's own receiving.
Sikhism
“The True Lord and Master is on the side of the True Guru; and so, all those who oppose Him waste away to death in anger, envy and conflict.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 307, Raag Gauree (Gauri ki Vaar), Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that people who resented the greatness the Lord had given the Guru could not take it away, and wore themselves out in envy and strife.
The Kural warns that one who resents what is given to others perishes along with his kin; Guru Ram Das describes the envious who begrudge God's gift to another wasting themselves away.
Greek Mythology
“When Envy travels to Athens, “wherever she passes she tramples the flower-filled fields, withers the grass, blasts the highest treetops and poisons homes, cities and peoples with her breath.”
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.787-796 (trans. A. S. Kline) [10]
Ovid's Envy does not stay inside one heart; her breath blights crops and poisons whole communities.
As in the Kural, where the envier's kin lose food and clothing, envy's damage spreads to the people around the envier.
Philosophy
“Emulation makes us take steps to secure the good things in question, envy makes us take steps to stop our neighbour having them.”
— Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.11 (trans. Rhys Roberts) [11]
Aristotle notes that envy is active: it moves people to stop others from getting good things.
The Kural's envier who resents what is given to others is Aristotle's envious man trying to stop his neighbour from receiving.

Real Life Example

Benedict Arnold resents promotions given to others
1777-1801 · Philadelphia, West Point (New York) and London
In February 1777 the Continental Congress promoted five brigadier generals junior to Benedict Arnold to major general and passed him over, and Arnold felt his honour insulted and threatened to resign. Although he was later made a major general, his seniority was not restored, and Mount Vernon and History.com describe his resentment over promotion as a motive for his later treason. In 1780 his plot to hand West Point to the British was discovered; he fled to the British side and spent his last years in England, distrusted on both sides of the Atlantic, dying in London in 1801.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A brave and gifted commander allowed grievance over honours granted to others to turn into betrayal, and lost both his reputation and his country.
The kural warns that one who resents what is given to others will perish together with his household, and Arnold's resentment of others' promotions led to his disgrace and exile.
