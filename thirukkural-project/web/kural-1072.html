Kural 1072

Baseness · Kayamai · கயமை

நன்றறி வாரிற் கயவர் திருவுடையர் நெஞ்சத்து அவலம் இலர்.
Nandrari vaarir kayavar thiruvudaiyar nenjaththu avalam ilar.

Meaning

Mean people are luckier than those who know what is good, because no worry ever troubles their hearts.

Elementary

Think of two children who broke a vase. One feels bad, says sorry, and worries about it all day. The other just shrugs and goes on playing. The second one looks happier, but only because he does not care about doing right. The Kural is joking: being carefree like that is nothing to envy.

University

The couplet is sarcastic praise. Nandru arivaar (நன்றறிவார், 'those who know the good') are contrasted with kayavar, who are thiru udaiyar (திருவுடையர், 'possessed of fortune, blessed'), because they have no avalam (அவலம், 'anxiety, grief') in the nenjam (heart). Parimelazhagar explains that the wise are anxious about what is right and its consequences in this world and the next, while the base feel no such care; Puliyur Kesikan glosses nandru as 'what is truly beneficial to oneself'. Kalaignar keeps the irony explicit ('in one way they are fortunate'). Pope: 'Than those of grateful heart the base must luckier be, / Their minds from every anxious thought are free!' Pope's 'grateful' reflects a reading of nandri as 'gratitude'; most Tamil commentators take nandru as 'the good'. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“And that happiness which is blind to self-realization, which is delusion from beginning to end and which arises from sleep, laziness and illusion is said to be of the nature of ignorance.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.39 (tr. A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
The Gita names a kind of happiness that comes from dullness and delusion and calls it tamasic, the lowest of the three kinds.
The carefree ease the Kural mocks in the base is just this blind, tamasic happiness.
Christianity
“For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked. For there are no bands in their death: but their strength is firm. They are not in trouble as other men; neither are they plagued like other men.”
— Psalm 73:3-5 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist admits he envied the wicked because they seemed free of the troubles that weigh on good people.
This is the Kural's ironic observation that the base seem luckier because nothing troubles them.
Islam
“Let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by [false] hope, for they are going to know.”
— Qur'an 15:3 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an lets the heedless enjoy their carefree life for now, warning that they will learn the truth later.
Like the Kural, it shows the base enjoying an untroubled life that is not really fortunate.
Judaism
“it is not in our hands [to explain the reason] either of the security of the wicked, or even of the afflictions of the righteous.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:15 (Mishnah Yomit, tr. Joshua Kulp, via Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Yannai admits that we cannot explain why the wicked live at ease while the righteous suffer.
The Mishnah faces the same puzzle the Kural turns into sarcasm: the base seem secure while the good are troubled.
Buddhism
“Easy is life for the shameless one who is impudent as a crow, is backbiting and forward, arrogant and corrupt.”
— Dhammapada 244 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha observes that life is easy for the shameless, while the next verse says it is hard for the modest who seek purity.
This is almost exactly the Kural's ironic claim that the base, free of conscience, have the easier life.
Jainism
“A man attached to pleasures and amusements will be caught in the trap (of deceit). (He thinks): 'I never saw the next world, but I have seen with my own eyes the pleasures of this life.'”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 5.5 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira describes the fool who lives carelessly for present pleasure, untroubled by thoughts of karma or the next life, and the lecture goes on to show his dread at death.
The Kural's base man is free of worry for the same reason: he does not care about what is good or what follows from his deeds.
Sikhism
“The world is asleep in Maya, the house of Shakti; it dances, jumps and sings in duality. The self-willed manmukh has no devotion.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 506, Raag Gujri, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das pictures the world as carefree and dancing while it is really asleep in Maya (worldly illusion), and says the self-willed person (manmukh) has no devotion.
The Kural sarcastically calls the base luckier than the wise because nothing troubles them; Guru Amar Das shows that this carefree dancing is really the sleep of people lost in illusion.
Greek Mythology
In Plato's Gorgias, Polus argues that the unjust tyrant Archelaus, who seized power by murder, is happy, and Socrates insists that the wicked man is the most miserable of all.
— Plato, Gorgias 470d-472d (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [10]
Polus judges by appearances: the wrongdoer seems to prosper without care, while Socrates holds that injustice ruins the soul.
The Kural's sarcasm makes Socrates' point: the base only seem fortunate.
Philosophy
“It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.”
— John Stuart Mill, Utilitarianism, ch. 2 (1863) [11]
Mill argues that the restless discontent of a thoughtful person is worth more than the easy contentment of someone who does not know what he is missing.
Mill states plainly what the Kural says through irony: the untroubled base are not the truly fortunate.

Real Life Example

Charles Ponzi's investment scheme
December 1919 – August 1920 · Boston, Massachusetts, United States
Charles Ponzi promised investors a 50 percent return within 45 days, and in about seven months some 30,000 people gave him more than ten million dollars. He paid early investors with money from later ones. After the Boston Post questioned his finances in July 1920, state auditors found him insolvent by August, and he was later convicted and imprisoned.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Ponzi's scheme became the standard name for this kind of fraud, and the Boston Post won a Pulitzer Prize for exposing it.
The kural sarcastically calls the base fortunate because no worry troubles them; Ponzi reportedly stayed confident to the end while thousands of ordinary savers bore the real risk.
