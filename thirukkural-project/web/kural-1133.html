Kural 1133

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

நாணொடு நல்லாண்மை பண்டுடையேன் இன்றுடையேன் காமுற்றார் ஏறும் மடல்.
Naanodu nallaanmai pandudaiyaen indrudaiyaen kaamutraar aerum madal.

Meaning

Once I had modesty and a man's honor. Today all I have is the palm-stem horse that lovesick men ride.

Elementary

Think of a proud, well-behaved prince who always acted with dignity. Then he falls so deeply in love that he will do anything, even something silly in front of everyone, to be heard. He says, 'I used to be so proper; now look at me!'

University

The couplet sets paṇṭu (பண்டு, 'formerly') against iṉṟu (இன்று, 'today'), and nāṇ (நாண், 'modesty, sense of shame') together with nal-āṇmai (நல்லாண்மை, 'good manliness, honourable valour') against the single possession now left, the madal. Parimelazhagar's tradition reads this as the hero's lament that love has stripped him of the two qualities that defined him socially. Kāmuṟṟār (காமுற்றார்) is 'those seized by love'; the madal is thus marked as the emblem of a class of sufferers rather than a personal eccentricity. Pope: 'I once retained reserve and seemly manliness; / To-day I nought possess but lovers' horse of palm.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa's play, King Pururavas, maddened by the loss of the nymph Urvashi, wanders the forest asking a peacock, a cuckoo, a swan, a bee and even a mountain whether they have seen her.
— Kalidasa, Vikramorvashiyam, Act IV (paraphrase) [3]
The great king forgets his royal dignity entirely in his grief and longing.
Like the Kural's speaker, Pururavas once had kingly honour and self-command, and now has only the open madness of love.
Christianity
Paul writes that whatever he once counted as gain, his status, lineage and reputation, he now counts as loss for the sake of Christ.
— Philippians 3:7-8 (KJV) (paraphrase) [4]
Paul describes giving up everything that once defined his honour for the one he loves above all.
The Kural's lover makes the same before-and-after reckoning: his old honour is gone, exchanged for love.
Islam
Rumi was a respected jurist and preacher in Konya until his meeting with the wandering dervish Shams of Tabriz; afterwards he gave himself to ecstatic poetry and whirling, to the scandal of many of his followers.
— Shams al-Din Aflaki, Manaqib al-'Arifin (paraphrase of the traditional account) [5]
The hagiographic tradition tells how love for Shams overturned Rumi's dignified public role.
Like the Kural's speaker, Rumi once had standing and reserve, and love left him with nothing but its open display.
Judaism
Samson, the mighty judge of Israel, reveals the secret of his strength to Delilah because of her persistent pleading; his hair is cut and his strength leaves him.
— Judges 16:15-20 (paraphrase; JPS 1917) [6]
The strongest man in Israel loses the very quality that defined him through his attachment to a woman.
The Kural's lover likewise laments that the 'good manliness' he once had has been taken from him by love.
Buddhism
In the Kusa Jataka, Prince Kusa, rejected by his bride Pabhavati, follows her to her father's kingdom and takes work as a potter, a basket-maker, a gardener and a cook, all to be near her.
— Kusa Jataka (Jataka 531) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Bodhisatta in this birth story lays aside royal dignity entirely for love.
Like the Kural's speaker, Kusa once had a prince's honour and now has only the humble tasks love has driven him to.
Jainism
“Having heard these well-spoken words of the virtuous lady, he returned to the Law like an elephant driven by the hook.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.46 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rathanemi, rebuked by Rajimati, recovers the self-control his passion had made him lose.
The Kural's lover says he once had reserve and now has only the madal; the Jain story shows the reverse path, from passion back to restraint.
Sikhism
“I am dishonored; I have lost my honor.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 324, Raag Gauree, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir accepts that in the world's eyes he has lost all standing because of his devotion, and treats this loss as no loss at all.
The Kural's lover laments that the modesty and good name he once had are gone, leaving only the madal; Kabir likewise confesses that his love has cost him his worldly honour.
Greek Mythology
Deianira reproaches Heracles that the hero who conquered monsters now sits among serving-women spinning wool for the Lydian queen Omphale, wearing her clothes while she carries his club and lion-skin.
— Ovid, Heroides 9 (Deianira to Hercules) (paraphrase) [10]
The greatest of heroes lays aside his manly identity for love of Omphale.
This is exactly the Kural's lament: 'nallāṇmai' (honourable manliness) once possessed, now given up for love.
Philosophy
“A man should allow himself to behave as lovers commonly do to their favorites—pressing their suit with supplications and entreaties, binding themselves with vows, sleeping on doorsteps, and submitting to such slavery as no slave would ever endure.”
— Plato, Symposium 183a (tr. W.R.M. Lamb, via Perseus) [11]
Pausanias notes that conduct which would bring sharp reproach in any other cause is tolerated in a lover.
The Kural's lover, like Plato's, is reduced to acts that would shame him in any other cause.

Real Life Example

John Donne's secret marriage costs him his career
December 1601-1602 · London, England
In December 1601 the poet John Donne, then secretary to the powerful Sir Thomas Egerton, secretly married the seventeen-year-old Ann More. When Donne told her father months later, Sir George More demanded his dismissal; Donne lost his post in 1602 and was briefly imprisoned. The marriage was upheld, but Donne found no regular employment for more than twelve years and lived on the help of friends, relatives and patrons.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A rising young man of standing exchanged his position and reputation for the woman he loved.
As the kural's lover says that he once had good name and honourable manhood and now has only his love, Donne's love left him with his wife but without the career and standing he had once enjoyed.
