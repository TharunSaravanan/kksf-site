Kural 321

Not Killing · Kollaamai · கொல்லாமை

அறவினை யாதெனின் கொல்லாமை கோறல் பிறவினை எல்லாந் தரும்.
Aravinai yaadhenin kollaamai koral Piravinai ellaam tharum.

Meaning

If you ask what doing good means, it is not killing any living thing. Killing brings every other kind of evil deed along with it.

Elementary

Imagine a row of dominoes. If you knock over the first one, all the others fall too. Valluvar says killing is that first domino: once someone is willing to take a life, every other bad act becomes easy. Not killing keeps the whole row standing.

University

Valluvar answers the question “what is aram (virtue)?” with a single negative act — kollaamai, abstention from killing — and then gives its mirror image: kōṟal (killing) “yields all other deeds” (piṟavinai), which the classical commentators, including Mu. Varadarasan, read as all non-virtuous deeds. The couplet therefore makes non-killing not merely one virtue among many but the generative root of the moral life, with killing as the root of vice. This places the Kural squarely within the broad śramaṇic and Brahmanical consensus of early-first-millennium India that ahiṃsā is the highest dharma, a position shared by Jain, Buddhist and epic Hindu sources. The structure of the verse — definition followed by its contrary — is typical of Valluvar’s aphoristic logic, compressing an entire ethical theory into seven words.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Abstention from injury is the highest religion.”
— Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Section CXV (K. M. Ganguli trans.) [4]
In the Mahabharata’s Anushasana Parva, Bhishma teaches Yudhishthira that ahimsa (non-injury) is the supreme dharma — the famous formula ahimsa paramo dharmah.
Exactly the Kural’s answer to “what is virtue?”: non-killing is virtue in its highest form.
Christianity
“Thou shalt not kill.”
— Exodus 20:13 (KJV) [5]
The sixth commandment in the Decalogue given at Sinai forbids the unlawful taking of human life.
Like the Kural, it places the prohibition on killing at the foundation of the moral law.
Islam
“whoever kills a soul unless for a soul or for corruption [done] in the land - it is as if he had slain mankind entirely. And whoever saves one - it is as if he had saved mankind entirely.”
— Qur’an 5:32 (Saheeh International) [6]
After the story of Adam’s two sons, the Qur’an declares that unjustly killing one person is morally equivalent to killing all humanity.
Both texts treat killing as the gravest of wrongs, one that contains the seed of every other evil.
Judaism
“if any man has caused a single life to perish from Israel, he is deemed by Scripture as if he had caused a whole world to perish; and anyone who saves a single soul from Israel, he is deemed by Scripture as if he had saved a whole world.”
— Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 (trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [7]
The Mishnah explains why God created a single human first: to teach that each life is an entire world.
Shares the Kural’s conviction that taking a life is not one wrong among many but a total, world-destroying wrong.
Buddhism
“All tremble at violence; all fear death. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should not kill nor cause another to kill.”
— Dhammapada 129 (Buddharakkhita trans.) [8]
The Buddha grounds the precept against killing in empathy: every creature fears death just as we do.
Same core teaching as the Kural — non-killing is the basic ethical act from which the rest of goodness flows.
Jainism
“The Arhats and Bhagavats of the past, present, and future, all say thus, speak thus, declare thus, explain thus: all breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain, nor treated with violence, nor abused, nor tormented, nor driven away. This is the pure, unchangeable, eternal law…”
— Ācārāṅga Sūtra I.4.1.1–2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884) [9]
The lecture of the Ācārāṅga called 'Righteousness' defines the eternal law of all the Jinas as non-injury to every living being.
Asked what aram is, Valluvar answers 'not killing'; the Ācārāṅga likewise makes non-killing the very definition of the eternal dharma.
Sikhism
“You kill living beings, and call it a righteous action. Tell me, brother, what would you call an unrighteous action?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1103, Raag Maaroo, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Kabir confronts those who kill animals in sacrifice and call it dharma, turning the question back on them: if killing is righteousness, what is left to call sin?
The Kural answers 'what is virtue?' with 'not killing'; Kabir reaches the same point from the other side, insisting that killing cannot be called righteousness.
Greek Mythology
Orestes, having killed his mother Clytemnestra, is hounded across Greece by the Furies (Erinyes), who pursue bloodguilt relentlessly until he is tried at Athens.
— Aeschylus, Eumenides [11]
In Aeschylus’ Eumenides the spilling of kindred blood unleashes a chain of pursuit, madness and pollution that no one can escape by ordinary means.
Echoes the Kural’s second line: one killing sets in motion a whole train of further evils.
Philosophy
Porphyry’s treatise On Abstinence from Animal Food argues that justice extends to all sentient creatures and that the philosopher who seeks purity must refrain from killing them.
— Porphyry, On Abstinence from Animal Food, Books 1–3 [12]
The Neoplatonist Porphyry (3rd c. CE) built a sustained philosophical case that harming living beings is incompatible with the virtuous, god-like life.
Like Valluvar, he makes non-killing a defining mark of virtue rather than an optional scruple.

Real Life Example

Tuscany becomes the first European state to abolish the death penalty
30 November 1786 · Grand Duchy of Tuscany (now Italy)
Grand Duke Peter Leopold of Tuscany, later Holy Roman Emperor Leopold II, promulgated a new penal code that abolished the death penalty, the first such code in Europe. The reform followed the debate opened by Cesare Beccaria's 1764 book On Crimes and Punishments. Capital punishment was later reintroduced in Tuscany, but 30 November is now marked each year as Cities for Life Day in memory of the 1786 abolition.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
For the first time a European state wrote into its law that the state itself would not kill, and the date is still commemorated by campaigners worldwide.
The kural answers the question of what virtue is with one act, not killing, and Tuscany made that refusal the foundation of its criminal law.
