Kural 641

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

நாநலம் என்னும் நலனுடைமை அந்நலம் யாநலத்து உள்ளதூஉம் அன்று.
Naanalam ennum nalanudaimai annalam Yaanalaththu ulladhooum andru.

Meaning

The gift of speaking well is a treasure all by itself. It is not just one of the many other good things a person may have.

Elementary

Imagine a treasure box holding gold, toys and books. A kind, clear way of speaking is not just one more thing in the box; it is like a special key that opens many other boxes. This kural says being able to speak well is a treasure all by itself.

University

The chapter opens the minister’s second great qualification after the preceding chapter on the minister (அமைச்சு, ch. 64): nā-nalam, literally “tongue-goodness”, is framed as a nalan-uṭaimai, a possession or wealth in the same grammatical mould as kalvi-uṭaimai or aṟivu-uṭaimai elsewhere in the text. The second line, yā-nalattu uḷḷatūum anṟu, says this good is “not contained within any other good”; the lengthened form uḷḷatūum is an alapeṭai (metrical prolongation). Parimelazhagar explains that eloquence is a distinct excellence because the other goods of a minister (lineage, learning, wealth) cannot be made effective in council without it, and Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah follow this reading of it as a unique, self-standing merit. Some later commentators, including Kalaignar, read the line more strongly as “the best of all wealth”, a slight shift from “not included among” to “superior to” the other goods. The text is stable across ytamizh.com and the older sandhi-joined edition at thirukkural-thamizh.org (நாநல மென்னு நலனுடைமை யந்நலம்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Vak Sukta of the Rigveda, Vāk (divine Speech) speaks of herself as a queen and gatherer of treasures, the power who makes the one she loves mighty, a seer and a sage.
— Rigveda 10.125 (Vak Sukta), trans. R. T. H. Griffith [3]
The hymn treats Speech not as a tool but as a sovereign goddess who holds wealth and gives greatness to those she favours; later tradition connects her with Sarasvatī.
Like the Kural, the hymn counts speech itself as a treasure, not an accessory to other treasures.
Christianity
“The officers answered, Never man spake like this man.”
— John 7:46 (KJV) [4]
Temple officers sent to arrest Jesus return empty-handed, giving as their only excuse the unmatched quality of his speech.
The officers treat Jesus’ speech as a power in a class of its own, which is what the Kural means by a good not contained in other goods.
Islam
“[And] taught him eloquence.”
— Qur’an 55:4 (Sahih International) [5]
Surah ar-Rahman lists al-bayān (clear, articulate expression) among God’s first gifts to humankind, right after creating the human being and teaching the Qur’an.
The Qur’an counts clear speech as a distinct divine gift, much as the Kural counts nā-nalam as its own kind of wealth.
Judaism
“A word fitly spoken Is like apples of gold in settings of silver.”
— Proverbs 25:11 (JPS 1917) [6]
The proverb compares a well-placed word to a precious object set in precious metal, a thing of both value and beauty.
Both texts describe good speech as treasure, with value apart from the speaker’s other goods.
Buddhism
In the Anguttara Nikaya’s list of pre-eminent disciples, the Buddha names the monk Vangīsa as foremost among those gifted with ready, inspired speech (paṭibhāna).
— Anguttara Nikaya 1 (Etadagga Vagga) [7]
Eloquence is recognised as a separate excellence worth naming, alongside wisdom, psychic power and other distinctions.
The Kural also treats eloquence as its own distinct merit, not something absorbed into other virtues.
Jainism
“Those who having investigated the Law expound it, are awakened and put an end to mundane existence; able to liberate both (themselves and others), they answer the well-deliberated questions.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga's lecture on the ideal monk counts the ability to expound the Law clearly as a gift of its own, one that frees both the speaker and those who hear him.
Valluvar calls goodness of the tongue a wealth in its own right; the Jain verse likewise gives skilled exposition a distinct worth, since it benefits others as well as oneself.
Sikhism
“Sweetness and humility, O Nanak, are the essence of virtue and goodness.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa (Asa ki Vaar, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Asa ki Vaar Guru Nanak names sweetness (mithat), above all in speech and bearing, together with humility as the very essence of every virtue.
The Kural calls goodness of the tongue a wealth in its own right, not one good among many; Guru Nanak likewise sets sweetness at the core of all goodness rather than treating it as a minor accomplishment.
Greek Mythology
Hermes, messenger of the gods, was honoured as Hermes Logios, patron of eloquence and persuasive speech, and the Homeric Hymn to Hermes shows the infant god talking his way out of trouble before Zeus.
— Homeric Hymn 4, To Hermes [10]
The Greeks gave eloquence its own divine patron, separate from the gods of war, wealth or wisdom.
Giving eloquence its own god reflects the Kural’s view that speech is a good in its own category.
Philosophy
In the Nicocles, Isocrates delivers his famous praise of logos: speech is what raises humans above animals, lets them found cities, make laws and invent arts, and good speech is the outward sign of a sound mind.
— Isocrates, Nicocles 5-9 [11]
Isocrates argues that the capacity for speech is the source of nearly every civilised good.
Isocrates, like Valluvar, ranks eloquence as a good of its own order rather than one virtue among many.

Real Life Example

Cicero, a 'new man', wins the consulship through his oratory
63 BCE · Rome, Roman Republic
Marcus Tullius Cicero came from a wealthy equestrian family with no consuls among his ancestors, and he built his career largely on his work as an advocate in the law courts, including his successful prosecution of Verres in 70 BCE. He rose through each office at the minimum legal age and was elected consul for 63 BCE as a novus homo, a 'new man', heading the poll. He is remembered as the greatest Roman orator, and his speeches against Catiline during his consulship are among his best-known works.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Without noble birth or a military record, Cicero reached the highest office of the Republic mainly through the power of his speech.
The kural calls the goodness of the tongue a wealth in its own right, not merely one good among others, and Cicero's rise shows eloquence serving as the capital that a lack of lineage denied him.
