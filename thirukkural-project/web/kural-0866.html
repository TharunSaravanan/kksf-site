Kural 866

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

காணாச் சினத்தான் கழிபெருங் காமத்தான் பேணாமை பேணப் படும்.
Kaanaach chinaththaan kazhiperung kaamaththaan penaamai penap padum.

Meaning

A person who is blinded by anger and full of endless desire is a good enemy to have, because they will bring themselves down.

Elementary

Imagine a rival who gets so angry he can’t see straight and wants everything he sees, right now. He runs around grabbing and shouting and forgets to defend himself. Having a rival like that is almost lucky, because his own temper and greed trip him up.

University

Kaanaach chinam (காணாச் சினம்) is anger that does not see, which Puliyur Kesigan explains as anger that blinds one to oneself and to others, and Varadarasanar as anger that does not see the truth. Kazhi perung kaamam (கழிபெருங் காமம்) is desire grown beyond all measure; Pappaiah specifies it as lust for women, while Varadarasanar and Kalaignar keep the broader sense of greed or great desire. The second line plays on the root penu (to cherish): penaamai (பேணாமை, non-cherishing, i.e. enmity) penap padum (பேணப் படும், will be cherished), so his hostility is ‘to be prized’ by others. The pairing of krodha and kama as twin enemies of self-government is common to classical Indian thought. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Arjuna asks what drives a man to sin against his will; Krishna answers that it is desire (kama), which turns into anger (krodha), all-devouring and the great enemy here in this world.
— Bhagavad Gita 3.36-37 [3]
The Gita names desire and anger together as the inner enemy that overpowers a person.
Valluvar names the same pair, anger and boundless desire, as what makes a man an easy foe.
Christianity
James teaches that each person is tempted when drawn away by his own lust, and that lust, when it has conceived, brings forth sin, and sin brings forth death.
— James 1:14-15 (KJV) [4]
Unchecked desire leads step by step to self-destruction.
Valluvar’s man of boundless desire is on that same downward path, which is why his enmity is no threat.
Islam
The Qur’an asks the Prophet whether he has seen the one who takes his own desire as his god, asking whether he can be a guardian over such a person.
— Qur’an 25:43 [5]
The verse portrays a person wholly ruled by desire as beyond help.
Valluvar’s man of boundless desire is likewise ruled from within, and so is easily overcome from without.
Judaism
The Philistine lords, unable to overcome Samson by force, use his passion for Delilah to learn the secret of his strength, and so capture him.
— Judges 16 (Tanakh) [6]
Samson’s enemies win not by strength but by exploiting his desire.
This is exactly why the Kural says such a man’s enmity is to be welcomed: his passions hand his foes the victory.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada says there is no fire like lust, no grip like hatred, no net like delusion and no river like craving.
— Dhammapada 251 [7]
The Buddha pictures desire and hatred as forces that seize and drag a person along.
Valluvar’s blind anger and boundless desire are the fire and the grip that leave a man defenceless.
Jainism
“If there were numberless mountains of gold and silver, as big as Kailâsa, they would not satisfy a greedy man; for his avidity is boundless like space. … He will sink through anger; he will go down through pride; delusion will block up his path; through greed he will incur dangers in both worlds.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 9.48 and 9.54 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
King Nami describes desire as boundless as space and says that the person ruled by anger, pride, delusion and greed brings about his own fall.
The Kural welcomes as an enemy the man blinded by anger and swollen with boundless desire because he will destroy himself, and Nami describes exactly that self-ruin through anger and limitless craving.
Sikhism
“Unfulfilled sexual desire and unresolved anger waste the body away, as gold is dissolved by borax.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 932, Raag Raamkalee, Dakhni Oankar (pauri 18), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In the Dakhni Oankar Guru Nanak says lust and anger eat away the person who harbours them, as borax melts gold.
The Kural says the enmity of a man blinded by anger and swollen with desire is welcome because he destroys himself; Guru Nanak likewise shows kaam and krodh consuming their own host.
Greek Mythology
Paris’s desire for Helen leads him to carry her off from Sparta, bringing the Greek armies down on Troy and destroying his own city.
— Homer, Iliad (background) and the Trojan cycle [10]
One prince’s unchecked desire brings ruin on his whole people.
Paris is the Kural’s man of boundless desire whose enmity his foes could welcome.
Philosophy
In Book IX of the Republic, Plato describes the tyrannical man as ruled by a master passion, a slave to desires that multiply without limit, and therefore the most wretched and least free of men.
— Plato, Republic IX (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
For Plato, the person governed by unlimited appetite is inwardly enslaved, however powerful he seems.
Valluvar sees the same inner slavery as an outward weakness enemies can exploit.

Real Life Example

The Otrar incident provokes Genghis Khan against Khwarezm
1218-1220 · Otrar, Kazakhstan, and Khwarezm, Central Asia
The governor of the Khwarezmian frontier city of Otrar seized a caravan of Muslim merchants travelling under Genghis Khan's protection and had them killed. The Khwarezm-Shah Ala al-Din Muhammad refused to give satisfaction and ordered the execution of Mongol envoys. Genghis Khan then invaded, and between 1218 and 1220 his armies overran the Khwarezmian empire across what is now Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan and Iran.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Greed for a caravan's goods and an angry rejection of diplomacy drew a far more dangerous enemy into a war that destroyed the shah's empire.
The kural says the enmity of one blinded by anger and swollen with desire is welcome to his foes, since he brings about his own defeat, as the shah and his governor did at Otrar.
