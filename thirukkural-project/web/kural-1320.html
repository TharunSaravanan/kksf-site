Kural 1320

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

நினைத்திருந்து நோக்கினும் காயும் அனைத்துநீர் யாருள்ளி நோக்கினீர் என்று.
Ninaiththirundhu noakkinum kaayum anaiththu-neer yaarulli noakkineer endru

Meaning

Even when I just sit and look at her, thinking of her, she gets angry and asks, "Who are you thinking of as you look at me like that?"

Elementary

Imagine staring happily at your favourite painting, and your sister asks, ‘Are you comparing it to some other painting?’ The husband just wants to admire his wife, but she pretends he must be comparing her to someone else.

University

Niṉaittiruntu nōkkiṉum means ‘even if I look, having (silently) contemplated’, i.e. gazing at her while dwelling on her beauty; she grows hot (kāyum) and asks yār uḷḷi nōkkiṉīr (‘with whom in mind did you look?’). Aṉaittum is read by Mu. Varadarasanar as ‘all (her features) by comparison’ (ollumaiyāka ellām) and by Solomon Pappaiah as gazing at each of her features, which she takes as matching them against another woman’s. As the chapter’s last couplet it caps the series: speech (1314-1316), sneezing (1317-1318), coaxing (1319), and finally even silent admiration all provoke pulavi. Pope: ‘I silent sat, but thought the more, and gazed on her. Then she cried out, “While thus you eye me o’er, tell me whose form you see.”’ The text agrees on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavata Purana, Krishna teases his devoted wife Rukmini by asking why she chose him over more suitable kings; she takes his playful words seriously and is overcome, and he must comfort her.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.60 (paraphrase) [3]
The scene shows a loving wife reading hurt into her husband’s light-hearted attention.
The Kural’s wife likewise reads a slight into her husband’s loving gaze, though here he meant no teasing at all.
Christianity
“Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee.”
— Song of Solomon 4:7 (KJV) [4]
The lover in the Song of Songs gazes on his beloved and finds her wholly beautiful, with no comparison to anyone.
This is what the husband is thinking as he gazes, even while his wife pretends he is comparing her to another.
Islam
“And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy.”
— Qur’an 30:21 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an presents spouses as a source of peace and affection for each other.
The husband’s quiet gaze is that tranquil affection; the wife’s mock anger is only a playful ripple on it.
Judaism
“My dove, my undefiled, is but one; she is the only one of her mother; she is the choice one of her that bore her.”
— Song of Songs 6:9 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Surrounded by queens and maidens without number, the lover insists that his beloved is the only one.
The husband’s gaze means just this: there is no one else he is measuring her against.
Buddhism
In the Bhariya Sutta, the Buddha describes seven kinds of wives, among them the ‘friend-wife’, who rejoices on seeing her husband as one friend rejoices at seeing another after a long time.
— Anguttara Nikaya 7.59, Bhariya Sutta (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha holds up a marriage where seeing each other brings simple gladness.
The husband’s loving gaze invites that gladness, though his wife, in play, answers with suspicion instead.
Jainism
“He who passionately hates (a colour), will at the same moment suffer pain. It is the fault of an undisciplined man that he is annoyed (by a colour); it is not the ‘colour’ itself that annoys him.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching places the source of annoyance in the mind of the one annoyed, not in the thing seen.
Nothing in the husband’s gaze is wrong; any annoyance is created by the wife’s own playful mind.
Sikhism
“Wherever I look, I see only You. The True Guru has inspired me to see You.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 55, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak describes a gaze so filled with the Beloved that he sees Him everywhere.
The Kural's wife asks whom her husband is thinking of as he gazes; in Gurbani the devotee's gaze holds only the Beloved, the very single-mindedness she wants.
Greek Mythology
Sappho’s famous poem describes watching the man who sits close to her beloved and listens to her sweet voice, and the overwhelming tumult the sight stirs in the watcher.
— Sappho, Fragment 31 (paraphrase) [10]
The poem is antiquity’s classic portrait of gazing, love and jealous turmoil all at once.
The Kural joins the same elements: a loving gaze and an imagined third person stirring jealousy.
Philosophy
In Plato’s Phaedrus, Socrates describes the lover who, gazing on the beauty of the beloved, is filled with awe and warmth and wants only to keep looking.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251a-c (paraphrase) [11]
Plato treats the lover’s gaze as a wholehearted response to the beauty in front of him.
This is the husband’s silent gaze in the Kural, wholly fixed on his wife, though she teasingly suspects otherwise.

Real Life Example

Rossetti paints his wife as Dante's Beatrice in Beata Beatrix
c. 1864-1870 · London, England
Dante Gabriel Rossetti married his model Elizabeth Siddal in 1860, and she died in 1862. In the following years he painted Beata Beatrix, now in Tate Britain, in which a figure based on his late wife represents Beatrice, the beloved of the poet Dante, in a dreamlike trance. Britannica notes that the painting shows he saw his love for his wife as like Dante's idealised love for Beatrice.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The painting is a famous example of an artist gazing at his own wife's features while thinking of a woman from literature.
The wife in this kural asks whom her husband is thinking of while he gazes at her, and in Beata Beatrix Rossetti literally looked at his wife's face and painted another woman, Dante's Beatrice.
