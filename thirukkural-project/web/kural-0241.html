Kural 241

Benevolence · Arulodamai · அருளுடைமை

அருட்செல்வம் செல்வத்துள் செல்வம் பொருட்செல்வம் பூரியார் கண்ணும் உள.
Arutchelvam selvaththul selvam porutchelvam, Pooriyaar kannum ula.

Meaning

Kindness is the richest of all riches. Money and things can be found even in the hands of low, mean people.

Elementary

Anyone can end up with a big pile of money, even a bully or a cheat. But a kind heart that cares about every living thing is something only good people have. So kindness is the richest treasure there is.

University

The chapter opens by reversing the ordinary hierarchy of value with a pun on selvam (wealth): arul-selvam, the wealth of grace, is ‘the wealth among wealths’, while porul-selvam, material wealth, is disqualified precisely because it is morally indiscriminate — it collects even with the pooriyaar, the base or vile. The argument is not that money is evil (Valluvar devotes the whole Book of Porul to acquiring it, and Kural 757 calls arul the child of love nursed by wealth) but that a good which the wicked can own equally well cannot be the highest good. Parimelazhagar, placing this chapter at the head of the ascetic section (Thuravaraviyal), glosses arul as compassion that flows to all living beings without any tie of kinship — the matured, universalised form of the anbu (love for one’s own) praised in Chapter 8. The logic echoes Socrates’ claim that virtue is not produced by wealth, and the Stoic and Buddhist ranking of inner goods above external ones.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“nonviolence, truthfulness, freedom from anger; renunciation, tranquility, aversion to faultfinding, compassion and freedom from covetousness”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.1-3 [3]
Krishna lists compassion (dayā bhūteṣu, ‘compassion toward beings’) among the qualities that make up the daivī sampad — literally the ‘divine wealth’ or endowment — of the godly person, in contrast to the demonic endowment described next.
The Gita, like the Kural, frames compassion as a kind of wealth (sampad) that outranks ordinary possessions, and places it next to freedom from covetousness.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts perishable earthly wealth with a lasting treasure, and the surrounding Sermon on the Mount identifies that treasure with mercy, almsgiving and love.
Both texts demote material riches in favour of an inner, moral treasure, though Jesus stresses impermanence while Valluvar stresses that even the wicked can own money.
Islam
“Wealth is not in having many possessions, but rather (true) wealth is feeling sufficiency in the soul.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6446 [5]
The Prophet redefines ghinā (richness) as an inner state rather than a quantity of goods.
Like the Kural, the hadith relocates true wealth from possessions to the soul; the Kural names that inner wealth as compassion, the hadith as contentment.
Judaism
“let not the rich man glory in his riches: But let him that glorieth glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me, that I am the LORD which exercise lovingkindness, judgment, and righteousness, in the earth”
— Jeremiah 9:23-24 (KJV) [4]
Jeremiah rejects wealth as a ground of pride and points instead to chesed (lovingkindness), the very quality God exercises in the world.
Both texts set riches below lovingkindness; Jeremiah adds that chesed is a divine attribute, matching the ‘almost divine’ register of arul.
Buddhism
“All the grounds for making merit leading to spontaneously arising (in heaven) do not equal one-sixteenth of the awareness-release through good will.”
— Itivuttaka 27 (trans. Thanissaro) [6]
The Buddha says every other form of merit, however great, is outshone by a mind of boundless good will (mettā), as the stars are outshone by the moon.
Both texts rank a universal, compassionate disposition as the supreme ‘wealth’ above every other kind of accumulation.
Jainism
“I have horses, elephants, and subjects, a town and a seraglio, power and command … how could he be without protection? Reverend sir, you speak untruth.” … “You are the protector of all unprotected beings, O ascetic.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 20.14-15, 20.56 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [7]
King Srenika boasts that his riches make him secure, but the young monk shows him that wealth protects no one, and the humbled king ends by calling the compassionate monk the protector of every unprotected being.
Valluvar ranks the wealth of compassion above material wealth, which even the basest can hold; the Uttaradhyayana likewise sets a king's treasure below the monk's care for all beings.
Sikhism
“Compassion is wealth, want of compassion useless.”
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, Chapter X, p. 129 (Nasihat Nama) [8]
In the Nasihat Nama (Instruction), which Macauliffe records as uttered by Guru Nanak to Mian Mitha, compassion is set among other virtues as the true wealth.
The Kural calls the wealth of compassion the wealth of all wealths, and this line says simply that compassion is wealth and its absence worthless.
Greek Mythology
Baucis and Philemon, a poor elderly couple, take in Jupiter and Mercury after a thousand wealthier households bar their doors; the rich neighbourhood is drowned while the couple’s hut becomes a temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 [9]
The only household that shows kindness to the disguised strangers is the poorest one, and it is the one the gods honour.
An illustration of the Kural’s claim: material wealth sat with the uncaring neighbours, while the true wealth was the poor couple’s compassion.
Philosophy
“virtue is not given by money, but that from virtue come money and every other good of man, public as well as private.”
— Plato, Apology 30b (trans. Jowett) [10]
Socrates tells the Athenian jury that his whole mission has been to persuade them to care for the soul before wealth, since money cannot produce goodness.
Both thinkers subordinate material wealth to moral excellence; Valluvar makes the excellence specifically compassion.

Real Life Example

Abdul Sattar Edhi builds Pakistan's largest welfare network while living with almost nothing
1951 onwards · Karachi, Pakistan
In 1951 Abdul Sattar Edhi established a charitable service in Karachi, funded by private donations, to provide basic emergency care, starting with a single ambulance that he drove himself. The Edhi Foundation grew into a nationwide network of ambulances, maternity centres, orphan care and shelters, and in 1997 it entered Guinness World Records as the largest volunteer ambulance organisation. Edhi never took a salary from the foundation, owned only a couple of sets of clothes and lived in a small room beside his office.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Edhi became one of the most honoured people in his country through compassion alone, while deliberately possessing almost nothing.
The kural calls compassion the wealth of all wealths and notes that money is found even among the base, and Edhi's life showed a person rich only in compassion yet esteemed above the wealthy.
