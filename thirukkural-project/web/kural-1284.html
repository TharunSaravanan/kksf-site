Kural 1284

Desire for Reunion · Punarchi Vidhumpal · புணர்ச்சி விதும்பல்

ஊடற்கண் சென்றேன்மன் தோழி அதுமறந்து கூடற்கண் சென்றதுஎன் னெஞ்சு.
Ootarkan sendraenman thoazhi adhumarandhu koodarkan sendradhuen nenju.

Meaning

My friend, I went to him meaning to sulk. But my heart forgot all that and went straight to making up with him.

Elementary

Imagine planning to stay cross with a friend who came back late from a trip, but the moment you see them you forget and run to hug them. That is what happens to the wife in this poem: her plan to be cross melts away.

University

The heroine addresses her companion (tōḻi): ūṭaṟkaṇ ceṉṟēṉ maṉ, ‘I did go toward sulking’, where the particle maṉ marks a past intention that came to nothing; but atu maṟantu, ‘forgetting it’, eṉ neñcu (‘my heart’) went kūṭaṟkaṇ, ‘toward union’. The neat opposition of ūṭal (lovers’ sulking) and kūṭal (coming together) carries the chapter’s theme: eagerness for reunion overruling the convention of feigned displeasure. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it this way; Solomon Pappaiah specifies that she planned to sulk over his fault before seeing him and forgot it once she saw him; Kalaignar generalises it as the special nature of love. The heart acting apart from the speaker’s will anticipates the next chapter, neñcoṭu pulattal (‘quarrelling with one’s heart’). The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
After nursing her anger through the night, Radha is persuaded by her friend that Krishna waits for her, and she goes to him, her pride dissolving as she enters the bower.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, cantos 11-12 (paraphrase) [3]
In Jayadeva’s poem Radha’s resolve to stay offended gives way to her eagerness to meet Krishna.
The Kural’s heroine likewise sets out to sulk and finds her heart has gone to reunion instead.
Christianity
“For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I.”
— Romans 7:15 (KJV) [4]
Paul describes the experience of intending one thing and finding oneself doing another.
The Kural’s heroine intends to sulk but her heart carries her to embrace; the divided will is the same, though Paul speaks of moral struggle.
Islam
“Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend.”
— Qur’an 41:34 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an teaches that answering hostility with goodness can turn estrangement into close friendship.
The Kural shows estrangement turning to closeness in a moment; the Qur’an names the same turn, though as a moral strategy rather than a lover’s impulse.
Judaism
“And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him; and they wept.”
— Genesis 33:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Esau, who had set out with four hundred men against his brother, embraces Jacob the moment they meet.
As in the Kural, an intended hostility vanishes at the meeting and the heart runs to embrace.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada compares the mind to an arrow shaft that the wise must straighten, describing it as fickle, unsteady and hard to guard.
— Dhammapada 33 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
Buddhist teaching recognises that the mind runs where it will unless it is trained.
The Kural’s heart that forgets the plan and runs to reunion is an example of this unruly mind, though the Buddhist aim is to restrain it.
Jainism
In the dialogue of Kesi and Gautama, Gautama explains that the mind is an unruly, dreadful horse that runs about, and that he keeps it in check by the discipline of the Law.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 23 (Kesi and Gautama) (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana pictures the mind as a wild horse that bolts unless reined in.
The Kural’s heart bolts toward reunion against the heroine’s plan; the Jain image captures that runaway mind, though it calls for restraint.
Sikhism
“The bumble bee becomes enticed by the lotus, intoxicated by its fragrance, and does not leave it, even for a moment.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 454, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan pictures the devotee drawn to the Lord as a bee is drawn to the lotus, unable to leave it.
In the Kural the wife set out to sulk, but her heart forgot and went straight to union; Guru Arjan's bee likewise cannot hold back from the lotus that draws it.
Greek Mythology
After Procris flees in anger and shame over a test of fidelity, Cephalus seeks her out, and the two are reconciled and live for years in harmony.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.690-758 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s couple quarrel and part, but their love draws them back together.
As in the Kural, displeasure gives way to the stronger pull of reunion.
Philosophy
In the Phaedrus, the charioteer of the soul tries to hold back the unruly horse, but at the sight of the beloved that horse drags the whole team forward toward him despite the charioteer’s resolve.
— Plato, Phaedrus 253c-254e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato’s image shows desire overpowering the reasoned intention of the soul when the beloved appears.
The Kural’s heroine intends to sulk, but her heart, like Plato’s horse, carries her straight to the beloved.

Real Life Example

Napoleon locks his door on Joséphine, then forgives her
October 1799 · Rue de la Victoire, Paris, France
After seventeen months away on the Egyptian expedition, Napoleon Bonaparte returned secretly to France in the autumn of 1799, angry at reports about his wife Joséphine’s conduct in his absence. Joséphine had driven south to meet him and missed him on the road, and when she reached their house in Paris he kept his bedroom door locked against her despite hours of pleading and tears, joined by her children Hortense and Eugène. In the end he let her in and they were fully reconciled, to the annoyance of his family.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Napoleon had come home determined to stay cold, but his resolve gave way almost at once.
The kural describes a heart that set out to sulk but forgot it and went straight toward reunion; Napoleon’s locked door, opened within a day or two, shows the same collapse of a planned sulk.
