Kural 512

Selection and Employment · Therindhu Vinaiyaadal · தெரிந்து வினையாடல்

வாரி பெருக்கி வளம்படுத்து உற்றவை ஆராய்வான் செய்க வினை.
Vaari perukki valampaduththu utravai Aaraaivaan seyga vinai.

Meaning

Give the work to someone who can find more ways to bring money in, make it grow, and spot and fix the problems that come up.

Elementary

Think of someone looking after a lemonade stand. A good manager finds more customers, makes the stand earn more, and spots problems early, like running out of cups, and fixes them. That’s the person who should run the stand.

University

Kural 512 defines administrative competence in three verbs: பெருக்கி (expand the வாரி, the channels of revenue), வளம்படுத்து (turn income into lasting prosperity), and உற்றவை ஆராய் (look into whatever befalls, i.e., foresee and remove obstacles). Parimelazhagar and the modern commentators (Varadarasan, Pappaiah, Kalaignar) all read உற்றவை as the hindrances that threaten wealth, so the ideal agent is productive and protective at once. The couplet is strikingly economic: Valluvar judges the officer by measurable growth, not by birth or favour. Kautilya’s Arthashastra sets out the same standard, promoting superintendents who raise revenue and keeping in permanent service those who increase it loyally.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Those who increase the king’s revenue instead of eating it up, and are loyally devoted to him, shall be made permanent in service.”
— Kautilya, Arthashastra 2.9 (Shamasastry) [3]
Kautilya’s manual of statecraft says officials who grow the treasury, rather than skimming it, should be kept on for good.
The Kural’s ideal worker is the one who widens revenue and builds prosperity; Kautilya says that is exactly who should keep the job.
Christianity
“Then came the first, saying, Lord, thy pound hath gained ten pounds. And he said unto him, Well, thou good servant: because thou hast been faithful in a very little, have thou authority over ten cities.”
— Luke 19:16-17 (KJV) [4]
In the parable of the pounds, the servant who multiplied his master’s money is rewarded with authority over ten cities.
The one who increases what he is given is the one trusted with bigger work, which is the Kural’s criterion.
Islam
“[Joseph] said, “Appoint me over the storehouses of the land. Indeed, I will be a knowing guardian.”
— Qur’an 12:55 (Sahih International) [5]
Joseph asks the king of Egypt to put him in charge of the nation’s grain stores, pointing to his knowledge and his ability to guard them.
Joseph both builds up reserves and guards against the coming famine, the growth-plus-foresight the Kural asks for.
Judaism
“And it came to pass from the time that he appointed him overseer in his house, and over all that he had, that the LORD blessed the Egyptian’s house for Joseph’s sake; and the blessing of the LORD was upon all that he had, in the house and in the field.”
— Genesis 39:5 (JPS 1917) [6]
Once Potiphar makes Joseph overseer, his whole household and fields prosper.
The right overseer makes the estate flourish, just as the Kural’s worker makes prosperity grow.
Buddhism
In the Kutadanta Sutta, a wise chaplain advises a king troubled by banditry not to punish harder but to give seed and fodder to farmers, capital to traders, and proper wages to officials; the land then grows prosperous and peaceful.
— Digha Nikaya 5, Kutadanta Sutta [7]
Growing the country’s sources of income removes the causes of disorder and fills the treasury.
Widening sources of income and removing what threatens prosperity is exactly the skill Kural 512 wants in an administrator.
Jainism
“During his reign he taught, for the benefit of the people, the seventy-two sciences, of which writing is the first, arithmetic the most important, and the knowledge of omens the last, the sixty-four accomplishments of women, the hundred arts, and the three occupations of men.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Rishabha §211 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22, 1884; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Before his renunciation, Rishabha, the first Tirthankara and first king, is credited with giving people the arts, crafts and occupations by which they live.
The kural praises the administrator who widens the sources of income and builds prosperity; Jain tradition gives its first king the role of founding the arts and occupations on which prosperity rests.
Sikhism
Guru Amar Das organised the Sikh congregations into twenty-two districts (manjis) whose leaders passed on the disciples' offerings. When Guru Ram Das began the costly work of digging the sacred pool at Amritsar and building the Harimandar, Sikhs were asked to set aside a tenth of their income (dasvandh), and masands were appointed to collect it and bring it to the Guru.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (Punjabi University, Patiala, ed. Harbans Singh), 'Dasvandh' (paraphrase) [9]
The Encyclopaedia of Sikhism describes how the early Gurus built up regular channels of income for the community and turned them into lasting institutions such as the shrine and the langar.
The Kural wants an administrator who widens the sources of revenue and turns them into growing prosperity; the early Sikh system of districts, tithes and collectors did just that for the community.
Greek Mythology
While Odysseus is away, his loyal swineherd Eumaeus builds pens and carefully tends and guards his master’s herds, keeping the estate going through twenty years of absence.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 14 [10]
Eumaeus is the model servant: he looks after and builds up his master’s wealth and guards it against the suitors’ waste.
He grows and protects what he has been given, like the Kural’s ideal worker, though on a small household scale.
Philosophy
Aristotle retells how Thales, mocked for his poverty, foresaw a great olive harvest, rented all the olive presses in advance and made a fortune, and adds that statesmen should know such methods because states often need money.
— Aristotle, Politics I.11, 1259a [11]
Aristotle treats knowing how to create wealth as a real part of statesmanship.
The Kural likewise treats the ability to open new sources of revenue as a key qualification for public work.

Real Life Example

Sergei Witte's term as Russia's finance minister
1892-1903 · St Petersburg, Russian Empire
As Russian minister of finance from 1892 to 1903, Sergei Witte oversaw construction of the Trans-Siberian Railway, encouraged foreign investment and industrial growth, and in 1897 put the ruble on the gold standard. Under him the state monopoly on the sale of spirits became the largest single source of government revenue. He was dismissed in 1903 after palace intrigue.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Witte was judged by results: he widened the state's income, turned it into railways and industry, and stabilised the currency.
The kural asks for someone who can widen the sources of income, turn them into lasting prosperity and deal with obstacles, which describes Witte's work as finance minister.
