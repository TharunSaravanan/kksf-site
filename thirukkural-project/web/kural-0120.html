Kural 120

Impartiality · Naduvu Nilaimai · நடுவு நிலைமை

வாணிகம் செய்வார்க்கு வாணிகம் பேணிப் பிறவும் தமபோல் செயின்.
Vaanigam seyvaarkku vaanigam penip Piravum thamapol seyin.

Meaning

For traders, the right way to trade is to protect other people’s goods and interests as carefully as their own.

Elementary

A good shopkeeper weighs your rice as carefully as if it were for her own family, and charges you the same fair price she’d want to pay herself. That’s what honest business means.

University

The chapter closes by applying impartiality to commerce, the everyday setting where people most often weigh and measure for one another. பிறவும் தமபோல் (“others’ [goods] as their own”) is the principle: the merchant must take no more and give no less than they would accept if the roles were reversed. Parimelazhagar notes the scale imagery carried over from Kural 118, since trade literally involves weighing. The doubled வாணிகம் (“trade … trade”) is emphatic: only this is real trade, and anything else is disguised theft. Parallels include the Qur’anic curse on those who give short measure, the Talmudic tradition that the first question at the heavenly judgment is “Did you conduct business faithfully?”, Hesiod’s counsel to give fair measure “or better,” and Kant’s honest shopkeeper who charges a child the same as anyone else. Kant adds that honesty practised only because it pays is not yet fully moral, which fits the Kural’s demand for inner straightness in Kural 119.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He is a perfect yogī who, by comparison to his own self, sees the true equality of all beings, in both their happiness and their distress, O Arjuna!”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.32 [4]
Krishna praises the person who judges others’ joys and sufferings by comparison with their own (ātmaupamya).
The same “as one’s own” standard the Kural applies to a merchant’s dealings.
Christianity
“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets.”
— Matthew 7:12 (KJV) [5]
Jesus gives the Golden Rule as the summary of the whole law.
The Kural applies the Golden Rule to buying and selling.
Islam
“Woe to those who give less [than due], Who, when they take a measure from people, take in full. But if they give by measure or by weight to them, they cause loss.”
— Qur’an 83:1–3 (Saheeh International) [6]
Surah al-Mutaffifin (“The Defrauders”) condemns traders who demand full measure for themselves but short others.
The exact opposite of the Kural’s ideal: these traders use a double standard, one for themselves and another for others.
Judaism
“when a person is brought to judgment for the life he lived in this world, they say to him in the order of that verse: Did you conduct business faithfully?”
— Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a (William Davidson/Koren–Steinsaltz translation) [7]
Rava teaches that the very first question asked at the heavenly judgment is whether one did business honestly (nasata ve-natata be-emunah).
Both make honest trade a central religious and moral test.
Buddhism
“All tremble at violence; life is dear to all. Putting oneself in the place of another, one should not kill nor cause another to kill.”
— Dhammapada 130 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada grounds ethics in putting oneself in another’s place.
The same self-other comparison underlies the Kural’s rule of treating others’ goods as one’s own.
Jainism
“Prompting others to steal, receiving stolen goods, underbuying in a disordered state, using false weights and measures, and deceiving others with artificial or imitation goods are the five transgressions of non-stealing.”
— Tattvārtha Sūtra 7.27 (English rendering on jainworld.com) [9]
The Jain layperson's vow of non-stealing explicitly forbids false weights and measures and adulterated goods, which binds merchants to honest dealing.
Valluvar says the true way of trade is to guard others' interests as one's own, and the Jain householder vow writes this into commerce by forbidding cheating in weights, measures and goods.
Sikhism
Macauliffe records that Nanak, as storekeeper for Governor Daulat Khan at Sultanpur, carried out his duties to everyone's satisfaction, giving most of his own allowance to the poor; when weighing out provisions and reaching thirteen, tera, he would repeat the word, which also means 'Thine'. When complaints of extravagance were investigated, the store was found full and his accounts correct, with money owed to him.
— Max Arthur Macauliffe, The Sikh Religion (1909), Vol. 1, Life of Guru Nanak, ch. III, pp. 33, 42-43 (paraphrase) [10]
The story presents Guru Nanak's honest handling of another's goods as an act of devotion.
It matches the Kural's rule for traders: guard other people's goods as carefully as one's own.
Greek Mythology
“Take fair measure from your neighbour and pay him back fairly with the same measure, or better, if you can; so that if you are in need afterwards, you may find him sure.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 349–351 (trans. Evelyn-White) [11]
Hesiod advises his brother to deal with neighbours using an honest measure, returning as much or more.
The same measure for self and other is the Kural’s formula for honest trade.
Philosophy
“it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not over charge an inexperienced purchaser; and wherever there is much commerce the prudent tradesman does not overcharge, but keeps a fixed price for everyone, so that a child buys of him as well as any other.”
— Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals, First Section (trans. T. K. Abbott) [12]
Kant’s famous shopkeeper charges everyone, even a child, the same fair price. Kant adds that doing so only out of self-interest would not yet show true moral worth.
The honest merchant who treats every buyer alike is the Kural’s ideal. Kant’s caveat about motive echoes Kural 119’s demand for an unbent heart.

Real Life Example

The first Fairtrade label, Max Havelaar
15 November 1988 · Netherlands, with coffee from Oaxaca, Mexico
Indigenous coffee farmers of the UCIRI cooperative in Oaxaca, Mexico, asked the Dutch development organisation Solidaridad for access to European markets on equitable terms. Rather than start its own coffee brand, Solidaridad created the Max Havelaar label, launched on 15 November 1988, which could be placed on coffee of any brand whose buyers paid the farmers a price above the market rate plus a premium. The first labelled coffee came from UCIRI and was sold through world shops and mainstream retailers across the Netherlands, and similar labels spread across Europe, leading in 1997 to the umbrella organisation Fairtrade International.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The scheme asked traders and shoppers to treat the farmers' interests as part of the bargain instead of simply paying the lowest possible price.
The kural says true trade means guarding others' interests as one's own; Max Havelaar turned that principle into a working label for ordinary commerce.
