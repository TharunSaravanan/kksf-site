Kural 1046

Poverty · Nalkuravu · நல்குரவு

நற்பொருள் நன்குணர்ந்து சொல்லினும் நல்கூர்ந்தார் சொற்பொருள் சோர்வு படும்.
NaRporuL nangunarndhu sollinum nalkoorndhaar soRporuL soarvu padum.

Meaning

Even when poor people speak true and wise things that they fully understand, their words are ignored and come to nothing.

Elementary

Imagine two children giving the same right answer in class, but everyone only listens to the one with the fancy new shoes. Valluvar noticed that people often ignore what poor people say, even when it is wise, and that this is unfair.

University

NaRporuL (நற்பொருள்) is ‘good matter’, which Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar specify as the sense of good books (nool), and nangunarndhu (‘having understood well’) stresses that the speaker is genuinely learned. Yet the soRporuL (சொற்பொருள், ‘the meaning of the words’) of nalkoorndhaar (நல்கூர்ந்தார், ‘those who have become poor’) suffers soarvu, here ‘failure, lapse’: it is not heard or heeded. The same word soarvu that meant inner weariness in 1044 here describes the social fate of the poor man’s speech, a deliberate echo. Solomon Pappaiah puts it simply: if the speaker is poor, his words are not honoured. Pope’s ‘Their sense from memory of mankind will fade away’ takes the lapse as being forgotten. The observation is descriptive and critical, not an endorsement of the bias. The text matches on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“He that has wealth is regarded as a learned man.”
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, Section VIII (tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
Arjuna bluntly notes that society credits the wealthy with learning, and adds that a poor man is accused falsely even when he stands near.
If wealth is taken for learning, then, as the Kural says, the poor man’s real learning goes unheard.
Christianity
“For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment; And ye have respect to him that weareth the gay clothing... and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool.”
— James 2:2-3 (KJV) [4]
James rebukes a congregation that honours the rich visitor and pushes the poor one aside.
The poor man is dismissed before he speaks, exactly the bias the Kural records, though James condemns it.
Islam
“And they said, ‘Why was this Qur’an not sent down upon a great man from [one of] the two cities?’”
— Qur’an 43:31 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Meccan elite questioned the revelation because it came through a man without great wealth or rank.
They judged the message by the messenger’s means, the very prejudice the Kural observes.
Judaism
“So I observed: Wisdom is better than valor; but The wisdom of the poor is scorned, And their words are not heeded.”
— Ecclesiastes 9:16 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023) [6]
Kohelet tells of a poor wise man who saved a city yet was forgotten, and concludes that the poor man’s wisdom is despised.
This is a near-exact match: wise words from the poor go unheeded.
Buddhism
“...whoever has no conviction with regard to skillful mental qualities... no discernment with regard to skillful mental qualities is, in the discipline of a noble one, said to be poor, destitute, & penniless.”
— Ina Sutta, AN 6.45 (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha redefines real poverty as the lack of conviction, conscience, persistence and discernment.
Against the world’s habit of dismissing the materially poor, the Buddha says the truly poor are those without wisdom, a pointed counterpoint to the Kural’s observation.
Jainism
“If a monk hears bad words, cruel and rankling ones, he should silently overlook them, and not take them to heart.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 2.25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The mendicant, who owns nothing, must expect contempt and abuse from householders and bear it without resentment.
Jainism assumes the poor will be slighted, as the Kural observes, and asks the voluntarily poor to meet it with equanimity.
Sikhism
“No one respects the poor man. He may make thousands of efforts, but no one pays any attention to him.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1159, Raag Bhairao, Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Kabir describes with sharp realism how society ignores the destitute, however hard they try to be heard.
The Kural says that even when the poor speak sound truths, their words are disregarded; Kabir likewise says that whatever the poor man does, no one pays him any attention.
Greek Mythology
When Odysseus, disguised as a beggar in his own hall, speaks wisely to the suitors, Antinous mocks him and hurls a footstool at him; his words carry no weight because he appears to be a destitute stranger.
— Homer, Odyssey 17 [10]
The rightful king is scorned because of his rags, even though his speech is shrewd.
Homer dramatises the Kural’s observation that a poor man’s well-understood words are brushed aside.
Philosophy
“A man’s word is believed in exact proportion to the amount of cash which he keeps in his strong box.”
— Juvenal, Satire 3 (tr. G.G. Ramsay, 1918) [11]
Juvenal’s Umbritius complains that in Rome a witness is asked first about his wealth and last about his character.
This is the Kural’s point stated almost as a formula: credibility tracks money, not understanding.

Real Life Example

The Jarrow March brings a petition of the unemployed to Parliament
5 October - 4 November 1936 · Jarrow to London, England, United Kingdom
After the closure of Palmer's shipyard left most of Jarrow's workforce without jobs, about 200 unemployed men, each medically examined, marched nearly 300 miles to London carrying a petition signed by some 12,000 people asking for new industry. Their MP, Ellen Wilkinson, presented the petition to Parliament on 4 November 1936, but Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin did not meet the marchers, the petition was received without being debated, and the march produced few immediate results.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The marchers' orderly, well-reasoned plea won public sympathy along the route yet was largely passed over by the government, though the march later came to be seen as a defining event of the 1930s.
The kural observes that even sound and well-understood words lose their force when spoken by the poor, and the Jarrow men's carefully argued petition was received in Parliament and left undebated.
