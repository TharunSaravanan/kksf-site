Kural 138

The Possession of Decorum · Ozhukkamudaimai · ஒழுக்கமுடைமை

நன்றிக்கு வித்தாகும் நல்லொழுக்கம் தீயொழுக்கம் என்றும் இடும்பை தரும்.
Nandrikku viththaagum nallozhukkam theeyozhukkam Endrum idumbai tharum.

Meaning

Good behaviour is the seed of good things. Bad behaviour always brings trouble.

Elementary

Plant an apple seed and you get an apple tree. Plant weeds and you get weeds. Being good is like planting a good seed, because nice things grow from it. Being mean only grows trouble.

University

The farming metaphor vittu (seed) makes nallozhukkam generative. Good conduct is not the good itself but the cause from which good (nandri, here ‘benefit’ or ‘virtue’ rather than ‘gratitude’, though one modern commentator reads it that way) grows over time. Its opposite, theeyozhukkam, gives idumbai (distress) endrum, ‘always’, which suggests a continuous harvest rather than a single punishment. Pappaiah extends the fruit to this life and the next (immai, marumai), which aligns the couplet with karmic causation, while Karunanidhi keeps it within this life. The seed image is nearly universal, from Paul's ‘whatsoever a man soweth’ to the Upanishadic ‘as he acts, so he becomes’ and Aristotle's view that character arises from like activities.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“according as he acts and according as he behaves, so will he be:--a man of good acts will become good, a man of bad acts, bad. He becomes pure by pure deeds, bad by bad deeds.”
— Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4.5 (tr. F. Max Müller, SBE 15) [3]
Yajnavalkya teaches that what a person does shapes what they become.
Both say conduct is the seed of what follows: good grows from good, and evil brings its own results.
Christianity
“For he that soweth to his flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption; but he that soweth to the Spirit shall of the Spirit reap life everlasting.”
— Galatians 6:8 (KJV) [4]
Paul uses sowing and reaping to show that the kind of life a person plants decides what they harvest.
It shares the Kural's seed metaphor and its two-sided outcome.
Islam
“Have you not considered how Allāh presents an example, [making] a good word like a good tree, whose root is firmly fixed and its branches [high] in the sky?”
— Qur'an 14:24 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an compares a good word to a deep-rooted tree that keeps bearing fruit.
It shares the Kural's image of goodness as something that grows from a seed or root.
Judaism
“For one commandment leads to another commandment, and transgression leads to another transgression; For the reward for performing a commandment is another commandment and the reward for committing a transgression is a transgression.”
— Pirkei Avot 4:2 (tr. J. Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
Ben Azzai teaches that good and bad deeds each multiply their own kind.
Both describe conduct as self-propagating, with good bearing good and bad bearing bad.
Buddhism
“Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.”
— Dhammapada 1 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
Actions done with a corrupt mind bring suffering that follows as surely as a cart wheel follows the ox.
This echoes the Kural's second half: bad conduct always brings sorrow. The next verse (Dhp 2) gives the good half.
Jainism
“As the burglar caught in the breach of the wall perishes by the work the sinner himself had executed, thus people in this life and the next cannot escape the effect of their own actions.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [8]
The Jain doctrine of karma holds that deeds bind subtle matter to the soul and ripen into their own results in this life and later ones.
Valluvar calls good conduct the seed of good and bad conduct a constant source of sorrow; the burglar crushed by his own tunnel is the Jain image of misconduct bearing its own painful fruit.
Sikhism
“As she has planted, so does she harvest; such is the field of karma.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 134, Raag Maajh, Baarah Maahaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Baarah Maahaa, Guru Arjan calls the body a field of deeds in which each person reaps what she has sown.
The Kural calls good conduct a seed and bad conduct a lasting source of sorrow; Gurbani uses the same sowing-and-reaping image for deeds.
Greek Mythology
“He does mischief to himself who does mischief to another, and evil planned harms the plotter most.”
— Hesiod, Works and Days 265–266 (tr. H. G. Evelyn-White) [10]
Hesiod warns that wrongdoing comes back on the one who does it.
This matches the Kural's claim that bad conduct brings sorrow to its doer.
Philosophy
“Thus, in one word, states of character arise out of like activities.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2.1, 1103b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that repeated acts build up into a settled character of the same kind.
Conduct is the seed of character, as the Kural's vittu suggests.

Real Life Example

Nicholas Winton's rescue of children from Czechoslovakia comes to light
1939; revealed 1988 · Prague, Czechoslovakia, and London, United Kingdom
Nicholas Winton, a London stockbroker, went to Prague at the end of 1938 and, working with colleagues including Trevor Chadwick and Doreen Warriner, organised transports that brought 669 children, most of them Jewish, from Czechoslovakia to Britain in 1939. He said little about it for nearly fifty years, until his wife found his 1939 scrapbook of the children's names and photographs. In 1988 he appeared on the BBC programme That's Life!, where he was surprised to meet some of the people he had rescued, now adults.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A single season of good work in 1939 grew, unseen, into hundreds of lives and the families that followed them, and was recognised only decades later.
The kural calls good conduct a seed from which good things grow; Winton's actions bore fruit long after they were done, in people he had not seen for half a century.
