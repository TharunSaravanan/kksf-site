Kural 1070

The Dread of Mendicancy · Iravaccham · இரவச்சம்

கரப்பவர்க்கு யாங்கொளிக்கும் கொல்லோ இரப்பவர் சொல்லாடப் போஒம் உயிர்.
Karappavarkku yaangolikkum kollo irappavar sollaadap pooom uyir.

Meaning

The life of a beggar leaves them the moment they hear 'no.' So where does the life of the person who says 'no' hide itself?

Elementary

When someone asks for help and hears a cold 'no', it can feel like all their hope has gone. The Kural wonders how the person who said 'no' while having plenty can keep living so easily, as if their heart has gone into hiding.

University

The chapter ends with a bitter rhetorical question built on a pun: karappavarkku yaangu olikkum kollo ('where does it hide for the concealers?'), the 'it' being uyir ('life'), which in the second line pooom ('goes away', with lengthening for metre) from the irappavar ('askers') as soon as the word of refusal is spoken (sollaada, 'when the word is uttered'). Commentators in the Parimelazhagar line, as given by Puliyur Kesikan and Mu. Varadarasanar, read it as amazement: since the refusal kills the asker, the refuser's own life ought to depart too, so where does it hide? Pope renders it 'E'en as he asks, the shamefaced asker dies; / Where shall his spirit hide who help denies?' and paraphrases that the very word that kills the beggar should also kill the refuser. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A widely quoted verse of the Mahabharata and Puranas says that when a guest leaves a house with his hope broken, he leaves his sins to the householder and takes away the householder's merit.
— Mahabharata, Santi Parva, and Vishnu Purana 3.9, verse on the disappointed guest (paraphrase) [3]
Hindu householder ethics hold that turning away a hopeful guest robs the householder of his own spiritual life.
Like the Kural, it says the refusal that crushes the asker should rebound on the one who refuses.
Christianity
“Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard.”
— Proverbs 21:13 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs warns that whoever shuts his ears to the poor will one day cry out and not be heard.
Like the Kural, Proverbs expects the refuser's own life to pay for the refusal.
Islam
“But as for he who withholds and considers himself free of need / And denies the best [reward], / We will ease him toward difficulty. / And what will his wealth avail him when he falls?”
— Qur'an 92:8-11 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an says the one who withholds and thinks himself self-sufficient will be eased towards hardship, and his wealth will not save him.
The Kural asks where the refuser's life can hide; the Qur'an answers that his wealth will not shelter him.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that anyone who averts his eyes from giving charity is regarded as though he were worshipping idols.
— Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 68a (paraphrase) [6]
Rabbinic teaching treats refusing charity as one of the gravest sins.
The Kural's amazement that the refuser's life does not depart fits this view of refusal as near-idolatry.
Buddhism
The Vasala Sutta says that whoever, when a brahmin or ascetic comes at mealtime, does not offer food but scolds him instead, is to be known as a true outcast.
— Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7 (paraphrase) [7]
The Buddha redefines the outcast as someone who refuses and insults a person asking for food.
The Kural's refuser, whose 'no' kills the asker, is the Buddha's true outcast.
Jainism
“If a person being tormented with poverty, rejecting true wisdom and allowing ignorance to abide in his mind, goes to a person and says, Give me alms, and if the person so asked refuse to give, will he not die from very shame at that moment?”
— Naladiyar, ch. 31 (Dread of mendicity), stanza 8 (English translation in The Indian Antiquary, via wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Jain Tamil poet says that a poor man who asks and is refused dies of shame on the spot.
This is the second line of the Kural: the asker's life departs at the word of refusal.
Sikhism
“It was not gathered without sin, and it does not go along with the dead.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Asa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn of Raag Asa, Guru Nanak says that hoarded wealth cannot be gathered without wrongdoing and cannot be carried past death.
The Kural bitterly asks where the life of the refuser will hide when the asker's life leaves him at the word 'no'; Guru Nanak answers that the hoarded wealth the refuser clings to will not go with him either.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid, Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as weary travellers, are turned away from a thousand homes; only the poor couple Baucis and Philemon take them in, and the gods then drown the whole neighbourhood that refused them.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8 (Baucis and Philemon) (paraphrase) [10]
The households that shut their doors on needy strangers are destroyed, while the generous poor are saved.
Ovid answers the Kural's question: the refusers' lives cannot hide forever.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes meanness (illiberality) as falling short in giving and exceeding in taking, says it is incurable and more natural to people than prodigality, and counts it a greater evil.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (tr. W. D. Ross) (paraphrase) [11]
For Aristotle the illiberal person, who will not give, has one of the most deep-rooted vices.
The Kural's refuser shows the vice Aristotle condemns.

Real Life Example

George III refuses to receive the Olive Branch Petition
July - September 1775 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, and London, England
In July 1775 the Second Continental Congress adopted the Olive Branch Petition, written chiefly by John Dickinson, professing loyalty to King George III and asking him to help end the conflict with the colonies. Richard Penn and Arthur Lee delivered it in London, but the king would not receive it, having already proclaimed on 23 August 1775 that the colonies were in open rebellion. Congress made no further attempt at reconciliation and declared independence the following July.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The refusal closed the last door to reconciliation, and within a few years Britain lost the thirteen colonies.
The kural asks where the life of the refuser can hide when a refusal crushes the one who asks, and the king's refusal of the colonists' petition led to the loss of Britain's own empire in America.
