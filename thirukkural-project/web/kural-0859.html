Kural 859

Hostility · Ikal · இகல்

இகல்காணான் ஆக்கம் வருங்கால் அதனை மிகல்காணும் கேடு தரற்கு.
Ikalkaanaan aakkam varungaal adhanai mikalkaanum kedu thararku.

Meaning

When good times are coming, a person does not even look at hostility. When harm is coming to them, they try to make their hostility bigger.

Elementary

When you are having a great day, a small bump does not bother you. But on a bad day, you might turn a tiny bump into a huge fight that makes everything worse. The poet says growing a quarrel is a sign that trouble is on its way.

University

The couplet reads hostility as a symptom of one’s turning fortune. Ikal kaanaan (இகல் காணான், ‘does not regard hostility’) when aakkam (prosperity) is coming; but to bring on kēdu (கேடு, ruin) he mikal kaanum (மிகல் காணும், ‘sees its increase’, magnifies it). Puliyur Kesigan and Pappaiah add that the fortunate man ignores hostility even with cause, while the one heading for ruin seeks it even without cause. Mu. Varadarasanar reads mikal as ‘seeking to win against it’. The underlying idea, that a mind inclines toward quarrel when its own fall is approaching, resembles the Sanskrit maxim vināśa-kāle viparīta-buddhi (‘at the time of ruin the mind turns perverse’), though Valluvar states it without fatalism. Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Udyoga Parva, Krishna travels to Hastinapura as envoy and offers Duryodhana peace for as little as five villages for the Pandavas; Duryodhana, on the brink of disaster, rejects every counsel of peace and even tries to seize Krishna, choosing the war that destroys his line.
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Bhagavad-yana) [3]
On the eve of ruin, Duryodhana magnifies the quarrel instead of ending it.
He is the Kural’s man who ‘looks to its increase’ when bringing ruin on himself.
Christianity
“Then said Jesus unto him, Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword.”
— Matthew 26:52 (KJV) [4]
Jesus warns that choosing to escalate conflict leads to one’s own destruction.
The Kural similarly ties the escalation of hostility to self-made ruin.
Islam
“Shall I not inform you of what is more virtuous than the rank of fasting, Salat, and charity? ... Making peace between each other. For indeed spoiling relations with each other is the Haliqah.”
— Jami` at-Tirmidhi 2509 [5]
The hadith calls the corruption of relationships ‘the shaver’, something that strips away what one has.
Like the Kural, it treats magnifying discord as a mark and cause of ruin.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that the Second Temple was destroyed, although its generation studied Torah and did good deeds, because of baseless hatred (sinat chinam), which it weighs as equal to idolatry, sexual sin and bloodshed together.
— Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 9b [6]
A society on the verge of catastrophe was marked by unprovoked hatred.
This echoes the Kural: hostility without cause accompanies approaching ruin.
Buddhism
“Mind precedes all mental states. Mind is their chief; they are all mind-wrought. If with an impure mind a person speaks or acts suffering follows him like the wheel that follows the foot of the ox.”
— Dhammapada 1 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that a corrupted mind brings suffering after it as surely as a cart follows an ox.
The Kural similarly sees the turn toward hostility as the mind bringing its own ruin.
Jainism
“Men who adhering to wrong principles acquire wealth by evil deeds, will lose it, falling into the snares (of their passions) and being held captive by their hatred.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 4.2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The lecture on impurity warns that wealth gained wrongly is lost once a person falls under the power of his passions and hatred.
The Kural ties a person's rising or falling fortune to whether he ignores or magnifies hostility, and the Uttarādhyayana likewise links losing one's wealth to being held captive by hatred.
Sikhism
“Those, whom the Creator Lord would destroy - first He strips them of virtue.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 417, Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a hymn in Raag Aasaa, Guru Nanak observes that those headed for destruction first lose their goodness.
The Kural says that a man bringing ruin on himself looks to magnify hostility; Guru Nanak likewise reads the loss of good judgment and virtue as the first sign of coming ruin.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ Antigone, the Chorus recalls the ancient saying that evil comes to seem good to the one whose mind a god is leading toward ruin, as Creon stubbornly hardens his quarrel with Antigone and Haemon.
— Sophocles, Antigone 620-625 [10]
The Greeks saw stubborn, worsening quarrel as a sign that doom was near.
This is close to the Kural’s observation that ruin-bound minds magnify hostility.
Philosophy
Seneca opens On Anger by describing anger as heedless of itself, eager to harm another, rushing onto weapons and greedy for a vengeance that drags the avenger down with it.
— Seneca, De Ira I.1 [11]
Seneca portrays hostile passion as a drive toward one’s own destruction.
The Kural similarly sees magnified hostility as the means by which a person brings on ruin.

Real Life Example

Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia
23-28 July 1914 · Vienna, Austria-Hungary, and Belgrade, Serbia
After the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the Austro-Hungarian government in July 1914 drafted an ultimatum to Serbia in terms intended to be impossible to accept, and delivered it on 23 July. Serbia replied on time on 25 July, accepting all but two of the demands and offering international arbitration, but Austria-Hungary declared the reply insufficient and declared war on 28 July. The war that followed ended in 1918 with the collapse of the Habsburg monarchy.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
By choosing to escalate rather than accept a largely conciliatory reply, the Habsburg government began the war that destroyed its own empire.
The kural says that a person heading for ruin looks to magnify hostility, and Austria-Hungary deliberately enlarged a dispute that Serbia had offered to settle, setting off its own downfall.
