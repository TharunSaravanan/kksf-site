Kural 403

Ignorance · Kallaamai · கல்லாமை

கல்லா தவரும் நனிநல்லர் கற்றார்முன் சொல்லா திருக்கப் பெறின்.
Kallaa thavarum naninallar katraarmun Sollaa thirukkap perin.

Meaning

Even uneducated people will be thought of as very good, as long as they stay quiet in front of learned people.

Elementary

If you don’t know much about a subject and you’re with experts, the smartest thing you can do is stay quiet and listen. People will actually think well of you for it. Talking would show what you don’t know; silence protects you and lets you learn.

University

The irony of nani nallar (‘exceedingly good’) is gentle but pointed: the unlearned person’s one available virtue is restraint. Parimelazhagar notes that silence both conceals the defect and avoids the harm of error. The verse inverts the previous kural — where the unlearned craved speech, here he is redeemed by abstaining from it. This is one of the most widely shared moral intuitions across cultures: Proverbs 17:28 (‘Even a fool, when he holdeth his peace, is counted wise’), Rabbi Akiva’s ‘a fence for wisdom is silence’, and the famous Sanskrit maxim of Bhartrhari that silence is the ornament of the unlearned in an assembly of scholars, which is an almost exact parallel. The Buddhist corpus adds the self-knowledge dimension: the fool who knows he is a fool is to that extent wise.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari’s Nitishataka says the Creator gave ignorance a covering that is entirely in one’s own control — silence — and that, especially in an assembly of the learned, silence is the ornament of the unlearned (vibhūṣaṇaṃ maunam apaṇḍitānām).
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka, verse 7 (numbering varies by edition) [4]
The unlearned always have a way to hide their ignorance: simply to stay quiet, above all among scholars.
This is nearly the Kural word for word: among the learned, the unlearned are best off silent.
Christianity
“Even a fool, when he holdeth his peace, is counted wise: and he that shutteth his lips is esteemed a man of understanding.”
— Proverbs 17:28 (KJV) [5]
A foolish person who keeps quiet will be taken for wise, because no one hears anything foolish from him.
Exactly the Kural’s claim: the unlearned become ‘very good’ by staying silent.
Islam
“Anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should talk what is good or keep quiet.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6018 (tr. Muhsin Khan) [6]
The Prophet taught that a believer should either say something good or stay silent.
Both treat silence as the right course when one has nothing sound to contribute.
Judaism
Rabbi Akiva teaches that silence is a protective fence around wisdom.
— Pirkei Avot 3:13 [7]
Just as a fence protects a field, keeping quiet protects and preserves wisdom.
The Kural likewise makes silence the safeguard of the person who lacks learning.
Buddhism
“A fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least to that extent, but a fool who thinks himself wise is a fool indeed.”
— Dhammapada 63 (tr. Buddharakkhita) [8]
Recognizing one’s own ignorance is already a kind of wisdom; the real fool is the one who thinks he knows.
The unlearned person who stays quiet before the learned shows exactly this self-awareness, and so earns the Kural’s praise.
Jainism
“One should always be meek, and not be talkative in the presence of the wise; one should acquire valuable knowledge, and avoid what is worthless.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 1.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Mahavira's first lecture on discipline tells the pupil to stay meek and quiet before the wise and to spend his effort gaining real knowledge.
Valluvar says the unlearned can still be counted good if they keep silent before the learned. The Jain rule of discipline asks for exactly that silence in the presence of the wise.
Sikhism
“To speak too much and babble is useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 661, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
In Raag Dhanaasree Guru Nanak warns that much talking turns into idle babble.
The Kural says the unlearned keep their worth only by keeping quiet before the learned; Guru Nanak likewise counts excess speech as worthless.
Greek Mythology
Diogenes Laertius reports that Pythagoras’s new pupils had to keep silence for five years, only listening to his discourses, before they were admitted to see him.
— Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers 8.10 [11]
In the Pythagorean school, beginners earned the right to speak by first listening in silence for a long time.
The same principle as the Kural: those who have not yet learned should be silent in the presence of those who have.
Philosophy
“Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.”
— Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, proposition 7 (tr. C. K. Ogden) [12]
Wittgenstein ends his Tractatus by saying that where language cannot meaningfully reach, one should not try to speak.
Both counsel silence at the boundary of what one can responsibly say, though Wittgenstein’s boundary is logical, not personal learning.

Real Life Example

George Washington stays almost silent at the Constitutional Convention
May-September 1787 · Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States
George Washington, whose formal schooling appears to have ended at about fifteen and who never attended college, was chosen to preside over the Constitutional Convention, where many delegates were college-educated men trained in law and the classics. As presiding officer he called on speakers but took almost no part in the substantive debates; according to the record, he first spoke on the issues on the final day, 17 September 1787, to support reducing the number of people each member of the House of Representatives would represent from 40,000 to 30,000. His presence is generally credited with lending the convention prestige and authority.
— Sources: [13] [14] [15]
Washington, who privately described himself as conscious of a defective education, let the convention's legal theorists argue while he presided, and his reputation for judgement rose rather than fell.
The kural says even the unlearned are counted very good if they keep from speaking in the presence of the learned, and Washington's near-silence among more formally educated colleagues only enhanced his standing.
