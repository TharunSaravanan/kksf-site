Kural 1138

The Abandonment of Reserve · Naanuth Thuravuraithal · நாணுத் துறவுரைத்தல்

நிறையரியர் மன்அளியர் என்னாது காமம் மறையிறந்து மன்று படும்.
Niraiyariyar manaliyar ennaadhu kaamam maraiyirandhu mandru padum.

Meaning

Love does not stop to think whether someone is self-controlled or deserves pity. It breaks out of hiding and shows itself in the open street.

Elementary

Have you ever tried to keep a happy secret, but your big smile gave it away? Love can be like that. Even when someone tries very hard to hide it, it shows on their face and everyone in town finds out.

University

The key words are niṟai (நிறை), 'firmness of mind, the power to keep one's feelings in', aḷiyar (அளியர்), 'those deserving pity', maṟai (மறை), 'secrecy', and maṉṟu (மன்று), the village common or public assembly ground where talk spreads. The opening phrase niṟai-ariyar is read in two opposite ways: Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar take it as 'lacking restraint', while Solomon Pappaiah reads 'very restrained' (ariyar as 'hard to overcome'), and Pope follows the second ('In virtue hard to move, yet very tender, too, are we'). Pope's 'we' reflects the common view that from here the heroine is speaking, lamenting that her love is about to become public gossip (alar), which leads into chapter 115. The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Rukmini, though a modest princess promised by her brother to another, sends a brahmin messenger with a letter to Krishna declaring her love and asking him to carry her away before the wedding.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.52 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana praises Rukmini's love for breaking through the reserve expected of her.
As in the Kural, love refuses to stay hidden in a well-guarded heart and makes itself known.
Christianity
“I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love.”
— Song of Solomon 5:8 (KJV) [4]
The beloved openly asks the women of the city to carry word of her love-sickness.
Like the Kural's speaker, she finds that her love has passed beyond private feeling into public knowledge.
Islam
“Some women of the city gossiped, ‘The Chief Minister’s wife is trying to seduce her slave-boy. Love for him has plagued her heart.’”
— Qur'an 12:30 (tr. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran) [5]
The Qur'an tells how the Egyptian noblewoman's passion for Joseph became the talk of the city.
The verse is a vivid case of the Kural's warning: love breaks out of secrecy and becomes public gossip.
Judaism
“When I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; Yea, and none would despise me.”
— Song of Songs 8:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved wishes her love could be shown openly in the street without bringing scorn.
She feels the same tension as the Kural: love pressing to be seen in public while society watches and judges.
Buddhism
The Buddha names three things that are carried on in secret rather than in the open, among them affairs with women, and contrasts them with things that shine openly, like the sun, the moon and the Dhamma.
— Anguttara Nikaya 3.129 (paraphrase) [7]
The discourse treats secrecy as a mark of what cannot bear the light.
The Kural observes that love does not stay secret; the Buddhist saying explains why lovers try to keep it so.
Jainism
“The daughter of the best king, true to self-control and her vows, maintained the honour of her clan and family, and her virtue, and spoke to him.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Faced with Rathanemi's sudden passion, Rajimati keeps her composure and her family's honour.
The Kural says love ignores a person's restraint and breaks into the open; the Jain text praises a woman whose restraint held firm.
Sikhism
“By various efforts, I have enshrined it within my heart; this jewel cannot be hidden by hiding it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 659, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Bheekhan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Bhagat Bheekhan says the jewel of the Lord's Name, though carefully kept within the heart, cannot be hidden however one tries.
The Kural says love does not respect restraint but breaks its secrecy and shows itself in the public square; Bheekhan's jewel of love likewise will not stay concealed.
Greek Mythology
In Euripides' Hippolytus, Queen Phaedra resolves to hide her love for her stepson in silence and even to starve herself, but her nurse draws the secret from her and it spreads, with tragic results.
— Euripides, Hippolytus (paraphrase) [10]
The play is built around a love that its bearer tries desperately to conceal and cannot.
Phaedra's story is a tragic form of the Kural's point: love will not stay behind the screen of restraint.
Philosophy
“There is no disguise which can long hide love where it exists, nor feign it where it does not.”
— François de La Rochefoucauld, Maxims, no. 70 (Project Gutenberg English edition) [11]
La Rochefoucauld observes that real love always shows itself in the end.
This is almost the Kural's own statement: love breaks through every attempt to keep it hidden.

Real Life Example

Crown Prince Harald's secret romance with Sonja Haraldsen becomes public
1959-1968 · Oslo, Norway
Crown Prince Harald of Norway and Sonja Haraldsen, who was not of royal descent, were a couple for several years before the relationship became public; rumours of a girlfriend he wished to marry spread in 1967 and 1968. The press and public then debated whether a commoner could become queen, and Harald made clear that if he could not marry Sonja he would consider staying unmarried for life. After about nine years together King Olav V consented, and they married in Oslo Cathedral on 29 August 1968.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A love kept quiet for years could not stay hidden forever, and once it came out it was argued over by the whole nation.
The kural says love does not stop to weigh restraint but breaks out of its secrecy into the public square; Harald and Sonja's long-hidden love ended up debated across all of Norway.
