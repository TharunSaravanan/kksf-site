Kural 713

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

அவையறியார் சொல்லல்மேற் கொள்பவர் சொல்லின் வகையறியார் வல்லதூஉம் இல்.
Avaiyariyaar sollalmer kolpavar sollin Vakaiyariyaar valladhooum il.

Meaning

People who start speaking without understanding their listeners do not know how words work. They have no real skill at all.

Elementary

Imagine telling a scary ghost story at a little kid’s birthday party. The story may be good, but you picked the wrong crowd, and now everyone is crying. Not knowing your listeners can spoil even good words.

University

This kural is the negative of 711. Avaiyariyaar (those who do not know the assembly) who take up speaking (sollal mer kolpavar) are thereby shown to be sollin vakaiyariyaar, ignorant of the kinds of words. Valladhooum il (they have no ability whatever) uses an alapedai, a lengthened vowel (thooum), for emphasis. Parimelazhagar’s point is that eloquence cannot be separated from knowledge of the audience. A speaker who misjudges the assembly proves thereby that he does not know how words work, however learned he may be. The sandhi text reads “சொல்லன்மேற் கொள்பவர்”, and “வல்லதூஉ மில்”, which divide into the same words as சொல்லல்மேற் and வல்லதூஉம் இல்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“That worst of men who entereth a place uninvited, and talketh much without being asked, and reposeth trust on untrustworthy wights, is a fool.”
— Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Section 33 (Vidura Niti), trans. K. M. Ganguli [4]
In his list of the marks of a fool, Vidura includes the man who talks at length when no one has asked him.
Talking “without being asked”, without reading the room, is how the Kural’s avaiyariyaar give themselves away.
Christianity
“He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him.”
— Proverbs 18:13 (KJV) [5]
The proverb warns that answering before you have listened brings shame on the speaker.
Speaking before you understand the matter or the assembly is the folly the Kural describes.
Islam
“And do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, the hearing, the sight and the heart - about all those [one] will be questioned.”
— Qur’an 17:36 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an forbids acting or speaking on matters you do not know, and warns that a person will answer for how he used his faculties.
The speaker who does not know the assembly is acting without knowledge, which this verse forbids.
Judaism
“The words of a wise man’s mouth are gracious; but the lips of a fool will swallow up himself.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:12 (KJV) [5]
Koheleth contrasts the gracious speech of the wise with a fool’s talk, which ruins the fool himself.
The Kural’s speaker who misreads the assembly is undone by his own words, as the fool is here.
Buddhism
“One is not wise because one speaks much. He who is peaceable, friendly and fearless is called wise.”
— Dhammapada 258 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada separates wisdom from talkativeness: a lot of speech is no evidence of wisdom.
The Kural likewise denies real ability to the person who is eager to speak but does not understand the assembly.
Jainism
“He who correctly knows the sacred texts, who practises austerities, who understands all details of the Law, who is an authentic interpreter, clever, and learned--such a man is competent to explain the entire creed.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.14.27 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga closes its lecture on the true monk by stating who is qualified to speak on the Law. By implication, one without that understanding should not take it up.
The Kural says that whoever speaks without the needed understanding has no real ability; the Jain verse sets out the competence a speaker must have.
Sikhism
“To speak too much and babble is useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 661, Raag Dhanasri, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak dismisses excessive talk as empty babble.
The Kural says one who speaks without understanding the assembly shows no real ability; Guru Nanak calls speech that is not grounded in understanding mere useless babble.
Greek Mythology
In the Greek assembly, Thersites, a man of disorderly words, rails at Agamemnon out of turn. Odysseus rebukes him and strikes him with the sceptre, and the assembled army laughs at him.
— Homer, Iliad 2.211-277 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer presents Thersites as the model of someone who speaks without any sense of place, rank, or occasion, and who is shamed for it.
Thersites is the avaiyariyaar of this kural: he speaks without knowing the assembly and ends up with no standing at all.
Philosophy
In his essay on talkativeness, Plutarch describes the chatterer who bursts into every conversation regardless of the company, is ignored or laughed at, and finds that nobody believes or values what he says.
— Plutarch, De garrulitate (On Talkativeness), Moralia 502B-515A (trans. W. C. Helmbold) [11]
Plutarch argues that the habitual talker loses the very thing speech exists for, which is being heard and trusted.
Plutarch reaches the Kural’s verdict: whoever speaks without regard to the listeners has no real power of speech.

Real Life Example

Wagner's Tannhauser is whistled off the stage of the Paris Opera
March 1861 · Paris Opera (Salle Le Peletier), Paris, France
Wagner revised Tannhauser for the Paris Opera and, to meet its convention, agreed to add a ballet, but he placed it in the first act rather than in its traditional place in the second. Members of the Jockey Club, who usually arrived after dinner for the second half of the evening and missed the dancing, disrupted the performances with whistles and catcalls, joined by political opponents of the production's sponsors. After 164 rehearsals, the opera was withdrawn after only three performances.
— Sources: [12] [13]
However strong the music, Wagner's disregard of what the Paris audience expected turned the premiere into a fiasco.
The kural says one who speaks without first understanding the assembly shows no real ability, and Wagner's refusal to accommodate the Paris Opera's audience sank the production despite its merits.
