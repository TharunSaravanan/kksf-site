Kural 391

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

கற்க கசடறக் கற்பவை கற்றபின் நிற்க அதற்குத் தக.
Karka kasadarak karpavai katrapin Nirka adharkuth thaga.

Meaning

Learn what is worth learning, and learn it fully, without gaps. Then live in a way that matches what you have learned.

Elementary

Imagine learning all the rules of football perfectly but then cheating in the game. That would make the learning pointless. This kural says two things: learn good things properly, then actually live by them.

University

The chapter on Kalvi opens with a two-step command. The first is கற்க கசடற: learn so that no flaw (kasadu) is left, which traditional commentators such as Parimelazhagar read as choosing the right texts and also removing one's own mental faults through study. The second is நிற்க அதற்குத் தக: stand, or remain, in conduct fitting to that learning. The verb நிற்க (to stand) is significant, because it pictures learning as a place one lives in and not only a set of facts one holds. Because the chapter sits in the Porul section, just after the chapter on kingship, this is also advice to rulers: a king's learning is shown in how he governs, not in how much he can recite. The same link between knowing and doing appears across many traditions, from the Epistle of James to the Stoic Epictetus.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the teacher's farewell address to graduating students in the Taittiriya Upanishad, the student is told to speak the truth, practise dharma, and never neglect study (svadhyaya).
— Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.1 (Shiksha Valli) [3]
This is a traditional convocation charge. When formal study ends, the teacher says learning must now become right conduct and must also continue as lifelong self-study.
Like the Kural, it treats finishing one's studies as the start of living by them. Both parts are there: study faithfully, then act accordingly.
Christianity
“But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves.”
— James 1:22 (KJV) [4]
James warns believers that hearing or knowing God's teaching is not enough. Anyone who does not act on it is fooling themselves.
This matches the second half of the Kural: once you have learned, stand by it in action.
Islam
“O you who have believed, why do you say what you do not do? Greatly hateful in the sight of Allāh is that you say what you do not do.”
— Qur'an 61:2–3 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an condemns a gap between what people profess and what they practise. Knowing and saying the right thing without doing it is called hateful to God.
It gives the negative side of the Kural's command. Once you know what is right, your conduct has to match it.
Judaism
“Anyone whose deeds exceed his wisdom, his wisdom is enduring, but anyone whose wisdom exceeds his deeds, his wisdom is not enduring.”
— Pirkei Avot 3:9 (Kulp translation, Sefaria) [6]
Rabbi Hanina ben Dosa teaches that learning lasts only when it is rooted in and outweighed by action. Wisdom without deeds withers away.
This is almost a Jewish twin of the Kural: learning is real only when conduct stands in line with it.
Buddhism
“Much though he recites the sacred texts, but acts not accordingly, that heedless man is like a cowherd who only counts the cows of others — he does not partake of the blessings of the holy life.”
— Dhammapada 19 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha compares someone who knows many texts but does not practise them to a hired cowherd. He counts cattle that are not his own and never gets their milk.
Same point as the Kural: learning that is not lived gives nothing to the learner.
Jainism
“One does not attain liberation (merely) by the (study of) scripture, if he has no faith with regard to the nature of reality; or one who has faith cannot attain Nirvana if he is devoid of moral discipline.”
— Pravacanasara III.237 (Kundakunda, tr. A.N. Upadhye; jainworld.com) [8]
Kundakunda insists that scriptural learning is necessary but not enough. Knowledge has to be joined with right faith and lived out as right conduct, the three jewels of the Jain path.
Valluvar tells us to learn flawlessly and then to stand firm in conduct worthy of what we have learned. Kundakunda says the same: learning without matching conduct does not reach the goal.
Sikhism
“Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 62, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that knowing the Truth is supreme, but living it out in one's conduct ranks higher still.
The Kural asks us to learn flawlessly and then to stand by what we have learned; Guru Nanak places conduct that matches the truth above knowledge of it, which is the second half of the couplet.
Greek Mythology
Athena is at once the goddess of wisdom (metis) and the patron of practical crafts such as weaving, shipbuilding and strategy. In Greek myth her wisdom always shows itself in skilled action.
— General Greek mythology (Homeric Hymn 28 to Athena; Hesiod, Theogony 886–900 on Metis) [10]
Athena's wisdom is never idle. It appears in finished work, sound counsel and well-fought battles.
Loosely parallel: Greek myth pictures true wisdom as knowledge expressed in deeds, as the Kural asks.
Philosophy
“Never call yourself a philosopher, nor talk a great deal among the unlearned about theorems, but act conformably to them.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 46 [11]
Epictetus tells his students that philosophy is proven by behaviour, not by talk. At a dinner party, do not lecture on how people should eat. Simply eat as one should.
This echoes the Kural's second line, where learning must be shown by standing in conduct that fits it.

Real Life Example

Gandhi reads Unto This Last and founds the Phoenix Settlement
1904 · Johannesburg to Durban, and Phoenix, Natal, South Africa
In 1904 Mohandas Gandhi's friend Henry Polak gave him John Ruskin's Unto This Last to read on the overnight train from Johannesburg to Durban. Gandhi later wrote that he could not put the book down, could not sleep that night, and resolved to change his life according to its ideals. Within weeks he moved the press of his newspaper Indian Opinion to a farm near Durban, the Phoenix Settlement, where he and co-workers such as Albert West lived and worked together as equals.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Gandhi did not treat the book as information to be admired; he reorganised his work and daily life around it almost at once, and later translated it into Gujarati as Sarvodaya.
The kural asks that one learn what is worth learning thoroughly and then stand in conduct that fits it, which is exactly what Gandhi did after one night's reading.
