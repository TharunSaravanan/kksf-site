Kural 1160

Unendurable Separation · Pirivaatraamai · பிரிவாற்றாமை

அரிதாற்றி அல்லல்நோய் நீக்கிப் பிரிவாற்றிப் பின்இருந்து வாழ்வார் பலர்.
Aridhaatri allalnoi neekkip pirivaatrip pinirundhu vaazhvaar palar.

Meaning

Many people, it seems, can bear the unbearable, push away the pain, live through being apart, and still go on living afterwards.

Elementary

Sometimes a grown-up says, ‘Lots of people manage when someone they love goes away,’ and you think, ‘Maybe they do, but I don’t know how I would!’ The wife in this couplet says something like that: other people may be able to bear it, but she is not sure she can.

University

The couplet strings together four participles: aritu āṟṟi (அரிது ஆற்றி), ‘bearing what is hard’ or consenting to the impossible; allal nōy nīkki (அல்லல் நோய் நீக்கி), ‘driving off the sickness of sorrow’; pirivu āṟṟi (பிரிவு ஆற்றி), ‘enduring the separation’; and piṉ iruntu vāḻvār (பின் இருந்து வாழ்வார்), ‘those who live on afterwards’, before the predicate palar (பலர்), ‘are many’. Commentators differ on the tone: Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan read it as a straightforward observation that many women endure, Solomon Pappaiah as a statement about wives whose husbands go to earn, while Kalaignar and Pope’s prose gloss (‘As if there were many indeed…’) take it as irony, so that the heroine implies she is not one of them. The chapter title, Pirivāṟṟāmai, ‘not being able to bear separation’, supports the ironic reading. Pope’s verse: ‘Sorrow’s sadness meek sustaining, Driving sore distress away, / Separation uncomplaining Many bear the livelong day!’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Held captive in Ravana’s Ashoka grove for many months, Sita endures separation from Rama, refusing every pressure and keeping herself alive by her hope in him.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda (paraphrase) [3]
The Sundara Kanda presents Sita as the model of a wife who bears an almost unbearable separation and survives it.
Sita is one of the ‘many’ the Kural speaks of, who endure the impossible and live on.
Christianity
“Rejoicing in hope; patient in tribulation; continuing instant in prayer.”
— Romans 12:12 (KJV) [4]
Paul urges believers to endure hardship patiently, sustained by hope.
Paul describes the patient endurance that the Kural says many manage, and that its heroine doubts she can.
Islam
“Rather, your souls have enticed you to something, so patience is most fitting. Perhaps Allah will bring them to me all together.”
— Qur’an 12:83 (Sahih International) [5]
Having now lost two sons, Jacob chooses beautiful patience and lives on in hope of reunion.
Jacob is one of the many who bear separation and keep living, the very thing the Kural’s heroine says others manage.
Judaism
The Talmud tells how Rachel, daughter of Kalba Savua, married Akiva and then sent him away to study Torah, enduring twelve years and then another twelve of separation in poverty until he returned as a great sage.
— Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 62b-63a (paraphrase) [6]
Rachel is honoured in rabbinic tradition for bearing a long separation for the sake of her husband’s learning.
Rachel is a clear example of the ‘many’ who agree to a hard parting and live through it.
Buddhism
“This is the greater: the tears you have shed while transmigrating & wandering this long, long time — crying & weeping from being joined with what is displeasing, being separated from what is pleasing — not the water in the four great oceans.”
— Samyutta Nikaya 15.3, Assu Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells his monks that, across countless lives, every being has wept over separations more than all the oceans hold.
In the Buddhist view everyone has endured separation from what is dear countless times, which gives the Kural’s ‘many’ a cosmic scale.
Jainism
Forsaken by Arishtanemi on her wedding day, Rajimati does not die of grief but enters the order, leads many relatives and servants to follow her, and eventually attains omniscience and final liberation.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.29-48 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) (paraphrase) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana shows Rajimati enduring her abandonment and turning it into a spiritual life.
Rajimati is one of the ‘many’ who bear separation and live on, though the Jain path transforms the grief rather than simply enduring it.
Sikhism
“Die before the one whom you love; to live after he dies is to live a worthless life in this world.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 83, Sri Raag ki Vaar (salok), Guru Angad (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Angad says that a true lover would rather die first than go on living once the beloved is gone.
The Kural's heroine says, with irony, that many seem able to bear separation and live on afterwards, implying she is not one of them; Guru Angad says outright that such a life after the beloved is worthless.
Greek Mythology
Penelope waits twenty years for Odysseus, putting off her suitors by weaving and unweaving Laertes’ shroud, grieving for him constantly yet keeping the household going until he returns.
— Homer, Odyssey 2 and 19 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer presents Penelope as the classic example of a wife who endures a long separation.
Penelope is one of the ‘many’ the Kural speaks of, who bear the pain of absence and live on.
Philosophy
Writing to Lucilius on the death of a friend, Seneca allows that tears may fall but urges that grief be moderate, noting that time eventually ends grief even for those who will not end it themselves.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 63 (paraphrase) [11]
Seneca treats the endurance of loss as something reason and time make possible for most people.
Seneca speaks for the ‘many’ who recover from separation; the Kural’s heroine doubts she will be among them.

Real Life Example

Nelson Mandela's prison letters to Winnie Mandela
1962 to 1990 · Robben Island and other prisons, South Africa
Nelson Mandela was held as a sentenced prisoner from 1962 to 1990, in Pretoria Local, Robben Island, Pollsmoor and Victor Verster prisons. At first he was allowed to write and receive only one 500-word letter every six months, and censors read everything he sent. Among the letters he wrote were letters to his wife, Winnie, in which he professed his love and supported her through her own struggles under apartheid; they were published in 2018 as The Prison Letters of Nelson Mandela.
— Sources: [12] [13]
For nearly three decades a husband and wife kept their bond alive through a few strictly limited and censored letters.
The kural speaks of people who somehow bear what seems unbearable, push back their sorrow and live on through a long separation, and the Mandelas endured such a parting for twenty-seven years.
