Kural 1279

The Reading of the Signs · Kuripparivuruthal · குறிப்பறிவுறுத்தல்

தொடிநோக்கி மென்தோளும் நோக்கி அடிநோக்கி அஃதாண் டவள்செய் தது.
Thodinokki mendholum nokki adinokki ahdhaandaval seydhadhu.

Meaning

She looked at her bangles, looked at her soft shoulders, and looked at her feet. That is what she did there.

Elementary

Without saying a word, the girl looks at her bangles, then her arms, then her feet. Her friend explains what she means: ‘If you leave, I will grow thin and my bangles will slip off, so I’d rather walk with you.’ Her looks said it all.

University

This couplet is a pure kuṟippu: three looks, at her toṭi (bangles), her meṉ tōḷ (soft shoulders) and her aṭi (feet), with the comment ‘that is what she did there’ (aḵtu āṇṭu avaḷ ceytatu). It is traditionally spoken by the confidante to the hero, explaining the heroine’s silent gestures. Varadarasanar and Kesikan identify the sign as uṭaṉpōkku, her resolve to go with him on his journey; Solomon Pappaiah spells out the logic: the bangles will slip and the shoulders waste if he leaves her, and her looking at her feet means she will walk with him. Kalaignar reads it the same way. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net, which print the āytam form அஃதாண் டவள்.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Rama is exiled to the forest, Sita refuses to be left behind and insists that a wife’s place is beside her husband, saying she will walk ahead of him through the wilderness.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda, sargas 27-30 (paraphrase) [3]
Sita chooses to share Rama’s hard journey rather than be separated from him.
The heroine’s look at her feet in the Kural signals the same resolve: she will go with him.
Christianity
“And Ruth said, Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God:”
— Ruth 1:16 (KJV) [4]
Ruth refuses to be parted from Naomi and vows to go wherever she goes.
The heroine’s silent glance at her feet carries Ruth’s spoken vow: where you go, I go.
Islam
When Abraham leaves Hagar and her infant in the barren valley of Mecca, she follows him asking to whom he is leaving them, until he indicates that God commanded it, and she accepts.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3364 (paraphrase) [5]
Hagar follows her departing husband and seeks his meaning when he gives no words.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Hagar confronts a husband’s departure; but where she stays by God’s command, the heroine signals she will go.
Judaism
“I remember for thee the affection of thy youth, the love of thine espousals; how thou wentest after Me in the wilderness, in a land that was not sown.”
— Jeremiah 2:2 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
God recalls Israel’s early love as a bride who followed her beloved into the wilderness.
The heroine’s look at her feet promises the same bridal devotion: to follow her husband even into a hard land.
Buddhism
When Prince Vessantara is banished to the mountains, his wife Maddi refuses to stay behind in comfort and goes with him into the forest with their children.
— Vessantara Jataka (Jataka 547) (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell and W.H.D. Rouse) [7]
Maddi chooses hardship beside her husband over safety apart from him.
The heroine of the Kural signals the same choice with a glance at her feet.
Jainism
After Arishtanemi renounces the world, Rajimati cuts off her hair, takes vows herself, and sets out for Mount Raivataka, where he had gone.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.29-33 (paraphrase of tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati follows her betrothed, but along the path of renunciation rather than of married life.
The Kural’s heroine resolves to go where her husband goes; Rajimati does too, though her journey is toward liberation.
Sikhism
“I tell them the state of my soul, and pray that I may be united with the Sovereign Lord King, my Friend.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 958, Raag Raamkalee, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes the seeker who tells the holy ones the state of her soul and asks them to help her meet the Beloved.
In the Kural the confidante explains the heroine's silent gestures to the husband; Guru Arjan likewise has the inner state made known through a go-between so that union may come.
Greek Mythology
Medea leaves her father’s house at night and flees with Jason aboard the Argo rather than be parted from him.
— Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.C. Seaton) [10]
Apollonius tells of a young woman who chooses to leave home and go with her beloved.
Udanpokku, the heroine’s resolve to go with her lover, is exactly what Medea does.
Philosophy
Augustine recalls that as an infant he learned what adults meant from their gestures, the movements of the face and eyes and limbs that he calls the natural language of all peoples.
— Augustine, Confessions I.8 (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Pusey) [11]
Augustine treats gesture and glance as a language understood before and beneath words.
The heroine’s three glances are such a natural language, and the confidante reads them perfectly.

Real Life Example

Florence Baker travels with Samuel Baker to find the Nile's sources
1860s · Upper Nile region of East Africa (now South Sudan and Uganda)
In 1861 the English explorer Samuel Baker went to Africa with Florence von Sass, who later became his second wife, and they spent about a year exploring Nile tributaries near the border of Sudan and Ethiopia. Florence went on with him into the interior in search of the sources of the Nile, as his confidante and fellow explorer. Their discoveries included Lake Albert and Murchison Falls, which earned Samuel the Royal Geographical Society's gold medal and a knighthood.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Rather than wait at home, Florence chose to share every step of a dangerous journey.
The kural's heroine silently looks at her bangles, her shoulders and her feet to show she will go with her husband rather than be left behind; Florence Baker did exactly that.
