Kural 263

Penance · Thavam · தவம்

துறந்தார்க்குத் துப்புரவு வேண்டி மறந்தார்கொல் மற்றை யவர்கள் தவம்.
Thurandhaarkku thuppuravu vendi marandhaarkol Matraiyavargal thavam.

Meaning

Is it because others wanted to give food and help to the people who gave up the world that they forgot to practice self-control themselves?

Elementary

Picture a whole village cooking lunch for a few hermits on the hill. Valluvar jokes: maybe everyone got so busy packing lunches for the hermits that they forgot to become good and disciplined themselves!

University

This is one of Valluvar’s rare ironic rhetorical questions (கொல் marks doubt). Read straightforwardly, it offers an indulgent explanation for why so few undertake tapas: society needs householders to feed the ascetics. Read more sharply — as Kalaignar does — it is a rebuke: supporting renunciants is no substitute for one’s own discipline. The verse also documents a real economic structure of early Indian religion, in which Jain, Buddhist and Brahmanical renouncers depended on lay donation, and in which donors believed they shared the merit of those they supported. Valluvar gently punctures the complacency that such a division of spiritual labour could produce.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Manu declares that just as all creatures live by depending on air, so the members of all the other orders of life (students, forest-dwellers, renunciants) subsist by the support of the householder, who is therefore the most excellent order.
— Manusmriti 3.77-78 (Bühler trans.) [3]
Brahmanical law explicitly makes the householder the economic base on which ascetics rely.
Describes the very arrangement Valluvar is teasing — householders feeding renunciants.
Christianity
“He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet’s reward; and he that receiveth a righteous man in the name of a righteous man shall receive a righteous man’s reward.”
— Matthew 10:41 (KJV) [4]
Those who host and support God’s messengers share in their reward.
Supports the ‘merit through support’ logic that Valluvar suggests has made householders forget their own tapas.
Islam
“[Charity is] for the poor who have been restricted for the cause of Allah, unable to move about in the land. An ignorant [person] would think them self-sufficient because of their restraint, but you will know them by their [characteristic] sign.”
— Qur’an 2:273 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an directs charity to those wholly devoted to God’s cause who cannot earn for themselves.
Parallels lay support of renunciants as a religious duty.
Judaism
Rashi explains that the tribe of Zebulun engaged in sea-trade and provided for Issachar, who devoted himself to Torah study, and so Moses blessed Zebulun first — the supporter shares in the scholar’s merit.
— Rashi on Deuteronomy 33:18 [6]
The ‘Issachar–Zebulun’ partnership became the classic Jewish model of lay support for full-time religious devotion.
A direct counterpart to householders feeding ascetics; Valluvar’s question probes whether the Zebulun role lets one neglect one’s own discipline.
Buddhism
The Sangha is praised as the ‘incomparable field of merit for the world’; monks on the dhutanga and alms-round path depend entirely on lay donors, who earn merit by giving.
— Dhajagga Sutta (SN 11.3) [7]
Buddhism formalized the monk–lay relationship: renunciants practise, householders feed them and gain merit.
Exactly the arrangement behind Valluvar’s question about why ‘others’ forgot tapas.
Jainism
“You have left the dreadful âsrama (that of the householder) and are wanting to enter another; (remain what you were), O king, and be content with observing the Pôsaha-days.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.42 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Indra, testing Nami, suggests he stay a householder and keep only the lay fasting days (pōṣadha), reflecting the Jain arrangement in which lay people support monks with alms while practising lesser austerities themselves.
Valluvar ironically asks whether householders forgot penance because they were busy feeding renunciants; the Jain scheme answers differently, expecting even householders to keep periodic fasts alongside their almsgiving.
Sikhism
“One who calls himself a guru or a spiritual teacher, while he goes around begging - don't ever touch his feet. One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has - O Nanak, he knows the Path.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Vaar Saarang (salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In Vaar Saarang Guru Nanak refuses honour to holy men who live by begging and gives it instead to the householder who earns by his own labour and shares what he has.
Valluvar wonders, perhaps ironically, whether householders have stopped doing penance because they are busy feeding renunciants; Guru Nanak answers that honest work and sharing (kirat karni, vand chhakna) is itself the true path.
Greek Mythology
The poor old couple Baucis and Philemon feed two disguised gods from their meagre store and are rewarded by being made priests of a temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 [10]
Hospitality to the sacred visitor is rewarded with sacred status.
Weak parallel: support of the holy earns merit, but there is no ascetic class or critique.
Philosophy
In Plato’s ideal city the guardians own no property and receive their sustenance from the producing citizens as a wage for their guardianship.
— Plato, Republic 416d-417b [11]
Plato builds a division of labour in which a disciplined, propertyless elite is maintained by others.
Resembles the lay–ascetic economy Valluvar describes, though Plato’s guardians are rulers, not penitents.

Real Life Example

Duke William of Aquitaine founds the Abbey of Cluny
910 · Cluny, Burgundy, France
William I, Duke of Aquitaine, signed a charter founding a Benedictine monastery at Cluny and endowed it with land. The charter says he gave part of his worldly goods for the benefit of his soul, and it requires the monks to pray for him, his family and others while living under the Rule of Saint Benedict. Cluny was placed under the protection of the pope, free from the duke's own control.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
Cluny became one of the most influential monasteries in medieval Europe, and its charter shows the common arrangement in which lay patrons supported monks and counted on their prayers for their own salvation.
The kural wryly asks whether people neglect their own penance because they are busy supplying the renunciants, and Cluny's founder openly paid monks to do the praying for his soul.
