Kural 1231

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

சிறுமை நமக்கொழியச் சேட்சென்றார் உள்ளி நறுமலர் நாணின கண்.
Sirumai namakkozhiyach chaetchendraar ulli narumalar naanina kan.

Meaning

Thinking of him who went far away and left you this sorrow, your eyes have cried away their beauty. Now they feel ashamed in front of the sweet-smelling flowers they once outshone.

Elementary

Imagine a girl whose eyes were once as bright as the flowers in her garden. Her dearest person goes on a long trip, and she cries so much that her eyes become red and tired. Now when she looks at the fresh flowers, she feels shy, because the flowers look brighter than her eyes do.

University

Chapter 124, uṟuppu nalaṉ aḻital (‘the wasting of the beauty of the limbs’), continues the kaṟpiyal sequence on separation (pirivu): the wife, parted from her husband, loses her nalam (bodily loveliness) limb by limb, eyes, shoulders, bangles, forehead. Pope’s prose gloss (‘your eyes weep for him who is gone afar’) follows the traditional reading, associated with Parimelazhagar, in which the confidante (tōḻi) speaks to the heroine. The key words are ciṟumai (here ‘distress, grief’, glossed துன்பம் by Mu. Varadarasanar), cēṭ ceṉṟār (‘he who has gone far’), and naṟumalar nāṇiṉa kaṇ (‘the eyes were ashamed before fragrant flowers’): Sangam convention compares a woman’s eyes to blue water-lilies (kuvaḷai), so eyes that once shamed the flowers now hang back before them. Solomon Pappaiah reads ciṟumai as the ‘weakness of not bearing separation’ left with her while he bore it and went. The couplet is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net; ytamizh writes the chapter title as one word (உறுப்புநலனழிதல்), thirukkural.net as two (உறுப்புநலன் அழிதல்).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
When Hanuman finally finds Sita in Ravana’s Ashoka grove, she is thin from fasting and grief, her face wet with tears, sighing again and again, a woman whose beauty is dimmed like a moon behind cloud as she thinks only of the far-off Rama.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara Kanda, sargas 15-19 (paraphrase) [3]
Valmiki shows separation from the beloved literally wearing away the heroine’s radiance.
Like the Kural’s heroine, Sita’s eyes and looks are spent by weeping for a husband who is far away.
Christianity
“Mine eye also is dim by reason of sorrow, and all my members are as a shadow.”
— Job 17:7 (KJV) [4]
Job describes grief so heavy that it dims his eyes and leaves his whole body a shadow of itself.
The Kural, too, makes the eye the first part of the body to lose its brightness under sorrow.
Islam
“And he turned away from them and said, ‘Oh, my sorrow over Joseph,’ and his eyes became white from grief, for he was [of that] a suppressor.”
— Qur’an 12:84 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an tells how Jacob’s long grief for his lost son Joseph physically ruined his eyes.
As in the Kural, longing for one who is far away is written on the eyes, which lose their former lustre.
Judaism
“Mine eye is dimmed because of vexation; It waxeth old because of all mine adversaries.”
— Psalms 6:8 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist, worn out with weeping through the night, says his eye has aged and dimmed from distress.
The Kural’s eyes likewise ‘grow old’ before their time, losing the beauty that once outshone the flowers.
Buddhism
In the story told when the Buddha first visited Rahula’s mother (Yasodhara), King Suddhodana relates that after the prince left home she lived as he did: on hearing he wore yellow robes she wore yellow, she ate one meal a day as he did, and she gave up garlands and perfumes.
— Canda-Kinnara Jataka (Jataka 485), introductory story (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell et al.) [7]
The Jataka commentary portrays the deserted wife setting aside flowers and adornment out of loyalty to her absent husband.
The Kural’s heroine also turns away from fragrant flowers, her beauty given up to grief for the one who went far away.
Jainism
“When the daughter of the king heard of the ordination of the Gina, laughter and gaiety forsook her, and she was overwhelmed with affliction.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
When Arishtanemi (Neminatha) left his own wedding procession to renounce the world, his bride Rajimati lost all her joy.
Rajimati’s brightness, like the heroine’s eyes in the Kural, is extinguished at once by the departure of the one she was to marry.
Sikhism
“Fareed, those eyes which have enticed the world - I have seen those eyes. Once, they could not endure even a bit of mascara; now, the birds hatch their young in them!”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378, Saloks of Sheikh Farid (salok 14) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Sheikh Farid looks at eyes once so lovely and delicate that they bewitched the world, and sees what has become of them.
The Kural's confidante tells the wife that her eyes, which once outshone the flowers, have wept away their beauty; Farid too sets the former loveliness of eyes against their ruin.
Greek Mythology
Ovid’s Clytie, abandoned by the Sun-god she loved, sits on the bare ground for nine days without food, living only on dew and her own tears, gazing at him as he crosses the sky, until her wasted body turns into a flower that still follows the sun.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.256-270 (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid turns a woman’s wasting for an absent lover into a metamorphosis: her body becomes a flower.
The Kural’s heroine weeps until her eyes are outdone by flowers; Clytie weeps until she becomes one.
Philosophy
Socrates describes the lover’s soul when parted from the beautiful one: the openings through which its wings grew dry up and close, and the soul, stung all over, writhes in pain, until the sight of the beloved again refreshes it.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-e (paraphrase; cf. tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato treats separation from the beloved as a bodily-feeling drought that withers the soul.
The Kural shows the same drying and withering, but on the outside, in eyes that lose their bloom.

Real Life Example

Ono no Komachi's poem of the faded flowers
Mid-9th century (collected in the Kokinshū, 905) · Heian-kyō (Kyoto), Japan
Ono no Komachi, a poet at the Japanese court in the mid-9th century, wrote a famous poem, preserved in the Kokinshū anthology compiled in 905 and later chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu, in which the colour of the flowers has faded while she has let her days slip away, lost in brooding as the long rains fell. Her poems often use fading blossoms as a sign of passing time and fading beauty.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The poem is one of the best-known expressions in Japanese literature of beauty withering while the heart is absorbed in sorrow.
The kural says the wife's eyes have wept away their beauty and now shrink before the flowers they once outshone, and Komachi's poem likewise sets her own fading against faded flowers during days of brooding.
