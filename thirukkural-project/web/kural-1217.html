Kural 1217

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

நனவினால் நல்காக் கொடியார் கனவனால் என்எம்மைப் பீழிப் பது.
Nanavinaal nalkaak kotiyaar kanavanaal en-emmaip peezhip padhu.

Meaning

He is cruel and not kind to me when I am awake. So why does he come into my dreams only to hurt me?

Elementary

Imagine a friend who never answers your calls in the daytime but keeps popping up in your dreams. You might grumble, ‘If you won’t come and play when I’m awake, why do you keep visiting me at night and making me miss you even more?’

University

The mood turns from gratitude to reproach. Koṭiyār, ‘the cruel one’ (honorific plural), is a lover’s accusation, and pīḻippatu, ‘tormenting’, from pīḻ (to afflict, squeeze), names the pain of a dream that teases without fulfilling. Eṉ is the interrogative ‘why’, and emmai the plural ‘us’, the heroine speaking of herself with the inclusive dignity common in akam speech. Both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print kaṉavaṉāl in the first line, where many editions have the regular instrumental kaṉaviṉāl; the sense is unaffected. Kalaignar glosses the torment as the dream ‘enlarging the grief of separation’. Pope: ‘The cruel one, in waking hour, who all ungracious seems, / Why should he thus torment my soul in nightly dreams?’

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Kalidasa’s exiled yaksha tells his wife, through the cloud, that when at last he finds her in a dream and stretches his arms into empty air to embrace her, even the forest deities shed tears like pearls at his plight.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Uttaramegha (paraphrase; verse number varies by recension) [3]
The ‘Cloud Messenger’ dwells on the cruelty of a dream-meeting that ends in arms closing on nothing.
The yaksha’s empty embrace is the torment the Kural’s heroine complains of: a lover who comes in dreams only to leave her more bereft.
Christianity
“When I say, My bed shall comfort me, my couch shall ease my complaint; Then thou scarest me with dreams, and terrifiest me through visions.”
— Job 7:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Job finds that even the refuge of sleep becomes a place of torment through dreams.
The Kural’s heroine makes the same complaint: the night that should console her brings dreams that afflict.
Islam
“[It is but] a mixture of false dreams, and we are not learned in the interpretation of dreams.”
— Qur’an 12:44 (Saheeh International) [5]
Pharaoh’s courtiers dismiss his troubling dream as a jumble of confused visions.
The Kural’s heroine might wish to dismiss her teasing dreams the same way, but they torment her precisely because they feel real.
Judaism
Shmuel, when he saw a bad dream, would say “And the dreams speak falsely” (Zechariah 10:2); when he saw a good dream, he would ask whether dreams speak falsely, citing “I speak with him in a dream” (Numbers 12:6).
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 55b (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud records a sage who answered distressing dreams by declaring them empty and welcomed good ones as true.
The Kural’s heroine is caught between those two attitudes: the dream-lover delights her, yet torments her because he will not come in truth.
Buddhism
In the Potaliya discourse the Buddha compares sensual pleasures to a dream: a man dreams of delightful parks, groves and lakes, but on waking sees none of them.
— Majjhima Nikaya 54, Potaliya Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Nanamoli and Bodhi) [7]
Buddhism uses the dream to show how pleasures promise satisfaction and leave nothing behind.
The Kural’s heroine feels exactly this cheat: the dream gives her the beloved and then leaves her with nothing, which is why she calls it torment.
Jainism
In the dialogue of Citta and Sambhuta, the monk Citta warns his brother, now a pleasure-loving king, that all pleasures in the end bring pain and that delight in them is empty.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 13, Citta and Sambhuta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that pleasures, however sweet, carry their own suffering.
The Kural’s heroine discovers this at night: the pleasure of the dream-lover is itself what pains her.
Sikhism
“In a dream, He came, and went away again; I cried so many tears.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 558, Raag Vadhans, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's soul-bride says her Lord visited her in a dream and left again, and she was left weeping.
The Kural's wife asks why the hard-hearted one who will not come while she is awake visits her dreams only to torment her; Guru Nanak's bride feels the same pain of a dream-visit that ends in tears.
Greek Mythology
Penelope tells the disguised Odysseus that dreams pass through two gates: those that come through the gate of ivory deceive and bring words that are never fulfilled, while those through the gate of horn come true.
— Homer, Odyssey 19.560-567 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer gives the separated wife a theory of deceiving dreams that promise what waking does not deliver.
The Kural’s ‘cruel one’ who comes only in dreams is just such an ivory-gate dream, sweet in sleep and false by day.
Philosophy
Lucretius compares lovers to a thirsty man who in sleep seeks to drink and is given only images of water, so that he thirsts still while drinking; so Venus mocks lovers with images that never satisfy.
— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 4.1097-1104 (paraphrase) [11]
For Lucretius, love fed on images, above all in dreams, inflames longing instead of quenching it.
The Kural’s heroine suffers exactly this: the dream-image of her husband torments because it cannot satisfy.

Real Life Example

Goya publishes The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters
1799 · Madrid, Spain
In February 1799 Francisco Goya advertised Los Caprichos, a series of 80 aquatint etchings, in the Diario de Madrid. Plate 43, The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters, shows an artist asleep at his drawing table while owls and bats close in and a lynx watches. Goya's caption explains that imagination abandoned by reason produces impossible monsters, but united with reason it is the mother of the arts.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The print became one of the most famous images in Western art of sleep bringing torment instead of rest.
The kural's heroine asks why the cruel one who will not come while she is awake visits her dreams only to torment her; Goya's sleeper is likewise beset in sleep by what he cannot escape.
