Kural 493

Knowledge of Location · Idanarithal · இடனறிதல்

ஆற்றாரும் ஆற்றி அடுப இடனறிந்து போற்றார்கண் போற்றிச் செயின்.
Aatraarum aatri adupa idanarinthu Potraarkan potrich cheyin.

Meaning

Even weak people can become strong enough to win if they pick the right place, protect themselves well, and then act against their enemy.

Elementary

A little cat on a high shelf can bat at a big dog that can’t reach it. The cat isn’t stronger, it just picked the right spot. Valluvar says even weak people can win if they choose their spot well and stay safe.

University

The verse turns on the wordplay ‘aatraarum aatri’: those who lack power (aatraar) come to have it (aatri) and destroy the enemy (adupa). ‘Potraar’ literally means ‘those who do not honour us’, a standard Kural word for enemies, and the next word ‘potri’ (having guarded, taken care) repeats the sound with the opposite meaning. Parimelazhagar reads ‘potri’ as the actor protecting himself, so the conditions for success are knowing the place and staying self-protected. Mu. Varadarasan and Solomon Pappaiah follow this reading; Kalaignar adds the idea of choosing the right place for the attack. The kural spells out what 492 implied: the right place can make up for missing strength, and not merely add to strength one already has.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells the meditator to set up a firm seat in a clean place, neither too high nor too low, and only then to begin controlling the mind.
— Bhagavad Gita 6.11–12 [3]
Even inner discipline starts with choosing and securing the right spot, because an unsteady position works against the practitioner.
The Kural’s ‘idan arinthu… potri’ (know the place, guard yourself, then act) matches the yogi’s order of preparation.
Christianity
“After these things Jesus walked in Galilee: for he would not walk in Jewry, because the Jews sought to kill him.”
— John 7:1 (KJV) [4]
John says Jesus deliberately worked in Galilee and stayed away from Judea while his opponents there were looking for a chance to kill him.
He kept his mission going by choosing a safer place of action, much like the Kural’s ‘potraarkan potri’ (guarding oneself among enemies).
Islam
“[Remember] when you were on the near side of the valley, and they were on the farther side, and the caravan was lower [in position] than you.”
— Quran 8:42 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran recalls where each side stood at Badr, when a small and poorly equipped Muslim force defeated a much larger Quraysh army.
The verse points to positioning at a battle where the weaker side won, which is the Kural’s claim.
Judaism
At Michmash, Jonathan and his armour-bearer climbed a narrow crag between two rocky outcrops and attacked a Philistine outpost from a position where the enemy could not bring its numbers to bear. Their success set off a wider Philistine rout.
— 1 Samuel 14:4–15 [4]
Two men beat a garrison by using a narrow, high approach the enemy did not expect.
The weak defeating the strong by knowing the ground is the Kural’s ‘aatraarum aatri adupa’.
Buddhism
“If only I had kept to my proper range today, to my own ancestral territory, this hawk would have been no match for me in battle.”
— Saṃyutta Nikāya 47.6, Sakunagghi Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [6]
A quail caught by a hawk talks its way free, returns to a ploughed field full of clods, dodges behind one, and the diving hawk breaks its own breast on it.
The weak bird beats the strong one only because it fights from ground it knows, which is exactly what this kural says.
Jainism
“As a tortoise draws its limbs into its own body, so a wise man should cover, as it were, his sins with his own meditation. He should draw in, as it were, his hands and feet, his mind and five organs of sense, the effect of his bad Karman, and every bad use of language.”
— Sutrakritanga I.8.16-17 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
The Sutrakritanga uses the tortoise, weak in itself but safe inside its shell, as the model for the monk who guards himself through restraint.
The kural says even the weak can win if they guard themselves well in a safe place; the tortoise image shows Jain self-protection, though the danger guarded against is karma, not an army.
Sikhism
“My True Guru is the power of the powerless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1142, Raag Bhairao, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan teaches that those who have no strength of their own become strong once they take shelter with the Guru.
The Kural says even the weak overcome their enemies when they take the right position and guard themselves; Gurbani says the powerless gain power by taking the Guru as their refuge.
Greek Mythology
Before Salamis, Themistocles argued that fighting in the narrow strait favoured the smaller Greek fleet, while open sea favoured the larger Persian one, and the Greeks won by fighting in the narrows.
— Herodotus, Histories 8.60 (tr. A. D. Godley) [9]
Herodotus records the argument and the victory that followed, which saved Greece.
The weaker side won by picking where to fight, as the Kural says.
Philosophy
“A Kid standing on the roof of a house, out of harm’s way, saw a Wolf passing by and immediately began to taunt and revile him. The Wolf… said, ‘Sirrah! I hear thee: yet it is not thou who mockest me, but the roof on which thou art standing.’”
— Aesop, The Kid and the Wolf (tr. G. F. Townsend) [10]
The fable ends with the moral that time and place often give the weak an advantage over the strong.
That moral restates Kural 493, together with the previous chapter’s point about time.

Real Life Example

The Swiss confederates defeat the Habsburg army at Morgarten
15 November 1315 · Morgarten, near Lake Ageri, Switzerland
Duke Leopold I of Habsburg led an army of knights to invade the valley of Schwyz, marching by a narrow route between the shore of Lake Ageri and the steep slopes of Morgarten. The men of Schwyz, joined by fighters from Uri, waited on the heights and attacked the column while it was strung out in the narrow ground. Leopold's army was routed and he escaped, and the confederates renewed their alliance in the Pact of Brunnen less than a month later.
— Sources: [11] [12] [13]
The victory helped secure the survival of the young Swiss confederation and gave the Swiss a lasting reputation as formidable foot soldiers.
The kural says even the weak can defeat their enemies if they choose the right place and keep themselves safe, and the confederates struck from the heights where the knights could not use their strength.
