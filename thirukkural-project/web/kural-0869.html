Kural 869

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

செறுவார்க்குச் சேணிகவா இன்பம் அறிவிலா அஞ்சும் பகைவர்ப் பெறின்.
Seruvaarkkuch chenikavaa inbam arivilaa anjum pakaivarp perin.

Meaning

People who fight will never be far from joy if their enemies are both foolish and scared.

Elementary

If you are playing a game and your opponent doesn’t understand the rules and is scared to make any move, winning becomes easy and fun. Valluvar says a foolish, frightened enemy is really a stroke of luck for the other side.

University

Seruvaar (செறுவார்) are those who attack or contend; sen ikavaa inbam (சேண் இகவா இன்பம்) is joy that does not go far away, i.e. abiding delight. Varadarasanar keeps this sense (pleasures will not depart), while Puliyur Kesigan reads it as ‘all lofty pleasures remain with them’. The enemy’s two qualities, arivilaa (அறிவிலா, without understanding, which Pappaiah specifies as without knowledge of what is just) and anjum (அஞ்சும், fearing), recapitulate the first two faults of 863. Kalaignar renders the joy specifically as the joy of victory. The verse is the offensive counterpart to the chapter’s earlier defensive lists. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Virata Parva, Prince Uttara boasts that he will defeat the Kaurava army alone, but on seeing it he panics and tries to flee, until Arjuna, disguised as Brihannala, takes the reins and wins the battle.
— Mahabharata, Virata Parva (Go-harana) (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [3]
The episode contrasts the boastful, frightened prince with the skilled warrior who routs the host.
Uttara is the ignorant, fearful kind of opponent whom the Kural says is a joy to face.
Christianity
Jesus says of the blind guides of his day: if the blind lead the blind, both shall fall into the ditch.
— Matthew 15:14 (KJV) [4]
Ignorance in those who lead brings ruin on them and their followers.
A foe led by such blindness is, as the Kural says, easy to overcome.
Islam
“O Prophet, urge the believers to battle. If there are among you twenty [who are] steadfast, they will overcome two hundred. And if there are among you one hundred [who are steadfast], they will overcome a thousand of those who have disbelieved because they are a people who do not understand.”
— Qur’an 8:65 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an promises that steadfast fighters will overcome much larger forces who lack understanding.
As in the Kural, an enemy without understanding becomes easy to defeat.
Judaism
In the covenant curses of Leviticus, God warns that a disobedient people will be so faint-hearted in the lands of their enemies that the sound of a driven leaf will put them to flight.
— Leviticus 26:36 (Tanakh) [6]
Fear can so paralyse a people that they flee when no one is pursuing.
Such a fearful foe is exactly what the Kural calls a joy to the attacker.
Buddhism
The Buddha describes five kinds of warriors, including one who loses heart at the mere sight of the cloud of dust raised by the enemy and cannot go into battle.
— Yodhajiva Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.75 [7]
The sutta uses these timid warriors as an image for monks who give up their practice at the first sign of temptation.
The warrior who falters at the dust cloud is the fearful foe of the Kural.
Jainism
“A man believes himself a hero as long as he does not behold the foe, as did Śiśupāla (before he beheld) the valorously-fighting, great warrior. They go forward to the head of the battle; but when the fight has begun the mother will not recognise her son, and he will be mangled by his foe.”
— Sūtrakṛtāṅga I.3.1.1–2 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; wisdomlib.org) [8]
The Sūtrakṛtāṅga uses the image of a boastful but untried fighter who collapses once he faces a real opponent, as a warning to novice monks who overrate their own strength.
The Kural says those who fight are glad to meet foes who are foolish and fearful, and the Jain image of the vain warrior who is crushed in his first real encounter shows exactly that kind of foe.
Sikhism
“Farid, in the midst of this beautiful earth, there is a garden of thorns. Those humble beings who are blessed by their spiritual teacher, do not suffer even a scratch.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1382, Salok Sheikh Farid (salok 82, Mahalla 5), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok added to Farid's by Guru Arjan, the world is full of thorns, but those blessed by their spiritual teacher pass through unharmed.
The Kural says those who fight will not lack joy if their enemies are foolish and fearful; Gurbani instead places the safety of the righteous in the Guru's blessing, not in the weakness of their foes.
Greek Mythology
Odysseus outwits the brutish Cyclops Polyphemus by telling him his name is ‘Nobody’, getting him drunk and blinding him, so that when Polyphemus cries for help his neighbours hear that ‘Nobody’ is hurting him.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 9 [10]
A powerful but witless enemy is overcome by cunning.
Polyphemus is the arivilaa, the foe without understanding whose defeat is a delight.
Philosophy
Sun Tzu teaches that if you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War III.18 (tr. Lionel Giles) [11]
Victory follows from knowledge, and an enemy without it is at a decisive disadvantage.
Valluvar’s ignorant foe is the one who knows neither, and so delivers victory to his opponent.

Real Life Example

Frederick the Great routs a divided army at Rossbach
5 November 1757 · Rossbach, Saxony, Germany
During the Seven Years' War, Frederick II of Prussia, with about 21,000 men, faced a combined French and Imperial army of at least 41,000 whose commanders, Soubise and Saxe-Hildburghausen, were at odds with each other. As the allied army marched in columns to outflank him, Prussian cavalry under Friedrich Wilhelm von Seydlitz struck from behind cover and the allied force broke. In less than two hours the allies suffered thousands of casualties and many prisoners, while Prussian losses were around 550.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A much smaller but well-led army won one of the most lopsided victories of the century against a larger force hampered by divided and poorly judged command.
The kural says those who fight will never lack joy if their enemies lack understanding, and Rossbach shows how an opponent's poor judgment hands victory to the attacker.
