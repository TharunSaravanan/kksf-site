Kural 460

Avoiding Mean Associations · Sitrinanjeraamai · சிற்றினஞ்சேராமை

நல்லினத்தி னூங்குந் துணையில்லை தீயினத்தின் அல்லற் படுப்பதூஉம் இல்.
Nallinaththin oongum thunaiyillai theeyinaththin Allar paduppadhooum il.

Meaning

Nothing helps a person more than good friends, and nothing causes more pain and trouble than bad friends.

Elementary

Good friends are the best help you can ever have. Bad friends are the worst trouble you can ever have. Nothing else comes close in either direction.

University

The chapter ends with a symmetrical superlative: nothing surpasses (ஊங்கு) good company as a support (துணை), and nothing causes as much suffering (அல்லல்) as bad company. The emphatic lengthening in படுப்பதூஉம் (an alapedai, or metrical vowel extension) stresses the second half, so the warning weighs more than the praise, in keeping with the chapter title. The couplet sums up the whole adhikaram and connects it to chapter 45: seek the great, avoid the small, because nothing else in life has as much power to help or harm.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Hitopadesha, a deer befriends a smooth-talking jackal against the advice of his true friend, the crow. The jackal leads him into a hunter’s snare, and only the crow’s cleverness saves him.
— Hitopadesha, Book 1 (Mitralabha) [4]
The fable sets the true friend’s help against the false friend’s harm.
This illustrates both halves of the Kural: good company is the greatest help and bad company the greatest danger.
Christianity
“He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed.”
— Proverbs 13:20 (KJV) [5]
The proverb sets out both outcomes: wisdom from wise company and ruin from foolish company.
Its two-part structure mirrors the Kural’s two-part verdict.
Islam
“Close friends, that Day, will be enemies to each other, except for the righteous.”
— Qur’an 43:67 (Sahih International) [6]
On the Day of Judgement, friendships built on wrongdoing turn into enmity, while righteous friendships last.
Both texts say bad company brings the greatest harm and good company the most lasting support.
Judaism
Nittai of Arbel taught: keep far from an evil neighbour, do not attach yourself to the wicked, and do not give up belief in retribution.
— Pirkei Avot 1:7 [7]
The Mishnah’s ethical teaching warns against closeness to the wicked because of the calamity it brings.
This matches the Kural’s warning that bad company brings more suffering than anything else.
Buddhism
“Association with fools is ever painful, like partnership with an enemy. But association with the wise is happy, like meeting one’s own kinsmen.”
— Dhammapada 207 (trans. Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Buddha compares the pain of foolish company with the joy of wise company.
This is a two-sided verdict matching the Kural almost exactly.
Jainism
“Like paddy multiplied through the goodness of the soil, persons will become good through the goodness of their respective families. Like the destruction of a good ship on the approach of a strong gale, goodness will be destroyed by bad company.”
— Naladiyar, ch. 18 'Good Society', stanza 9 (English tr. in The Indian Antiquary; wisdomlib.org) [9]
The Naladiyar sets the growth of goodness through a good setting against the way bad company wrecks goodness, like a ship broken by a gale.
This mirrors the closing superlative of the kural, where nothing helps more than good company and nothing harms more than bad. The Naladiyar sets the same two outcomes side by side.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, associate with the Holy people, who will take you to Nirvaanaa in the end. Do not associate with the faithless cynics; they would bring you to ruin.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1369, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Kabir sets the two kinds of company side by side: holy company stays with you to the end and brings liberation, while the company of the faithless brings destruction.
The Kural ends the chapter by saying no help is greater than good company and nothing causes more affliction than bad company; Kabir gives the same two-sided verdict.
Greek Mythology
Stranded on Thrinacia, Odysseus’s crew follow Eurylochus’s bad counsel and slaughter the cattle of Helios. Zeus destroys the ship and all of them; only Odysseus, who refused, survives.
— Homer, Odyssey, Book 12 [11]
Following a bad companion’s lead brings total ruin.
This illustrates the Kural’s second half: nothing afflicts like bad company.
Philosophy
Socrates asks Meletus whether it is not true that the good do good to those near them and the evil do evil, and whether anyone would rather be harmed than helped by those he lives with.
— Plato, Apology 25c–e [12]
Socrates takes it as obvious that one’s companions are the nearest source of both benefit and harm.
This is the same two-sided principle as the Kural.

Real Life Example

Narendranath Datta meets Sri Ramakrishna
1881 · Dakshineswar, near Calcutta (Kolkata), India
In 1881 Narendranath Datta, a college student troubled by religious doubt, went to meet the teacher Sri Ramakrishna at the Kali temple in Dakshineswar. He became a frequent visitor and disciple, and Ramakrishna chose him as leader of his young followers. After Ramakrishna's death in 1886 Narendranath led them into a monastic brotherhood and, as Swami Vivekananda, went on to found the Ramakrishna Mission in 1897.
— Sources: [13] [14]
One meeting and the friendship that followed turned a doubting student into one of modern India's most influential spiritual leaders.
The kural says there is no greater help than good company, and the company of Ramakrishna shaped the whole course of Vivekananda's life.
