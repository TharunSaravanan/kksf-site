Kural 916

Wanton Women · Varaivin Makalir · வரைவின் மகளிர்

தந்நலம் பாரிப்பார் தோயார் தகைசெருக்கிப் புன்னலம் பாரிப்பார் தோள்.
Thannalam paarippaar thoyaar thagaiserukkip punnalam paarippaar thol.

Meaning

People who care about their own good name will not go to the arms of women who show off their charm and sell cheap pleasures to anyone.

Elementary

If you care about being known as honest and kind, you will be careful about who you spend your time with. You would not team up with someone who sells their ‘friendship’ to anyone, because you value your good name.

University

The couplet repeats the verb paarippaar (பாரிப்பார்) in two senses: those who paarippaar thannalam, ‘cherish or spread their own goodness (or fame)’, will not touch the shoulders of those who paarippaar punnalam, ‘spread their petty charms abroad’. Thagai-serukki (தகைசெருக்கி) is ‘being proud of their attractiveness or skill’. Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesigan take thannalam as one’s own fame, Mu. Varadarasanar as one’s ‘good conduct’, and Parimelazhagar as the reputation that comes from virtue. Textual note: thirukkural.net prints the first verb as பாரப்பார் (paarappaar), whereas ytamizh.com and the standard Parimelazhagar text read பாரிப்பார்; the repeated verb and all four commentaries support பாரிப்பார், so the variant looks like a typographical slip.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self.”
— Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (tr. Swami Mukundananda) [3]
Krishna places the responsibility for one’s rise or fall on oneself.
Kural 916’s thannalam paarippaar are those who choose to raise themselves and not to lower their own worth.
Christianity
“A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches, and loving favour rather than silver and gold.”
— Proverbs 22:1 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs ranks reputation above wealth.
Those who guard their thannalam (good name) in Kural 916 make exactly this choice.
Islam
In Surah Yusuf, the wife of al-Aziz tries to seduce the young Joseph behind closed doors; he refuses, seeking refuge in God and saying he will not betray his master’s trust, and he flees.
— Qur’an 12:23-25 [5]
Yusuf’s refusal is the Qur’an’s model of a person who guards his integrity under pressure.
He is the type of the thannalam paarippaar who will not touch what would stain him.
Judaism
When Potiphar’s wife urges Joseph to be with her, he refuses, asking how he could do such a great wickedness and sin against God, and when she seizes his garment he leaves it and flees.
— Genesis 39:7-12 (JPS 1917, Sefaria) [6]
Genesis presents Joseph as the young man who protects his integrity even at personal cost.
Joseph embodies Kural 916: one who values his own goodness does not take what is offered.
Buddhism
“Not to be contented with one's own wife, and to be seen with harlots and the wives of others — this is a cause of one's downfall.”
— Parabhava Sutta, Sn 1.6 (tr. Narada Thera) [7]
The Parabhava Sutta lists such conduct among the causes of a person’s decline.
Kural 916 states the positive side: those who protect their standing stay away.
Jainism
“In the assembly he pronounces holy (words), yet secretly he commits sins; but the wise know him to be a deceiver and great rogue.”
— Sutrakritanga 1.4.1.18 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Sutrakritanga says that a man who hides his sexual lapses still loses his good name among the discerning.
Kural 916 says that those who care about their own good name stay away from women who sell their favours, and this verse shows how such a lapse destroys that reputation.
Sikhism
“The Sikh ought to treat beautiful women of others as his mothers, sisters, and daughters.”
— Vaaran Bhai Gurdas, Vaar 29, Pauri 11 (English translation as published on BaniDB/SikhiToTheMax and searchgurbani.com) [9]
In a pauri describing the Gursikh’s daily conduct, Bhai Gurdas sets the standard for how a Sikh looks at women who are not his wife: as mother, sister or daughter. This became a widely cited rule of Sikh sexual ethics.
The Kural says those who guard their good name will not seek the arms of women offering cheap favours, and Bhai Gurdas gives the Sikh form of that same self-respecting restraint.
Greek Mythology
Queen Anteia tries to win the young hero Bellerophon, but he refuses her because he is upright of heart; in revenge she falsely accuses him to her husband.
— Homer, Iliad 6.155-170 [10]
Homer presents Bellerophon as the noble-minded young man who would not compromise his honor.
He is an early Greek picture of the thannalam paarippaar of Kural 916.
Philosophy
“If I can get them with the preservation of my own honor and fidelity and greatness of mind, show me the way and I will get them; but if you require me to lose my own proper good that you may gain what is not good, consider how inequitable and foolish you are.”
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 24 (tr. Elizabeth Carter) [11]
Epictetus refuses any gain that would cost him his integrity.
Valluvar’s self-respecting man likewise refuses pleasure that would cost him his good name.

Real Life Example

John Profumo resigns after misleading Parliament
5 June 1963 · London, United Kingdom
John Profumo, Britain's Secretary of State for War, had a brief relationship in 1961 with Christine Keeler, who was also involved with a Soviet naval attaché, Yevgeny Ivanov. In March 1963 Profumo told the House of Commons there had been no impropriety in the relationship, but in June he admitted that this statement was untrue and resigned from the government and from Parliament. The scandal contributed to the fall of Harold Macmillan's government later that year, and Profumo spent the rest of his life in charity work in London's East End.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
A senior minister lost his office and political reputation because of an association he first tried to deny.
The kural says those who care for their good name will not seek such company, and Profumo's case shows how quickly a public reputation can be lost through it.
