Kural 1179

Eyes Consumed with Grief · Kanvithuppazhithal · கண்விதுப்பழிதல்

வாராக்கால் துஞ்சா வரின்துஞ்சா ஆயிடை ஆரஞர் உற்றன கண்.
Vaaraakkaal thunjaa varindhunjaa aayidai aaranjar utrana kan.

Meaning

When he does not come, my eyes cannot sleep, and when he comes, they still cannot sleep. Either way, they suffer terrible pain.

Elementary

Imagine waiting up for someone you love to come home: you can’t sleep because you’re waiting. Then they come, and you still can’t sleep because you’re so happy, and maybe worried they’ll leave again. The lady’s eyes get no rest either way.

University

The couplet is a neat antithesis: vārākkāl tuñcā, ‘when he does not come, they do not sleep’, and varin tuñcā, ‘if he comes, they do not sleep’; āyiṭai, ‘between these two’, the eyes uṟṟaṉa ār añar, ‘have met with grievous pain’. Commentators supply the reasons: in absence the eyes watch for his coming, and in presence they fear his departure (Solomon Pappaiah, Puliyur Kesikan: pirivu añci, ‘dreading separation’). Kalaignar generalises it to ‘such suffering can only belong to lovers’ eyes’. Pope: ‘When he comes not, all slumber flies; no sleep when he is there; / Thus every way my eyes have troubles hard to bear.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Gopi Gita, the cowherd women tell Krishna that when he goes to the forest by day, even a moment without seeing him feels like an age, and when they do see his face, they curse the creator who made eyelids that blink and interrupt their gaze.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.31.15 (Gopi Gita) (paraphrase) [3]
The gopis find no rest either in Krishna’s absence or in his presence: absence is an age, and presence is spoiled by the blink of an eye.
This is the Kural’s double bind exactly: the eyes suffer when he does not come and are restless even when he does.
Christianity
“I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh…”
— Song of Solomon 5:2 (KJV) [4]
The bride lies down but cannot truly rest, because her heart is always listening for the beloved.
The Kural’s eyes know the same half-sleep: they cannot let go of watchfulness for him, whether he is absent or near.
Islam
In Nizami’s romance, Majnun, separated from Layla, wanders the desert sleepless with longing, and even when he is allowed near her he cannot be at peace, so consuming is his love.
— Nizami Ganjavi, Layla and Majnun (paraphrase) [5]
Majnun’s love leaves him no rest, whether apart from Layla or close to her.
His restlessness on both sides of meeting mirrors the Kural’s eyes that sleep neither when the beloved stays away nor when he comes.
Judaism
“Thus I was: in the day the drought consumed me, and the frost by night; and my sleep fled from mine eyes.”
— Genesis 31:40 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Jacob reminds Laban of his years of service, during which he gave up sleep; he had first served seven years out of love for Rachel.
Jacob’s eyes, like the Kural’s, lost their sleep in a life shaped by love.
Buddhism
“Long is the night to the sleepless; long is the league to the weary. Long is worldly existence to fools who know not the Sublime Truth.”
— Dhammapada 60 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Dhammapada begins its chapter on the fool with the image of the sleepless night that never seems to end.
The heroine’s eyes know that long night, whether her husband is away or present.
Jainism
“How, then, can a man who is devoted to ‘colours,’ ever derive any happiness from anything? He suffers pain at the time of their enjoyment to procure which he had suffered misery.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.32 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana says that the one attached to what the eye loves suffers both while seeking it and while enjoying it.
This is the Kural’s double bind in Jain terms: pain when he is absent, and pain still when he is present.
Sikhism
“The pain of separation has grown shy, for I have obtained the Blessed Vision of His Darshan; His Ambrosial Glance of Grace has filled me with bliss.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 460, Raag Aasaa, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan says that once the soul-bride has seen her Lord, the pain of separation slinks away and His glance fills her with bliss.
The Kural's eyes find no sleep either when he is away or when he comes, because even his presence brings fear of parting; in Gurbani, by contrast, the Beloved's arrival ends the pain of separation and brings rest.
Greek Mythology
Throughout the Odyssey, Penelope weeps for her absent husband night after night until Athena sheds sleep on her eyelids; and even after Odysseus returns, the couple stay awake through the night of their reunion talking.
— Homer, Odyssey 1.363-364, 19.603-604 and 23.300-343 (paraphrase) [10]
Homer’s Penelope cannot sleep for grief in Odysseus’s absence, and the night of his return is also a night without sleep.
Her sleepless nights in both absence and presence give the Kural’s double bind a gentler, happier mirror.
Philosophy
Socrates describes the lover’s soul, parted from the beloved, as maddened and in pain, unable to sleep by night or stay still by day, and running to wherever it hopes to see the beautiful one.
— Plato, Phaedrus 251d-e (paraphrase) [11]
In the Phaedrus, the absence of the beloved leaves the soul sleepless and restless, relieved only by seeing him.
The Kural’s eyes suffer the same sleeplessness when he is away.

Real Life Example

Franz Kafka's engagements to Felice Bauer
1912-1917 · Prague (now Czech Republic) and Berlin, Germany
Franz Kafka met Felice Bauer at the home of his friend Max Brod in Prague on 13 August 1912. Over the next five years he sent her more than five hundred letters and postcards, and the two became engaged twice, but each engagement was broken off, the second and last time in December 1917. Felice later married another man and, years afterwards, gave Kafka's letters to Schocken publishers, who published them in the 1960s.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kafka's letters show a man who longed for Felice when they were apart and drew back when marriage came close, so that neither distance nor nearness gave him peace.
The kural says the eyes cannot sleep when the beloved is away, nor when he comes, and suffer in between, which describes the restless back-and-forth of Kafka's two engagements.
