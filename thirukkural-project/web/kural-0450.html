Kural 450

Seeking the Aid of Great Men · Periyaaraith Thunaikkodal · பெரியாரைத் துணைக்கோடல்

பல்லார் பகைகொளலிற் பத்தடுத்த தீமைத்தே நல்லார் தொடர்கை விடல்.
Pallaar pakaikolalir paththaduththa theemaiththe Nallaar thodarkai vidal.

Meaning

Losing the friendship of good people is ten times worse than making many enemies.

Elementary

Having lots of enemies is bad. But this kural says losing the friendship of good people is ten times worse than that.

University

The chapter closes with a calculus: ‘paththu aduththa’ (ten times over) compares two harms. Enmity from many (pallaar) is external and can be countered; abandonment of good friends (nallaar thodarkai vidal) removes the very counsel and support by which enemies are countered — so it multiplies every other danger. The verb ‘vidal’ (letting go) places responsibility on the one who drops such friendships, whether through pride, neglect, or preference for flatterers.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Stung by Vidura’s repeated advice to make peace with the Pandavas, Dhritarashtra angrily tells him to leave; Vidura goes to the Pandavas in the forest, and the king, soon realizing his loss, sends Sanjaya to bring him back.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva (tr. K. M. Ganguli) [4]
Casting off the one truly good counselor proves a grave loss even the king immediately feels, and the Kaurava line is later destroyed in the war he tried to prevent.
Forsaking the good is shown as a self-inflicted wound worse than enmity.
Christianity
“Be not deceived: evil communications corrupt good manners.”
— 1 Corinthians 15:33 (KJV) [5]
Paul warns that bad company corrupts good character.
The reverse side of the Kural: leaving good company for bad is corrosive.
Islam
“Oh, woe to me! I wish I had not taken that one as a friend.”
— Quran 25:27–28 (Sahih International) [6]
On the Day of Judgment, the wrongdoer bites his hands in regret for having followed a misleading friend instead of the Messenger’s way.
Abandoning the good companion for the wrong one is shown as a supreme, lasting regret.
Judaism
“But he forsook the counsel of the old men, which they had given him, and consulted with the young men that were grown up with him.”
— 1 Kings 12:8 (KJV) [5]
Rehoboam rejects the elders who had served Solomon and follows his young peers; ten tribes break away and the kingdom is split.
Forsaking the good counselors costs more than any external enemy could.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada advises not to associate with evil companions or seek the company of the vile, but to associate with good friends and seek the company of noble men.
— Dhammapada 78 [7]
The Buddha directs his followers to cling to good companions and away from bad ones.
Implicitly treats the loss of good friends as a serious harm, as the Kural does.
Jainism
“A good man should long to live with his teacher in order to perform his duties, knowing that he who does not live with his teacher will not put an end to his mundane existence. Making manifest the conduct of the virtuous, an intelligent (monk) should not leave the (company of his teacher).”
— Sutrakritanga 1.14.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The Sutrakritanga warns that leaving the company of the virtuous teacher costs nothing less than liberation itself, so the wise do not leave it.
Valluvar judges giving up the friendship of the good to be ten times worse than the enmity of many. The Jain text likewise treats leaving good company as the greatest loss, since it closes the way out of samsara.
Sikhism
During the siege of Anandpur in 1705, some Sikhs signed a disclaimer (bedava) disowning Guru Gobind Singh and left; shamed at home and led back by Mai Bhago, they rejoined the Guru and fell fighting at Khidrana (Muktsar), where the Guru tore up the bedava at the dying Mahan Singh's request.
— Encyclopaedia of Sikhism (ed. Harbans Singh, Punjabi University), 'Bedava', as published on thesikhencyclopedia.com (paraphrase) [9]
Abandoning the Guru's company proved a far deeper loss to these Sikhs than the danger they fled, and they won back honour only by returning to it at the cost of their lives.
The Kural says giving up the friendship of the good is ten times worse than the enmity of many; the deserters found leaving the Guru worse than facing the besieging armies.
Greek Mythology
In Iliad 1, Agamemnon insults Achilles and takes his prize; Achilles withdraws, and the Greeks suffer more at the Trojans’ hands than at any time in the war. In Iliad 9 Agamemnon admits his folly and offers great gifts to win him back.
— Homer, Iliad 1 and 9 [10]
Alienating his best ally costs Agamemnon more than all the enemy’s efforts.
Losing the good friend is worse than the enmity of many.
Philosophy
“You might just as well take the sun out of the sky as friendship from life; for the immortal gods have given us nothing better or more delightful.”
— Cicero, Laelius de Amicitia (On Friendship), §47 (ch. 13) (tr. E. S. Shuckburgh) [11]
Laelius answers philosophers who advise holding friendship loosely to avoid anxiety, or seeking it only for advantage: to remove friendship from life, he says, is like removing the sun from the sky.
Like the Kural, Cicero treats the loss of good friends as a darkness that no other good can make up for, though he does not weigh it against the enmity of many.

Real Life Example

Louis XVI dismisses the reforming minister Turgot
12 May 1776 · Versailles, France
Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot, appointed controller general of finances by Louis XVI in 1774, proposed the Six Edicts, which among other things abolished compulsory road labour by peasants and the Paris guilds. The privileged orders, court factions and the Parlement of Paris opposed the reforms strongly. In May 1776 Louis XVI gave way and dismissed Turgot, and most of his reforms were abandoned.
— Sources: [12] [13]
By giving up an able and honest minister to quiet powerful opponents, Louis XVI lost one of the best chances of reforming France's finances before the crisis that led to the Revolution.
The kural warns that abandoning the friendship of the good is ten times worse than the hostility of many, and Louis chose to placate many opponents at the cost of his best counsel.
