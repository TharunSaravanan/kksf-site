Kural 1322

The Pleasures of 'Temporary Variance' · Oodaluvakai · ஊடலுவகை

ஊடலின் தோன்றும் சிறுதுனி நல்லளி வாடினும் பாடு பெறும்.
Oodalin thoandrum sirudhuni nallali Vaatinum paadu perum.

Meaning

The small pain that a lovers' sulk brings may make his kindness droop for a while, but it will come back even stronger.

Elementary

A plant that is not watered for one day droops a little, and when you water it again it seems to stand up even taller. A short, small quarrel is like that: love looks a little wilted for a moment, then bounces back stronger.

University

The couplet sets ciṟu tuṉi (‘a small distress’, tuṉi meaning grief or estrangement) arising from ūṭal against nal aḷi (‘good tender love’), which may vāṭiṉum (‘even if it should wither’, a plant metaphor) yet pāṭu peṟum (‘will gain greatness’). Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah take aḷi as the husband’s love that seems to fade during the sulk and then grows in worth; Puliyur Kesikan adds pinnar (‘afterwards’), making the gain explicitly temporal, and Pope renders the sense as feigned anger making drooping affection ‘bloom again’. Both ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net print the same text; the botanical verb vāṭu (to wilt) is characteristic of the Kural’s Inbam imagery.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Rasa-lila of the Bhagavata Purana, when the gopis grow proud of Krishna’s attention he suddenly vanishes; they search the forest for him in anguish, and when he reappears their joy and his tenderness are greater than before.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.30-32 (paraphrase) [3]
The tradition presents a brief separation within love as a way of deepening it.
As in the Kural, a short spell in which love seems to wilt ends with that love shining more brightly.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath:”
— Ephesians 4:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul allows anger but sets it a short limit, so that it ends before it can harden.
The Kural’s sulk is a short, harmless anger of exactly this kind, ending before it does damage.
Islam
“And if a woman fears from her husband contempt or evasion, there is no sin upon them if they make terms of settlement between them - and settlement is best.”
— Qur’an 4:128 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur’an encourages spouses to settle tensions between them, calling reconciliation the best course.
The Kural assumes the same arc: a passing distress between spouses resolved into something better than before.
Judaism
“For His anger is but for a moment, His favour is for a life-time; weeping may tarry for the night, but joy cometh in the morning.”
— Psalms 30:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The psalmist contrasts a brief anger with a lasting favour, and a night of tears with morning joy.
The Kural’s ‘small distress’ is just such a passing anger, after which love returns more glorious.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada teaches that hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world, but only by non-hatred, and calls this an eternal law.
— Dhammapada 5 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita; paraphrase) [7]
Hostility ends only when one side lets go of it rather than answering in kind.
The Kural’s sulk passes for the same reason: the husband answers it with tenderness, not anger.
Jainism
“He will sink through anger; he will go down through pride; delusion will block up his path; through greed he will incur dangers in both worlds.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 9.54 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
King Nami tells Indra that anger and pride drag a person down rather than raising him.
Where the Kural says the pain of a sulk will win glory, Jain teaching warns that anger and pride (the stuff of a sulk) lead downward.
Sikhism
“Suffering is the medicine, and pleasure the disease, because where there is pleasure, there is no desire for God.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 469, Raag Aasaa (Asa di Vaar), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that pain can heal the soul by renewing its longing for God, while easy comfort makes it forget.
The Kural says the small pain of a sulk makes love wilt only to bloom more richly; Gurbani likewise sees passing pain as the medicine that makes longing for the Beloved grow.
Greek Mythology
“I must go and see them that I may make peace between them; they have been quarrelling, and are so angry that they have not slept with one another this long while; if I can bring them round and restore them to one another’s embraces, they will be grateful to me and love me for ever afterwards.”
— Homer, Iliad 14 (tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Hera pretends she is off to reconcile the primeval couple Oceanus and Tethys, whose quarrel has kept them apart.
The image of an estranged couple restored to each other’s embraces is the Kural’s withered love made glorious again.
Philosophy
On his last day Socrates, rubbing his leg after the fetters are removed, remarks how strangely pleasure is related to pain: they never come to a man together, yet whoever pursues one is generally compelled to take the other, as if they were joined at a single head.
— Plato, Phaedo 60b-c (paraphrase) [11]
Plato has Socrates notice that pain and pleasure follow close upon each other.
The Kural’s small pain of sulking is followed, by the same linkage, by the pleasure of love regained.

Real Life Example

Tennyson and Emily Sellwood marry after ten years apart
June 1850 · Shiplake, Oxfordshire, England
Alfred Tennyson fell in love with Emily Sellwood in 1836 and the two came to consider themselves engaged, but in 1840 all relations between them were broken off. In 1849 Tennyson resumed his correspondence with Emily, and in June 1850, less than two weeks after the publication of his poem In Memoriam, they were quietly married at Shiplake Church. Their marriage, which lasted until his death, is described as happy and full of tenderness and affection.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It is a well-documented case of a love that seemed to have withered for a decade and then returned to become a lifelong marriage.
The kural says that love may droop for a while and then return with greater glory, which is what happened when Tennyson and Emily Sellwood came back together after ten years apart.
