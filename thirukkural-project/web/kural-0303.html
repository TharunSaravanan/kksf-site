Kural 303

The Not Being Angry · Vekulaamai · வெகுளாமை

மறத்தல் வெகுளியை யார்மாட்டும் தீய பிறத்தல் அதனான் வரும்.
Maraththal vekuliyai yaarmaattum theeya Piraththal adhanaan varum.

Meaning

Forget your anger toward anyone at all, because every kind of harm grows out of anger.

Elementary

Anger is like a seed that grows weeds. If you keep it, bad things grow out of it: mean words, fights, broken friendships. So the kural says: let go of anger, no matter who made you angry.

University

The verb maraththal ('forgetting', 'letting go') is significant: Valluvar does not ask merely for suppression but for the anger to be dropped from memory, since resentment that is stored continues to generate harm. Yaarmaattum ('toward anyone whatsoever') universalises the precept, removing the distinctions of power drawn in 301 and 302. The second line offers a causal claim: evils (theeya) are 'born' (piraththal) from anger, making it a root vice rather than one fault among many. This genealogical view of anger as a source of other wrongs parallels the Buddhist treatment of dosa as one of the three roots of unwholesome action and the Gita's chain from desire to anger to ruin.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“There are three gates leading to this hell – lust, anger and greed. Every sane man should give these up, for they lead to the degradation of the soul.”
— Bhagavad Gita 16.21 (Bhagavad-gita As It Is) [3]
Krishna names anger as one of three gateways to ruin and commands that it be abandoned.
Like the kural, the Gita calls for giving anger up entirely because destruction flows from it.
Christianity
“Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: Neither give place to the devil.”
— Ephesians 4:26-27 (KJV) [4]
Paul allows that anger may arise but insists it not be kept overnight, since lingering wrath gives the devil a foothold.
Parallels the kural's 'forget anger': do not hold on to it, because harm grows from it.
Islam
“A man said to the Prophet, ‘Advise me!’ The Prophet said, ‘Do not become angry.’ The man asked (the same) again and again, and the Prophet said in each case, ‘Do not become angry.’”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6116 [5]
Asked for comprehensive advice, the Prophet gives a single repeated instruction to avoid anger.
Like the kural, this treats giving up anger as a master key, since so much wrongdoing springs from it.
Judaism
“Thou shalt not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD.”
— Leviticus 19:18 (JPS 1917) [6]
The Torah forbids both revenge and holding a grudge, pairing this with the command to love one's neighbour.
The ban on 'bearing a grudge' is close to the kural's 'forget anger'; both demand that resentment be released, not stored.
Buddhism
“‘He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.’ Those who do not harbor such thoughts still their hatred.”
— Dhammapada 4 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that rehearsing grievances keeps hatred alive, while letting them go ends it.
Nearly the kural's own advice: forgetting the offence is how anger, and the evils it breeds, are ended.
Jainism
“If a layman abuses a monk, he should not grow angry against him; because he would be like a child, a monk should not grow angry. If a monk hears bad words, cruel and rankling ones, he should silently overlook them, and not take them to heart.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 2.24–25 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Abuse (ākrośa) is one of the twenty-two hardships (parīṣaha) a Jain monk must learn to bear. He is told neither to answer it with anger nor to store it up in his mind.
'Not take them to heart' is close to Valluvar's 'forget' (maraththal): the offence is to be dropped from the mind, not merely held back for the moment.
Sikhism
“Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his saloks Bhagat Kabir sets forgiveness (khima) at the very centre of the spiritual life: where it is present, God is present.
The Kural asks us to let go of anger toward anyone at all, because every evil is born of it. Kabir treats that letting go, forgiveness, as the place where God dwells.
Greek Mythology
“Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans.”
— Homer, Iliad, Book 1, opening lines (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Western literature's first epic opens by naming its subject as one man's wrath and the endless suffering it caused.
Homer and Valluvar agree that anger is a fountain of evils; the Iliad is effectively a narrative proof of the kural's second line.
Philosophy
“Next, if you choose to view its results and the mischief that it does, no plague has cost the human race more dear.”
— Seneca, On Anger (De Ira), Book I (trans. Aubrey Stewart, 1900) [11]
Seneca lists the slaughters, poisonings, and ruined cities that anger has produced.
A direct philosophical counterpart to 'evils are born of anger'.

Real Life Example

The Peace of Westphalia declares perpetual oblivion and amnesty
24 October 1648 · Munster and Osnabruck, Westphalia, Germany (Holy Roman Empire)
The treaties of Munster and Osnabruck, together known as the Peace of Westphalia, ended the Thirty Years' War. Their second articles declared that there should be on both sides a perpetual oblivion and amnesty for all hostile acts committed since the beginning of the troubles. Grievances and claims arising from the war were to be buried in perpetual oblivion so that they could not be used against the other side.
— Sources: [12] [13]
After decades of devastating war, the peacemakers wrote into law that past injuries must be forgotten, recognising that remembered grievances would only breed new conflict.
The kural asks us to let go of anger toward anyone and forget it, because every evil is born from anger, and the Westphalian treaties made such forgetting a binding legal duty.
