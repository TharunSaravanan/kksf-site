Kural 1082

Mental Disturbance Caused by Her Beauty · Thagaiyanankuruthal · தகையணங்குறுத்தல்

நோக்கினாள் நோக்கெதிர் நோக்குதல் தாக்கணங்கு தானைக்கொண் டன்ன துடைத்து.
Nokkinaal nokkethir nokkuthal thaakkanangu thaanaikkon tanna thudaiththu.

Meaning

When she looks back at me, it is as if a goddess who attacks had come with a whole army.

Elementary

Have you ever been looked at so seriously that you suddenly felt shy and could not look back? Here the young man looks at the girl, and when she looks back at him he feels as if an entire army has charged at him.

University

The kural turns on the repeated root nokku (நோக்கு, 'look'): nokkinaal ('she who looked'), nokkethir ('against my look') and nokkuthal ('the looking back'). Thaakkanangu (தாக்கணங்கு) is an anangu that strikes or attacks on its own, and thaanai (தானை) is an army; Mu. Varadarasanar explains that her return glance is like such a self-attacking deity arriving with a whole army. Commentators in the Parimelazhagar line take the point to be that one glance already wounds, so a returned glance doubles the assault. Pope renders: 'As if the matchless goddess' hand / Led forth an armed band.' The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa's Kumarasambhava, Kama, the god of love, waits with his flower-bow as Parvati approaches the meditating Shiva, and shoots at the moment her glance meets his, so that even the great ascetic is shaken.
— Kalidasa, Kumarasambhava, Canto 3 (paraphrase) [3]
Love in Indian poetry is pictured as an archer-god whose arrows fly with a beloved's glance.
The Kural likewise turns a returned glance into an armed attack by a deity.
Christianity
“Thou art beautiful, O my love, as Tirzah, comely as Jerusalem, terrible as an army with banners.”
— Song of Solomon 6:4 (KJV) [4]
The lover in the Song of Songs finds the beloved both beautiful and overwhelming, like an army with banners.
The Kural uses the same military image: her look arrives like an army.
Islam
“And women in the city said, ‘The wife of al-ʿAzeez is seeking to seduce her slave boy; he has impassioned her with love. Indeed, we see her [to be] in clear error.’”
— Qur'an 12:30 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur'an describes the wife of al-Aziz as having been overpowered by love for Joseph through his beauty.
Both texts show beauty overpowering the one who looks at it, though the Qur'an judges the passion as error.
Judaism
“You have captured my heart, / My own, my bride, / You have captured my heart / With one [glance] of your eyes, / With one coil of your necklace.”
— Song of Songs 4:9 (THE JPS TANAKH: Gender-Sensitive Edition, RJPS 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
The lover says that a single glance of the beloved's eyes has taken his heart captive.
The Kural, too, says one returned look is enough to overcome him.
Buddhism
“Sensual passions are your first army.”
— Sutta Nipata 3.2, Padhana Sutta (tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
In the Padhana Sutta the Buddha-to-be names the forces Mara sends against him as armies, and sensual passion leads them.
The Kural's image of a glance arriving like an army matches the Buddhist picture of desire as an attacking army.
Jainism
“A Śramaṇa, engaged in penance, should not allow himself to watch the shape, beauty, coquetry, laughter, prattle, gestures, and glances of women, nor retain a recollection of them in his mind.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Mahavira's discipline lists glances among the things a monk must not dwell on, because they disturb the mind.
The Kural shows why: a single returned glance overwhelms the one who meets it.
Sikhism
“Kabeer, the True Guru, the Spiritual Warrior, has shot me with His Arrow. As soon as it struck me, I fell to the ground, with a hole in my heart.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1374, Saloks of Bhagat Kabir (salok 194) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a salok included in the Guru Granth Sahib, Bhagat Kabir describes the Guru's word as an arrow that strikes the devotee to the ground and pierces his heart.
The Kural says the woman's answering glance strikes like a goddess leading an army; Kabir uses the same image of being struck down by a warrior's single shot, here the Guru's word.
Greek Mythology
In Ovid's tale, Cupid shoots Apollo with a golden arrow so that the god falls in love the instant he sees the nymph Daphne, and even the mighty archer-god is helpless.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.452-567 (paraphrase) [10]
Greek and Roman myth make love an armed god whose arrows strike through the eyes.
The Kural's 'attacking goddess with an army' is the Tamil form of love as a weapon-bearing deity.
Philosophy
“...as he receives the effluence of beauty through the eyes, the wing moistens and he warms.”
— Plato, Phaedrus 251b (tr. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Plato describes beauty as a stream that enters the lover through the eyes and overpowers him.
For the Kural, too, the eyes are the gate through which the attack comes.

Real Life Example

Petrarch first sees Laura
6 April 1327 · Avignon, France
The Italian poet Petrarch recorded in a note in his copy of Virgil that he first saw the woman he called Laura on 6 April 1327 in Avignon, in the church of Sainte-Claire. He wrote hundreds of love poems to her, collected in his Canzoniere. In the same note he recorded that she died in Avignon on 6 April 1348.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Petrarch's poems to Laura shaped the love sonnet for centuries across Europe.
The kural describes the overwhelming force of the beloved's returning glance; a single sight of Laura in church captured Petrarch for the rest of his life.
