Kural 868

The Might of Hatred · Pakai Maatchi · பகை மாட்சி

குணனிலனாய்க் குற்றம் பலவாயின் மாற்றார்க்கு இனனிலனாம் ஏமாப் புடைத்து.
Gunanilanaayk kutram palavaayin maatraarkku inanilanaam emaap pudaiththu.

Meaning

A person with no good qualities and many faults ends up with no friends to help, and that makes it easy for enemies to win.

Elementary

If someone is never kind and always does mean things, little by little all his friends leave. When a problem comes, he is alone, and that makes it easy for anyone against him. Good habits are what keep friends by your side.

University

The couplet is a causal chain: gunanilan (குணனிலன், without good qualities) plus kutram pala (குற்றம் பல, many faults) yields inanilan (இனனிலன், one without kin or allies, from inam, ‘one’s own group’), and that condition is emaappu (ஏமாப்பு, security or advantage) for the maatraar (மாற்றார், adversaries). Varadarasanar and Pappaiah both stress that it is the friendlessness itself, not any action of the foe, that becomes the enemy’s strength. Kalaignar condenses this to ‘he loses his supporters and is easily brought down’. The verse links this chapter back to the Natpiyal chapters on friendship that precede it. Text identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Ramayana, Ravana scorns his brother Vibhishana’s counsel to return Sita and insults him; Vibhishana leaves Lanka and joins Rama, bringing vital knowledge of Lanka’s defences.
— Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (Vibhishana’s defection) [3]
Ravana’s arrogance and wrongdoing drive away his own wise kinsman, who becomes a decisive asset to the enemy.
This is the Kural exactly: a faulty man loses his own people, and his loss is the foe’s gain.
Christianity
Jesus says that if a kingdom is divided against itself it cannot stand, and if a house is divided against itself it cannot stand.
— Mark 3:24-25 (KJV) [4]
A community that loses its inner cohesion collapses.
The Kural’s man who has driven away his allies stands in just such a divided house.
Islam
“So by mercy from Allāh, [O Muḥammad], you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude [in speech] and harsh in heart, they would have disbanded from about you.”
— Qur’an 3:159 (Sahih International) [5]
The Qur’an tells the Prophet that his gentleness kept his followers together, and harshness would have scattered them.
It is the positive mirror of the Kural: good character keeps allies, bad character loses them.
Judaism
When the people ask Rehoboam to lighten their burden, he rejects the elders’ advice and answers harshly; the ten northern tribes break away and the kingdom is split.
— 1 Kings 12:1-20 (Tanakh) [6]
A king’s harshness and poor judgement cost him most of his own people.
Rehoboam shows how faults, not enemies, can leave a ruler without allies.
Buddhism
When King Ajatasattu plans to attack the Vajjians, the Buddha says they cannot be conquered so long as they meet in harmony, honour their elders and keep their customs.
— Maha-parinibbana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 16 [7]
The Vajjians’ strength lies in their unity and good conduct, not in their armies.
Valluvar describes the reverse: a man whose faults have stripped him of allies has lost exactly this protection.
Jainism
“As a bitch with sore ears is driven away everywhere, thus a bad, insubordinate, and talkative (pupil) is turned out.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.4 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The opening lecture of the Uttarādhyayana says that a monk of bad character finds no welcome anywhere and is driven out of every community.
The Kural says a man with no virtues and many faults ends up without allies, and the Jain simile shows the same result: faults leave a man unwanted and alone.
Sikhism
“The Simmal tree is straight as an arrow; it is very tall, and very thick. But those birds which visit it hopefully, depart disappointed. Its fruits are tasteless, its flowers are nauseating, and its leaves are useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 470, Raag Aasaa, Asa di Vaar (salok before pauri 14), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak's image of the silk-cotton tree shows something imposing but without any good quality, so that those who come to it go away and do not stay.
The Kural says a man with no virtues and many faults ends up with no allies; the Simmal tree, tall but fruitless, likewise keeps no one who comes to it.
Greek Mythology
In the Iliad, Agamemnon insults Achilles by seizing his prize Briseis; Achilles withdraws from the fighting and the Trojans drive the Greeks back to their ships.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 1 and 8-16 [10]
A leader’s arrogance drives away his greatest ally, to the great benefit of the enemy.
Agamemnon’s fault turns his own camp’s loss into Troy’s advantage, as the Kural predicts.
Philosophy
“Therefore the bad man does not seem to be amicably disposed even to himself, because there is nothing in him to love; so that if to be thus is the height of wretchedness, we should strain every nerve to avoid wickedness and should endeavour to be good; for so and only so can one be either friendly to oneself or a friend to another.”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, IX.4, 1166b (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle argues that a wicked man, having nothing lovable in him, is at odds with himself and cannot be a true friend to others. In the next chapter he adds that bad men cannot hold together in common purpose.
This supports the Kural's claim that a man without virtues and full of faults ends up without allies.

Real Life Example

Richard II is deserted and deposed
1399 · England and Wales
Richard II's authoritarian rule and reliance on favourites alienated many nobles, and in 1399, on the death of John of Gaunt, he confiscated the vast Lancaster estates that should have passed to his exiled cousin Henry Bolingbroke. While Richard was in Ireland, Henry landed in England, and leading nobles and many others joined him, while many of the soldiers Richard brought back to Wales deserted. Richard surrendered in August and was deposed, and Henry was crowned as Henry IV.
— Sources: [12] [13]
A king who had alienated those around him found, at the moment of need, that almost no one would stand with him.
The kural says a man of many faults ends up without allies, and that very friendlessness becomes his enemy's strength, as Richard's isolation became Henry's opportunity.
