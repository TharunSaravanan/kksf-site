Kural 805

Familiarity · Pazhaimai · பழைமை

பேதைமை ஒன்றோ பெருங்கிழமை என்றுணர்க நோதக்க நட்டார் செயின்.
Pedhaimai ondro perungizhamai endrunarka nodhakka nattaar seyin.

Meaning

If a friend does something that hurts you, think that it came either from not knowing better or from being so close that they felt free to do it.

Elementary

If your best friend borrows your favourite book and spills juice on it, think: ‘Maybe they didn’t know it mattered so much, or maybe they just felt at home with my things.’ Don’t decide straight away that they meant to hurt you.

University

Pedhaimai (பேதைமை, ‘ignorance, folly’) and perungizhamai (பெருங்கிழமை, ‘great intimacy, strong right of friendship’) are the two charitable explanations the couplet offers for an act that is nodhakka (நோதக்க, ‘fit to cause pain’). The particle ondro (ஒன்றோ) divides the commentators. Mu. Varadarasanar and Kalaignar read it as ‘either … or’: ignorance or intimacy. Solomon Pappaiah reads ‘not ignorance only, but also great intimacy’. Puliyur Kesigan reads it as rejecting ignorance altogether: think of it as intimacy, not ignorance. The pairing is close to Arjuna’s pramādāt praṇayena vā (‘through carelessness or through affection’) in Bhagavad Gita 11.41, a parallel worth noting even though no direct influence can be shown. The text is the same in both base sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“I was ignorant of Your majesty, showing negligence and undue affection.”
— Bhagavad Gita 11.41 (trans. Swami Mukundananda; Sanskrit pramādāt praṇayena vāpi) [3]
Arjuna explains his past familiarity with Krishna by two causes: pramāda (negligence) and praṇaya (affection).
The Gita offers the same two explanations as the Kural, pedhaimai and perungizhamai: an offence comes either from not knowing or from too much closeness.
Christianity
From the cross, Jesus prays for those crucifying him, asking the Father to forgive them because they do not know what they are doing.
— Luke 23:34 [4]
Jesus treats even a grave wrong as coming from ignorance and asks for pardon on that ground.
Like the Kural’s first explanation, pedhaimai, ignorance becomes the reason for charity.
Islam
The Prophet warned believers against suspicion, calling it the most untruthful kind of talk, and told them not to spy on one another.
— Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Good Manners (Adab), hadith on suspicion [5]
Hadith literature urges believers to think well of one another (husn al-zann) instead of assuming bad intent.
The Kural likewise tells us to assume harmless causes when a friend’s act stings.
Judaism
“appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion and judge all men with the scale weighted in his favor.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:6 (Mishnah Yomit, trans. Joshua Kulp, Sefaria) [6]
The Mishnah teaches dan le-khaf zekhut: judging others favourably by tipping the scale toward innocence.
Reading a hurtful act as ignorance or intimacy is this favourable judgement applied to a friend.
Buddhism
The Dhammapada advises attending to one’s own deeds, done and undone, rather than to the faults of others and what they have or have not done.
— Dhammapada 50 [7]
The verse moves attention away from cataloguing others’ wrongs.
Declining to dwell on a friend’s offence is the habit of mind the Kural recommends.
Jainism
“But for the following fifteen good qualities he is called well-behaved: if he is always humble, steady, free from deceit and curiosity; if he abuses nobody; … if he listens to friendly advice; … if he is not angry with friends; if he speaks well even of a bad friend behind his back …”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.10–13 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Among the marks of a well-disciplined monk, the Uttaradhyayana lists not being angry with friends, not holding on to wrath, and speaking well even of a bad friend.
The Kural asks us to put a friend's hurtful act down to ignorance or intimacy, not malice, and the Jain canon likewise asks the disciplined person not to be angry with friends and to speak kindly even of a friend who errs.
Sikhism
“Kabir! Where there is spiritual wisdom, there is righteousness and Dharma. Where there is falsehood, there is sin. Where there is greed, there is death. Where there is forgiveness, there is God Himself.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1372, Salok Bhagat Kabir 155 (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Kabir ranks forgiveness (khima) as the highest of the virtues he lists, the place where God Himself is present.
The Kural tells us to excuse a friend's hurtful act as ignorance or the freedom of great intimacy; Kabir's salok gives the Sikh ground for that charity, since forgiveness is where God dwells.
Greek Mythology
Achilles lets Patroclus fight in his armour but warns him only to drive the Trojans from the ships. Carried away, Patroclus pursues them to the walls of Troy and is killed, bringing Achilles terrible grief, yet Achilles never blames him and mourns him above everyone.
— Homer, Iliad 16-18 [10]
The disobedience that cost Achilles his dearest friend came from eagerness and loyalty, not malice.
Achilles reads his friend’s fatal act as zeal, not betrayal, much as the Kural asks.
Philosophy
Epictetus advises that when someone treats you badly or speaks ill of you, you should remember that they act from what seems right to them, and that they, not you, bear the harm of their mistaken view.
— Epictetus, Enchiridion 42 [11]
The Stoic method is to explain another’s wrong by their own mistaken judgement, which takes the sting out of personal offence.
This is the Kural’s first explanation, pedhaimai, turned into a mental discipline.

Real Life Example

Carlyle forgives John Stuart Mill after the French Revolution manuscript is burned
1835 · London, England
Thomas Carlyle gave his friend John Stuart Mill the only complete manuscript of the first volume of his history of the French Revolution for comment. While it was in Mill's household it was burned by a maid who mistook it for waste paper and used it to light a fire. Mill, distressed, paid Carlyle compensation; Carlyle rewrote the volume, the book was published in 1837, and Mill was one of the first to praise it.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Carlyle's response turned a disaster that could have ended the friendship into one of the most famous examples of literary forbearance, and the rewritten book made his reputation.
The kural asks us to put down a hurtful act by a friend to ignorance or to the closeness that made them careless, and Carlyle treated the loss caused by Mill's household as an accident rather than an offence.
