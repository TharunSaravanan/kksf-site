Kural 532

Unforgetfulness · Pocchaavaamai · பொச்சாவாமை

பொச்சாப்புக் கொல்லும் புகழை அறிவினை நிச்ச நிரப்புக்கொன் றாங்கு.
Pochchaappuk kollum pukazhai arivinai Nichcha nirappuk kon raangu.

Meaning

Just as being poor day after day slowly destroys a person’s mind, being careless and forgetful destroys a person’s good name.

Elementary

If a child is hungry every single day, it is very hard for them to think clearly at school. In the same way, if someone keeps forgetting their promises and jobs, people slowly stop trusting and admiring them, until their good name is gone.

University

The verb கொல்லும் (‘kills’) does strong work in both halves: பொச்சாப்பு kills புகழ் (fame) just as நிச்ச நிரப்பு kills அறிவு (understanding). Parimelazhagar glosses நிச்ச நிரப்பு as filling one's belly every day by begging. When this happens to a learned man, the humiliation and sin involved bring contempt on him and destroy the regard he enjoyed. In the same way, forgetfulness in a man of fame brings contempt, because he fails to guard himself and his affairs fall apart, and so it destroys his standing. Pavanar notes that நிரப்பு (literally ‘fullness’) is an auspicious euphemism for poverty, and he cross-refers to Kural 1046, where even the sound words of the poor go unheeded. Manakkudavar reads this couplet as part of a set on holding firm in worldly affairs. Parimelazhagar groups 531-532 as the couplets that state the fault of heedlessness. Editions split the final words either as நிரப்புக்கொன் றாங்கு or as நிரப்புக் கொன்றாங்கு; the sense is the same.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined.”
— Bhagavad Gita 2.63, trans. Swami Mukundananda [4]
Krishna traces the downward chain in which the loss of smṛti (memory, mindfulness) destroys discernment and finally the person.
Like the Kural, the Gita treats a failure of remembering as something that kills, not merely inconveniences.
Christianity
“I know thy works, that thou hast a name that thou livest, and art dead. Be watchful, and strengthen the things which remain, that are ready to die”
— Revelation 3:1-2 (KJV) [5]
The church at Sardis still has a reputation for life, but through lack of watchfulness it has become spiritually dead, and it is told to wake up before what remains dies too.
This is a good name emptied out by heedlessness, which is exactly the loss the Kural describes.
Islam
“And be not like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. Those are the defiantly disobedient.”
— Qur'an 59:19 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur'an presents forgetting God as self-destroying: the forgetful end up losing even their own selves and their true standing.
As in the Kural, forgetfulness is not a small slip but something that eats away what a person is worth.
Judaism
“whoever forgets one word of his study, scripture accounts it to him as if he were mortally guilty”
— Mishnah, Pirkei Avot 3:8, trans. Joshua Kulp [7]
Rabbi Meir, basing himself on Deuteronomy 4:9, treats the careless forgetting of what one has learned as a matter of life and death, though he excuses forgetting that happens despite real effort.
Like the Kural, it treats forgetfulness as something that can kill.
Buddhism
“Ever grows the glory of him who is energetic, mindful and pure in conduct, discerning and self-controlled, righteous and heedful.”
— Dhammapada 24, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [8]
The Dhammapada ties a person's glory (yasa) directly to heedfulness and mindfulness.
It is the positive side of the Kural: heedfulness makes glory grow, and heedlessness kills it.
Jainism
“There are five causes which render wholesome discipline impossible: egoism, delusion, carelessness, illness, and idleness”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.3 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [9]
Carelessness (pramada) is named among the forces that destroy a monk's discipline.
Valluvar says forgetfulness kills a man's fame as poverty kills his intellect; the Jain text says carelessness kills the discipline on which a monk's worth rests.
Sikhism
“O Nanak, they do not forget the Naam from the mind; they are honored in the Court of the Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 85, Siree Raag (Siree Raag ki Vaar, salok), Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Guru Amar Das ties true honour to never letting the Name slip from the mind.
The Kural says forgetfulness kills a man's good name; Guru Amar Das states the converse, that unbroken remembrance is what wins honour.
Greek Mythology
Elpenor, the youngest of Odysseus's crew, is heavy with wine and falls asleep on Circe's roof. Woken by the noise of departure, he forgets to use the ladder, falls, and breaks his neck. In the underworld he begs Odysseus for a burial mound so that men to come will know of him.
— Homer, Odyssey 10.552-560 and 11.51-80 [11]
One moment of forgetfulness costs Elpenor his life and nearly his memory among men, which he has to plead for.
The Kural's point is that heedlessness kills a person's name, and Elpenor's fate shows it.
Philosophy
Seneca opens his letters to Lucilius by observing that some of our time is snatched away and some slips from us, but that the most disgraceful loss of all is the one caused by our own carelessness.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 1 (trans. R. M. Gummere) [12]
For Seneca the losses we cause through negligence are worse than those forced on us, because they were within our power to prevent.
The Kural likewise says heedlessness steadily wastes something precious, in this case a good name.

Real Life Example

Fred Merkle's base-running lapse
23 September 1908 · Polo Grounds, New York City, United States
In a game tied 1-1 between the New York Giants and the Chicago Cubs, the Giants appeared to score the winning run in the bottom of the ninth inning on a single by Al Bridwell. Fred Merkle, the 19-year-old runner on first base, headed off the field without touching second base as fans poured onto the field, the Cubs' Johnny Evers claimed a force-out at second, and the umpire ruled Merkle out, so the game ended in a tie. The two teams finished the season level, the Cubs won the replayed game and the National League pennant, and Merkle, though he went on to play in five World Series, was mocked for years with nicknames such as Bonehead.
— Sources: [13] [14]
One moment of inattention by a capable player became the thing he was remembered for, overshadowing the rest of a long career.
The kural says forgetfulness kills a person's good name, and one forgotten step to second base shaped Fred Merkle's reputation for the rest of his life.
