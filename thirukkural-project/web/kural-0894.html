Kural 894

Not Offending the Great · Periyaaraip Pizhaiyaamai · பெரியாரைப் பிழையாமை

கூற்றத்தைக் கையால் விளித்தற்றால் ஆற்றுவார்க்கு ஆற்றாதார் இன்னா செயல்.
Kootraththaik kaiyaal viliththatraal aatruvaarkku aatraadhaar innaa seyal.

Meaning

When weak people harm strong people, it is like waving to Death with their own hand to come and take them.

Elementary

A tiny mouse that pulls a lion’s tail is basically waving and calling, Come and get me! When someone weak picks a fight with someone much stronger, they are inviting their own trouble.

University

The image is vivid: kootram (கூற்றம், Death, Yama, the god who divides life from body) is summoned kaiyaal viLiththu (கையால் விளித்து, by beckoning with the hand). The comparison applies to aatraadhaar (ஆற்றாதார், those without power) doing innaa (இன்னா, harm) to aatruvaar (ஆற்றுவார், the powerful). Solomon Pappaiah specifies the three kinds of power, of wisdom, wealth and army, and adds that it is the weak who strike first; Mu. Varadarasanar stresses that Yama would come of himself anyway, so the weak only hasten it. The couplet carries forward kural 891’s key word aatruvaar, binding the chapter together. Text is the same in both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Sabha Parva, Shishupala heaps insults on Krishna at Yudhishthira’s Rajasuya sacrifice; after his hundredth offence Krishna’s discus slays him.
— Mahabharata, Sabha Parva (Sisupala-badha), trans. K. M. Ganguli [3]
A ruler who kept provoking a far greater power, despite warnings, called his own death upon himself.
Shishupala is the Kural’s weak man beckoning Death with his own hand.
Christianity
“Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand?”
— Luke 14:31 (KJV) [4]
Jesus points to the common sense of a king who weighs his strength before going to war against a stronger foe.
The Kural warns of what happens when the weaker side skips that reckoning and strikes anyway.
Islam
Surah al-Fil recalls how the army of the elephant marched to destroy the Kaaba and was struck down, made like eaten straw.
— Qur’an, Surah 105 (al-Fil) [5]
A force that attacked what God protected was destroyed despite its apparent might.
As in the Kural, assaulting a far greater power brings one’s own destruction.
Judaism
“The wrath of a king is as messengers of death: but a wise man will pacify it.”
— Proverbs 16:14 (KJV) [4]
The proverb says that provoking a ruler’s anger is like sending for death, and the wise avoid it.
This is nearly the Kural’s own image of summoning Death by provoking the powerful.
Buddhism
The Nidanakatha tells how Mara and his army assailed the Bodhisatta beneath the Bodhi tree with storms and weapons, and were utterly routed.
— Jataka Nidanakatha (trans. T. W. Rhys Davids, Buddhist Birth Stories) [6]
Even a host of attackers could not prevail against one of supreme spiritual strength.
It shows the futility of the weaker attacking the truly mighty that the Kural describes.
Jainism
“You may as well dig rocks with your nails, or eat iron with your teeth, or kick fire with your feet, as treat contemptuously a monk.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 12.26 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
Bhadrā lists three absurd, self-wounding acts to show how futile and harmful it is for ordinary people to scorn a monk of great spiritual power.
The Kural says that when the powerless harm the powerful they are beckoning Death with their own hand, and Bhadrā's picture of kicking fire is the same harm turned back on oneself.
Sikhism
“Slandering the Saints, one's life is cut short.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 279, Raag Gauree (Sukhmani), Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Arjan opens his catalogue of the slanderer's fate with the shortening of life itself.
The Kural says the weak who harm the strong are beckoning Death with their own hand, and Gurbani likewise links offending the holy with an early end.
Greek Mythology
The satyr Marsyas challenges Apollo to a musical contest, loses, and is flayed alive as punishment.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.382-400 (Marsyas) [9]
A lesser being who challenged a god paid for it with his life.
Marsyas beckoned his own doom by provoking a far stronger power, as the Kural warns.
Philosophy
“Hence, though an obstinate fight may be made by a small force, in the end it must be captured by the larger force.”
— Sun Tzu, The Art of War 3.10 (trans. Lionel Giles) [10]
Sun Tzu observes that a weaker force that insists on fighting a stronger one will in the end be overwhelmed.
This is the strategic version of the Kural’s warning that the weak who strike the strong invite their own end.

Real Life Example

The Anglo-Zanzibar War
27 August 1896 · Zanzibar Town, Zanzibar (now Tanzania)
When the Sultan of Zanzibar died on 25 August 1896, Khalid ibn Barghash seized the palace and proclaimed himself sultan against the wishes of Britain, which had declared a protectorate over Zanzibar. Britain issued an ultimatum to stand down by 9 am on 27 August, and when Khalid refused, British warships bombarded the palace. The war was over in under an hour, the shortest on record; Khalid fled to the German consulate and Britain installed its own candidate as sultan.
— Sources: [11] [12]
A ruler with a handful of guns defied the strongest navy in the world and lost everything in less than an hour.
The kural says that when the powerless provoke the powerful it is like beckoning Death with one's own hand, as Khalid's defiance of the Royal Navy shows.
