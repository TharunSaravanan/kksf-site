Kural 1210

Sad Memories · Ninaindhavar Pulampal · நினைந்தவர் புலம்பல்

விடாஅது சென்றாரைக் கண்ணினால் காணப் படாஅதி வாழி மதி.
Vitaaadhu sendraaraik kanninaal kaanap pataaadhi vaazhi madhi.

Meaning

Long live you, Moon! Please do not set, so my eyes can find him, the one who went away but never left my heart.

Elementary

When someone you love is far away, you might look up at the moon and think, ‘They can see the moon too!’ The lady in this poem asks the moon not to go down, so that she can keep looking, as if the moon could help her find him.

University

The heroine addresses the moon (mati) with a blessing (vāḻi, ‘may you live long’) and a plea: paṭāa-ti, ‘do not set’ (paṭu, ‘to go down, set’), so that she may see with her eyes (kaṇṇiṉāl kāṇa) him who left without leaving (viṭāatu ceṉṟār), that is, who went away but never left her heart. Parimelazhagar’s celebrated gloss explains that since the moon is looked at by both lovers, their eyes meet in it, so she ‘sees’ him at least by the measure of the eye. Manakkudavar and Parip Perumal read paṭāatu (‘the moon does not set’) and take it as a complaint: if the moon set, she could sleep and meet him in dreams; Parimelazhagar notes this reading and its sense. Kalingar is reported to have placed this couplet in chapter 127 (avarvayiṉ vitumpal) instead (all as summarised on kuralthiran.com). Pope renders: ‘Set not; so may’st thou prosper, moon! that eyes may see / My love who went away, but ever bides with me.’ The standard text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Kalidasa’s Meghaduta the exiled Yaksha, separated from his wife, sends a monsoon cloud as his messenger across India, and laments that he can find her likeness only in parts of nature, her face in the moon, her glances in the eyes of startled deer.
— Kalidasa, Meghaduta, Uttaramegha (paraphrase; cf. tr. Arthur W. Ryder) [4]
Kalidasa’s separated lover reaches the beloved through the sky and sees her in the moon.
Like the Kural’s heroine pleading with the moon, the Yaksha makes a heavenly body the go-between with his absent spouse.
Christianity
“Sun, stand thou still upon Gibeon; and thou, Moon, in the valley of Ajalon.”
— Joshua 10:12 (KJV) [5]
Joshua commands the sun and moon to stand still so the day of battle may be completed.
The heroine’s plea ‘do not set’ asks of the moon what Joshua asks, though for love’s sake rather than victory’s.
Islam
Abraham, watching the night sky, sees the moon rise and says ‘This is my lord’, but when it sets he says that unless his Lord guides him he will be among those who go astray, and turns to the One who never sets.
— Qur’an 6:77 (paraphrase; cf. Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an uses the setting moon to teach that only what never disappears deserves one’s heart.
The heroine begs the moon not to set; Abraham learns from its setting to seek a Beloved who never departs, a contrasting use of the same image.
Judaism
The Talmud teaches that whoever blesses the new moon in its proper time is as one who receives the face of the Divine Presence.
— Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 42a (paraphrase) [7]
In the rite of blessing the moon, looking at the moon becomes a way of greeting the unseen Beloved.
Parimelazhagar’s reading, that both lovers’ eyes meet in the moon, has a Jewish counterpart in the moon as a meeting place with an unseen Presence.
Buddhism
“Seek no intimacy with the beloved and also not with the unloved, for not to see the beloved and to see the unloved, both are painful.”
— Dhammapada 210 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [8]
The Dhammapada names not seeing the beloved as a basic form of suffering.
The heroine’s plea to the moon arises from the very pain the Dhammapada names, though the Buddha’s remedy is to loosen the attachment.
Jainism
Encamped by a lake at night, Prince Pavananjaya watches a female cakravaka bird crying in anguish through the night for her mate, and, realising how his neglected wife Anjana must have suffered, he secretly returns to her that same night.
— Hemachandra, Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra, Book 7, story of Anjana and Pavananjaya (paraphrase; cf. tr. Helen M. Johnson); also in Vimalasuri’s Paumacariya [9]
The Jain Ramayana uses the night-separated cakravaka birds to awaken a husband to the pain of separation.
The night scene of a pining bird and a husband moved to return matches the Kural’s moonlit night of longing for an absent spouse.
Sikhism
“If You are the moon, then I am the partridge in love with it.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 658, Raag Sorath, Bhagat Ravidas (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [10]
Bhagat Ravidas compares his devotion to the chakor, the partridge of Indian poetry that gazes at the moon all night.
The Kural's wife begs the moon not to set, so that her eyes may find the beloved who went away but never left her heart; Ravidas uses the same moonlit image of eyes fixed in longing on the Beloved.
Greek Mythology
Sappho tells Atthis that their friend, now far away in Lydia, outshines the Lydian women as the rosy-fingered moon after sunset surpasses all the stars, and that she wanders, remembering gentle Atthis with longing.
— Sappho, fragment 96 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Anne Carson) [11]
Sappho uses the moon to link the beloved far away with those who long for her at home.
As in the Kural, the moon becomes the shared light that binds separated lovers across distance.
Philosophy
Seneca thanks Lucilius for writing often, saying that if pictures of absent friends are pleasant, a letter is more so, for it brings real traces and real evidence of an absent friend.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 40.1 (paraphrase; cf. tr. R.M. Gummere) [12]
Seneca values any token that lets the absent friend be present in some measure.
The heroine seeks such a token in the moon, which both lovers can see and so ‘meet’ in, as Parimelazhagar explains.

Real Life Example

Su Shi's Mid-Autumn moon poem for his absent brother
Mid-Autumn Festival, 1076 · China
On the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival in 1076, after drinking until dawn, the poet Su Shi wrote a ci lyric to the tune Shuidiao Getou for his younger brother Su Zhe (Ziyou), from whom he had been separated for seven years. The poem speaks to the bright moon and ends with the wish that, though far apart, the two might share its beauty together.
— Sources: [13] [14]
The poem is still recited at every Mid-Autumn Festival as the classic expression of missing someone under the same moon.
The kural's heroine begs the moon not to set so that she may see the one who left yet never left her heart; Su Shi likewise turns to the moon as the link to a loved one far away.
