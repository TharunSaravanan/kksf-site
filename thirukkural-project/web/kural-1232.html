Kural 1232

Wasting Away · Uruppu Nalanazhithal · உறுப்புநலனழிதல்

நயந்தவர் நல்காமை சொல்லுவ போலும் பசந்து பனிவாரும் கண்.
Nayandhavar nalkaamai solluva poalum pasandhu panivaarum kan.

Meaning

My eyes have turned pale and keep filling with tears. They seem to tell everyone that the one I love is no longer kind to me.

Elementary

When you are sad, you might try to hide it, but your red, teary eyes give you away, and people can guess something is wrong. The girl in this verse feels her pale, wet eyes are telling the whole town that the person she loves is far away and not caring for her right now.

University

The heroine imagines her eyes as informers: pasantu (‘having turned sallow’, from pacappu/pacalai, the greenish-yellow pallor of love-sickness that Chapter 119 treats at length) and paṉi vārum (‘streaming with dew’, i.e. tears) they ‘seem to speak’ (colluva pōlum) of nayantavar nalkāmai, the ‘unkindness’ or ‘failure to bestow grace’ of the one she desired. Nalkāmai is the negative of nalku, ‘to show favour, to be gracious’, a word used of kings and gods as well as lovers. Mu. Varadarasanar and Puliyur Kesikan add that the eyes tell this ‘to others’ (piṟarkku), which makes the poem about exposure as much as grief: the body betrays what modesty (nāṇ) would conceal. Pope renders: ‘The eye, with sorrow wan, all wet with dew of tears, / As witness of the lover’s lack of love appears.’ The text is identical on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In Jayadeva’s Gita Govinda, Radha’s friend goes to Krishna and describes Radha’s state in his absence: she finds sandal paste and moonlight painful, she is wasted and tearful, and her whole condition testifies to his neglect.
— Jayadeva, Gita Govinda, Canto 4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Barbara Stoler Miller) [3]
The friend’s report makes Radha’s suffering body the evidence of Krishna’s absence.
In the Kural the heroine’s pale, tearful eyes play the friend’s part, announcing the lover’s unkindness to everyone.
Christianity
“Have mercy upon me, O LORD, for I am in trouble: mine eye is consumed with grief, yea, my soul and my belly.”
— Psalms 31:9 (KJV) [4]
The psalmist points to his worn-out eye as visible proof of his distress.
The Kural’s eyes likewise display inner grief openly, as if testifying to it.
Islam
In The Ring of the Dove, Ibn Hazm lists the outward signs by which love betrays itself, such as the lover’s fixed gaze, sleeplessness, sighing, and, when the beloved is absent or unkind, wasting and pallor that others can read.
— Ibn Hazm, The Ring of the Dove (Tawq al-Hamama), chapter on the signs of love (paraphrase; cf. tr. A.J. Arberry) [5]
The Andalusian scholar treats the lover’s body as a text that onlookers can read even when the lover keeps silent.
The Kural’s heroine fears exactly this: her pale, wet eyes are telling others what she would not say.
Judaism
“Mine eyes do fail with tears, mine inwards burn, my liver is poured upon the earth, for the breach of the daughter of my people.”
— Lamentations 2:11 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
The poet of Lamentations describes eyes exhausted by tears as the outward face of a heartbreak.
The Kural’s tear-streaming eyes are likewise the visible sign of an inner wound.
Buddhism
The Buddha asks which is greater, the water of the four oceans or the tears his monks have shed over the long round of births while weeping at union with what is displeasing and separation from what is pleasing; the tears, he says, are greater.
— Samyutta Nikaya 15.3, Assu Sutta (paraphrase; cf. tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The sutta makes separation from the dear the source of an ocean of tears.
The heroine’s streaming eyes are one small instance of the tears the Buddha says separation always brings.
Jainism
The Uttaradhyayana’s lecture on carelessness teaches that the eye’s attachment to pleasing colour and form leads to suffering: one who is passionately fond of colours comes to grief and is never satisfied.
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra, Lecture 32, verses on the eye and colour (paraphrase; cf. tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Jain teaching traces the misery of the senses to raga, attachment, beginning with the eye.
In the Kural the eye that once delighted in the beloved is now the very organ that suffers and shows the loss, which Jain doctrine would read as the fruit of attachment.
Sikhism
“The eyes of the soul-bride are filled with tears; I dwell upon Your Glorious Virtues. How can I meet my Beloved Lord God?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1111, Raag Tukhari, Barah Maha, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In his Barah Maha in Raag Tukhari, Guru Nanak's soul-bride weeps as she remembers her Lord's virtues and asks how she can meet Him.
The Kural's wife says her pale, streaming eyes tell everyone that her beloved has stopped being kind to her; Guru Nanak's bride also shows her separation through eyes filled with tears.
Greek Mythology
As Penelope listens to the disguised Odysseus tell of her husband, her tears flow down her cheeks like snow melting on the mountain-tops, so that her fair cheeks seem to dissolve as she weeps for the man who is sitting beside her.
— Homer, Odyssey 19.204-209 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer pictures a faithful wife’s face visibly wasting with tears for a long-absent husband.
Penelope’s melting cheeks, like the Kural’s streaming eyes, publish her grief for an absent husband before others.
Philosophy
Plutarch tells how the physician Erasistratus discovered that Prince Antiochus was wasting away from a hidden love for his stepmother Stratonice: whenever she entered, the prince showed Sappho’s symptoms, faltering speech, burning flushes, pallor and sudden faintness.
— Plutarch, Life of Demetrius 38 (paraphrase; cf. tr. Bernadotte Perrin) [11]
A Greek physician reads a secret love straight off a patient’s body.
The Kural’s heroine fears the same thing: her pallid, wet eyes tell onlookers what she has not said.

Real Life Example

Sappho describes the symptoms of longing in fragment 31
6th century BCE · Lesbos, Greece
In the poem now known as fragment 31, the Greek poet Sappho describes watching the person she loves talking with a man and lists what longing does to her body: her tongue breaks, a thin fire runs under her skin, sweat pours down, trembling seizes her and she is greener than grass. The ancient critic Longinus quoted the poem in his treatise On the Sublime as a model description of the symptoms of passion.
— Sources: [12] [13]
It is the most famous ancient description of love's effect on the body, and it survives because a later critic thought it a perfect example.
The kural's eyes, gone sallow and tearful, tell everyone what the heart would hide, and Sappho's poem shows the same thing: a body whose pallor and trembling announce the speaker's longing.
