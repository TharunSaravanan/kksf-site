Kural 649

Oratory · Solvanmai · சொல்வன்மை

பலசொல்லக் காமுறுவர் மன்றமா சற்ற சிலசொல்லல் தேற்றா தவர்.
Palasollak kaamuruvar mandramaa satra Silasollal thetraa thavar.

Meaning

People who do not know how to say a few words without faults are surely the ones who love to say many words.

Elementary

Some people talk and talk because they can’t find the right few words. Someone who knows exactly what to say can say it quickly and clearly, like a good arrow that hits the target in one shot.

University

The couplet sets pala (many) against cila (few), and māc aṟṟa (stainless, faultless) qualifies the few words. Kāmuṟuvar, “they desire”, turns wordiness into a craving. Maṉṟa is an emphatic particle, “certainly, surely”, which Mu. Varadarasanar renders as uṇmaiyāka. Tēṟṟātavar means those who are not able or not skilled: Parimelazhagar takes it as inability to express a matter in few faultless words, so wordiness is a symptom of lacking the skill, not a separate vice. Pope’s rendering (“Who have not skill ten faultless words to utter plain”) adds a number not in the Tamil. In the older edition the second line reads சிலசொல்ல றேற்றா தவர், where sandhi turns collal tēṟṟā into colla ṟēṟṟā.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Sanskrit poet Śrīharṣa defines eloquence (vāgmitā) as speech that is measured and substantial (mitaṃ ca sāraṃ ca), a line that became a common saying.
— Śrīharṣa, Naiṣadhīyacarita 9.8 [3]
The epic poem Naiṣadhīyacarita places true eloquence in brief speech full of substance, not in many words.
Few words full of substance is the Kural’s ideal, and its opposite is the lover of many words.
Christianity
“But when ye pray, use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking.”
— Matthew 6:7 (KJV) [4]
Jesus rejects the idea that more words make speech more effective.
Trusting in much speaking is the fault the Kural traces to lacking skill with a few good words.
Islam
“The Prophet (ﷺ) used to talk so clearly that if somebody wanted to count the number of his words, he could do so.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 3567-3568 [5]
Aisha describes the Prophet’s speech as measured and distinct, in contrast to someone who rushed through narrations.
Countable, deliberate words are the few faultless words the Kural praises.
Judaism
“all my days I grew up among the sages, and I have found nothing better for a person than silence. Study is not the most important thing, but actions; whoever indulges in too many words brings about sin.”
— Pirkei Avot 1:17, trans. Joshua Kulp [6]
Shimon son of Rabban Gamaliel warns that many words lead to fault.
Too many words bringing sin is the reverse of the Kural’s few flawless words.
Buddhism
“Better than a thousand useless words is one useful word, hearing which one attains peace.”
— Dhammapada 100, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita [7]
The Dhammapada values one meaningful word above a flood of empty ones.
One useful word against a thousand useless ones is exactly the Kural’s few against many.
Jainism
“One should always be meek, and not be talkative in the presence of the wise; one should acquire valuable knowledge, and avoid what is worthless.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 1.8 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The opening lecture on discipline warns the pupil against talkativeness and links it to the worthless, as opposed to valuable knowledge.
Valluvar says those who love to say many words are those unable to say a few faultless ones; the Jain verse likewise sets idle talk against what has real worth.
Sikhism
“To speak too much and babble is useless.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 661, Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak dismisses much talking as empty babble, since God knows everything without being told.
The Kural says those who cannot speak a few flawless words are the very ones who love to speak many; Guru Nanak likewise rejects wordiness as worthless.
Greek Mythology
“the speech of Menelaus ran trippingly on the tongue; he did not say much, for he was a man of few words, but he spoke very clearly and to the point”
— Homer, Iliad 3.213-215, trans. Samuel Butler [10]
Antenor remembers Menelaus as a speaker who used few words but made each count.
Menelaus is the Kural’s master of few, faultless words.
Philosophy
Pascal ends one of his Provincial Letters by apologising that he made it longer than usual only because he had not had the time to make it shorter.
— Blaise Pascal, Provincial Letters, Letter 16 (1656) [11]
Pascal’s joke makes the point that brevity takes more skill and effort than length.
Length as the fallback of one without time or skill is exactly the Kural’s diagnosis.

Real Life Example

Lincoln's two-minute Gettysburg Address follows a two-hour oration
19 November 1863 · Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, United States
At the dedication of the national cemetery at Gettysburg, the main address was a two-hour oration of some 13,000 words by Edward Everett, the best-known orator of the day. President Abraham Lincoln then spoke for barely more than two minutes, delivering 272 words. The next day Everett wrote to Lincoln that he wished he had come as near to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as Lincoln had in two minutes.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Lincoln's brief, carefully chosen words became one of the most celebrated speeches in history, while the far longer oration is now largely forgotten.
The kural says those who cannot say a few flawless words are the ones who love to say many, and the contrast at Gettysburg, acknowledged by Everett himself, shows the power of a few faultless words.
