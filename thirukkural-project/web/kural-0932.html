Kural 932

Gaming (Gambling) · Soodhu · சூது

ஒன்றெய்தி நூறிழக்கும் சூதர்க்கும் உண்டாங்கொல் நன்றெய்தி வாழ்வதோர் ஆறு.
Ondreydhi noorizhakkum soodharkkum undaangol nandreydhi vaazhvadhor aaru.

Meaning

Gamblers win one and lose a hundred. Can such people ever find a way to live a good life?

Elementary

Imagine trading a hundred of your marbles for the chance to win one. You would soon have none left. Valluvar asks: if someone keeps doing that, how could life ever go well for them?

University

The couplet works through a stark ratio, ondreydhi noorizhakkum (ஒன்றெய்தி நூறிழக்கும், 'gaining one, losing a hundred'), and then a rhetorical question ending in -kol (உண்டாங்கொல், 'will there be?'), which expects the answer no. Soodhar (சூதர்) means gamblers; aaru (ஆறு) is 'way, path', so nandreydhi vaazhvadhor aaru is 'a path of living that attains good'. The particle -um in soodharkkum ('even for gamblers') implies that other sinners may still find such a path, but gamblers, as a class, are shut out. Puliyur Kesikan on thirukkural.net softens the arithmetic to 'losing by hundreds out of the hope of gain', and Kalaignar reads it psychologically: a single win lures the player on into a hundred losses. Both base-text sites agree on the wording.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“As of a costly horse grown old and feeble, I find not any profit of the gamester.”
— Rig Veda 10.34.3, trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith [3]
The repentant gambler of the Rig Veda admits his habit has yielded no profit, only decline.
Both say the gambler's way of life never arrives at any good.
Christianity
“But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition.”
— 1 Timothy 6:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that a craving to get rich leads people into traps that end in ruin.
The Kural's gambler, chasing one gain, is the person who falls into this snare.
Islam
“...In them is great sin and [yet, some] benefit for people. But their sin is greater than their benefit.”
— Qur'an 2:219 (Saheeh International) [5]
The Qur'an sets the small benefit of gambling against its much larger harm.
This is the Kural's one-gained-hundred-lost reckoning in moral terms.
Judaism
“Wealth may dwindle to less than nothing, / But one who gathers little by little increases it.”
— Proverbs 13:11 (JPS Tanakh, Gender-Sensitive Edition, 2023, on Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs contrasts wealth that vanishes with wealth built slowly through steady effort.
The gambler's path leads to nothing, while the patient path the Kural implies leads to a good life.
Buddhism
“There are, young householder, these six evil consequences in indulging in gambling: (i) the winner begets hate, (ii) the loser grieves for lost wealth, (iii) loss of wealth...”
— Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), trans. Narada Thera [7]
The Buddha lists what gambling brings: hatred if you win, grief and loss if you lose.
Like the Kural, it shows that neither winning nor losing gives the gambler a good life.
Jainism
“As a man for the sake of one Kâkinî (risks and) loses a thousand (Kârshâpaṇas), or as the king lost his kingdom (and life) by eating a mango-fruit which he was strictly forbidden (by his physician)”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 7.11 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Mahāvīra compares the fool who trades lasting goods for petty pleasures to a man who loses a thousand coins for the sake of a single tiny one.
Valluvar says gamblers gain one and lose a hundred; the Uttarādhyayana's man who stakes and loses a thousand coins for one kākiṇī expresses the same ruinous arithmetic.
Sikhism
“I have seen the world to be such a gambler; all beg for peace, but they forget the Naam, the Name of the Lord. ... People beg for peace, but they receive severe pain.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 222, Raag Gauree Gwaarayree, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak looks at the whole world and sees a gambler: everyone stakes their life on winning happiness, forgets the Name that actually gives it, and ends up with far more pain than they began with.
The Kural’s gambler gains one and loses a hundred and never reaches anything good, and Guru Nanak’s gambling world asks for peace and receives great pain.
Greek Mythology
Patroclus, as a boy, killed a playmate in a quarrel over dice and had to leave his home for exile at the house of Peleus.
— Homer, Iliad 23 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
One small game cost Patroclus his home and set the course of his life.
A tiny stake leading to a huge loss echoes the Kural's one gained, a hundred lost.
Philosophy
“Both, then, since they are willing to make gain from wrong sources, are sordid lovers of gain...”
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.1 (trans. W. D. Ross) [11]
Aristotle says the gambler and the robber both look for gain where it should not be sought.
The Kural asks whether such a source of gain can ever lead to a good life, and Aristotle answers no.

Real Life Example

Lord Holland agrees to pay the gambling debts of his son Charles James Fox
November 1773 · London, England
Charles James Fox, the brilliant young member of Parliament and later Britain's first foreign secretary, became notorious in his early twenties for reckless gambling, which caused his parents great unhappiness; within a month of reaching Oxford he had written to his father that he hoped never to play again but could not promise it. In November 1773 his father, Lord Holland, agreed to pay his son's debts up to one hundred thousand pounds, and an Eton College account records that on one occasion his father paid debts of 140,000 pounds. Even after a year in which he won enormously, Fox was soon in debt again, and in 1781 bailiffs were in his house while he was running a faro bank at Brooks's club.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Fox's occasional large wins never changed the overall result: his gambling consumed sums his family and friends repeatedly had to make good.
The kural says gamblers gain one and lose a hundred, and Fox's winning seasons were swallowed by losses so large that his father had to pay debts of over a hundred thousand pounds.
