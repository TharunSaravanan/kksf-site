Kural 712

The Knowledge of the Council Chamber · Avaiyarithal · அவையறிதல்

இடைதெரிந்து நன்குணர்ந்து சொல்லுக சொல்லின் நடைதெரிந்த நன்மை யவர்.
Idaitherindhu nankunarndhu solluka sollin Nadaitherindha nanmai yavar.

Meaning

Good people who know how words work should choose the right moment, understand the subject fully, and only then speak.

Elementary

If you want to ask your mom for something, you don’t ask while she is rushing to catch a bus. You wait for a good moment and think it through first. Wise speakers wait for the right time too.

University

Where 711 stressed the audience, this kural stresses the moment: idai (interval, opening) is glossed by Parimelazhagar and Mu. Varadarasanar as the court’s sevvi, its receptive moment, when the hearers are ready and willing to listen. Nankunarndhu (having understood well) requires full command of the matter, and Solomon Pappaiah adds that the speaker must avoid faults of both word and sense (sorkutram, porutkutram). Sollin nadai (the gait or course of words) refers to how each of the three kinds of word conveys its meaning, which parallels sollin thokai in 711. The parallel structure of 711 and 712 (sollin thokaiyarindha thooimaiyavar / sollin nadaitherindha nanmaiyavar) presents purity and goodness as twin moral qualities of the ideal counsellor. The text is the same in all sources consulted, apart from the sandhi joining (“சொல்லி னடைதெரிந்த”).

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Austerity of speech consists in speaking truthfully and beneficially and in avoiding speech that offends.”
— Bhagavad Gita 17.15 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [4]
Krishna counts disciplined speech, meaning speech that is true, helpful, and does not upset the listener, as a form of spiritual austerity (tapas).
The Kural’s speaker, who waits for the right opening and speaks only what is fully understood, is practising this austerity of speech.
Christianity
“Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.”
— Ephesians 4:29 (KJV) [5]
Paul asks that speech be measured by its effect on the hearers: it should build them up and do them good.
Like the Kural, it keeps the listeners’ good in view when deciding what to say.
Islam
“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best.”
— Qur’an 16:125 (Saheeh International) [6]
The Qur’an tells the Prophet to call people with wisdom (hikmah) and fine instruction. Commentators understand hikmah here as fitting the message to the hearers and the occasion.
Speaking “with wisdom” and “in a way that is best” is the Kural’s discerning, well-timed speech.
Judaism
“A time to rend, and a time to sew; a time to keep silence, and a time to speak.”
— Ecclesiastes 3:7 (KJV) [5]
Koheleth teaches that every action has its proper season, including speech and silence.
The Kural’s idai, the right opening for speech, is Koheleth’s “time to speak”.
Buddhism
“In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, and endearing & agreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them.”
— Abhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 58 (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu) [7]
The Buddha tells Prince Abhaya that he says only what is true and beneficial, and that even then he chooses the proper time to say it.
“A sense of the proper time” is the Kural’s idaitherindhu, knowing the moment.
Jainism
“To give way to: anger, pride, deceit and greed, laughter, fear, loquacity and slander; these eight faults should a well-disciplined monk avoid; he should use blameless and concise speech at the proper time.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 24.9–10 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
Bhāṣā-samiti, carefulness in speech, is one of the five samitis every Jain monk observes. It requires speech that is free of passion, sparing, and timed correctly.
The Kural's call to choose the right moment and speak only after understanding fully matches the Jain rule of 'blameless and concise speech at the proper time'.
Sikhism
“First, examine the merchandise, and then, make the deal.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1410, Salok Vaaran Thay Vadheek, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
In a trader's maxim, Guru Nanak says one must first understand what one is dealing with before committing to the transaction.
The Kural says the speaker should understand the matter fully before speaking; Guru Nanak gives the same order: understand first, then act.
Greek Mythology
Shipwrecked and naked, Odysseus meets the princess Nausicaa. He weighs whether to clasp her knees as a suppliant or to plead from a distance, and decides that gentle words spoken from where he stands suit the moment best.
— Homer, Odyssey 6.141-148 (trans. Samuel Butler) [10]
Homer stops the action to show Odysseus judging what kind of approach the situation calls for before he says a word.
Odysseus reads the moment before speaking, as the Kural requires.
Philosophy
“They would be sure to ask him whether he knew ‘to whom’ he would give his medicines, and ‘when,’ and ‘how much.’”
— Plato, Phaedrus 268a-b (trans. Benjamin Jowett) [11]
Socrates argues that anyone who only knows speech techniques is like a man who knows drugs but does not know which patient to give them to, when, or in what dose. Real rhetoric, like real medicine, requires that knowledge.
“To whom” and “when” are the Kural’s two demands: know the assembly and know the moment.

Real Life Example

Nehru's Tryst with Destiny speech at the midnight of independence
14-15 August 1947 · Constitution Hall, New Delhi, India
The Constituent Assembly of India met at eleven at night on 14 August 1947, and Jawaharlal Nehru moved a resolution that its members take a pledge of service to India after the last stroke of midnight. Introducing it, he said: At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom. As the clock struck twelve, the President and members stood and took the pledge.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Nehru timed his words to the exact moment of independence, so that the speech and the event it described became one.
The kural tells good speakers to pick the right moment before they speak, and Nehru delivered his most famous words at the very hour they were meant for.
