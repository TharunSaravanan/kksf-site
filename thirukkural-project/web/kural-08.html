Kural 8

The Praise of God · Kadavul Vaazhthu · கடவுள் வாழ்த்து

அறவாழி அந்தணன் தாள்சேர்ந்தார்க் கல்லால் பிறவாழி நீந்தல் அரிது.
Aravaazhi andhanan thaal serndhaark kallaal, piRa vaazhi neendhal aridhu.

Meaning

Except for those who cling to the feet of the gracious One who is like an ocean of virtue, it is hard for anyone to swim across the other ocean — the ocean of worldly existence.

Elementary

Picture two huge oceans: one is how good God is, and the other is all of life’s ups and downs. This kural says the only way to cross the hard ocean is to hold onto the good one.

University

“Aravaazhi” (“ocean of dharma/virtue”) figures the divine as boundless moral reality, an ocean whose scale matches the second ocean it lets one cross — “piravaazhi,” the vast ocean of worldly existence one must traverse. The parallel construction is a deliberate rhetorical mirroring, reinforcing that only something of equal or greater vastness than the ocean of worldly existence can carry a being across it.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Even if you are considered to be the most sinful of all sinners, when you are situated in the boat of transcendental knowledge, you will be able to cross over the ocean of miseries.”
— Bhagavad Gita 4.36 [3]
Krishna says that no matter how sinful someone has been, real spiritual knowledge works like a boat sturdy enough to carry them across the entire sea of suffering.
The Kural’s exact image — an ocean that can only be crossed by boat/aid — appears here almost identically.
Christianity
“I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me.”
— John 14:6 (KJV) [4]
Jesus states plainly that he is the sole route to God the Father — not one option among several, but the way itself.
Matches the Kural’s exclusivity claim: only one path gets you across, and it runs through the divine specifically.
Islam
“Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a traveler.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6416 [5]
The Prophet Muhammad frames the whole of worldly life as a temporary journey through unfamiliar territory, not a place to settle down in.
Shares the Kural’s underlying picture of life as something to be traveled through, though it's a general life-orientation teaching rather than a specific claim that only God enables the crossing.
Judaism
At the Red Sea, escape is physically impossible until God parts the water himself — the Israelites don’t build a way across, God makes one for them.
— Exodus 14:21-22 (Tanakh, Torah) [4]
The sea only becomes crossable through direct divine action, not human effort or ingenuity.
A literal, historical version of the Kural’s image: an impassable sea made passable only by direct divine action.
Buddhism
The Buddha compares his own teaching to a raft — genuinely necessary to cross the water, but meant to be let go of once you've reached the other side.
— Majjhima Nikaya 22 (Alagaddupama Sutta) [6]
The Dharma gets you across, but it isn’t the destination — you’re meant to release it once it’s done its job.
Shares the Kural’s crossing-the-water structure, though it's the teaching/practice itself that's the vessel here, not devotion to a personal God.
Greek Mythology
The dead cannot cross into the underworld on their own — they need Charon the ferryman, and even he requires payment before he’ll take them.
— Greek mythological tradition (Charon and the Styx) [7]
Crossing over isn’t something you can do by yourself; it requires both a specific helper and the right toll.
The same basic claim as the Kural: crossing over requires help from something/someone beyond yourself, not just willpower.
Philosophy
“Let us flee then to the beloved Fatherland... The Fatherland to us is There whence we have come, and There is the Father.”
— Plotinus, Enneads, I.6 [8]
Plotinus urges that the soul's true home isn't reachable by boat or road — it requires an inward turn back toward its divine source.
A philosophical version of the Kural’s ocean — the real crossing isn't geographic, it's a return to the source.
