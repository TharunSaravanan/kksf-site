Kural 400

Learning · Kalvi · கல்வி

கேடில் விழுச்செல்வம் கல்வி யொருவற்கு மாடல்ல மற்றை யவை.
Kedil vizhuchchelvam kalvi oruvarku Maadalla matraiyavai.

Meaning

Learning is the one great treasure that can never be destroyed. Compared with it, all the other things people own are not real treasure at all.

Elementary

Toys can break, money can be lost, and a house can burn down. What you have learned stays in your head, and no one can steal it. That is why Valluvar calls learning the best treasure of all.

University

The chapter ends with its thesis. Learning is கேடில் விழுச்செல்வம், 'imperishable, excellent wealth', and all other things are மாடு அல்ல, 'not wealth'. மாடு (maadu) is an old Tamil word for wealth that originally meant cattle, the earliest form of property, which gives the claim an archaic force. The argument works on two levels. Materially, learning cannot be stolen, taxed, destroyed or divided. Evaluatively, only learning deserves the name 'wealth' at all. Placing this conclusion in the Porul (Wealth) section is pointed, since Valluvar redefines the very wealth that section is about. The same claim appears almost word for word in the Sanskrit verse on vidyā-dhana, the wealth of knowledge that thieves cannot steal and kings cannot seize.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A famous Sanskrit subhashita says: “na cora-hāryaṃ na ca rāja-hāryaṃ na bhrātṛ-bhājyaṃ na ca bhāra-kāri; vyaye kṛte vardhata eva nityaṃ vidyā-dhanaṃ sarva-dhana-pradhānam.” Thieves cannot steal it, kings cannot seize it, brothers cannot divide it, and it is no burden to carry. When spent, it only grows. The wealth of knowledge is the foremost of all wealth.
— Traditional Sanskrit subhashita on vidyā-dhana (widely cited; exact textual origin uncertain) [3]
The verse lists every way ordinary wealth can be lost and shows that none of them applies to learning.
An almost exact parallel: learning is imperishable wealth, and it is the chief of all wealth.
Christianity
“How much better is it to get wisdom than gold! and to get understanding rather to be chosen than silver!”
— Proverbs 16:16 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs ranks wisdom and understanding far above precious metals as the truly valuable possession.
A strong match: learning outranks material wealth.
Islam
“The learned are the heirs of the Prophets, and the Prophets leave neither dinar nor dirham, leaving only knowledge, and he who takes it takes an abundant portion.”
— Sunan Abi Dawud 3641 (graded sahih by al-Albani) [5]
The Prophet teaches that the real inheritance of the prophets is knowledge, not money, and that whoever gains it has received a rich share.
An excellent match: knowledge is the true wealth, and coins are not the inheritance worth having.
Judaism
Midrash Tanchuma tells of a scholar on a ship full of merchants. They mock him for carrying no goods, and he says his merchandise is greater than theirs. Pirates seize everything the merchants own. When they land, the destitute merchants are ignored, while the scholar is welcomed and honoured by the townspeople for his learning.
— Midrash Tanchuma, Terumah 1 (on Proverbs 4:2) [6]
The story shows in narrative form that learning is the one possession pirates cannot take and the one that provides for its owner anywhere.
A perfect narrative parallel: all other wealth disappears, and learning alone remains indestructible and valuable.
Buddhism
The Nidhikanda Sutta contrasts treasure buried in the ground, which can be lost, stolen or forgotten, with a 'treasure' of generosity, virtue, restraint and self-control. That inner treasure goes with its owner, cannot be taken by others, and cannot be stolen by thieves.
— Nidhikanda Sutta, Khuddakapatha 8 [7]
The Buddha redefines real treasure as inner qualities that no loss can touch.
Like the Kural, it declares ordinary wealth perishable and inner wealth imperishable. Its treasure is merit and virtue rather than learning as such.
Jainism
“As the ocean of inexhaustible water, the delight of Svayambhû, which is full of precious things of many kinds, (has no equal), neither has a very learned monk.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 11.30 (cf. 11.26) (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana ends its praise of the learned by comparing them to an inexhaustible ocean full of precious things. An earlier verse in the same lecture (11.26) compares them to 'a well-guarded storehouse … filled with grain of many kinds'.
Valluvar calls learning the one imperishable wealth, beside which all else is not wealth. The Jain images of an inexhaustible, treasure-filled ocean and a well-guarded storehouse present learning as unequalled and undiminishing riches in the same way.
Sikhism
“The Siblings of Destiny meet together, and eat and spend, but these resources do not diminish; they continue to increase.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 186, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan speaks of the spiritual treasure of the Name: however much those who gather in holy company use and share it, it never runs out and only grows.
The Kural calls learning the one imperishable wealth beside which nothing else is wealth; Guru Arjan describes a spiritual treasure with the same quality of never being used up.
Greek Mythology
King Midas asks Dionysus that everything he touches should turn to gold. When even his food and drink turn to metal, he begs to be freed from the gift.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85–145 [10]
The myth shows that material wealth, however great, cannot sustain life and may even destroy it.
It supports the negative half of the Kural: other possessions are not true wealth. It says nothing directly about learning.
Philosophy
Seneca tells of the philosopher Stilpo. His city was captured and he lost his wife, children and property, yet when the conqueror asked whether he had lost anything, he answered that all his goods were with him, meaning his wisdom and virtue.
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 9.18–19 [11]
For the Stoics, the only true possessions are those of the mind, which no conqueror can take.
An excellent match: everything else can be lost, but the wealth of the mind is imperishable and alone deserves to be called wealth.

Real Life Example

China's universities carry their learning to Kunming in wartime
1937-1946 · Kunming, Yunnan, China
After the outbreak of war with Japan in 1937, Peking University, Tsinghua University and Nankai University left their campuses in the north, regrouped first in Changsha and then moved to Kunming in the far southwest. There they operated together as the National Southwest Associated University in poor, makeshift buildings until 1946. Among its students were Chen-Ning Yang and Tsung-Dao Lee, who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1957.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The universities lost their buildings, libraries and homes, yet the knowledge of their teachers and students survived and produced some of the century's great scientists.
The kural calls learning the one wealth that cannot be destroyed, and the wartime university showed that when everything else was left behind, learning travelled on intact.
