Kural 778

Military Spirit · Padaiccherukku · படைச்செருக்கு

உறின்உயிர் அஞ்சா மறவர் இறைவன் செறினும் சீர்குன்றல் இலர்.
Urin uyir anjaa maravar iraivan Serinum seerkundral ilar.

Meaning

Soldiers who do not fear for their lives when war comes do not lose their eagerness to fight, even if the king angrily stops them.

Elementary

A team captain may tell an eager player to sit out this game. A truly dedicated player doesn’t sulk or lose heart; they stay ready and keen. The verse says the best soldiers keep their spirit high even when told to wait.

University

Urin (“if it comes,” i.e., if battle arises) uyir anjaa maravar (warriors who do not fear for life) remain seer kundral ilar (without loss of excellence or ardour) even if iraivan serinum, “the king is angry,” which the traditional commentary reads as the king forbidding them to fight. Maravar derives from maram, martial valour, and is the classic Tamil term for the warrior class. Solomon Pappaiah and Puliyur Kesikan read it as the king restraining them; Kalaignar reads it as the king’s displeasure not diminishing their sense of duty. The couplet thus praises discipline without loss of spirit: zeal survives even the leader’s rebuke.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“If you do not act according to My direction and do not fight, then you will be falsely directed. By your nature, you will have to be engaged in warfare.”
— Bhagavad Gita 18.59 (trans. A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada) [3]
Krishna tells Arjuna that his warrior nature will drive him to fight even if he resolves otherwise.
The Kural’s maravar likewise keep their martial spirit even when held back; it is part of their nature.
Christianity
“Then Peter and the other apostles answered and said, We ought to obey God rather than men.”
— Acts 5:29 (KJV) [4]
Ordered by the authorities to stop preaching, the apostles refuse to let the ban dampen their mission.
Their zeal undimmed by a ruler’s prohibition parallels the Kural’s warriors, though the apostles’ cause is non-violent.
Islam
“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Allah does not like transgressors.”
— Qur’an 2:190 (trans. Mustafa Khattab, The Clear Quran, via Quran.com) [5]
The Qur’an sets limits even on legitimate fighting.
The Kural praises warriors whose zeal survives restraint; the Qur’an insists that zeal must operate within restraint, a complementary emphasis.
Judaism
“The Ark and Israel and Judah are located at Succoth, and my master Joab and Your Majesty’s men are camped in the open; how can I go home and eat and drink … ? As you live, by your very life, I will not do this!”
— 2 Samuel 11:11 (JPS Tanakh, Revised 2023, via Sefaria) [6]
King David repeatedly urges the soldier Uriah to leave the front and rest at home; Uriah refuses out of solidarity with his comrades in the field.
Uriah’s soldierly spirit does not slacken even when the king himself tries to turn him aside, as the Kural describes.
Buddhism
“A tamed elephant is led into a crowd, and the king mounts a tamed elephant. Best among men is the subdued one who endures abuse.”
— Dhammapada 321 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The trained royal elephant is the Dhammapada’s image of power held in discipline.
The Kural’s warriors, keen yet obedient to the king’s check, resemble the tamed war elephant; Buddhism prizes the taming over the fighting.
Jainism
“He who always acknowledges his allegiance to his teacher, who has religious zeal and ardour for study, who is kind in words and actions, deserves to be instructed.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 11.14 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [8]
The ideal disciple combines steady loyalty to his teacher with zeal of his own that does not fade.
The Kural's warriors keep their ardour even when the king angrily holds them back, and the Jain disciple likewise joins obedience to his superior with undimmed zeal.
Sikhism
“If my body were afflicted with pain, under the evil influence of unlucky stars; and if the blood-sucking kings were to hold power over me - even if this were my condition, I would still worship and adore You, and my longing to chant Your Praises would not decrease.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 142, Raag Maajh (Vaar Maajh, salok), Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak declares that no suffering and no oppressive ruler could lessen his eagerness to praise God.
The Kural's warriors lose none of their fervour even when the king angrily holds them back; Guru Nanak's longing likewise does not diminish under hostile kings.
Greek Mythology
Insulted by Agamemnon, who takes his prize Briseis, Achilles withdraws from the fighting and refuses to return despite the Greeks’ losses.
— Homer, Iliad, Books 1 and 9 (paraphrase) [10]
The Iliad’s central plot turns on a great warrior whose zeal collapses under his king’s offence.
Achilles is the counter-example to the Kural: a hero whose ardour does fail when the king angers him.
Philosophy
Socrates tells the jury that, just as he held his post at Potidaea, Amphipolis and Delium where his commanders placed him, he will not abandon the post the god assigned him, even if ordered to stop philosophising.
— Plato, Apology 28d-29d (paraphrase) [11]
Socrates uses his soldier’s steadfastness as the model for keeping his calling under pressure from authority.
Like the Kural’s warriors, Socrates keeps his spirit undiminished even when those in power are angry with him.

Real Life Example

No. 303 Polish Squadron's first victory while still held back from combat
30 August 1940 · RAF Northolt and the skies over Hertfordshire, England, United Kingdom
No. 303 Squadron, formed of Polish airmen at RAF Northolt in August 1940, had not yet been declared operational and was flying a training exercise when Pilot Officer Ludwik Paszkiewicz saw enemy aircraft, broke formation and shot down a German Messerschmitt Bf 110. On landing he was reprimanded by his British commander for indiscipline and then congratulated for his success. The squadron was declared operational the next day and became the most successful Fighter Command unit in the Battle of Britain.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Kept out of the fight by those in authority, the Polish pilots lost none of their eagerness to defend the country that had taken them in.
The kural says warriors who do not fear for their lives keep their fervour even when their king holds them back, and the Polish airmen's zeal survived both official doubt and a reprimand.
