Kural 1219

The Visions of the Night · Kanavunilaiyuraithal · கனவுநிலையுரைத்தல்

நனவினால் நல்காரை நோவர் கனவினால் காதலர்க் காணா தவர்.
Nanavinaal nalkaarai noavar kanavinaal kaadhalark kaanaa thavar.

Meaning

Only women who never see their loved ones in dreams will complain about men who are not kind to them while they are awake.

Elementary

Suppose your friend moved away, but you write letters every week. Someone who has never had a pen pal might say, ‘Your friend forgot you!’ But you know better, because you hear from them all the time. This woman says the same about her dreams: people who don’t have such dreams just don’t understand.

University

The heroine turns outward to defend her husband against criticism. Nōvar, ‘they grieve over / reproach’, has as subject kaṉaviṉāl kātalark kāṇātavar, ‘those who do not see their lovers through dreams’; nalkārai repeats the chapter’s epithet for the man who ‘shows no grace’. The implication is that her own dreams vindicate him, since he visits her every night. Commentators differ on whom she means: Varadarasanar and Kalaignar make it general (‘women who do not see their lovers in dreams’), while Pappaiah specifies the young women of the town who are unmarried or have never known separation and so blame her husband as loveless. Pope: ‘In dreams who ne’er their lover’s form perceive, / For those in waking hours who show no love will grieve.’ Text identical on both sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Uddhava, sent by Krishna to console the gopis with philosophy, is humbled by the depth of their love for the absent Krishna and comes to wish he could be even a creeper in Vrindavan touched by the dust of their feet.
— Bhagavata Purana 10.47 (paraphrase) [3]
The Bhagavata Purana shows an outsider, learned but untouched by such love, misjudging the gopis until he sees their devotion from within.
Like the critics in the Kural, Uddhava first speaks without knowing the inner experience of the lovers; once he sees it, his view reverses.
Christianity
“It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep.”
— Psalms 127:2 (KJV) [4]
The psalm describes sleep itself as a gift given to those who are beloved.
The Kural’s heroine sees her dream-visits as such a gift; those who lack it are left with ‘the bread of sorrows’ and complaint.
Islam
“They said, ‘By Allah, you will not cease remembering Joseph until you become fatally ill or become of those who perish.’”
— Qur’an 12:85 (Saheeh International) [5]
Jacob’s sons, who do not share his bond with Joseph, chide him for his unceasing longing.
Like the critics in the Kural, Jacob’s sons judge a love they do not share and cannot see from inside.
Judaism
Rabbi Zeira says that anyone who sleeps seven days without a dream is called evil, since it suggests that God does not wish to appear to him even in that indirect way.
— Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 55b (William Davidson Edition, via Sefaria) [6]
The Talmud treats an absence of dreams as a sign of estrangement from the divine.
The Kural similarly marks those who do not dream of their beloved as the ones who lack the bond, and who therefore misjudge.
Buddhism
Kisa Gotami, mad with grief, carries her dead child from house to house seeking medicine; told to fetch mustard seed from a house where no one has died, she finds none and comes to understand loss from within.
— Dhammapada Commentary on verse 114 / Therigatha commentary, Kisa Gotami (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.W. Burlingame) [7]
The Buddhist story shows that understanding of another’s sorrow comes only from shared experience.
The Kural’s critics blame her husband because they have no share in her dream-experience; Kisa Gotami learns compassion only when she sees her grief is common to all.
Jainism
“Seeing that these fourteen illustrious, beautiful, lucky, blest, auspicious, fortunate, great dreams were taken from her by the Kshatriyani Trisala, she awoke.”
— Kalpa Sutra, Life of Mahavira §31 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 22) [8]
The Brahmani Devananda, from whose womb the embryo of Mahavira is transferred, wakes to find her auspicious dreams have passed to another woman.
Devananda is the Jain picture of one who has lost her blessed dreams; the Kural notes that it is those without such dreams who grieve and complain.
Sikhism
“Even in her dreams, she does not meet her Husband Lord.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 127, Raag Maajh, Guru Amar Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Amar Das describes the self-willed soul-bride who is so far from God that she does not meet Him even in her dreams.
The Kural's wife says only women who never see their lovers in dreams blame men who stay away; Guru Amar Das likewise singles out the bride who does not meet her Husband even in dreams as the truly forsaken one.
Greek Mythology
The suitor Agelaus tells Telemachus that while there was hope of Odysseus returning, Penelope’s waiting was understandable, but that it is now clear he will never come home, and she should marry the best of the suitors.
— Homer, Odyssey 20 (paraphrase of the Samuel Butler translation) [10]
Homer gives voice to outsiders who, not sharing Penelope’s hope, declare the absent husband gone for good.
Agelaus is the Kural’s critic in action, judging the absent husband lost because he cannot see what keeps the wife’s faith alive.
Philosophy
Adam Smith argues that we have no immediate experience of what others feel and can form an idea of it only by imagining ourselves in their situation.
— Adam Smith, The Theory of Moral Sentiments I.i.1 (paraphrase) [11]
For Smith, sympathy depends on imaginatively entering another’s circumstances.
The Kural’s critics, never having been visited by a lover in dreams, cannot enter the heroine’s situation, and so they misjudge her husband.

Real Life Example

Tchaikovsky and Nadezhda von Meck: a bond without meeting
1877–1890 · Russia
Nadezhda von Meck, the widow of a railway magnate, contacted Tchaikovsky in 1876 and from 1877 paid him an allowance of 6,000 rubles a year, which let him leave teaching at the Moscow Conservatory and compose full time. They exchanged more than 1,200 letters, but she insisted they never meet, and apart from two accidental encounters they never did. In 1890 she abruptly ended the allowance and the correspondence.
— Sources: [12] [13]
For thirteen years an intense, trusting relationship existed entirely at a distance, without the two ever sitting together.
The kural's heroine says only those who never see their lovers in dreams blame them for not coming in waking life; von Meck sustained a deep bond with someone she chose never to meet, finding presence without meeting.
