Kural 927

Abstinence from Liquor · Kalunnaamai · கள்ளுண்ணாமை

உள்ளொற்றி உள்ளூர் நகப்படுவர் எஞ்ஞான்றும் கள்ளொற்றிக் கண்சாய் பவர்.
Ullotri ulloor nagappaduvar enjnjaandrum kallotrik kansaai pavar.

Meaning

People who drink in secret until their eyes droop will have their secret found out by the people of their town, who will always laugh at them.

Elementary

If you sneak cookies before dinner, the crumbs on your shirt give you away. The Kural says people who drink in secret are the same: their sleepy, drooping eyes tell everyone, and the whole neighbourhood ends up laughing.

University

The verb otri (ஒற்றி, 'in hiding') is used twice. Kal otri (கள்ளொற்றி) is drinking toddy secretly, and ul otri (உள்ளொற்றி) is the townsfolk spying out what is inside. Kansaaibavar (கண்சாய்பவர்) are 'those whose eyes droop', the physical sign that betrays them. Ulloor (உள்ளூர், one's own town) makes the mockery (nagappaduvar, நகப்படுவர், 'will be laughed at') come from neighbours, not strangers. Mu. Varadarasanar reads ul otri as the townsfolk probing into hidden matters. Kalaignar stresses that the reeling eyes cannot be hidden. thirukkural.net prints the couplet without the closing full stop; the wording is otherwise identical on both base sites.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
The Manusmriti prescribes that a twice-born man convicted of drinking sura be branded on the forehead with the sign of a tavern flag, so that his offence is publicly visible and he is shunned.
— Manusmriti, Chapter 9 (punishments for the great sins) (Bühler trans.) [3]
Drinking is made permanently public, so everyone in the community knows.
As in the Kural, the drinker's secret ends in public exposure and scorn.
Christianity
“But they also have erred through wine, and through strong drink are out of the way; the priest and the prophet have erred through strong drink, they are swallowed up of wine, … they err in vision, they stumble in judgment.”
— Isaiah 28:7 (KJV) [4]
Isaiah publicly exposes priests and prophets whose drinking shows in their failing sight and judgment.
Even respected men cannot hide drink; it shows in the eyes and steps, as the Kural says.
Islam
When a man who had drunk wine was brought to the Prophet and beaten as a penalty, some onlookers cursed him; the Prophet forbade them, saying not to help Satan against their brother.
— Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Hudud (the drinker brought for punishment) [5]
The drinker's exposure brings the community's scorn, which the Prophet himself checks.
The townsfolk's ridicule the Kural describes is real, though Islam here restrains it with mercy.
Judaism
“Who cries, ‘Woe!’ who, ‘Alas!’; Who has quarrels, who complaints; Who has wounds without cause; Who has bleary eyes? Those whom wine keeps till the small hours, Those who gather to drain the cups.”
— Proverbs 23:29-30 (JPS Tanakh 1985, via Sefaria) [6]
Proverbs names the drinker's tell-tale bleary eyes, earned in late-night drinking.
The ‘bleary eyes’ are the Kural's kansaai, drooping eyes that give the secret away.
Buddhism
The monk Sāgata, famed for taming a fierce nāga, was given strong drink by admiring lay followers and was found lying drunk at the town gate; the Buddha rebuked him before the Sangha and laid down the rule against intoxicants.
— Vinaya Pitaka, Pacittiya 51 (Sāgata story) [7]
A celebrated monk is exposed in public, and his fall brings the whole order into disrepute.
As in the Kural, drink exposed at the town gate turns respect into public shame.
Jainism
The Daśavaikālika Sūtra warns the monk not to drink surā or other intoxicants, and describes one who drinks alone in secret, thinking that nobody knows, whose faults and deceit nonetheless become plain for others to see, so that his disgrace grows.
— Daśavaikālika Sūtra 5.2 (second part of the chapter on alms-seeking, Piṇḍaiṣaṇā; vv. 36 ff. in standard numbering) [8]
Jain monastic teaching treats secret drinking as a double fault, the drinking and the deceit of hiding it, and says such a monk is found out and loses his good standing.
Valluvar says those who drink in secret until their eyes droop will be found out and laughed at in their own town; the Daśavaikālika makes the same point, that the secret drinker who thinks no one sees him is exposed.
Sikhism
“Their falsehood and fraud cannot remain concealed; their false appearances fall off in the end.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 303, Raag Gauree, Gauree ki Vaar, Guru Ram Das (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Ram Das says that people who live behind a false front are found out in the end, when the coating wears off. What is done in secret does not stay hidden.
The Kural’s secret drinker, betrayed by his drooping eyes and laughed at by his own town, is one case of the exposure Guru Ram Das describes.
Greek Mythology
Phrygian peasants find old Silenus wandering drunk, bind him with garlands and lead him to King Midas.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book 11 [10]
Dionysus' companion is caught in drink and paraded before a king.
The drunk who stumbles is found out and made a spectacle by the locals, as in the Kural.
Philosophy
“Besides, we forget who we are, we utter words that are halting and poorly enunciated, the glance is unsteady, the step falters, the head is dizzy, …”
— Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 83.21 (Gummere trans.) [11]
Seneca lists the bodily signs that give drunkenness away.
‘The glance is unsteady’ is the Kural's kansaai: the eyes betray the hidden drinker.

Real Life Example

George Cassiday, the Man in the Green Hat, exposes Congress's secret drinking
1920-1930 (arrest 18 February 1930) · United States Capitol, Washington, D.C., United States
Throughout Prohibition, a bootlegger named George Cassiday quietly delivered liquor in a leather briefcase to the offices of members of the House and Senate, beginning with two House members who had voted for Prohibition. After a 1925 arrest in the House office building made him famous as the Man in the Green Hat, he moved his trade to the Senate side, until Prohibition agents caught him with six bottles of gin in the senators' parking lot on 18 February 1930. He then wrote a front-page series for the Washington Post describing how he had helped about 80 percent of lawmakers break the law, and senators demanded in vain that his client list be published to clear their names.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Lawmakers who publicly supported Prohibition while drinking in private became a national symbol of hypocrisy once their supplier told his story.
The kural warns that those who drink in secret will have their secret found out and be laughed at by their own townspeople, and Congress's hidden drinking was exposed and mocked across the nation.
