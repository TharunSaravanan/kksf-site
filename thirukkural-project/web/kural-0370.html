Kural 370

The Extirpation of Desire · Avaavaruthal · அவாவறுத்தல்

ஆரா இயற்கை அவாநீப்பின் அந்நிலையே பேரா இயற்கை தரும்.
Aaraa iyarkai avaa neeppin annilaiye Peraa iyarkai tharum.

Meaning

Desire by its nature can never be satisfied. Whoever throws it away gains, at that very moment, a lasting happiness that never changes.

Elementary

Trying to fill a bucket that has a hole in the bottom never works. You pour and pour, and it’s never full. Valluvar says wanting is that leaky bucket. Stop pouring into it, and you find a calm that never runs out.

University

The final couplet crowns the chapter with a mirrored wordplay: aaraa iyarkai (a nature that is never filled) against peraa iyarkai (a nature that never departs). Insatiability is what defines desire, and permanence is the reward for giving it up, the unchanging state that commentators identify with liberation (veedu). The word annilaiye (“in that very state” or “at that very moment”) suggests the reward is not postponed to some later heaven but is the same thing as the act of letting go. As the last couplet on renunciation before the chapter on Destiny that closes the Book of Virtue, it sums up Valluvar’s whole ascetic path: the endlessly hungry becomes the endlessly at peace.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“Thus the wise living entity’s pure consciousness becomes covered by his eternal enemy in the form of lust, which is never satisfied and which burns like fire.”
— Bhagavad Gita 3.39 (Bhagavad-gītā As It Is) [3]
Krishna describes desire (kama) as duspura, “never to be satisfied”, a fire that only grows the more it is fed.
The Gita’s “never satisfied” matches the Kural’s aaraa iyarkai, the nature that is never filled.
Christianity
“Jesus answered and said unto her, Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again: But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life.”
— John 4:13-14 (KJV) [4]
Jesus contrasts water that leaves people thirsty again with “living water” that ends thirst forever.
Both contrast an endless thirst with an everlasting, unchanging fullness.
Islam
“If Adam’s son had a valley full of gold, he would like to have two valleys, for nothing fills his mouth except dust. And Allah forgives him who repents to Him.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 6439 [5]
The Prophet teaches that human greed is bottomless and only death (“dust”) finally fills it.
This is a direct parallel to the Kural’s aaraa iyarkai: desire is by nature never full.
Judaism
“He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver; nor he that loveth abundance with increase: this is also vanity.”
— Ecclesiastes 5:10 (KJV) [4]
Kohelet observes that the lover of wealth is never satisfied, however much he gains.
Both identify never being satisfied as the defining nature of desire.
Buddhism
“There is no satisfying sensual desires, even with the rain of gold coins. For sensual pleasures give little satisfaction and much pain. Having understood this, the wise man finds no delight even in heavenly pleasures. The disciple of the Supreme Buddha delights in the destruction of craving.”
— Dhammapada 186-187 (trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [6]
The Buddha teaches that even a rain of gold cannot satisfy desire, and that the wise delight in the destruction of craving.
This matches both halves of the Kural: desire is insatiable, and joy lies in ending it.
Jainism
“The more you get, the more you want; your desires increase with your means. Though two mâshas would do to supply your want, still you would scarcely think ten millions sufficient.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 8.17, with Jacobi's note on the Kapila legend (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895; sacred-texts.com) [7]
In the story Jacobi summarises from the commentary, the student Kapila is offered whatever he asks by a king. He keeps raising the sum in his mind until it reaches ten thousand millions. Then he suddenly sees that desire can never be filled, renounces on the spot as a self-enlightened sage, and speaks this verse.
Valluvar says desire is by nature never satisfied, and whoever casts it off at once gains a nature that never changes. Kapila's verse states the insatiability, and his sudden enlightenment on giving desire up acts out the second line.
Sikhism
“Earning a thousand, he runs after a hundred thousand. Satisfaction is not obtained by chasing after Maya.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 278-279, Raag Gauree, Sukhmani Sahib, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
In Sukhmani Guru Arjan shows the endless escalation of wanting: every gain only starts the chase for more, and no satisfaction comes from Maya.
The Kural says desire by nature can never be filled; Guru Arjan pictures exactly that insatiable nature, and the same passage goes on to place lasting peace in the Name.
Greek Mythology
Erysichthon cuts down Ceres’ sacred oak and is punished with Famine. The more he eats, the hungrier he becomes. He spends his wealth, sells his daughter, and finally devours his own body.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.738-878 (paraphrase) [9]
His curse is an appetite that can never be filled and only grows as it is fed.
He is a mythic picture of the Kural’s aaraa iyarkai, the nature that is never satisfied.
Philosophy
“The wealth required by nature is limited and is easy to procure; but the wealth required by vain ideals extends to infinity.”
— Epicurus, Principal Doctrines 15 (trans. R. D. Hicks) [10]
Epicurus distinguishes natural needs, which have limits, from vain desires, which have no limit and can never be satisfied.
Both identify limitless, insatiable desire as the problem, and both locate peace in stepping out of it.

Real Life Example

Bhutan adopts Gross National Happiness
1970s · Bhutan
In the 1970s Bhutan's fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, coined the term Gross National Happiness and asserted that it mattered more than measures of economic output such as gross national or domestic product. Bhutan's development plans have since been guided by this idea, which weighs material growth alongside wellbeing, culture and the environment. The government later developed a Gross National Happiness Index to measure it.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Bhutan made it national policy that endless growth in output is not the goal, setting a lasting kind of wellbeing above ever-rising consumption.
The kural says desire by its nature can never be satisfied and that lasting contentment comes only when it is set aside, an idea Bhutan applied to a whole economy by refusing to make ever-greater output its highest aim.
