Kural 484

Knowledge of Apt Timing · Kaalamarithal · காலமறிதல்

ஞாலம் கருதினுங் கைகூடுங் காலம் கருதி இடத்தாற் செயின்.
Gnaalam Karudhinung Kaikootung Kaalam Karudhi Itaththaar Seyin.

Meaning

Even if you aim to win the whole world, you can get it, as long as you choose the right time and the right place to act.

Elementary

A surfer can ride a huge wave, but only by paddling out at the right moment and at the right spot. Pick the right time and place, and even very big goals become reachable.

University

Gnaalam karudhinum means ‘even if one sets one’s mind on the earth’, that is, universal dominion, and kaikoodum means ‘it will come into one’s hand’. The condition joins kaalam karudhi (considering the time) with idaththaal seyin (acting by means of the place). This anticipates the next chapter, Idanarithal (Knowing the Place, Kurals 491–500). Parimelazhagar explains that idam here means a place of action favourable to oneself and difficult for the enemy. Time and place are treated as the two coordinates of successful action, as in the kāla and deśa of Sanskrit statecraft. Both sources give identical text; ‘karudhinung’ carries the nasal sandhi of karudhinum before k-.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
A widely quoted verse of Chāṇakya Nīti tells a person to think again and again about what the time is, who are friends, what the place is, what income and expenditure are, who one is, and what one’s strength is.
— Chāṇakya Nīti 4.18 [3]
Chanakya’s checklist puts time and place first among the questions to settle before acting.
The same two coordinates, kaalam and idam, are what the Kural says make even world-conquest possible.
Christianity
Speaking at the Areopagus in Athens, Paul says that God made all nations from one blood and fixed both their appointed times and the boundaries of where they live.
— Acts 17:26 (KJV) [4]
Paul presents history as ordered by the right times and the right places, under divine providence.
The Kural gives the same two coordinates, time and place, as the conditions for great achievement.
Islam
After the Quraysh allies broke the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, the Prophet marched on Mecca in 630 CE. He had gathered a large force secretly and approached from several directions at once. The city surrendered with almost no fighting.
— Qur’an 48 and 110; sira accounts of the Conquest of Mecca (Fath Makka) [5]
Years of patience were followed by a move at a well-chosen moment and by well-chosen routes, which won the Prophet’s central goal with little bloodshed.
Choosing the moment (the broken treaty) and the approach (several routes into Mecca) brought the greatest aim within reach, as the Kural promises.
Judaism
“…and who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?”
— Esther 4:14 (KJV) [4]
Mordecai urges Esther to act. She is now queen, in the one place (the Persian court) where she can save her people, at the one moment when it matters.
Being in the right place at the right time turns an apparently impossible rescue into a real one.
Buddhism
After his awakening, the Buddha at first hesitated to teach. When Brahmā Sahampati asked him, he surveyed the world and saw beings like lotuses at different stages of emergence from the water. Judging the time right, he went to the Deer Park at Isipatana to give his first sermon to the five ascetics, who were ready to understand.
— Saṃyutta Nikāya 6.1 (Āyācana Sutta) and the Vinaya Mahāvagga narrative [6]
The Buddha chose both the moment and the place and audience for the teaching that would spread across Asia.
A world-changing aim was achieved by judging kaalam and idam precisely.
Jainism
In Hemacandra's account of the first universal monarch, Bharata, son of Rishabhanatha, sets out on his conquest of the six divisions of Bharatavarsha only after the discus-jewel (cakra-ratna) appears in his armoury, and by a long, staged campaign brings the whole land under his rule. Jain tradition then shows him renouncing it all and attaining omniscience.
— Trishashtishalakapurushacharitra of Hemacandra, Book 1, Bharata's conquest (tr. Helen M. Johnson, GOS; paraphrase) [7]
The Jain Puranic tradition does contain a world-conquest that succeeds when the sign of the right moment has appeared, but it treats such dominion as worldly and ultimately to be given up.
The kural promises that even the whole world comes within reach if time and place are rightly chosen; Bharata's digvijaya is the Jain example of exactly such a conquest, though Jainism values his later renunciation more highly.
Sikhism
Ranjit Singh rose to pre-eminence among the clans of the Sikh confederacy, captured Lahore in 1799 and went on to extend his rule across the Punjab, founding the Sikh kingdom over which he ruled as maharaja.
— Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Ranjit Singh" by Khushwant Singh (paraphrase) [8]
Starting as one chief among many, Ranjit Singh built a kingdom out of a divided Punjab by moving at the right moment and from the right base.
The Kural says even the whole world can be won by one who picks the right time and place; Ranjit Singh's rise from Lahore to rule of the Punjab is a Sikh historical case of such a gain.
Greek Mythology
Zeus did not challenge Cronus while still a child hidden on Crete. He grew up in secret and freed his swallowed siblings. He then released the Cyclopes and Hundred-Handers from beneath the earth, gaining thunderbolts and allies, before fighting the Titans. He won rule of the cosmos.
— Hesiod, Theogony, lines 468–506 and 617–735 [9]
The eventual lord of the world waited for the time of his strength and chose his ground and allies before fighting.
Dominion over the world came through timing and position, as Valluvar says.
Philosophy
“Without that opportunity their powers of mind would have been extinguished, and without those powers the opportunity would have come in vain.”
— Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, ch. VI (tr. W. K. Marriott) [10]
Machiavelli says Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus owed nothing to fortune but opportunity: Cyrus, for example, had to find the Persians discontented with the Medes and the Medes softened by long peace before he could found his empire.
As in the Kural, the winning of great kingdoms depends on acting when time and circumstance are right.

Real Life Example

The Greek fleet defeats the Persians in the straits of Salamis
September 480 BCE · Strait of Salamis, Greece
The Greek allied fleet, smaller than the Persian navy of Xerxes, took position in the narrow strait between the island of Salamis and the Attic coast. The Persian ships moved into position during the night and entered the strait in the early morning, where the confined waters hampered the larger fleet, and the Greek triremes rammed and boarded many of them. The defeat forced Xerxes to postpone his land campaign, giving the Greek city-states time to unite against him.
— Sources: [11] [12]
By fighting where their smaller numbers counted for most and the enemy's for least, the Greeks turned back the largest empire of their age.
The kural says even the world can be won by one who chooses the right time and acts in the right place, and Salamis was won by forcing the battle into a place of the Greeks' choosing.
