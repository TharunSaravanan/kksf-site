Kural 1313

Feigned Anger · Pulavi Nunukkam · புலவி நுணுக்கம்

கோட்டுப்பூச் சூடினும் காயும் ஒருத்தியைக் காட்டிய சூடினீர் என்று.
Koattup-pooch chootinum kaayum oruththiyaik kaattiya sootineer endru

Meaning

Even if I wear flowers picked from a tree, she gets angry and says I put them on to show off to some other woman.

Elementary

Suppose you wear a new cap to school and your best friend frowns, ‘Who are you trying to impress?’ You only wanted to look nice! The husband here wears flowers and his wife pretends he must be dressing up for someone else.

University

From here the speaker is generally taken to be the husband, reporting the ‘fine points’ of his wife’s sulking. Kōṭṭuppū means flowers that bloom on tree branches (kōṭu), as opposed to creeper or water flowers; even if he wears (cūṭiṉum) such a garland, she grows hot with anger (kāyum), saying ‘you wore them to show (kāṭṭiya) to some woman (oruttiyai)’. Puliyur Kesikan reads the line as the husband putting the flowers on her, while Mu. Varadarasanar, Solomon Pappaiah and Kalaignar take him to be wearing them himself; Pope follows the latter: ‘She cries, “Pray, for what woman now do you put on your flowers?”’ The text is the same on ytamizh.com and thirukkural.net.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
In the Harivamsa and later retellings, Krishna gives a blossom of the heavenly Parijata tree to Rukmini, and Satyabhama, hurt that the flower went to another wife, sulks until Krishna promises to bring her the whole tree.
— Harivamsa, Vishnu Parva, Parijata-harana episode (paraphrase) [3]
The story makes a single flower the trigger for a queen’s jealous, sulking anger.
In the Kural, too, a garland of flowers is enough to make the wife suspect another woman.
Christianity
“Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame.”
— Song of Solomon 8:6 (KJV) [4]
The Song of Songs admits that love brings jealousy with it, burning as fiercely as love itself.
The wife’s quick flare of jealousy over a garland is a small spark of the fire this verse describes.
Islam
When one of the Prophet’s wives sent a dish of food to the house where he was staying, the wife of that house struck the servant’s hand so the dish broke; the Prophet gathered the pieces and said, “Your mother (my wife) felt jealous,” and replaced the dish.
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5225 (sunnah.com) [5]
The Prophet met a wife’s sudden jealousy with gentle understanding instead of anger.
Like that broken dish, the garland in the Kural is an innocent object that jealousy turns into a grievance.
Judaism
“Wrath is cruel, and anger is overwhelming; but who is able to stand before jealousy?”
— Proverbs 27:4 (JPS 1917, via Mechon-Mamre) [6]
Proverbs ranks jealousy as harder to withstand than plain anger.
The husband finds that no innocent explanation of his flowers can stand before his wife’s jealousy.
Buddhism
“From endearment springs grief, from endearment springs fear. For one who is wholly free from endearment there is no grief, whence then fear?”
— Dhammapada 212 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha teaches that fondness for someone breeds fear of losing them.
The wife’s fear that the flowers are meant for another woman grows out of her very fondness for her husband.
Jainism
“If you owned the beauty of Vaiśramaṇa, the pleasing manners of Nalakūbara, if you were like Purandara himself, I should have no desire for you.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 22.41 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
Rajimati tells the would-be lover Rathanemi that no amount of beauty or charm will win her.
The husband’s adornment likewise fails to soften his wife, who reads his flowers as a fault rather than a charm.
Sikhism
“O soul-bride, without your Husband Lord, what good are your decorations?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 18, Siree Raag, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Nanak teaches that the soul's adornments mean nothing unless they are for the Beloved.
The Kural's wife accuses her husband of wearing a garland to show some other woman; Gurbani also asks whom one's adornment is for, saying only the Beloved gives it meaning.
Greek Mythology
In Sophocles’ Women of Trachis, Deianira, afraid that Heracles has come to love the captive Iole, sends him a robe to win back his love, not knowing it is poisoned.
— Sophocles, Trachiniae (paraphrase) [10]
The tragedy shows how a wife’s jealous fear, centred on clothing and adornment, can spiral out of control.
The Kural’s scene is the gentle, comic version: an adornment provokes a wife’s suspicion of another woman.
Philosophy
Spinoza defines jealousy as hatred toward a loved object joined with envy of a rival, arising when we imagine the one we love being bound to another as closely as to us.
— Spinoza, Ethics III, Prop. 35 and Scholium (paraphrase) [11]
For Spinoza, jealousy begins in imagination: we picture the beloved united with someone else.
The wife imagines her husband dressing up for another woman, exactly the imagined rival Spinoza describes.

Real Life Example

Othello, the play of the misread handkerchief, is staged at court
1604 · London, England
William Shakespeare wrote Othello in about 1603 to 1604. The Revels accounts for 1604 to 1605 record a performance of the Moor of Venis by the King's Men at the court of King James I, one of the earliest records of the play. In the story the villain Iago uses a handkerchief that Desdemona has innocently lost to persuade her husband Othello that she has given it to another man as a love token.
— Sources: [12] [13]
The play became one of the most famous studies of jealousy in world literature because it shows how an innocent object can be twisted into false proof of love for someone else.
Like the wife in this kural who decides that a simple garland must have been worn to impress another woman, Othello reads an innocent handkerchief as a sign meant for a rival.
