Kural 333

Impermanence · Nilaiyaamai · நிலையாமை

அற்கா இயல்பிற்றுச் செல்வம் அதுபெற்றால் அற்குப ஆங்கே செயல்.
Arkaa iyalbitruch selvam adhu petraal Arkupa aange seyal.

Meaning

Riches never stay for long. So when you have them, use them right away to do good deeds that will last.

Elementary

If you are given ice cream on a hot day, you should share it right away before it melts. This kural says money is like that ice cream: it won't stay, so use it for good things now, while you still have it.

University

The couplet turns on a deliberate play between “arkaa” (that which does not stay) and “arkupa” (things that do stay, that is, enduring deeds), both drawn from the verb ‘ar’, to abide. Valluvar thus converts a descriptive claim about wealth into an imperative: since wealth is non-abiding, use it for what abides, and do so “aange” (then and there, at once). Commentators identify the enduring deeds with the householder virtues discussed earlier in the work, such as hospitality, charity and helpfulness (oppuravu). The logic is an ethical economics: a perishable asset should be exchanged immediately for an imperishable one before it spoils. The same move, turning transient riches into lasting merit, appears in the Gospel's ‘treasures in heaven’, the Quran's ‘enduring good deeds’, and Bhartrhari's teaching that wealth has only three destinies.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Bhartrhari teaches that wealth has only three possible destinies: it is given away, it is enjoyed, or it is destroyed. Whoever neither gives nor enjoys it is left with the third.
— Bhartrhari, Nitishataka, v. 43 (numbering varies by edition) [3]
The Sanskrit poet says wealth that is not given away or used will simply be lost.
Both texts start from wealth's instability and conclude that it should be turned into giving before it disappears.
Christianity
“Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal:”
— Matthew 6:19-20 (KJV) [4]
Jesus tells his listeners to stop hoarding wealth that decays and to store up lasting treasure through a godly life.
Swap perishable treasure for imperishable treasure: this is exactly the Kural's ‘arkaa’ turned into ‘arkupa’.
Islam
“Wealth and children are [but] adornment of the worldly life. But the enduring good deeds are better to your Lord for reward and better for [one's] hope.”
— Quran 18:46 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran contrasts wealth, which is decoration for this life, with ‘al-baqiyat as-salihat’, good deeds that endure.
The Quran's ‘enduring good deeds’ are the Kural's ‘arkupa’ (things that last), set against wealth that does not.
Judaism
“Riches profit not in the day of wrath: but righteousness delivereth from death.”
— Proverbs 11:4 (KJV) [4]
Proverbs teaches that wealth is useless at the decisive moment, while righteousness (tzedakah, which also came to mean charity) saves.
Both texts rank righteous action above wealth because only the former survives the final reckoning.
Buddhism
“Truly, misers fare not to heavenly realms; nor, indeed, do fools praise generosity. But the wise man rejoices in giving, and by that alone does he become happy hereafter.”
— Dhammapada 177 [6]
The Buddha teaches that hoarding leads nowhere good, while giving brings lasting happiness.
Giving turns passing wealth into lasting benefit, which is what the Kural urges.
Jainism
“Whenever you die, O king, and leave all pleasant things behind, the Law alone, and nothing else in this world, will save you, O monarch.”
— Uttarādhyayana Sūtra 14.40 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45, 1895) [7]
Queen Kamalāvatī tells King Ishukāra that riches are left behind at death and only dharma endures.
The Kural urges using unstable wealth at once for lasting deeds; the Uttarādhyayana likewise contrasts possessions that must be left with the Law that alone endures.
Sikhism
“One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1245, Raag Saarang, Saarang ki Vaar, Salok, Guru Nanak (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [8]
Guru Nanak names the person who earns honestly and shares part of it as the one who knows the true path; this is the Sikh pillar of vand chhakna, sharing one's earnings.
The Kural says that since wealth does not stay, it should be used at once for good deeds that last; Guru Nanak likewise points the householder's wealth towards giving.
Greek Mythology
The poor old couple Baucis and Philemon gladly share their meagre food and wine with two disguised strangers who turn out to be Zeus and Hermes; the gods save them from a flood and turn their hut into a temple.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.618-724 [9]
Ovid's tale rewards a couple who used what little they had for hospitality right away, without holding back.
Giving at once from what you have, rather than clinging to it, is the Kural's instruction for using unstable wealth.
Philosophy
Lady Philosophy asks Boethius whether riches are precious by their own nature, and argues that money shines more brightly when it is spent on others than when it is hoarded, since avarice makes people hated and generosity makes them honoured.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book II, prose 5 [10]
In Boethius' dialogue, Philosophy shows that wealth only has real value when it is given away.
Both texts say the only good use of passing wealth is to put it at once into generous action.

Real Life Example

Jamsetji Tata endows half his fortune for the Indian Institute of Science
1898 · Bombay (Mumbai), India
In 1898 the industrialist Jamsetji Tata set aside 14 buildings and four landed properties in Bombay, worth about 30 lakh rupees and roughly half his wealth, as an endowment for a research university of science. He kept the offer open through years of negotiation with the colonial government and reaffirmed it in a codicil to his will shortly before he died in 1904. The Indian Institute of Science was established in Bangalore by a vesting order in 1909 and admitted its first students in 1911.
— Sources: [11] [12]
Tata turned a large part of a fortune that could have been lost or spent into an institution that has trained generations of Indian scientists. The gift has outlasted him by more than a century.
Valluvar says that since wealth does not stay, one should at once turn it into deeds that do stay, and Tata converted half his fortune into a lasting institution of learning.
