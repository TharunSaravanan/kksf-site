Kural 1226

Lamentations at Even-tide · Pozhudhukandirankal · பொழுதுகண்டிரங்கல்

மாலைநோய் செய்தல் மணந்தார் அகலாத காலை அறிந்த திலேன்.
Maalainoi seydhal manandhaar akalaadha kaalai arindha thilaen.

Meaning

While my husband had not yet left my side, I never knew how much pain evening could bring.

Elementary

When your best friend is next to you, you do not notice how quiet the house is. Only when they go away do you notice the silence. The wife says she never knew evenings could hurt until her husband left.

University

The couplet turns on the contrast between maṇantār akalāta kālai (‘the time when he who wed me had not departed’) and the present, in which mālai nōy, ‘the evening sickness’, has made itself known. Kālai here is a pun: in the previous couplet it meant ‘morning’, but here it is the old Tamil word for ‘time, season’, a play the commentators make explicit by glossing it as kālam. Solomon Pappaiah stresses that the same evening once gave her joy; Varadarasanar and Kesikan read aṟintatilēṉ as ‘I did not know at all’. Pope: ‘The pangs that evening brings I never knew, / Till he, my wedded spouse, from me withdrew.’ Both web sources give the same text.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
Nala, ruined at dice, slips away at night while Damayanti sleeps in the forest; she wakes alone, searches for him, and laments that she never imagined he would leave her.
— Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana (paraphrase; cf. tr. K.M. Ganguli) [3]
The Mahabharata’s tale shows a devoted wife who discovers the full pain of the night only after her husband has gone.
Like Damayanti, the Kural’s wife learned what night-time grief was only after her husband left her side.
Christianity
“Can the children of the bridechamber mourn, as long as the bridegroom is with them? but the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken from them, and then shall they fast.”
— Matthew 9:15 (KJV) [4]
Jesus explains that there is no mourning while the bridegroom is present, but sorrow will come when he is taken away.
The Kural states the same truth from the wife’s side: while her bridegroom was with her she knew no evening sorrow, and it came only when he left.
Islam
“And the heart of Moses’ mother became empty [of all else]. She was about to disclose [the matter concerning] him had We not bound fast her heart that she would be of the believers.”
— Qur’an 28:10 (Saheeh International) [5]
After setting her infant afloat, Moses’ mother feels the full emptiness of separation, and God steadies her heart.
The Kural’s wife likewise discovers an emptiness she had never known once her beloved is gone.
Judaism
“I opened to my beloved; But my beloved had turned away, and was gone. My soul failed me when he spoke. I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.”
— Song of Songs 5:6 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
The beloved in the Song of Songs discovers his absence only after the moment of nearness has passed.
The Kural’s wife also learns the depth of her love’s pain only when he has gone and she cannot find him.
Buddhism
In Ashvaghosha’s Buddhacarita, when the charioteer Chandaka returns without Prince Siddhartha, his wife Yashodhara falls to the ground and laments bitterly that her husband has gone and left her alone.
— Ashvaghosha, Buddhacarita, canto 8 (paraphrase; cf. tr. E.B. Cowell, SBE 49) [7]
The Buddhist epic gives voice to the wife whose life changed in an instant when her husband departed.
Yashodhara, like the Kural’s wife, learns the full weight of grief only when her husband has gone away.
Jainism
“How can a man who passionately desires ‘colours,’ be happy while he gets, keeps, uses, loses, and misses (those things). Even when he enjoys them, he is never satisfied.”
— Uttaradhyayana Sutra 32.28 (tr. Hermann Jacobi, SBE 45) [8]
The Uttaradhyayana teaches that whatever we cling to will bring pain at the stages of losing and missing it.
The Kural’s wife has reached the ‘losing and missing’ stage, and finds a pain she never suspected while she still possessed his company.
Sikhism
“If my Husband Lord comes to my home, then I obtain everything.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1361, Phunhay, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan's bride-soul says that the Lord's presence in her home is all she needs; everything else follows from it.
The Kural's wife never knew evening could hurt until her husband left; the Guru's bride likewise finds that with the Husband at home nothing is lacking, so pain enters only with his absence.
Greek Mythology
Laodamia, newly married to Protesilaus, writes to him after he sails for Troy, describing how his departure has turned her days and nights into anxious grief she never knew while he was home.
— Ovid, Heroides 13 (Laodamia to Protesilaus) (paraphrase) [10]
Ovid’s heroine is a young wife who learns the meaning of separation only when her husband goes away.
The Kural’s wife and Laodamia make the same discovery: the pain of absence was unknown until the husband left.
Philosophy
Lady Philosophy reminds Boethius that in every adversity of fortune, the most wretched kind of misfortune is to have once been happy.
— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book 2, prose 4 (paraphrase; cf. tr. H.R. James) [11]
Boethius observes that past happiness sharpens present loss.
The wife’s evenings hurt because they were once happy with her husband, exactly the bitterness Boethius describes.

Real Life Example

Maria Theresa goes into lifelong mourning for Francis Stephen
1765 · Innsbruck and Vienna, Austria
In 1765 Emperor Francis Stephen died suddenly at Innsbruck, where the imperial family had gathered for the wedding of their son Leopold. His widow, Empress Maria Theresa, cut her hair short, wore widow's black for the rest of her life, largely withdrew from public life and gave up most of her jewellery. She lived about fifteen more years and was buried beside him in a double sarcophagus in the Capuchin crypt in Vienna.
— Sources: [12] [13]
Her mourning is a well-documented example of a ruler whose life was visibly changed by the loss of her husband.
The kural's wife says she never knew such suffering while her husband was still at her side, and Maria Theresa's life after 1765 was marked by a grief she had not known while Francis Stephen lived.
