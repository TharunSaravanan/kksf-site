Kural 83

Cherishing Guests · Virundhombal · விருந்தோம்பல்

வருவிருந்து வைகலும் ஓம்புவான் வாழ்க்கை பருவந்து பாழ்படுதல் இன்று.
Varuvirundhu vaigalum ombuvaan vaazhkkai, Paruvandhu paazhpadudhal indru.

Meaning

A family that welcomes guests every single day will never be ruined by doing it.

Elementary

People sometimes worry: if I keep giving food away to visitors, won't I run out? This kural says no — like a well that keeps filling back up the more you draw from it, a home that keeps feeding guests doesn't dry up. Giving every day doesn't empty the house.

University

Having established the duty, Valluvar now answers the obvious objection: won't constant hospitality bankrupt you? The reply hinges on two compact words — பருவந்து (suffering hardship, being worn down) and பாழ்படுதல் (falling into waste, ruin). வைகலும் ('every day') is essential: the claim is specifically about habitual, repeated hospitality, not a single grand feast. Mu. Varadarajan's modern commentary reads the verse as a promise that such a life will never be ruined 'on account of that' — the causal clause matters, since Valluvar is not promising immunity from all misfortune but denying that hospitality is the cause of any. This is characteristic of the Kural's ethical naturalism: rather than invoking divine reward, it states a claim about how generous households actually fare, one echoed in the near-universal folk motif of the vessel that never empties.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
“The riches of the liberal never waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him.”
— Rig Veda 10.117.1 (trans. Ralph T. H. Griffith) [4]
Rig Veda 10.117, the hymn 'Liberality,' is an unusually direct piece of Vedic social ethics, opening with the declaration that the gods did not ordain hunger as a form of death and that generosity is self-sustaining.
This is the Kural's exact claim — the giver's substance is not depleted by giving.
Christianity
“For thus saith the LORD God of Israel, The barrel of meal shall not waste, neither shall the cruse of oil fail, until the day that the LORD sendeth rain upon the earth.”
— 1 Kings 17:14 (King James Version) [5]
A starving widow at Zarephath gives Elijah the last of her flour and oil during a famine; her jar and cruse then supply her household continuously for the duration of the drought.
The story is the Kural's promise dramatised: a household that feeds the arriving guest is not emptied by it.
Islam
“The food for two persons is sufficient for three, and the food of three persons is sufficient for four persons.”
— Sahih al-Bukhari 5392, Book of Food and Meals [6]
A practical maxim of the Prophet used to remove the standard excuse for turning someone away — that there simply isn't enough — by insisting that a household's portion always stretches.
Answers exactly the anxiety the Kural addresses: feeding one more arrival will not impoverish the house.
Judaism
Abraham, sitting at his tent door in the heat of the day, sees three strangers, runs to meet them, bows, fetches water for their feet, hurries Sarah to bake fresh cakes and has a calf dressed for them — and in return receives the promise that Sarah will bear a son.
— Genesis 18:1–15 (King James Version) [5]
Genesis 18 became the founding text of hachnasat orchim; the rabbis noted that Abraham, aged and recovering from circumcision, still ran, and that the visit brought his household its future rather than costing it anything.
The household that lavishes itself on arriving guests is not diminished but established — precisely the Kural's claim.
Buddhism
In the Sīha Sutta the Buddha lists the results of generosity visible in this present life: the generous giver is dear and agreeable to many, good people keep company with him, a good reputation spreads about him, he enters any assembly confidently — and only fifth does he mention rebirth in a heavenly world.
— Aṅguttara Nikāya 5.34, Sīha Sutta (To General Sīha) [7]
The sutta was preached to a general who wanted to know whether giving pays off now rather than only after death; the Buddha answers that four of the five fruits are immediate and worldly.
Like the Kural, it insists that habitual giving strengthens rather than erodes the giver's actual position in this life.
Jainism
“Not saying they have very little, not saying they have not anything, let them ever exercise fruitful charity to all. Like the pitcher of the mendicant who enters the house-door for alms, it will, in due course, gradually become full.”
— Nālaṭiyār ch. 10 'Liberality', st. 9 (= st. 99), English translation in The Indian Antiquary vol. II (1873) [8]
The Jain-authored Tamil Nālaṭiyār answers the fear that giving drains a household. Regular giving fills up over time, like a mendicant's alms-pitcher filled a little at each door.
Valluvar promises that daily hospitality never wears a household into ruin, and the Nālaṭiyār makes the same promise with the image of a pitcher that only fills.
Sikhism
“The Siblings of Destiny meet together, and eat and spend, but these resources do not diminish; they continue to increase.”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 186, Raag Gauree, Guru Arjan (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
Guru Arjan describes a treasure that grows the more it is shared and spent together; the hymn's treasure is the divine Name, though the image draws on the experience of shared food and wealth.
Like the Kural's assurance that daily hospitality never ruins a household, Gurbani holds that what is shared is not exhausted but increases.
Greek Mythology
Jupiter and Mercury, disguised as travellers, are turned away from a thousand doors before the poor old couple Baucis and Philemon take them in; during the meal the wine bowl keeps refilling itself, and afterwards their cottage becomes a temple while the inhospitable neighbourhood is drowned.
— Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.611–724 (Baucis and Philemon) [10]
Ovid's most famous theoxeny takes pains to show that the couple's poverty was never an obstacle — they had almost nothing, gave all of it, and ended with more than they began.
The self-refilling bowl is a mythic image of the Kural's exact promise: the hospitable household is never worn down by its hospitality.
Philosophy
Aristotle argues that the virtues are acquired by repetition: we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, and generous by doing generous acts — habituation is what makes a disposition stable rather than exhausting.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1, 1103a–1103b [11]
For Aristotle, repeated generous action does not drain the agent but settles generosity into character, so that giving becomes easier and more natural rather than harder.
Supplies a philosophical mechanism for the Kural's 'day after day': habitual hospitality strengthens the host rather than wearing him out.

Real Life Example

Guru Amar Das makes the free kitchen central to Sikh life
16th century (guruship of Amar Das, 1552-1574) · Punjab, India
The Sikh langar, a free communal kitchen introduced by Guru Nanak, was strengthened by the third Guru, Amar Das, who made it a rule that anyone wishing to see him had first to eat in the langar, where all sit in rows as equals. The Mughal emperor Akbar is recorded as having eaten in the langar. The Guru ka Langar at the Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple) in Amritsar serves cooked food to visitors of any religion or background, day and night, with its costs met from temple funds.
— Sources: [12] [13] [14]
The langar shows that feeding all comers every day can be sustained across centuries without exhausting those who provide it.
The kural says the household that tends arriving guests day after day will never be worn down into ruin, and the langar has fed guests daily for generations without collapsing.
