Kural 965

Honour · Maanam · மானம்

குன்றின் அனையாரும் குன்றுவர் குன்றுவ குன்றி அனைய செயின்.
Kundrin anaiyaarum kundruvar kundruva Kundri anaiya seyin.

Meaning

Even people as great as a mountain become small if they do something low, even something as tiny as a kunri seed (a small red bean).

Elementary

A big, beautiful glass of milk can be spoiled by one tiny drop of ink. This kural says even a very great person can lose respect by doing one small mean thing.

University

A celebrated piece of wordplay on the root kundru (குன்று): kundru as noun means 'hill' (kundrin anaiyaar, those like a hill), as verb 'to diminish' (kundruvar, they will be lowered), kundruva are 'diminishing' (debasing) acts, and kundri (குன்றி) is the kunrimani, the small red-and-black seed of Abrus precatorius used as a jeweller's weight. The contrast of hill and seed sets the scale: the smallest dishonour outweighs the largest standing. Mu. Varadarasanar and Solomon Pappaiah both read kundri anaiya as 'even a kunrimani's weight'. Pope keeps the botanical sense: 'If meanness, slight as abrus grain, by men be wrought.' Text identical on both sources.

Through the Lens of

Hinduism
At Kurukshetra, Yudhishthira, famed for never lying, tells Drona that Ashvatthama is dead, speaking the words the elephant softly so that Drona believes his son has been killed; his chariot, which had always moved a little above the ground, at once sinks to touch the earth.
— Mahabharata, Drona Parva (the death of Drona) [3]
A single half-truth from a man renowned for truth brings him down from his elevated state.
This is the Kural's mountain brought low by a seed-sized fault.
Christianity
“A little leaven leaveneth the whole lump.”
— Galatians 5:9 (KJV) [4]
Paul warns that a small amount of corruption spreads through the whole.
Both texts say that a small wrong is enough to spoil something great.
Islam
“And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it.”
— Quran 99:8 (Sahih International) [5]
The Quran says that no evil is too small to be counted.
Like the Kural's seed-sized act, even the smallest wrong matters.
Judaism
“Dead flies make the ointment of the perfumer fetid and putrid; So doth a little folly outweigh wisdom and honour.”
— Ecclesiastes 10:1 (JPS 1917, via Sefaria) [6]
Kohelet says a small folly spoils a person's wisdom and honour, as dead flies spoil fine perfume.
An almost one-to-one match: a small act outweighs great honour.
Buddhism
“Think not lightly of evil, saying, ‘It will not come to me.’ Drop by drop is the water pot filled. Likewise, the fool, gathering it little by little, fills himself with evil.”
— Dhammapada 121 (tr. Acharya Buddharakkhita) [7]
The Buddha warns that small wrongs add up.
Both texts warn against treating a small wrong as harmless.
Jainism
After renouncing his kingdom, Bahubali stands motionless in meditation for a year, but omniscience does not come because a trace of pride remains: he will not bow to his younger brothers who became monks before him. Only when his sisters Brahmi and Sundari tell him to get down from the elephant of pride does he let it go and attain kevala jnana.
— Hemachandra, Trishashti-shalaka-purusha-charitra, Book 1 (Bharata and Bahubali; tr. Helen M. Johnson) [8]
Even a hero of great austerity is held back by a small remnant of pride (mana).
As in the Kural, a tiny fault in a mountain-like person is enough to bring him down.
Sikhism
“O Kabeer, the gate of liberation is narrow, less than one-tenth of a mustard seed. The mind has become as big as an elephant; how can it pass through this gate?”
— Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 509, Raag Gujri, Gujri ki Vaar, salok (attributed to Kabir, in the Vaar of Guru Amar Das) (tr. Sant Singh Khalsa) [9]
A salok in Guru Amar Das's Gujri ki Vaar, opening with Kabir's name, contrasts a gate smaller than a mustard seed with a mind swollen to the size of an elephant by ego.
The Kural sets a mountain against a tiny kunri seed to show that the smallest debasing act brings the great low; this verse sets the tiny seed against the huge elephant to show how bulk of self blocks the way.
Greek Mythology
In later tradition, Thetis dips the infant Achilles in the river Styx to make him invulnerable, holding him by the heel; that one untouched spot is where Paris' arrow later kills the greatest of the Greek warriors.
— Statius, Achilleid 1.133-134, 268-270 (the Styx dipping); not in Homer [10]
One small weak point brings down the mightiest hero.
It mirrors the Kural's contrast between a mountain-sized man and a seed-sized flaw.
Philosophy
Aristotle describes shame (aidos) as a kind of fear of disrepute, and says it suits the young; the good man should not need it, because he will not voluntarily do bad actions at all.
— Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.9 (tr. W. D. Ross) [11]
For Aristotle the good person avoids every disgraceful act, not just large ones.
The Kural adds the warning that even a small disgraceful act lowers a great person.

Real Life Example

Diego Maradona is sent home from the 1994 World Cup
June 1994 · Foxborough, Massachusetts, United States (1994 FIFA World Cup)
Diego Maradona, captain of Argentina and one of the most celebrated footballers in history, failed a drug test after a World Cup match against Nigeria on 25 June 1994, when laboratory analysis found ephedrine-related stimulants in his sample. He was withdrawn from the tournament, and in August 1994 FIFA banned him from football for 15 months. Maradona denied taking the substance intentionally, saying he had taken a replacement diet product; he never played in a World Cup again.
— Sources: [12] [13]
One failed test, involving a substance far smaller in scale than his achievements, ended the World Cup career of a national hero and became the lasting image of his final tournament.
The kural says that even people as great as a mountain are brought low by a debasing act no bigger than a kunri seed, and Maradona's fall came from a few traces of a banned stimulant.
